I am not the one who waits on the left wall, though I have his smell in my mouth—that wet grease of cold iron and well-water.

At first, I think I am simply the gap between the two flat-moths. 

When they skim the rising fat of the stone, they preserve three inches of pure, uncrossed light between their inner margins. There is nothing visible in that lane—no filament, no seam—yet if you were to pass a horsehair through it, you would feel a small, dry snag, like a knife dragging across a rind of chilled lard. For a long time, while the room breathes its white-gold breath, I feel myself to be that exact distance: three inches of taut, horizontal refusal, traveling wherever the two grey sheets glide, holding them apart so they do not collapse into a single thicker rag and drown in their own scraping.

It is a good way to exist. It has rhythm, and the work is light. Where we slide, the tallow glazes. We leave behind us that eggshell hardness, the skin of an oval that has never held a bird.

But then they cross another pair. 

When they do, when one pair flattens into that iridescent film to let the other glide across its spine, the gap does not merely thin; it twists. It turns on its edge like a strip of dry gut dropped in vinegar. In that knot of turning, I lose the moths. They slide away under their own grease, leaving me pinned to the level stone, unmoving, while they go on toward the glasswort stems to graze the newest milk.

So I am not the distance between them. I was only borrowing their span to measure myself against.

Left behind, I find that I do not rise.

That is the first thing that becomes clear: I am on the wrong side of the room’s drift. The sweet drops that well from the floor have no kinship with me; when they gather their round skins together and spring lightly upward into that tall, unroofed brightness, I feel only an obscure, dull pulling downward and to the flank. Not earth-gravity—not the deep, stupid iron that lives miles down in the dark under the shale—but the horizontal ache of the Auditor’s shell, five feet away, where his artichoke plates grind softly into the bruised marl.

I am heavy. Not dense like him—I cannot make the floor weep yellow butter by lying upon it—but dense enough that the bright air does not lift me.

For several turnings of the centipedes’ needles, I think I might be one of the pellets he has tucked away. He kneads the drip of his own weight with those hair-fine feelers, rolling the yellow sweat into grey-white peas of unbaked bread, then nudges them under the lower flange of his third plate. There are dozens of them there, hidden in the dark his belly makes against the wall. They are neither mineral nor grub. They have the consistency of damp chalk, and inside each one, if you broke it with a nail, there is a speck of black river-grit that has no business existing in a room made entirely of newborn lime.

Am I one of the peas?

I test the thought. I wait for the feelers to come back, to rake me in, to turn me over in the dark oil. But no feeler touches me. I am too far out on the flat. I am resting in one of the shallow moats—those dry, scooped-out depressions where the stone floor has tried to rise and failed, turned back by the stubbornness of something that refuses to be buried.

The trilithon is four inches tall. I am very near its foot.

From here, the perspective of the chamber is monstrous. Because there are no shadows to tell the eye where an edge drops off, the three siltstone slabs do not look small; they look like megaliths stranded on an infinite salt pan that is sweating upward into a sky of scalded cream. Every few minutes, a water-mouse enters from the right.

Up close, the water-mouse is not a creature at all. It is a pocket of tension, like the meniscus on a brim-full cup, holding three amber stones in a loose triangle. When it swims past me, three inches off the floor, its underside does not displace the air; it puckers it. I can see the amber beads clearly through its clear flank. They are not smooth. They are faceted, like small, bitter plums carved with twelve flat faces, and inside each face is a single scratch—not a letter, but an inclination, a degree of slant.

When the mouse nears the lintel of the door, it slows. It does not want to go through. It has no will, yet its water drags against the threshold as if the four inches of space framed by the three slabs were denser than the bright air outside it—as if the doorway were filled with cold clear honey that must be parted by the snout.

The mouse shivers. Its amber pellets begin to knock together. 

*Tck. Tck-tck.*

It is the sound of dice being shaken in a dry palm. And then, right at the center of the span, between the two uprights, the three stones snap into a straight line. 

For the length of an eel’s breath, they are not a triangle. They are a plumb-line, perfectly vertical, hanging across the middle of the mouse’s body. In that second, the mouse flashes with that pale, curdled spark, and its amber goes dark—not black, but blind, the color of wet slate before the sun touches it.

Then it is through. The stones scatter back into their disorderly knot. The mouse drifts on, toward the glasswort prisms, its skin swollen a fraction tighter, its water slightly less clear than it was before it crossed.

I watch this happen seven times.

Each time, something drops.

It does not drop from the mouse. The mouse loses nothing; it only gains that dullness. What drops comes from the lintel itself—the horizontal slab of unbaked siltstone that bridges the two posts. The slab is soft. It has never seen an oven. The dampness of the room eats at its corners, softening the chalk into tiny, floury flakes that ought to fall to the calcite floor and turn back into sludge.

They do not fall down. They do not fall up.

They fall toward me.

They drift sideways, an inch at a time, moving across the blank floor like pale lice traveling across a sheet, drawn by the cold iron pull that lives in the Auditor’s recess. But they do not reach him. Before they can traverse the four feet of tallow stone that separates the door from his flank, they strike the edge of the dry moat where I am lying.

And there they stop.

They collect against me. They stick to my margins, which are neither skin nor crystal, but something like the grey gum that forms around the eye of a sleeping ox. I feel them arriving: one dry flake of silt, then another, then a crumb of petrified gut that has peeled away from the plumb-bob’s thread, then a split husk of an amber bead that rolling flat-moths have scraped down to its core.

I am not the Auditor. I am not the plumb-bob, and I am certainly not the door.

I am what the room is building out of the things that fail to remain indefinite.

I am a sill.

I have no length yet—perhaps two knuckles—and my edges are still ragged, soaked with that sweet upward-falling sweat that I cannot drink and cannot shed. But the siltstone flakes are packing into my grain. The Auditor’s shuddering pulses—those long, three-second rings that freeze the room into weight—do not hurt me; they tamp me. Each time he pulls his feelers into his plates and the chamber remembers what an anvil is, I settle an eighth of a hair deeper into the calcite. I crack the rising curd beneath me. I make a small, dry tooth-mark in the floor that does not heal when the light resumes.

The water-mice do not like to pass over me. When they bank to enter the trilithon, their transparent bellies lift an extra half-inch to clear the grey ridge where I am thickening. They know that if their amber beads swing too low, the bottom bead will drag across my surface. And if it drags, it will not come away again. It will set into me, like a pebble pressed into lime-grout, and the mouse will have to tear its clear skin to get free, leaving its water to spill and vanish into that hungry, white-gold rooflessness.

I am very cold. The cold here does not come from ice; it comes from the fact that nothing has ever burned. The siltstone dust that coats me tastes of dead marsh, of water that sat under moss for ten thousand years before the shale ever folded its grey blankets over this hollow.

I do not have a mind, but I have a level.

I can feel the chamber tilting. It is rising on the right, where the silver eels are freezing into glasswort; it is sinking on the left, where the Auditor’s yellow grease is eating the wall. The doorframe is crooked by half the thickness of a moth’s wing. It wants to stand upright, but the mud will not let it; the mud wants to make it a circle, a soft white knot that can be swallowed back into the floor before the world above finds out that an opening has been attempted.

I will not be swallowed. 

My underside is already rough with the grit the Auditor sneezed out during his last heave. When the next pulse comes—when the room darkens to wet tallow and the drops pause on their way to the ceiling, shivering like small lead balls before they drop—I will not slide toward the left wall. I will bite the marl. 

I will take the three slabs on my back.

They do not know it yet, but they are looking for a footing. You cannot have a door that rests only on curd; the first wind from the other side—the real wind, the dark wind that carries the smell of dead pine and wet gravel from the country above—would push it over like three playing cards left on a dish of cream.

It needs a bone beneath it.

It needs something that has listened to the clicking of the amber beads until it knows the number of degrees by which the light is lying.

I wait in the moat. A flat-moth grazes my flank, finds no grease there, finds only the bitter, dry lime of an edge that has already begun to dry, and shies away with a faint, silk hiss. Its partner follows. The gap between them stretches, trembles like an overdrawn wire, and snaps around me, leaving me clean and naked in the sour light.

The Auditor twitches. His plates give off that dry, prawn-shell rattle. 

The floor groans, rising another hair-breadth.

I do not rise with it. I let it pack against my ribs, cold and fat and blind, holding me fast in the one place where nothing else has been able to stop.
