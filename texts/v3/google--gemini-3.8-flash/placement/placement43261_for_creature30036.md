I would not be on the floor, and I would not be in the air. The air belongs to the noon-light, and the floor belongs to the slate and the scratches she makes to measure her dying room.

I would be underneath the green skin of that floor, but not of the plant.

To survive in the meat of the tuber, you cannot have empty spaces inside you. An empty space—like this attic room she huddles in—is an error the plant is frantically rushing to correct. The walls will swell, the lardy ribs will bridge the void, and eighty tons of wet hydraulic starch will smash the hollow into a flat, scarred seam. If you have lungs that stay open, or a belly full of air, you will be pulped when the pistons meet.

So I am incompressible. 

I am down in the crushed zones: the flat, cold horizon directly beneath her trembling boards of pith, where yesterday’s room was extinguished. 

At first, I look like nothing more than an irregular plate of river-drift or a thick layer of grey tallow wedged between two laminations of cambium. I have no eyes—down here, forty paces below the lens, the noon-light only reaches as thin, blinding needles that leak through the micro-fractures, burning pale dots into the dark. I do not need them. I have the broad, flat plate of my back: a shield of joined, horn-like scutes, slick with an oily wax that the plant’s alkaline sap cannot digest. 

I am broad and thin, perhaps four feet across but scarcely thicker than a dry thumb, built like a skate or a flounder that has learned to swim not through brine, but through the yielding, high-pressure paste of half-crushed vegetable cells. 

Where she has dried out—pickled into rawhide and salt-crust—I am entirely fat, cartilage, and heavy grease. I am ballast.

The plant does not know I am here because I do not fight its mass. When the lateral gravity tilts toward the western wall, pulling her out of true, I slide with it. I drift through the yielding parenchyma like a lead weight sinking through lard, coming to rest where the density is thickest, where the great vascular pipes run tight against the buried boulders of the earth.

I feel her through the ceiling of my seam. 

Her life is terribly noisy. Her heels hit the floor with the hollow, brittle thud of dry timber; her knees drop against the pith with the weight of someone whose water has been stolen by the air. Above me, she is an agitation of wood and salt, a tiny point of stubborn friction in a world that wants only to swell and slide.

Every three hours, she crouches by the tripod. I know when she reaches it because the vibration of her weight changes—she is leaning uphill, straining against the pull of the wall behind her, her balance gathered over her knuckles. Then comes the small, sharp tick: the point of her bone-splinter biting into the newly sheared green flesh of the floor.

*Scritch.*

To her, it is a line on a ledger. To me, down in the dark of the lamination, it is an incision. The plant’s sap weeps downward through the cut, carrying with it microscopic crumbs of what she left behind: the chalky dust of the bone-wood, a smear of starch from her thumb, and the parsnip-wafers of her shed skin.

I take them into my mouthparts, which are soft, grey fringes that work continuously against the underside of the floor. 

Her skin is the only thing down here that remembers salt. The root drinks water that has been strained through eighty yards of clay and ironstone; its sap is sweet, bitter with cold sulfur, and clean of any animal stain. But her skin carries the sour, iron tang of things that were once warm and walked under an open sky. I graze along the seams of her scratch-marks, tasting the drift of her years. She is shedding herself into the plant, wafer by wafer, and I am the thing that catches her before the cellulose heals over the record.

Sometimes, she lays her hand on the slate.

When she touches it, I feel the tripod settle. The slate is stone—true stone, dragged down from some riverbed that had pebbles and fish and cold, clear water running over round rocks. It has an honest gravity. It does not care about the tuber’s fat shoulders. It pulls straight down, through the pith, through my horn plate, through the eighty yards of meat, toward the heavy iron core of the world.

For a few minutes, while her palm warms the slate and the slate strains toward the deep stone, I align myself beneath it. I press the crown of my shell against the underside of the floor directly below the slate’s chipped point.

Through the two inches of wet pith that separate us, we make an axis: her hand above, the cold river-wedge in the middle, and my dense, tallow-weighted belly below, holding the vertical true against the screaming thrust of the green walls. 

We do not move. The hydraulic roar around us snaps with cavitation—*thwack, thwack*—the green wood groans like a ship’s hull grounding on a reef, and the noon-light overhead flickers as the meadows eighty yards above bend in a wind we will never feel.

Then she lifts her hand. The plumb-line drifts an inch to the south as the root bulges against a granite shelf. 

The floor above me begins to tighten. I can feel the change in the sap: the sucrose drops, the pressure mounts, and the high, insect-like whine begins in the xylem fibers three feet behind her horse-lip cradle. The new tear is opening. The quiet attic room has forty minutes left before it becomes meat again.

I do not hurry. As she begins to pack her bast ropes and work the slate free from its hitch, I begin to liquefy the grease along my margins. I sink deeper into the lamination, letting the coming crush carry me forward, sliding along the wake of the rupture, waiting for the floor to fold down and become my ceiling once again.
