Not in the open air, certainly. The air here is an afterthought, just the space between two kinds of wet. 

If I look for where I begin, it isn’t with an eye or an intention. It is more like an adjustment to a load.

At first, there is the temptation to be the water itself, but the water here has no self-respect; it is too busy being used. It is being boiled, spit through brass glands, dragged under the lead pigs of the flywheels, or thickened with lime until it can barely slide over the bricks. And to be the engineer would be too lonely in a human way—a man living on bone-shiver and salt mullet, waiting for his knees to fill with fluid like the bottom of an old air-pump.

No, it starts lower, where the temperature stops changing between day and night.

There is a place—about four feet above the bilge line, just behind the crank-pit of the eastern beam engine—where a six-inch supply pipe runs through the crook of an ironwood root. The pipe has been leaking at a flanged joint since before the last war. It doesn’t squirt; it weeps a thick, alkaline tea that has soaked the horsehair lagging until the whole bundle looks like the thigh of a dead mule. The root, finding that warmth eighty years ago, didn't go around it. It cupped it. It grew flat and broad, like an open palm, and then it closed its fingers around the iron. 

Where the two meet, there is an inch-thick layer of something that cannot be scraped off with an adze. It is neither wood nor oxide. It is an intermediate paste: pulverized bark, crushed magnesium, boiled cylinder grease, and the fine, dark frass left behind by the crickets that hollowed out the wrap before the heat got too dense for them.

That is where I find myself. Not as an observer perched on a truss, but as the *give* in that joint.

If I am anything with legs, they are very short ones, or they have been tucked away so long against the warm curve of the casing that they are only little horn studs now, good for bracing against the iron threads. My back is flat. It has to be; the clearance between the root-wood and the flange bolts is less than the thickness of a tobacco leaf. My shell is not clean chitin; it has taken up the iron soot until it has the dull, leaden drag of cast iron that has been seasoned with tallow. 

I do not hear the engine. To hear a thing, there must be a silence before it and a silence after it, so the ear can measure the shape of the blow. Here, there has been no silence since the fires were lit under the Cornish boilers when the brick was still yellow. 

Instead, the stroke is simply the climate. 

When the beam rocks down to the south, the whole building leans an eighth of an inch into the river mud. You feel the ironwood root tighten its grip around the steam pipe—a tiny, hydraulic clench of wood fibers drinking in the grease that sweats from the joint. That is the *thunk*. Then the counterweight swings up through the sludge trough, throwing its fan of warm, oily mist against the underside of the floor plates, and the pipe relaxes, expanding perhaps the width of three diatoms. That is the *slosh*. 

Between the *thunk* and the *slosh*, there is a tiny migration of warmth. It moves through me. It tastes of dead tallow, sulfur from the ditch water, and the sour tannin of the ironwood bark. 

I am not alone in the seam, though nothing here has any business with company. 

Just beside my left flank, where the pipe-wrap has turned into a kind of greasy peat, there are three or four of the crickets. They are not the white ones from the upper rafters; they have lived down here near the joint so long that their bodies are the color of a wet slate shingle. They don’t drum. The iron here is too thick to ring; if you struck it with a leg, it would sound like hitting a horse’s hoof with a wooden spoon. They simply press their bellies into the warm flange and wait. What they are waiting for is not the season—seasons do not exist three feet from a continuous twenty-pound head of steam—but the slow, microscopic scaling of the metal. 

Every three or four months, a flake of rust-scale lets go of the flange. It does not fall, because the root is holding it fast, but it *settles*. It gives a faint, dry *tink* through the wood. When that happens, the crickets move half an inch to get their mouthparts into the new sulfur-meal exposed beneath the scab, and I shift with them, sliding along the oil-track, my belly flat against the warm, sweating skin of the pipe.

Higher up, the man’s clogs pass on the catwalk. 

To the frogs in the taro, he is an irregular engine. To me, he is only a cold spot. Where his ash-wood sole comes down on the diamond plate, he draws off a fraction of a calorie from the iron; for three strokes of the beam, the iron above me is cooler by a hair, and the condensation thickens into a bead that runs down the fillet of the weld and touches my antennae. It is the only fresh water I ever know: sweetish, flat, tasting faintly of coal smoke and the zinc from the roof trusses that rotted out sixty years ago.

I take that drop. It takes twenty minutes to swallow it.

There is no reason to move faster. The engines do not turn fast; the river figs do not swallow an H-beam in a human lifetime; the mud in the sludge drains moves with the speed of cooling lard. If I were to scurry, I would lose my purchase on the grease-film and be washed down into the sump, where the eels would turn me over twice in their gray lips and spit me into the lime-slurry because my hide is too hard with iron-salts to chew.

Down here, between the wood that is becoming iron and the iron that is becoming mud, you do not need eyes. The light that bounces off the bayou water through the culvert arches never reaches behind the accumulator anyway. 

It is enough to feel the stroke arrive through the timber—*one*—to feel the flange swell against the root—*two*—and to hold the seam shut with your own small, greasy bulk while the river tries to find a way into the steam, and the steam tries to turn the whole dark pocket back into cloud.
