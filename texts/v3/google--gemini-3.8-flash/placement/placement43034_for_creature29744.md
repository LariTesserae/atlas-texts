The heart is the slowest pendulum in the room, but it is not the only one. 

Above, sixty fathoms through the pale turquoise glass, the gale is swinging the lake’s ceiling like a hammered copper kettle. You do not hear the wind as air; you hear it as mass—the weight of the sky leaning hard against three square miles of cold skin, shoving the whole tarn a hand’s-width south, then letting it slosh back against the western granite. The water doesn't rush, but the pressure alters in the sinuses with every gust: *press, release, press, release.*

I would not be among the upper tiers where the young people work the mortise joints and chase the char from the wicker screens with flat, greased palms. That is quick work, for people whose blood still remembers the snap of the sun-warmed grass their great-grandmothers knew. Nor am I deep enough yet to have moss growing behind my knees.

I am in the middle slack, where the long intestine-tubes drift.

My job—if you could call something you do between the eighth and ninth pulse a job—is the seams of the goat-gut. 

The air-reeds are forty paces long, each one sewn from twenty dried bellies turned inside out, scraped thin as tallow-leaf, and rubbed with birch-tar until they turn the amber of a smoked pike’s belly. They rise out of the inverted bells like long, pale tapeworms, their top ends buoyed by dried bladder-casks that dance just beneath the surf of the tarn, where the air is chopped into foam by the mountain storm. Down those wet guts the mountain air drains—sucked by the cold, pulled by the weight of water into the wells where the divers go to pant.

Gut does not rot in this water, but it softens. It grows slack. A slack reed kinks, and a kinked reed starves the bell below, and then three carpenters have to abandon their chisels and come clawing up the rope-walks with their eyes rolling back like boiled eggs.

So I hang.

I have twelve slate rings sewn into the skirt of my horsehide apron—four at each hip, four across the groin—and a collar of river-pebbles drilled through with copper wire. It is just enough to keep my toes three inches above the braided sedge walk without my having to tread. When I need to move, I do not kick; kicking clouds the water with fine green floc from the moss-reef, and once the water is milky, you cannot see a tear in the gut until you are on top of it. I hook two fingers into the wickerwork and draw myself along like a slug on a fence.

My skin has gone the dull, curdled grey of lard that has sat three winters in a stone cellar. The blubber across my collarbones is three fingers thick, solid as harness-sole, so that when I lean my neck against the ironwood pilings, I feel no edge—only the broad, flat thrum of the timber singing to the lake bed.

In my left hand I carry the horn awl. Between my teeth, gripped by the flat molars, is three yards of dried sinew soaked in pine-grease. 

When you find a blister in the intestine, you do not patch it from the outside. The water would peel the patch away in an hour. You have to work your arm up through the seam of the tube—past the greasy, lukewarm throat where the air is trickling down—until your knuckles are inside the dry breath itself.

That is the strangest thing I know: the arm wet and numbed to the marrow by sixty fathoms of snow-melt, while the hand inside the gut is dry, hot, and smelly with lowland wind. 

Sometimes, when the storm above is very high, the air coming down the pipe tastes of distant things that have no business in a tarn. It tastes of dead heather from the north screes. It tastes of woodsmoke from some shepherd’s hearth five valleys over, small and sour and thin. I hold that smoke between my fingers inside the goat-skin while my face hangs out in the clear, freezing blue, watching the char drift between my knees. The char do not care about the shepherd. They bump their milky noses against the greasy hide of my harness, searching for the salted rind where my skin meets the leather, their little red spots burning like embers through the lime-colored light.

A diver passes below me, falling rather than swimming toward the storehouse tier. He does not look up, but his thumb-ring taps twice on his hip-slate—*clink-clink*—the strike traveling through the basin so hard it clicks in the teeth of my lower jaw. 

*Clear below,* the iron says.

I tap the horn awl against my ballast-stone once in answer: *Holding.*

The air in my own lungs is ninety minutes old now. It has ceased to be air; it is a warm, sour broth of carbon that my chest-wall holds without straining. The vascular slits behind my ears are open, fluttering like tiny velvet sleeves as the glacial current draws through them. They give almost nothing—just a tiny, cold prickle of life, like salt on an open cut—enough to keep the blood in the back of my brain from turning black and heavy. 

I will have to go into a bell soon. My fingers are beginning to hook into claws; the cold is creeping inward from the knuckle-joints toward the fat-pad at the base of the thumb. When the thumb stops bending, you drop the awl, and an awl dropped down here takes three days to find in the moss-mat, if the crabs do not drag it under their ramps first.

I pull myself five paces down the line to where the bell hangs like a drowned basket. 

I duck. I push up.

The sound cuts out as if someone had dropped an ox-hide over my head. 

The roar of the storm-lake vanishes. In here, inside the dark of the dome, there is only the wet, greasy *lap-lap* of the water-line against the wicker rim, and the noise of old Brenn breathing.

Brenn is seventy years in the water. He sits on the resting-spar with his chin on his knees, his broad chest heaving like a forge bellows. The tallow on his face has gone rancid and grey around his nostrils, and his nictitating membranes are half-drawn, giving his milky amber eyes the dead, filmed look of a winter pool. He does not turn his head when my wet crown breaks the surface. He simply leans his tallow-slick shoulder three inches to the left, making room on the cedar spar.

I pull myself up until my waist is out of the water. The air is disgusting. It smells of sixty men’s dried spit, boiled mutton grease, and the sour damp of felt that hasn't seen the sun since the ice broke four months ago. 

I let go of the breath I was carrying. It comes out of me in a long, rattling groan that makes the goat-gut siphon shudder where it enters the roof.

Then I take the dome’s air in. 

It hits the back of the throat like vinegar. My chest expands—creaking, the wide, flat ribs spreading until the hide apron strains against the lead rivets. The velvet slits behind my jaw clamp shut instantly, dry and tight, sealing against the air. For twenty heartbeats, the world is only the hard, hot iron of blood returning to the small vessels in the temples. The head aches with it. The sight clears; the amber in Brenn’s eyes turns from grease to horn.

"The three-line is weeping at the third knuckle," I say. My voice sounds thin, dry, and grotesque after the roundness of the water—a croak like a raven on a cairn.

Brenn does not look up from his knees. He strikes his thumb-ring against his hip-slate with a small, flat *tick* that sounds absurdly weak in the air. 

"Let it weep," he says. His voice has the heavy, phlegm-choked rattle of men whose lamellae have begun to stiffen into gristle. "The cold is coming down from the gap anyway. The water will be ice at the top by tomorrow night. The reed will freeze shut at the bladder."

"Not if the wind holds."

"The wind is turning," he says. "I felt the granite groan in the west footing an hour ago. The basin is settling toward the sheep-pass."

He reaches into the pocket of his apron and pulls out a lump of fat-cured loach, grey and shining with brine. He breaks it with two blackened thumbnails and offers me half. It is cold as stone, but the fat is thick and coats the throat, tasting of mud and ancient larch-bark. We chew together in the dark of the wicker dome, our wet legs dangling down into sixty fathoms of turquoise light, while above our heads the mountain slowly adjusts its hips against the ice.

Presently, my fingers are my own again. The sinew between my teeth has softened enough to knot.

I do not say goodbye to Brenn; speech in the bells is a waste of the tallow-breath. I let my hips slide off the spar.

The water takes me back with a huge, deep-throated *whump* that rings through the ribs like a bell. The silence of the air vanishes, replaced instantly by the magnificent, terrible clangor of the drowned town: the mallets five tiers down, the slow creak of the larch-timbers leaning against the ballast, the muffled thunder of the sheep-cliff miles above grinding its teeth against the frost.

I let my slate rings carry me down two yards, until I am level with the tear in the three-line. The char drift back to me, curious, their soft mouths tapping against the greasy hem of my skirt.

I raise the awl. I spit the wet sinew into my palm. I slip my greased arm back up into the dry wind of the world above, and I begin to sew.
