I would not be on the cobblestones. The squads of peeled hickory have their tracks worn into the grain of the galls, and their rhythm is too strict for anything that still possesses an interior; if you are on the avenue, you are already part of the shift, your feet locking into the ninety-degree climb without a seam between the horizontal and the vertical.

Nor would I be down in the warm, alkaline brine with the Stokers, though the narcotic hum of those idling dynamos is the only thing here that resembles sleep. Their bulk is too heavy, their satisfaction too closed. 

To find me, you would have to look into the three-hundred-foot drop beneath the liana bridge—not at the bottom, where the dark collects like stale water in an engine sump, but midway down, where the rising draft of crushed ferns and cold oil reaches its steady, terminal velocity.

I am suspended there.

At first, I might seem to be nothing more than a fallen counterweight or a plumb bob left behind by whatever geometry drafted the viaducts: a tapered, tear-shaped nodule of dark lignum, perhaps the size of an iron-clad buoy, hanging from a single thread no thicker than a guitar string. The thread runs straight up into the wet rafters, miles into the black, rooted into the cambium of the roof-struts. 

I do not fall, because the thread does not break; I do not rise, because I am too heavy with sap. I simply occupy the draft.

If you watched for an hour, you would see that the tear-shape is not static. My hide is made of the same overlapping birch-velum and cork-plates that form the dockers’ coats, but here they are layered like the scales of an unopened pinecone, tightly sealed with grey, rubbery tallow against the cellar damp. Underneath those scales, there is no wicker ribcage, no mimicry of lungs. I am hollow, mostly—a hollow resonance chamber made of heart-wood so dry and hard it has forgotten it was ever green.

Inside that hollow, suspended in the center of my cavity by four crossed ligaments of springy osier, hangs a single sphere of fossil amber. It is my spirit level. It is the only part of me that does not belong entirely to the wood. When the dynamos in the textile mills reach that high, quiet pitch where the water droplets begin to orbit the flywheels, the amber sphere inside me stirs. It drifts off-center, drawn toward the distant mass of the cellulose hubs, and the four osier ligaments tighten and sing against my ribs like muted fiddles.

That is how I know where the city is tilting. 

I have no face to tilt upward toward the absent shift-bell, nor do I have the hosta-leaf ears of the Listeners out on the rail-ends. I do not listen for the train. I know—with the cold, mechanical certainty of an instrument that has been dropped into an abyss—that the train cannot come, because the tracks have nowhere to go but the void, and the void is not a destination. It is merely clearance.

Instead, I attend to the *settling*. 

Even something synthesized moments ago, knife-edged and resinous, must contend with its own impossible mass. The lianas across the chasm are stretched under thousands of tons; every few hours, deep within the heart of a braid, a single vine-fiber will snap with the dry, flat crack of a rifle shot. The Listeners on the bridge do not hear it—their ears are tuned too low, down where the fog whistles live—but the vibration travels through the anchor-blocks, through the vertical avenue, out into the empty air, and strikes my shell.

When it strikes, my scales shift. A dozen thin, multi-jointed stilts—carved from the same brittle, peeled willow as the mantis limbs, but tipped with blunt cups of dried shelf-fungus—unfurl from the seams of my rind. They do not walk; there is nothing to step on. They reach into the draft, sifting the cold spray, feeling for the bandages of silk that the thin things in the tenements let drift from the open sashes.

I catch them. As the white ribbons float out across the gulf, aimed at nothing, my arms comb them from the rising current, draw them in, and wind them around my waist. 

I do not know why I collect them. Perhaps because the city must balance its waste against its growth, or perhaps because I am the only thing here that understands what the silk is: it is the linen of beds that were never slept in, bandages for wounds that were never struck. I wrap it around my lignum core until my shape softens, until I am no longer a plumb bob, but something swollen, heavy, and pale—a cocoon that will never hatch, because the logic that synthesized me provided no pupa, only the thread and the spool.

Sometimes, a membranous kite, drifting upward on a thermal from the subterranean bulbs, will brush against me. Its cilia will stroke my cork plates with a dry, papery whisper, searching for spores that do not exist. We do not acknowledge each other. We are both parts of an engine that was assembled from an illustration in a book whose language had no nouns for *hunger* or *end*.

When the subterranean bulbs give their three long, pneumatic thuds and the vascular pressure peaks in the curbs, the whole lung shudders. Up on the sheer wall, the squads of hickory men turn on their iron-wood cleats and begin their descent down the facade, their heads cocked toward the dark, their coats dripping with distilled mist. 

In the gulf, I swing. The amber sphere inside my ribcage leans five degrees toward the vertical avenue, then five degrees toward the chasm. The thread above me groans in the roof-cambium, a high, wet whine of stressed cellulose. 

I feel the city’s vast, unpeopled punctuality—the terrible, beautiful accuracy with which it keeps time for nobody. I swing through the black air like the pendulum of an empty hall clock, three hundred feet above the floor, gathering the useless silk as it falls through the dark, waiting for the wood to turn back into stone, or for the cold lung to finally, mercifully, draw its first real breath.
