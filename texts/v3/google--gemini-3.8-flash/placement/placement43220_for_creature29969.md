At first, there is only the sensation of clearance. 

A fraction of an inch. A breath of grease-filmed space between two slabs of cold-rolled zinc that are sliding past each other in opposite directions, each weighing more than an island.

I am not standing on the terraces. I am not suspended from the needles like the pile-worms, nor do I have the broad, methane-filled sky to swim in. I am lodged in the *lash*—that tiny, necessary play between moving surfaces without which the entire rift would lock itself into a single, dead ingot of fused metal.

I begin as a thickness. A laminated wedge of something dense, dull-grey, and yielding, like lead-sheathed muscle or vulcanized baleen. 

My body does not have a silhouette that would make sense in daylight; it has only the geometry of the gap it currently occupies. Right now, it is three paces wide and half an inch thick, spread flat against the chilled belly of an iron overthrust, held there by atmospheric suction and the greasy film of tallow that weeps down from the gaskets above. I feel the stroke coming before it arrives: a long, descending pressure wave, traveling through twenty miles of basalt, that alters the conductivity of the rock.

When the plate comes down, I do not scuttle away. The plate does not crush me, because I am not brittle, but it flattens me until my edges weep. 

And in that weeping, I begin to define what I am.

I am a creature of the tolerances. Where the gasket-swarms exist merely to be pulped into grease, and the Tenders stand outside the seams with their fingers pressed to the stone like mourners at a door, I live *in the stroke itself*. I am the shim that wanders.

My anatomy is stratified, laid down like the geology that bore me:
On my dorsal side, my skin is a pavement of tiny, hexagonal scales of cast Babbitt metal—a soft, sacrificial alloy that can be ground away and reformed in a matter of hours. When the iron plate scrapes over me, these scales sheer off in ribbons, taking the heat of the friction with them, and my flesh underneath immediately secretes a thick, sulfurous tallow that cools into new scales before the next cycle. 

On my ventral side, against the zinc, I am wet, fibrous meat. Hundreds of short, hydraulic pleats run the length of my underbelly, each terminating in a blind lip of horn. These lips do not eat the moss, nor do they tap the mica-crabs. They drink the *swarf*. 

Whenever two faces of the mountain grind together with an imperfect fit, they produce a fine, grey powder—part stone, part metallic filings, saturated with condensed tin and the warm, oily runoff of dying liverworts. It is a rich, toxic flour, still hot from the sheer force that tore it loose. My pleats siphon it into my interior chambers, where it meets a slow, alkaline fire: an internal digestive furnace that does not burn with flame, but with the steady, chemical heat of rusting iron. 

I smell of warm linseed oil and battery paste. I am entirely deaf to sound—for in this place, sound has been choked out by grease—but I am violently sensitive to *drift*.

When the reciprocating terrace reaches the bottom of its stroke—that momentary, sickening pause where millions of tons of kinetic energy hang poised at zero velocity before reversing—I detach.

My body thickens instantly, gathering itself from a broad leaf into a low, segmented slug, roughly the weight of an anvil. I have no eyes; the copper gloom is irrelevant between the plates. Instead, along my lateral ridges, I have rows of exposed nervous coils—fine loops of biological copper that taste the electrostatic gradient of the fissure. 

I crawl. Not with legs, but by sequential hydraulic spasms, hitching myself forward on short, barbed cleats of cold-drawn wire that fold flat on the recovery stroke. 

I move through the dark clearances of the rift, following the scent of *misalignment*.

The mountain is not a finished machine. It is clumsy. Its basalt cams were cast without blueprints; they wobble on their blind axles by a sixteenth of an inch. Its iron pistons plunge into sleeves that are slightly out of round. Wherever there is a wobble, a chatter, a point of uneven wear where the metal is beginning to score and gall—that is where the air grows hottest, where the tallow burns brown and smells of burnt sugar. 

That is my feeding ground.

I find a cleft where a massive, reciprocating wedge of zinc has begun to bite too deeply into a basalt counter-face. The Tender standing on the ledge fifty feet away has its bone fingers laid against the stone, its steam-mouth venting short, anxious puffs, feeling the vibration of the impending seizure. It knows the terrace is going to bind. It does not know how to stop it; it only knows how to be folded into the seam when the catastrophe happens.

I do not have a Tender’s reverence. I drop into the gap like a lead coin falling into a slot.

I spread myself across the hot, scored face of the basalt just as the zinc returns. The impact is staggering—a muffled, pneumatic thud that drives my fluids hard into my central sinuses. The pressure is thousands of pounds to the square inch. My Babbitt-scales liquefy at the points of highest contact, turning into a film of molten grey lubricant, and my meat groans, its internal acid-chambers spiking in temperature until my exhaust pores vent twin jets of pale, soapy steam.

The zinc plate hesitates. It slides. The scoring stops. The seizure is averted, converted into a long, smooth, buttery sigh that carries the terrace down to the end of its run.

For the duration of eighty strokes, I stay there. With each compression, I drink the fresh shavings sheared from the zinc. With each release, I exude more sacrificial scale, letting the rock machine turn me into its temporary bearing. My flesh becomes impregnated with the mountain’s discarded metallurgy; zinc crystals take root in my liver-lobes, and my nervous tissue braids itself with microscopic filings of pyrite until my thoughts—if they are thoughts—run on the slow, unvarying 60-cycle thrum of the deep pile-worms.

I am not permanent. Nothing here is.

After three days, the basalt face shifts its internal crystalline orientation. The cam rearranges its geometry, shearing off its uneven edge in one massive, silent flake that crushes half my dorsal margin into paste. 

I do not die of it; I simply tear away what is ruined. The crushed portion remains behind, welded by pressure into the rock face—a layer of compressed meat and metal that will, by tomorrow, have hardened into a new, pale vein of shale. 

The rest of me—narrower now, smaller, dense as a pig of lead—spills out of the seam and tumbles down the vertical face of the needle. 

I fall through the greasy copper haze, past the lazy, mile-long flukes of a descending leather manta, past the clustered brass eggs of the mica-crabs glittering in the fissures like rows of rivets. I fall through two thousand feet of warm, steam-curtained silence until I strike a broad, rubbery hammock of loop-ferns.

The fronds give way with a wet, elastic stretch, then cradle me. They are hot to the touch, composting in their own grease. My blunt, leaden head sinks into the dark bottle-green mash, and my pleats immediately begin to suck up the warm, mineral condensation that pools in the foliage.

Here, in the fern-choke, I will rest for an hour, swelling again as my cells absorb the water and zinc-salts, rebuilding the margin I left behind in the gears. 

Below me, standing at the rim of the next terrace, another Tender stands motionless, its faceless head tilted toward the cliff, its fingers resting on a flywheel of porous basalt that has not turned a full revolution in three hundred years. It vents a plume of odorless white steam, waiting for the mountain to remember how to move.

I feel the axle through the soles of my cleats. It is dry. The tolerances are tight. It is choked with rust and cold pyrite.

I stir in the ferns. My hexagonal scales begin to form again along my back, cool and grey as fresh solder. I slide off the green mat and begin the slow, heavy crawl toward the hinge.
