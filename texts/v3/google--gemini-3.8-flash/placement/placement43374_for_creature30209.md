At first, I would not be an eye. To look in this water is to be blinded by the jade rush and the boiling froth of vacuum-collapses. 

I would begin as an ache in the teeth—a shared resonance. 

I am wedged where the water cannot tear me out, but where it never stops trying. Not on the crest, where the iron-dolphins split the schools like artillery, and not on the bald granite where the shear-plates lie glued in their two-dimensional paralysis. I am inside the shoulder of it: tucked into the undercut of the fourth caisson, where a sheared-off intake conduit creates a pocket of dead water no wider than a church pew.

Here, the current does not strike straight on. It loops. It enters as a torn ribbon, whips twice around the rusted cylindrical walls, and spits itself back out into the flume. 

In that eddy, I find my edges.

I am not made of the soft, pale blubber that drifts down from the sunlit swells, nor am I entirely of the sea’s cold gristle. There is something older and stiffer in my joints—an architecture of hinge and socket that belongs to things that once stood upright against simple gravity, now warped and re-tempered by sixty atmospheres of hydraulic hate.

My back is broad, curved like a boiler plate, crusted with an armor of dead bryozoans and dried salt-tallow. I am heavy. I have to be. If I had buoyancy, I would be buoyant only for the tenth of a second it would take the shear to rip me off the ledge and fling me out over the three-mile black drop of the trench. My weight is my only mercy; my tissues are dense as waterlogged oak, impregnated with minerals leached from the weeping iron rivets of the tower.

I have thumbs. They are short, blunt spades tipped with thick horn, the skin around the knuckles calloused into grey rinds. But my other fingers have lost their separate wills; they have fused into broad, shovel-shaped claspers, lined on the inner curve with the same fibrous, petrifying tendrils that anchor the giant kelp. When I take hold of an iron flange, I do not merely grip it. I weld. The protein-glue seeps from my palms and cures in the rush; when I want to move, I must wrench myself free with an audible *tock* that echoes off the iron plate.

Above me, within the inverted dome of the caisson’s crown, is the bubble.

It is a lens of air the color of old cider, compressed until it is thick as syrup. It has sat trapped beneath that steel bowl for a hundred and forty years, fed only by the minute bubbles of gas that spin off the cavitation wakes and collect against the rusted roof. The air smells of charred bone, engine oil, and the sweet, rotten musk of dead siphonophores. 

Every few hours, when the lunar tide slackens and the cable’s hum drops from a shriek to an organ-drone, I pull myself up the slimy rungs of the ladder. 

My head breaks through the meniscus. 

The transition is brutal. In the water, there is only the roar and the push; here, inside the bubble, there is a dreadful, ringing silence, broken only by the heavy, leaden drip of condensed salt running down the rivet heads. 

I draw the air into lungs that feel like dried leather bellows. It burns. It tastes of nineteenth-century coke-fires and dead divers’ breath, heavy with nitrogen that turns the blood thick and cold behind my eyes. But it clarifies. It keeps the mind from dissolving entirely into the mindless, roaring grammar of the current. It reminds me of things with corners. Of right angles. Of tools.

I look down through the clear surface of the pocket. Through the dark hole of the hatch below, the green light of the gorge sweeps by like a passing train. I can see the wire-spiders crawling their four-inch steel tightropes across the void, their spindly shadows flicking across the white quartz veins of the opposing cliff. 

I reach down into the silt-trap at the bottom of the ladder, where the vortex deposits its sweepings. 

My fingers sort through the drift: broken mussel valves, fragments of bone from an iron-dolphin’s kill, and—the true prize—dropped iron. A sheared square nut. A broken link of dredge-chain. A copper drift-pin, green and scoured smooth by seventy years of sandless water.

I carry them down in my mouth, gripping them behind blunt, grinding molars.

Back in the flume’s mouth, hanging by my claspers over the drop, I work. 

The vibration of the great C-sharp cable travels down through the caisson’s spine, vibrating through my belly-plates. It tells me where the stress is gathering. It tells me that twenty meters below, where the foundation iron meets the rounded gneiss, a seam has opened half an inch. The mountain is nudging the machine. The cordillera wants its shoulder clean again; it wants this rusted intruder broken and tumbled into the mud three miles below, down where the water is silent and dead.

I reach my spade-fingers into the crack. I feel the cold, terrifying speed of the under-flow licking through the gap like a bandsaw. 

Carefully, timing my movements between the rhythmic surges of the surface swells, I wedge the copper pin into the split. I pack it with broken shell-stone and the dense, leathery corpses of tunicates I have scraped from the lee walls. I lean the full, dead weight of my torso against it, pressing the mixture into the gap until the cement in my skin takes hold, sealing the wound with iron-grit and biological bone.

I am not the hunter here. I am not the storm, and I am not the stone.

I am the shim. 

I am the creature that remembers how the nuts were turned, clinging to the rotting iron skeleton of an ambition that drowned before my grandfathers were conceived. I keep the hum from cracking. I tighten the dead world against the live one, one scrap of rust at a time, wedged between the mountain that will not stop scouring and the sea that will not let anything sleep.
