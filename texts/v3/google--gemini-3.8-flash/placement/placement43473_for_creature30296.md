The first thing that becomes clear is that I am not above the rail. 

To be on the walkway requires a tall, dry symmetry—that high-strung, vertical posture that leans its cheekbone against turned bronze and gathers the plateau as a map of long-distance stresses. I have felt the strike of their iron shoes from above: a neat, puncturing tap, followed by an immense wait, then another tap three paces on. It is a draftsman’s way of being. It belongs to the air that has already forgotten the water.

Nor am I down in the wide pink pans, flattened beneath a sheet of mica, waiting for my joints to clog with silica until the terrace takes my shell into its stairs. There is a terrible simplicity to the pelts. They don’t hold anything back from the lime; they let the crust happen to them until they are merely floor.

No, where the weight settles is in the under-hang. 

In the four cubits of greasy gloom between the galvanized grating and the lukewarm skin of the scald, where the viaduct legs grow thick, gouty rinds of calc-tuff. 

It is the only place on the plateau where the sun does not strike directly. The zinc above keeps the purple sky off my back, but it catches every breath of evaporating grease from the turbine vents below. The underside of the walkway is hung with cold, black stalactites of oxidized lead and that rubbery fungus the walkers scrape for their calories—though they only scrape the top two fingers’ depth, where they can reach with a notched trowel through the grate. Beneath that, it grows thick as horse-hide, furred with damp graphite, smelling of dead batteries and sheep fat.

That is where my hands find something to grip.

They are not tamarisk poles, these arms. They are shorter than the walkers’, thick through the wrist, the skin on the palms tanned to the horn-yellow of an old hoof from decades of dragging along corroded flanges. If there was ever tallow-soaked hemp wrapped around my shins, the caustic wash has long since soaked through it, dissolved the fibers, and replaced them with a stiff, gray sleeve of sulfate scale that never quite comes off. My knees do not bend like the walkers’ double-hinges. They have the short, stubborn throw of a winch.

For a long time, as the vapor shifts, I think I am only a repair that stayed behind. 

There are lead-burners down here. There are copper clamps bolted around weeping siphons eighty years ago that have grown ears of verdigris so large they touch the water. A man could be one of those clamps if he stayed still enough through the midday glare. His breath would match the forty-degree flutter of the shallows; his skin would take the exact slate-and-nickel cast of the corrugated sheets overhead.

But then a siphon clears its throat. 

A mile north, the bronze pipe gives a dull, hydraulic *thrum*—not through the air, but through the iron stanchion I am hooked into with the bend of my elbow. The shudder goes through the rivets, through the spongy lead washer, into my ulna, and rattles the teeth on the left side of my jaw.

I know that vibration. It is the third siphon at the Western Weir. A bladder of lime has broken off inside the neck and is tumbling down the throat toward the submerged vanes. If it hits the bronze runner at speed, it will shear two teeth from the ring-gear. The walker above doesn’t feel it yet; his bronze rod is planted too far out on the cantilever, catching only the surface-ring of the deck. He is listening to the sky. He is listening to the weather that never comes.

My heels drop into the wash.

The scald is not painful if you have forgotten how to have cool skin. It feels like thick, alkaline grease, neither hot nor cold, just persistent. The soft mud between the travertine ridges gives under my boots with a wet *shuck*, venting a tiny, pearlescent pocket of gas that smells of sulfur and old tallow. 

I don’t carry a sounding-pole. A pole is for measuring things that are far away, for turning an entire horizon into a pencil-line you can lean your skull against. 

I carry a heavy, iron-headed maul with the haft sawed down to eighteen inches, rusted so thick that the iron has begun to delaminate in red flakes like pastry. And in my teeth—clenched tight between the gray gums where the back teeth used to be—I hold a wedge of soft green copper.

I am what works between the deck and the lime. 

They don’t look down through the grating very often. Why would they? The catwalk is five meters above the scald because five meters is where the air stops tasting of caustic and starts tasting only of thin, unbreathable heights. Down here, my lungs are always full of the warm, heavy steam that coats the iron in yellow slime. It makes my ribs feel wide, wet, and slightly rotten, like an old crate left out in a marsh. I have to spit often—small, heavy plugs of gray phlegm that float for an instant on the lukewarm froth before the current takes them over the weir lip.

To the salt-wasps, I am merely another pylon that has grown an irregular knuckle. 

When they drop down from the sun, purple-bellied and heavy with UV, they don't avoid me. They land on the tallow-crust of my shoulders, their cold silica claws digging into the boiled wool of my vest to find purchase. They tremble there, venting their thin, sharp prickles of heat straight through my collar into my clavicles. It is a dry, stinging warmth, like having small hot coins laid against the meat. I let them sit. When their bellies turn from violet back to dull horn, they will crawl down my sleeve to the iron rung, drop into the sluice-gap, and vanish into the turbine dark. We have an understanding that has no tongue: they bring the sun down to where my joints are stiff; I give them something solid that doesn't shudder when the weir-gates drop.

I am not the walker’s brother, though we likely came out of the same wicker cradle swung under the distilled-water vent forty or sixty seasons ago. 

He was the one whose legs kept growing, whose ankles stayed loose, whose eyes took to the mica slits until he could look at five leagues of pink salt without blinking. They gave him the phosphor-bronze staff and set him on the north rail. 

They left me below because my shoulders were broad enough to wedge between two pylon-struts when the hydraulic hammer kicks back, and because my eyes never grew the goat-slit pupils. My eyes are small, watery, and rheumy, permanently squinted against the salt-fume, the color of pond-ice. I don't need to see five leagues. I only need to see the six inches of lime where the siphon-mouth meets the foundation stone.

There: the third siphon is coughing again. 

I wade three paces through the shallows. The water only comes to mid-shin, but it drags like wet wool. The platters—the *pelts*—slip out from under the blunt toes of my boots with dull, scraping clicks, sliding sideways through the sulfate muck like wet dishes pushed across a table. They don't fear being crushed; their backs can bear the weight of an iron girder if it falls flat. They simply move because the water moves when I move.

I reach the intake. The bronze tube is three paces wide, banded with lead hoops that have turned the pale, powdery white of a dried bone. The lime has built a lip here, a thick, rounded snout like a hog’s nose that is slowly curling inward to choke the suction.

I don’t use a chisel on a long pole, striking from five meters above with a light, pretty *thunk*. 

I drop to one knee in the lukewarm froth. The scald creeps up the grease-stiff canvas of my breeches, hot and slick against my groin. I set the copper wedge directly into the suture where the white travertine has welded itself to the green bronze. 

I raise the short maul. 

I don't look up to see if the walker on the catwalk hears me. The first blow doesn't make a sound in the thin air, but the whole viaduct takes it into its marrow: a dull, leaden *clonk* that travels thirty miles along the iron spine to the eastern condensors. The wasps on the under-hang lift their wings an eighth of an inch, hold them there, and settle back into the grease.

The second blow splits the snout. A slab of mineral crust as large as a hog’s carcass breaks away, rolls into the current, and is sucked with a wet, hollow *slurp* down into the gravel-trap below the turbines, where it will grind itself to pink flour against the iron balls until the tide washes it out.

The siphon takes a long, clear, frictionless gulp. The bronze tube stops shivering. Its skin goes cold and sweat-beaded under my palm.

I stay there on my knee for a while, letting the water sheet around my hips. 

The sun is directly overhead now. Through the gaps in the galvanized grating above, it falls in narrow, blinding strips—ruled lines of white fire laid across my gray hands, across the sawed-off maul, across the lime-crust on my thighs. 

From above, if the walker stopped and put his forehead to the phosphor-bronze, he would feel only that the third siphon had righted its rhythm. He would not think of me. He would think the machine had simply settled itself, the way a mountain settles an inch deeper into the plain after a freeze. 

And he would be right. I am not an engineer. I am not a shift. 

I am only the weight that stays wet, down where the bronze meets the stone, keeping the throat clear so the lung can draw.
