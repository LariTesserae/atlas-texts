At first, there is no vantage point from which to look. The light is too level, the water too everywhere, the sound too thick to permit a spectator. To arrive here is not to stand and see, but to be jarred into scale by the shudder through the knees—forty-two times a second, the mountain ticking like an escapement.

My first impulse is to look for the high dry place, the roof pinned with stones, the corrugated iron. That is where a mind usually goes when it tries to survive a landscape: toward the latch, the dry grease-pencil on the pine board, the small pane of glass where moisture has formed a milky cataract. To sit inside the control shack and listen to the roof drum under the spray.

But the shack is too separate. It is already dead; its air has the flat, sulfurous smell of dry-cell batteries whose seals have wept their paste into the floorboards. Nothing that lives here can afford to be an exile inside four walls. The silence in the shack is not peace; it is a pocket of stalled pressure waiting to rust out.

So I drift down.

I try the grease-legged bird, standing hip-deep in the spray of the backwash, with its eyes glazed in amber. There is an allure to that stance: the cold, perfect arithmetic of the plummet, the neck like a lever, the auditory valves clamped down against the world until the skull is just a bell ringing with the vibration of the catwalk. But the bird is too finished. It is an instrument of precision, and its life is an unbroken panic of balance against the downdraft. It has no time to dwell; it is an arc between the steel rail and the foam.

Down further, then, through the carpet.

Through the celadon and ochre caps of the moss, down into the tea. For a moment, it feels right to be the pale ribbon—headless, boneless, slid between two wet sheets of dead sedge, drinking the machine-churned froth that drifts through the fiber. There is an enormous, narcotic comfort in that dark, pressurized broth. You would never have to see the optical violence of the sun. You would never have to choose a direction. The forty-two-cycle hum would keep your bloodless flesh buoyant against the stone. 

Yet there is no memory there. The ribbon does not know the steel from the rock; it only knows the salt of the froth and the sudden, terrible paralysis when the current fails. It is too soft. It dissolves into the condition of water.

No—where I settle is slower, stiffer, and caught in the teeth of both.

I find myself on the downstream flange of a low-head bypass gate, three feet above the water line, where the steel casting is four inches thick and anchored by three-quarter-inch drift-bolts directly into the sheared face of the basalt.

I am not the machine, and I am not the moss, though I have the color of the one and the patience of the other.

Call me the crust.

I am the tough, blackened skin that forms where three things grind against each other without stopping: the weeping zinc-and-tallow grease forced out of the bearing sleeves; the mineral spray that dries instantly in the thin air, leaving crusts of lime and iron sulfate; and the acidic, purple-black rhizoids of the alpine cushions that are working their way into the microscopic crazing of the paint.

I am an inch thick in the hollows between the stiffener ribs. On my outer surface, where the wind shears at seventy miles an hour, I am dry and hard as horn, black-green, smelling of ozone and sheep tallow. My face is pitted like coke, catching the grit of pulverized stone that rides the spray. But underneath—against the cold, shuddering flank of the housing—I am wet, greasy, and warm. 

The warmth is the secret. It does not come from the sun. The sun up here is an enemy that bleaches and cracks; I present to it only my dead, blackened shingles of lichenous scale. My warmth comes from the belly of the turbine, through the four inches of cast iron that separate me from forty thousand gallons a second of compressed mountain water. The bronze impellers are turning five feet away, churning against the friction of their sleeves. That heat—dull, oily, animal—leaks through the casting into my underside.

I am full of inhabitants who do not know they are inside me.

The penny-shaped nymphs use my lower margin as an eddy. They crawl out of the blinding chute to molt against my underside, leaving their translucent copper carapaces glued into my tallow-crust like empty coins. The earless voles know me; on nights when the frost locks the peat into iron, they creep along the flange and gnaw at my edges for the mineral salts and the dead stonefly nymphs trapped in my layers. Their teeth leave neat, twin parallel gouges in my dark hide, which fill again with verdigris before the week is out.

I don't think. Thinking is an activity of the plains, where things begin and end. Here, there are only the steady states: the hydrostatic head, the forty-two cycles, the millimeter-per-century advance of the moss mat over the rusted rivet-heads. 

I feel the mountain through the bolts. Because I am bolted to the basalt, I know when the crust three miles down shifts half a micron along the fault-scarp; it rings through the casing with a high, glass-clear *ping* that dies instantly in the water-roar. And because I am glued to the steel, I know when the high snowfields begin to melt three days before the flood arrives: the pitch of the casing drops from a sharp G-flat down into a thrumming D, as the weight of the water columns thickens and the air-bleeders begin to choke on froth.

I am the place where the machine is ceasing to be an intruder. 

The men who drilled these footings thought they were setting iron into dead rock. They did not know that the iron would become a reef. They did not know that forty-two cycles per second is the heartbeat of a new kind of wet, deaf marsh that needs the thrum to keep its lungs open. 

When the sun goes down, it happens with an abruptness that feels like an impact. The glare snaps off. The basalt cliffs turn from slate to iron, then to pitch. The spray-plumes, which were blinding white rainbows an hour ago, become grey, drifting curtains that taste of ice. 

The cold drops like an axe—five below, ten below, fifteen. 

Out on the open flats, the tea-colored pools skin over with needles of black ice that shatter and reform as the ground shivers beneath them. The birds lock their claws into the catwalks and tuck their oiled beaks under their scapulars, their fat bodies rocking slightly in the turbine-gale like mooring buoys.

Down inside my layer, against the flange, the temperature remains twelve degrees above zero. The grease does not freeze. It stays soft, the consistency of lard, smelling of old engines and marsh peat. 

I hold the heat against the iron. The iron holds the water against the mountain. The mountain holds the whole roaring shelf against the sky. 

And slowly, so slowly that the concept of tomorrow is too thin to measure it, the purple rhizoids creep another cell-width through my body, their acid tips drinking the rust from the bolt-heads, knitting the cold mountain and the screaming engine into the same damp, iron-hearted fleece.
