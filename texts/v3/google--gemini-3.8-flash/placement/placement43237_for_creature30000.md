At first, there is only the sensation of being pressed—not crushed, for crushing implies a threshold crossed, a yield point surrendered to violently. This is simply the condition of existing under load: forty thousand pounds per square inch of clean, deadweight gravity, held forever between two faces of ground hematite that have never been allowed to touch.

I would not be on the open pan. The sky—that violet-white vacancy—is for things that can afford to dissipate their mass, or things broad enough to ride the acoustic columns. And I would not be the boiler-beast, lumbering under the terrible obligation of its own expanding chambers, forever venting its guts into the dry air just to avoid rupturing its seams.

I would be found in the slip.

To understand me, you have to understand the terror of the clean face. In an atmosphere without vapor, where every trace of grease is baked into brittle resin and every atom of moisture was abolished before the basalt cooled, two pieces of identical iron pressed together will do something monstrous: they will weld. With no oxide barrier between them, no film of fluid to confuse their boundaries, their crystalline lattices recognize each other across the gap. The atoms slip across. The seam vanishes. What were two functioning parts become a single, immovable lump of dead ore, seized forever in a cold, permanent lock.

The valley’s greatest danger is not the hunter with its pre-stressed silicon spine. The valley’s danger is *arrest*—the sudden, catastrophic wringing together of things that should slide.

And so I am what lives in the gap to keep the world from freezing shut.

***

My body does not have a silhouette that could be drawn against the violet sky. I have no profile to speak of, because my thickness is never more than three millimeters, though my perimeter spills out across six or seven square yards of the hematite floor, pooling and puckering like a spilled ingot of lead-bearing babbit that has refused to set.

I am heavy—dense with the dead, dull weight of antimony, powdered graphite, and soft, untempered copper. I have no skeleton of structural steel; structural steel is arrogant, it rings like a bell, it snaps under shear. I am entirely composed of shear. I am thousands of paper-thin leaves of compressed flake-metal, held together by an internal electrostatic drag, sliding over one another like a dropped deck of brass cards. 

I do not walk. I do not roll on bevels. I move by *smear*.

Where the train-length engine’s driving rods drop toward the rock—where the bronze shoe drags across the burnished rut—I am already there. I do not stay beneath the shoe; that would be annihilation. I ride the wake of the clearance. As the shoe lifts by half a thousandth of an inch on its eccentric return stroke, I weep into the opening. 

I feel the descent of the foot not as a blow, but as a total acoustic blotting out. The bedrock drone, which everywhere else sounds like the thrum of an idling marine plant, becomes inside my body a pure, white-hot silence. The load presses my laminated foil plates together until the air between my leaves is driven out, until my atoms groan and flatten. 

And there, in the absolute center of the squeeze, I feed.

I do not eat metal. I eat the *burr*. 

Where the engine’s shoe scuffs the hematite, it raises microscopic ridges—ragged, microscopic crests of cold-worked iron that catch and tear. If left alone, these microscopic tears would gall. They would strip ribbons of metal from the plate, create heat, and weld the beast to the mountain. 

My upper surface is velvet-soft, composed of micro-porous tin that conforms instantly to every tooth and gouge in the beast's steel sole. My under-surface is slick with an impossibly fine film of hexagonal boron-nitride flakes, which my body secretes by grinding the mineral salts of the red dust through my internal lamellae. 

I take the jagged tooth of the burr into myself. I let it shear off inside my soft belly. I swallow the torn flake of white iron, absorb its static charge to keep my leaves sliding, and pack the spent, pulverized iron dust into tiny, dense tablets that I extrude from my trailing fringe as I creep forward. 

When the beast’s foot lifts again, the rock is not scarred; it is mirror-bright, and the steel above it has been wiped clean of its own torn skin.

***

It is a slow life, measured not in steps, but in strokes. 

I do not have eyes. The violet-white furnace of the sky would only blind me; what use is light to an entity whose whole universe is defined by the distance between two polished flats? But I have an exquisite sense of *run-out*. 

Through the miles of hematite floor, I can feel when a flywheel three valleys away begins to wear its basalt spindle out of round. I feel the eccentricity as a periodic hitch in the bedrock drone—a tiny, rhythmic skip in the thrum, no thicker than a hair, that tells me the axle is cutting into its sleeve. 

If it were close, I would migrate toward it. It would take me eighty years to flow across the graphite pan, oozing from one cold-rolled tier to the next, dropping down the joints of the structural cliffs like a thick, silvery batter, drawn by the smell of hot bronze and the sweet, dry screech of metal that has lost its clearance.

I would reach the bearing housing. I would flatten myself into a shim. I would slide between the spinning basalt and the screaming sleeve, absorbing the wobble into my own yielding flesh, sacrificing my outer laminations layer by layer, letting them be ground into amber-colored flour until the flywheel found its true center again and settled back into its ancient, mindless sixty-minute revolution.

I am not the engine. I am not the power. 

I am the *margin*.

***

Sometimes, in the heat of midday, when the sun stands directly over the graphite flats and the violet light turns the shadows into cut obsidian, I find myself caught between cycles. The great engines do not move; they are heat-soaking, their riveted boilers ticking softly as the iron plates expand against their stays with dry, single clicks.

In those hours, I rest in the shadow of a wrist-pin five feet thick.

The air around me is still. It smells of hot transformer oil and the sour, sun-cured leather of the dust. Above me, forty feet in the air, the underbelly of the beast curves away like the hull of a dry-docked freighter, its iron seams white with the alkaline powder left by the disk-parasites.

A needle-flea drops from the chassis. It strikes my back with a tiny, dry *ping* and tumbles across my leaves. 

It does not harm me; I have no circuits to interrupt, no tension for its diamond points to release. It scuttles across my soft tin skin, searching for a groove to lock into, but finds only the infinite, greasy yield of my layered plates. After a moment, baffled by the absence of resistance, it springs back into the shadow of the drive-housing, its leaf-spring legs ringing like a tiny fork against the steel.

I do not mind the fleas. I do not mind the parasites that dissolve the iron above me into silver trails. They are creatures of the structure; they believe the world is made of plates and bolts.

They do not know how thin the world really is. They do not know that beneath every foot of steel, beneath every tooth of the ungulate’s counter-rotating knees, there is only a film of clearance no wider than a breath—and that if that clearance should ever fail, if the dust should ever clear and the clean stone should touch the clean iron without me between them, the plateau would seize in a single, thunderous stroke.

The flywheels would crack their axles. The long boilers would freeze to their beds. The acoustic gliders would fall from the sky as the sound died out of the air.

And so I stay flat. I take the weight as it comes down—heavy, hot, and indifferent. I spread myself out until I am nearly nothing, a shadow of soft metal between two continental jaws, holding them apart with nothing more than the willingness to be ground, to slip, and to remain.
