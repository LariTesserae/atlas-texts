There is no first breath, because there is nothing to draw in that would need lungs, and no moisture to keep a throat from tearing like dry vellum. 

Consciousness here does not wake up; it settles. It arrives the way heat transfers between two flat plates of iron clamped together in the dark. 

At first, there is only the contact. Six distinct lines of pure, unforgiving compression. 

Not points—*lines*. Long, narrow channels where dense, horn-grained material grips an edge so acute it would cleave marrow. But there is no marrow. Underneath the shoes, the basalt does not yield, and the shoes do not yield to the basalt. They fit each other like a mortise and tenon cut by the same blind machine. 

For a long time, there is no need to move. To move would mean altering the friction, and friction is the only thing that holds the darkness in place. 

Gradually, the geometry expands outward from those six locked channels. 

I am not standing on the mountain. I am keyed into it. 

Above the six lines of contact, the weight gathers—low, broad, spread out like a dropped ingot of lead. My bulk is an arrangement of thick, overlapping shingles of dry slate, cold on their outer faces where the starless vacuum brushes them, but searingly hot underneath. With an agonizing, mechanical rhythm, the shingles lift a fraction of an inch. A dry draft of zinc and heated quartz washes into the gill-slits beneath; the heat bites into the fibrous mineral core of me, recharging the slow, heavy leverage stored in the joints. 

*Tock.*

The shingles snap shut. The sound travels straight down through my limbs into the knife-edge of the ridge. A hard, clean, wooden-sounding impact that disappears into miles of solid stone. 

Sight comes not as an opening of eyes, but as a sudden blooming of ache. 

Down in the gut of the ridge, four miles below where the fin plunges into the floorless black, there is a point that is burning too bright. To whatever lived in a wetter world, it would look like nothing at all. To me, it is an unbearable, feverish throb: a fault plane twisting under torsion, screaming with friction. The rock there is glowing a violent, blinding infrared, heating up as the crystalline lattice begins to fail. 

The ridge is going to shear. 

The wind comes up then, carrying the dry, metallic rattle of a dozen shear-flecks skimming past my flank. They slip into the microscopic cleavage of the slate plates, scrape their fill of lithium dust, and tilt off into the draft with a hiss like scattered needles. They do not matter. They are the dust that blows off the anvil.

What matters is the needle.

Three hundred paces along the razor-spine, where the fin tapers into a black glass horn no thicker than an arm, a plinth stands. It was placed there when the stone was younger—perhaps twenty tremors ago, perhaps ten thousand. It is dry-stacked slate, topped with a broad, flat basin that sits empty, balanced against the black sky.

The horn beneath it is vibrating. The gusting zinc-draft is hitting the vertical face at an angle that catches its natural harmonic. The horn is singing—a high, razor-thin shriek of pure resonance that travels through my foot-grooves as an agonizing itch. If the wind stays in that quarter for another fifty pulses, the vibration will find the microscopic flaw where the glass cooled too fast. The horn will explode into a million falling razors.

I begin to shimmy.

It is not walking. It is a slow, alternating translation of mass. The left front lever disengages its groove, lifts two inches, slides forward along the crest, and seats itself with a dry *snick*. The rear lever on the opposite side drives downward, shoving fifty tons of ballast forward. The rock beneath groans—a low, grinding complaint of ironstone forced to take a load it did not ask for. 

Step. Clamp. Shift. 

The world is entirely balance. To my left, a vertical drop of four miles into the dark where discarded spires lie shattered in heaps. To my right, a shear-face running down into a fissure that is breathing dry, suffocating heat from a new break. There is no vertigo, because vertigo requires a fear of falling, and the fall is simply another vector. If the ridge breaks, I will draw the levers into the slate belly, lock the shingles until I am a single, dense wedge of stone, and ride the avalanche down to where the ruins gather. And then, from the bottom, I will begin to climb again. 

Step. Clamp. Shift.

I reach the base of the needle. 

The singing is unbearable now. The glass is hot to my stress-pits—not with tectonic heat, but with the frantic, furious heat of resonance, the molecules tearing at each other as the wind bows the spire. 

I do not look up. I wedge my forward spars into the climbing-notches cut into the plinth's dry-stacked base. I hoist the dead weight of my body up the fitted slate, plate by plate, the slate groaning under the sudden application of mass.

At the summit, the basin waits. It is worn smooth—not by water, but by the keratin bellies of those who have curled into it before me. 

I haul myself over the lip. I tuck the six lever-limbs beneath my core, sliding each shoe into its designated recess under the heavy brim of the carapace. I lower the massive, blunt iron-horn of my snout into the hollow of the bowl. 

I am no longer an animal. I am a counterweight. 

The plinth takes my fifty tons. Instantly, the pitch of the needle drops. 

The shrieking glass sound stutters, chokes, and falls two octaves into a dull, manageable drone. The vibration through my plates settles into a rhythmic, low-frequency hum that vibrates the gill-beds pleasantly. 

The wind howls against the fin, but the needle cannot dance to it anymore. It is pinned. It is loaded. I am the lead weight on the end of the reed that keeps the reed from snapping.

Miles away, through the black that has never seen a star, a pulse strikes the bedrock.

*CLACK.*

The sound hits my basin from below, channeled upward through the needle. It is clean, dry, and immensely heavy. 

Forty-two miles to the north-northwest, on the ridge of slag that overlooks the sulfur vents, another one has struck its plinth with its tail. 

I feel the strike travel through my shingle-plates, read the vector of the impact, read the news carried in the shockwave: *The deep fault has slipped three feet. The thermal bloom is dying. The lower fin has stabilized.*

I wait three slow gill-breaths. 

I lift my dense, calcified tail-spar from the notch in my carapace, bring it back, and let it fall against the rim of the basin.

*CLACK.*

The needle rings with the blow, sending the answer back down into the root of the mountain, through the ironstone, through the blind dark, across the empty chasms of falling glass:

*The horn is tuned. The wind has no purchase. We hold.*

Then I lower the tail, pull the shingles tight against the vacuum, and let the baking kiln-warmth of the world seep upward into my belly, waiting for the rock to tell me what to brace next.
