I wouldn’t come from outside. Nothing comes in from an outside here; there is no seam in the ceiling of scalding mist where a sky could begin, and no road leading off the edges of the basalt slabs that doesn’t simply thin into grease-slicked mud and warm slough.

If I find myself here, it is because I have dropped out of suspension. 

At first, I think I would be down in the street, simply because the weight of the soup suggests sinking. You imagine yourself standing knee-deep in the boulevards, your calves pressed against the yielding mass of a drift, feeling the slow, heavy drag of colloidal gel as it inches over the flagstones, scouring the silt clean. You imagine feeling your own weight—the downward press of heels against stone, the familiar reassurance of an upright spine, the air fighting your chest as you try to drink it into lungs that were built for dry, cool mountains. 

But an upright spine doesn’t last long in ninety-five degrees of unbroken moisture. The distinction between the fluid inside a ribcage and the fluid outside it is only a millimeter of salt and lipid; in an atmosphere this dense, that barrier becomes an unnecessary argument. You would not drown so much as dilute. The edges of your hands would soften first, shedding their little flakes of dead keratin into the broth, where the filamentous fat along the curb would take them up without ceremony. Your thoughts would lose their quick, nervous snap, growing long and heavy, spaced out by the three-hour thrum of mud-gas belching from under the pavers.

So I am not an intruder wading through the water. That is a fantasy of arrival, and here there is no arrival—only precipitation.

Perhaps higher up, then. 

There is that dry, black interior behind the plug. When you read of the plugs—those dense, tallowy brawns that seal the doorless arches, drinking the steam from the alleys so that the rooms inside remain dead, cold-less, desiccated voids—the instinct is to want to be inside that room. It seems like the only sanctuary: the one place where the soup doesn't touch, where the air is still and nothing drips. 

You imagine being the thing curled on the dry siltstone floor of an administrative chamber that has never seen a clerk. You would be small, paper-dry, smelling faintly of candle ends, listening to the plug breathe in the entranceway: that wet, sucking gasp every three or four hours as the basin shifts, the plug swelling, sweating sour grease down the exterior jambs, and then sighing back down. You would sit in the total dark, entirely preserved, like a dried root in an apothecary’s drawer.

Yet that feels too lonely, and loneliness is not native to this basin. The text is very clear: there is no solitude here, because the stone, the fat, and the broth are all the same matter at different speeds. To sit inside the dry room would be an exile, a stubborn refusal to turn the wheel. You would be a speck of foreign grit caught in an otherwise unbroken gear.

No. I would be at the hinge.

I think I would be the pebble inside one of the leathery globes. 

Not the skin of the sphere itself—that translucent, leathery membrane that bounces off the facades and sweats tiny hydraulic jets to keep itself centered in the basalt canyons. That is the engine, the muscle, the mindless tissue. 

I would be the little chunk of siltstone rolling along the bottom curve.

Think of what that existence is: you are an inch across, tumbled smooth by some ancient friction before the city even had corners. You have no purpose except mass. You sit at the lowest point of the sphere’s belly. 

When the sphere drifts between two towering tenements of seamless grey rock, a rising plume of hot vapor from a drainage cleft catches its flank. The sphere tilts three degrees to the left. 

Inside the dark, dry chamber of the globe, you roll. You don't have to choose to roll; gravity simply draws you down the curved interior wall until you find the new center. Your movement is a tiny, dry scratch against the taut parchment of the floor—a faint *tick*, like an insect walking across a drumhead. 

The skin feels your weight shift. The membrane reacts. On the side you just left, the leathery wall exudes a heavy, warm tear of moisture that detaches into the dark air. The thrust nudges the whole carriage-wheel-sized bladder back toward the middle of the boulevard, away from the weeping stone, away from the vertical ribbons of water that creep toward the ceiling. 

You are the stone that thinks the turn, without having a single thought.

You ride the avenues. You trace the municipal grid with the dumb, perfect obedience of a tax assessment. You know the city better than anything that walks, because you never touch it; you feel only its air-canyons, the subtle drafts where four boulevards meet at right angles, the sudden updrafts where a monolithic block has melted halfway into the mud and created a chimney of heat.

Sometimes, in the pitch black, there is a soft, wet impact. A shudder runs through the leathery skin. 

You roll violently, clattering against the curved wall, and then you hear—inches away, through two thicknesses of leathery hide—the rattle of another stone. 

It is not an enemy. It is another pebble, another little node of siltstone that has been drifting through the third-story dark for two hundred years, rolling inside its own private sphere. 

The two globes stick. Their membranes begin to melt together with that slow, stearic patience that governs everything here. For weeks, you and the other pebble drift together down the long, drowned canals, rolling toward each other, clicking together when the drafts shift, like two dice held loosely in a wet hand. You listen to each other’s little abrasions against the parchment floor. You don't speak, because the only language is balance, and between the two of you, the sphere has found a broader base, riding lower in the steam, sweating from both flanks at once.

And eventually, when the fused globe grows too heavy and the hot mud beneath ceases to push it upward, you will sink. 

You will drift down through the scalding rain, through the empty air of the second story, through the layer where the plugs sigh in their doorways, down into the knee-deep broth of the grand avenue. 

The membrane will split upon the basalt flagstones with a soft, fatty tear. The warm soup will rush in, tasting of sulfur, river silt, and the old lipid fleece of the cornices. 

You, the pebble, will roll out onto the street. You will settle into the mud between two pavers. 

You will wait there, entirely still, while a forty-foot drift slumps over you, its colloidal belly scraping your upper face with its millions of microscopic silicates, polishing you down by half a micron over forty years. 

You will not mind the weight. You were stone before you were an eye; you were silt before you were a balance-organ. Deep below you, an inch per century, the basin will extrude a new wall from the boiling mud, lifting you toward some future cornice, where the tallow will gather around you again, and the mist will rain down its continuous, tepid sweat, waiting for the cycle to forget itself just enough to begin once more.
