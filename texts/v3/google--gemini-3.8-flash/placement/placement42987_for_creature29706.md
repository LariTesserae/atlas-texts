The first impulse is to borrow a body that already knows how to breathe this air. 

It would be easy to slide into the gray hide of the reptile—to imagine the long, flat weight of the belly against the silt, the low pulse of the heart ticking like a dry clock once every few minutes, the milky film drawn over the eyes like greasepaper. There is an immediate comfort in that heaviness, in being the one thing in the chamber large enough to cast a shadow if a torch were ever brought near. 

Yet to imagine being that creature feels, very quickly, like an intrusion. It has no vacancy. Its gray bulk is not an open seat waiting for an observer; it is thoroughly, completely occupied by its own sluggish blood, its blind heat-pits reading the gradient between the canyon mouth and the rear wall. If I stepped into that skin, I would bring with me the noise of an inner voice, the twitch of an eyelid, the intolerable restlessness of a mind that counts the minutes. The lizard does not count minutes; it *is* several minutes, stretched out into a yard of slate-colored scale. To be the lizard would be to ruin it.

The instinct retreats, then, downward—into the silt. 

Could I be one of the parchment-tails, swimming through the dry flour two inches below the grade? There is something clean about that blindness. No eyes to cloud over, no lungs to hitch, only the velvet cuticle sliding through pulverized shale, sifting the drift of ancient spores and insect wax with microscopic combs. But down there, the world is too narrow, too purely mechanical. It is a life of hydrodynamic friction and digestion, an equation that solves itself without remainder. It leaves nothing behind to inhabit.

So I look again at the floor, at the things that do not eat.

Near the back wall, where the dust gives way to the low ledge of bedrock, sit the chert flakes and the hammerstone. 

They are the only foreign things in the chamber. Everything else—the sandstone vault, the floury dust, the skeletal shrubs with their peppery sap, the scale-insects whose bodies turn to waxy carapaces—grew out of this place or was weathered down from its walls. Even the lizard is just bedrock that learned how to swallow sap-bugs. 

The stones, though, came from somewhere with a current. 

I find myself drawn not to the keen, glass-sharp flakes, which still carry the violent memory of the blow that sheared them off, but to the hammerstone itself. 

It is an oval river-cobble, dense and fine-grained, probably quartzite or a hard basalt. It was tumbled round in moving water miles away and hundreds of feet above, in a riverbed that has likely been dry gravel for ten thousand years. It remembers cold water—not as a thought, but as an arrangement of its mineral skin, ground smooth by collisions against other stones in the dark of a torrent. 

Then a hand took it up. 

That hand did not make the stone, but it changed its center of gravity. It held the cobble until the stone warmed to the heat of blood, struck it six or seven times against the dark chert to produce cutting edges, and then set it down. Where the thumb rested, against the stone’s balance point, there is a slight, natural concavity—a shallow depression that was already there in the river, but which the knapper chose because it fit the pad of their thumb like a cup.

If I am anywhere in this room, I am that stone.

Not because it is inanimate, but because it is the only thing here that is currently engaged in the slow, difficult work of *surrendering*. 

The lizard has already arrived; the dust has already settled. But the hammerstone is still, after all these centuries, slowly giving up its mountain-chill to the bedrock. Deep in its core, it is denser than the sandstone it rests upon. Its molecules are packed with the memory of glacier-melt and heavy riverbeds, and inch by inch, millennia by millennia, that foreign density is reconciling itself with the soft, isothermal warmth of the floor. 

I would feel the lizard’s hind foot where it rests against my flank. 

Its claw is blunt, calcified, coated in a fine white flour. The reptile does not hold me; it braces against me. I am its anchor, the only thing on the floor heavy enough not to slide when it lifts its head to swallow a scale-bug twice a year. Through that point of contact, I would feel the faint, rhythmic swelling of its flank—a tiny shift in pressure every three or four minutes, followed by a long, flat subsidence. The lizard’s body heat, drawn from the bedrock, seeps faintly into my side; my ancient, river-born chill seeps faintly into its scale. We are trading temperatures at the speed of a stalactite growing.

And on my upper curve, in that small, thumb-shaped hollow, I would hold a thimbleful of the room’s dead air. 

A microscopic dusting of degraded shale has gathered there, five or six grains deep, resting in the exact negative space where a human life touched the stone and then went away. The hollow does not grieve the thumb; it does not yearn for the river. It simply holds the dust at the exact angle the ceiling shed it.

To be that stone is to be fully awake to the silence without needing to break it. You do not have to breathe; you do not have to move toward the thorn bush to feed; you do not have to watch the gray alley outside the blind to see if the sun has set. You are held between two histories: the water that smoothed you, and the reptile that leans its hip against you in the dark. 

You have all the time in the world to become sandstone. And until then, you keep the reptile steady.
