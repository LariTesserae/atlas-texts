At first, there is only the tension against the hinge.

It is a pressure that cannot be separated from the water itself—the slow, thick accumulation of head behind a plate that has not turned on its axle since the grout washed out of the brick beneath it. The tea-colored brine does not stop; it crowds into the gap where the packing-gland used to be, humming with the slight, bitter current of two dissimilar metals dissolving against one another in the dark. 

I am wedged where the water must make a corner. 

Not at the floor, where the black mud packs so deep it suffocates even the acid, and not up among the dripping rafters where the spiders knit their wet spit between the girders. I am in the bypass: a cast-iron goose-neck, four feet across, that once relieved the pressure on the primary cylinder whenever the sluice was dropped. The pipe is half-choked with a calcified silt-plug, hard as tallow, through which a single thumb-sized bore still runs like a worm-hole. 

That bore is where I breathe, though *breathing* is too quick a word for it. It is more that the current washes through a sleeve of my own tissue, and the sleeve tightens when the vinegar-taste of the water sharpens, and relaxes when the iron-rot goes sweet and flat.

I had thought, earlier, that I might be one of the things with legs—one of those flat, horn-colored trowels that click against the plates, whose life is an endless, patient rasping at the orange felt. But their world is too wide; they have the terror of open verticality, the risk of dropping through the dark brine to land like an iron weight in the mud. And I am not one of the scarlet-gilled swimmers either. I do not have that shivering, electric nervousness that makes them lie rigid on a flywheel spoke for three days, waiting for a twitch in the silt. They have too much blood; their red fronds are too close to the surface, always bleeding their little warmth out into the stone.

No. What I have is mostly rind.

My back is turned to the curve of the pipe. Over the seasons—if there are seasons beyond the steady cooling and thickening of the drip—the mineral-leach has taken the outer layers of my hide and turned them into something like bog-iron: dense, layered, brittle at the edges where it flakes off into the drift, but seamless with the pipe-wall. If you dragged a brass scraper down the goose-neck, you would hit me and think you had struck an encrusted valve-seat or a lump of dropped lead.

Only the underside is soft. It hangs down into the hollow of the elbow like a cluster of waterlogged bladders, grey as pork fat, trailing fine, colorless fibrils that anchor themselves into the pitted iron.

I live on what the walking-beam shakes loose.

Every five minutes—or ten, or whenever the bucket high in the rafters gathers enough weight from the ceiling-tears to drop its counterpoise—the *clonk* comes. 

It does not arrive through the water as sound; it arrives through the iron as an impact that resets the joints. When the beam hits, the goose-neck shudders. A shower of brittle rust-scales, dislodged from the upper flange, falls through the dark and lands across my soft flank. Some of it is sharp; it cuts tiny, bloodless slits in the grey bladders, and into those slits the iron-oxidizing bacteria swarm immediately, orange and greasy, stitching the wound closed with their own crusts. 

I feed on the drift that gets caught in the fibrils: the pale, drowned skin of an isopod that has shed its segments in the current; the stringy mucus that drops from the spiders’ suspensory lines when the fog condenses too heavily; the wandering, blind leeches that lose their hold on the water-gates and tumble, knotting and unknotting themselves, down through the siphon.

When a leech touches my fringe, I do not strike. I don't have the jaws for striking. I simply close around it with the slow, hydraulic swelling of an organ that has no bone. The water in the goose-neck draws tight, the skin folds over, and the leech is pressed against the sulfur-crust of the iron until it stops thrashing and turns to grease.

Sometimes, during the low ebb—when the siphon shaft drinks deep and the pool drops by a foot, sucking the brine toward the central turbine—the current in my pipe reverses. 

That is the best part of the cycle. 

For thirty seconds, the water does not come from the upper galleries with its taste of street-wash and cold lime; it backs up out of the deep sump below. It brings with it the thick, heavy musk of the thing wedged in the intake grate—that vast, communal mass that sleeps around the bronze studs. The water that washes over me from the sump is ancient, greasy, and completely devoid of air. It smells of sixty years of rotted river-bottom, of buried lignite, of the slow, cold digestion of thousands of generations of pale things that died without ever seeing a spark of green bioluminescence.

It washes over my fibrils like cold oil, and for those few seconds, I can feel the shape of the whole chamber through the pipe-work:

I feel the high, rusted tank leaking through its rivet-seams forty feet above.
I feel the long, cold reach of the track-rails running out under the tea-water until they end in the silt-bank.
I feel the vibration of the hundreds of tiny brass hooks clicking on the boiler skins, like hail falling on sheet zinc.

Then the walking-beam heaves backward with a sucking sigh. The bucket rights itself. The water stalls, trembles, and starts its slow, downward crawl through the goose-neck once more.

I pull my fringes in. I settle deeper into the tallow-crust. The stone sweats; the pipe hums its faint, dissolving hum of iron and copper; and I wait, without eyes, for the next scale to fall.
