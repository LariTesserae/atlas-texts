At first, there is only the sensation of tooth-enamel ringing against itself.

Not pain, exactly, but the dry, chalky chatter of upper incisors biting lower incisors sixty times a second, driven not by a nerve in the head but by the flange beneath the left heel. The heel takes it from the steel; the shin takes it from the heel; the pelvis tilts to pass the tremor up the spine, where it collects in the back of the throat like swallowed grit.

If you asked me where my hands were, I would have to look down through two miles of empty, yellow-tinted steam to find them. They are clamped around a secondary gusset plate forty feet below the rim of the west cistern, and they do not feel like hands. They feel like lead sinkers that have taken the shape of a ninety-degree angle.

I did not begin by knowing what I was. 

For an hour—or three seasons, which are the same length up here if you measure them by the travel of the bevel-teeth—I thought I might be the iron itself, cooled just enough on its outer skin to grow a crust of grease-loving moss. There is very little difference between the temper of the steel plates and the skin on the inner side of my thighs: both are the color of an old kettle boiled dry on a peat fire, both are scored with parallel scrape-marks where the grit-drifts have scourged them, and both sweat a greasy, brownish moisture whenever the south bypass valve lets go its breath.

Then something soft and wet struck my shoulder—a lump of midges, blind and mashed together by the downdraft—and I wiped it away with the knuckle of my thumb. The gesture was too small for iron. Iron does not flick away the dead.

So I am meat. But not the soft meat that lives down where the chimneys end.

***

My ribs do not rise and fall. There is no room in the chest for that kind of pump; if my lungs took in air the way a valley-dweller’s do, the sheer acoustic weight of the planetary gears would burst the alveoli before the breath was half-drawn. Instead, the wall of the chest is an arched shield of cartilage, heavy as an ash shovel, that rocks forward and back on a single, thick hinge between the collarbones. It breathes like a bellows in an armorer’s shop: a short, hard draw that smells of tallow, parboiled sheep-flank, and the sulfurous lick of the chimney jackets, followed by a long, whistling hiss through the teeth.

I am wedged into the crook of Stanchion Nine, where the cross-tie meets the vertical column in a knuckle of thirty-two five-inch bolts. 

It is a quiet place, if quiet means that the iron only screams at the pitch of a hot file drawn across an anvil, rather than the wet, bone-splintering thunder of the center pit. Here, the air curls in an eddy. The great plume of vapor rising from the low boilers hits the cold stratospheric draft that leaks through the dome, and the two together drop a rain that is neither water nor oil, but a warm, yellowish butter that coats everything three inches deep.

My work is the drain-grooves.

A boy—if we had boys, though we have only small girdermen whose jaws have not yet thickened to the anvil-width—might look at the sixteenth stanchion and see only a wall of rust. But I know that between the eighth and ninth courses of rolled plate, there is a copper gutter four fingers wide, hammered into place when the consortiums still had names that could be pronounced without spitting. If that gutter chokes with tallow-sludge, the condensation backs up into the rivet-lines. If it stays there through the cold tilt of the fourth quarter, it freezes; if it freezes, it shears the rivets off with a sound like a four-pounder gun going off in an iron cellar.

So I have my gouge.

The gouge was once the wrist-pin of an air-pump, beaten thin on one edge against the drive-shaft’s counter-flange while the shaft was still red-hot eighty years ago. It has no wooden handle; wood rots in two weeks up here, turning into an orange mush that the flange-ticks eat like curd. The tang is wrapped in fifteen layers of swift-skin, dried hard with linseed and lashed with copper wire that I dug out of a dead telegraph conduit near the roof-ribs.

I push it along the seam. *Scrit. Scrit. Chk.*

The sound does not travel into the air. Nothing travels into the air except the roar. But through the thumb-joint, through the radius and the ulna, through the hard, gristly pad where my elbow rests on the iron, the scrapings come back to me:

*Iron... iron... dead lichen... tallow... scale... cold brass...*

When the gouge hits lead, the note goes dull, like a spade hitting lard. That is where a rivet has started to draw. I reach into the pouch at my belly—made of the same stiff, vulcanized rubber-leaf as the ferns—and take out a plug of cold-hammered scrap. I set it against the weeping seam, drop my jaw until my lower teeth bite the iron plate to take the measure of the rebound, and bring the heel of my palm down on the drift.

One.

The pylon shudders through my jaw, an enormous, slow wave that tells me the planetary gear is taking the weight of the noon counterweight three miles down.

Two.

The lead spreads. The yellow steam stops puffing through the crack. The seam is dumb again.

***

Sometimes I look out.

It takes a long time to look out, because turning the neck requires unseating the jaw from the brace, and once the jaw is off the metal, the balance goes strange. The emptiness between the pillars is so wide that the eye finds nothing to rest upon except the swifts, and the swifts are never still; they are slate-colored smears falling along the air-currents, gone before you can name the beam they crossed.

Across the vacancy—six hundred paces of yellow vapor that smells of boiling soup—I can see the flank of Stanchion Ten.

It looks like an upright mountain that has been turned on a lathe. On its catwalk, a mile above the cloud deck, there is a patch of white that does not move with the steam. It has been there since I was the length of a scraper. It is an old one, one of those whose hips have grown solid with lime from the water-joints. He has wedged his legs between two angle-irons and let the rubber-ferns grow over his shins.

He does not use a gouge anymore. He has become a damper.

When the cross-wind hits the dome from the northwest—the blue, thin wind that carries no meat and smells of salt and empty sky—the sixteenth pillar has a tendency to sing. Not the deep churn of the gear, but a high, thin whistle that shakes the marrow-midges out of the moss-mats before they can breed. A pillar that whistles is losing its tension; it means the diagonal tie-rod is stretching.

So the old one sits there with his shoulders flat against the web of the beam, his head thrown back so the nape of his neck touches the cold flange, his whole carcass turned into five stone of gristle and chalk-gout pressed into the vibration. He drinks the singing through his collarbones and turns it into heat. You can see the steam curling off his forehead in the mornings—a straight, thin finger of tallow-fume rising up toward the dome ribs, where the veil-rays drift with their skirts spread wide to catch it.

Yesterday—or twelve inches of gear-travel ago—he tapped three times with his heel.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

A long pause, during which the grease-drop fell from the cistern, struck the crown wheel, and atomized into the fog.

Then two taps. Fast.

*Cl-clang.*

He was telling the sector that the south cistern is leaning half an inch to the abyss. The cable is slackening on the lee side.

I did not answer him with the hammer. My hands were slick with red lead, and besides, the seventh stanchion was already answering. The sound came through the floorplates under my knees: a heavy, flat double-strike that tasted of zinc. *We know. The winch is choked with fern-root. We are burning it out.*

***

At night—though there is no night, only the dome turning from the color of wet tallow to the color of cold lead—I crawl into the hollow of an inspection portal.

It is four feet deep, three feet wide, bored into the solid cast of the stanchion to let the consortium’s men check the internal counter-spindles when the world was new. The men are gone; their boots were too thin and their ears had holes in them through which the roar entered and made them crazy. But the hole remains, dry and greasy, lined with fifty generations of swift-moult and the brittle, papery wing-cases of midges that crawled in here to rot.

I tuck my knees against my breastbone. My feet, curved like iron hooks, lock into the drainage scupper at the lip of the hole so the wind cannot pluck me out if a gale strips the catwalks bare.

In the dark, with my forehead pressed against the inner wall of the shaft, I can hear the heart of it.

It is not one shaft. It is three, nested one inside the other like the tubes of an engine-fitter’s lens, turning in opposite directions to cancel the torque that would otherwise twist this cradle off its legs and screw it into the clouds. The inner shaft turns fastest: once every three days. It groans with a long, sliding heave, a sound like an iron stove being dragged across a floor of sea-salt.

The second shaft turns once a month. It does not groan; it ticks. Every sixty seconds, a tooth the size of an ox-cart drops into a bronze dog with a thump that lifts my hips three inches off the swift-down.

The third shaft—the great one, the skin of the world—does not seem to turn at all. But if you hold your tongue against the iron for an hour, your spit will taste of brass at the beginning and tin at the end. That is how we know it is still bringing the deep work up from the dark.

I close my eyelids. They are thick and pale, like lard-skins, without lashes to catch the grit.

Behind them, there is no picture of a flat earth. I have heard the old ones tap out stories of a place where the iron sits on dirt, and the dirt goes on until it hits water that has no bottom and no stanchions to carry it. But that is talk for those whose jaws have softened.

There is only the pylon.

There is the thirty-foot bevel that must have its fat before the third watch.

There is the cold blue light coming through the ribs above, where something huge and thin—a veil-ray with a tear in its trailing skirt—is drifting through the steam, waiting for me to miss my footing on the wet iron so it can follow the draft down to where I stop.

It will wait a long time. My nails are hooked two inches into the rivet-heads, my gouge is cold against my ribs, and the seventh gear has just begun its stroke. 

The cradle is true. The teeth are holding. I press my jaw to the rust and take the drift into my bones.
