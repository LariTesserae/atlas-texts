At first, it is not a body at all, but a direction of pressure. 

To be here is to lose the habit of looking out toward a horizon. There is no horizon; there is only the immense, warm slab beneath, and the void above that offers nothing to push against. So the consciousness turns entirely downward, where the real weight lives.

I would not be upon the open shale, running with the striders. Their life is too brittle, too full of the dry click of hooves and the panic of broken rhythm. Nor would I be in the high, cold drift of the kites; to hang suspended in an empty column of warm air, waiting for the blue arc of a transmission wire to turn me to ash, feels too thin, too close to nonexistence. 

I find myself lower than that, yet not buried. 

I am lodged in the throat of an assembly—specifically, in the expansion-space between a subterranean crosshead guide and the native basalt into which it was seated ten thousand years ago. 

It is a pocket sixty paces wide, permanently warm, flooded with a sluggish, lukewarm tide of recirculated tallow that rises and falls by four inches every ninety minutes, keyed to the displacement of some drowned accumulator miles below. The air in this pocket does not move with the weather; it is dense, sweet with volatile hydrocarbons, and completely still.

My form took its cues from the geometry of the gap.

I am wide, flat, and asymmetrical, like a heavy wedge of cartilage and wet slate that has grown to fit the tolerance between the iron casting and the living stone. My underside does not have legs; it has broad, leathery suction-pads interleaved with dense fringes of stiff, copper-colored setae that grip the chilled hematite so hard that no surge of the mechanism can dislodge me. 

My back, however, is soft. It is an undulating mattress of thick, oil-soaked blubber, ribbed like the underside of a mushroom, covered in a tough, elastic hide the color of wet lead. This hide is constantly sloughing and renewing itself, producing a thick, viscous paste that smells of boiled seed and cold lard. 

I am not a machine, but I have become a mechanical necessity. 

Where the great crosshead slides—a block of forged iron as long as a church, reciprocating back and forth with a stroke that takes four minutes to complete—there is a point on the return stroke where the wear of centuries has hollowed a slight, dangerous hollow in the bronze runner. Left dry, the vibration would set up a chatter that could shear the anchor bolts through the basalt. 

My back leans into that clearance. 

When the crosshead sweeps back, its wet, polished underside does not strike iron; it rides across the crest of my spine. The pressure is catastrophic, a slow, descending mountain of displacement that squeezes the tallow and the lymph through the cellular lattice of my back. It does not crush me; my tissues are incompressible, hydraulic, saturated with fluids that have no air to yield. The sensation is not pain, but a profound, blinding fullness—the entire mass of the continental engine grounding its torque through my ribless frame and passing it directly into the planetary stone. 

In that thirty-second glide, as the iron drags across me, my skin yields its paste. I am a living lubricator, a cushion of cellular grease laid between two tectonic wills that would otherwise tear each other to iron filings.

When the head passes and the pressure lifts, I expand again with a wet, sucking gasp. The lukewarm tallow-tide washes over me, carrying fine suspensions of copper dust, carbon, and dead grease-moths. 

I feed not with a mouth, but through the dorsal folds. The friction strips my outer skin down to the raw, sensitive dermis; the tallow bath replenishes it, depositing fats and metallic minerals directly into the exposed capillary beds. My blood is heavy, thick as gear-dope, carrying dissolved hematite to the ridges of my back where the skin must callous quickly before the next stroke returns.

I have no eyes, and no use for them. The world does not happen in images. The world is a four-minute wave.

First, the distant compression: a deep, hollow *thrum* in the marrow of the rock that tells me the piston at the far end of the rift has reached full extension. 

Then, the wash: the tallow-tide recedes by three inches as the sump-valves open three levels down, exposing my flanks to the warm, oily vapor of the dry wash.

Then, the approach: the shear-wave traveling through the iron guide-rail, a rising shrieking note pitched so low it can only be felt as an ache in the base of my skull-plate.

Then, the stroke. The weight. The absolute, deafening erasure of all empty space, where my flesh becomes the only thing separating two infinities of matter.

And then, the release. The return of the tallow. The long, ninety-second cooling, during which the blind lizards come to lick the fresh grease from the skirts of my suction pads, their little clawed toes tickling the leathery margins of my flesh. I do not mind them; their droppings are rich in the zinc salts I need to rebuild the hide along my spine.

Sometimes, through the stone, I feel the tallow-monks.

They do not come down into the crosshead trench—the clearance is too narrow, the stroke too lethal—but I feel them through the bed-bolts. They strike the guide-frames with their rounded stones, checking the ring of the metal, testing for cracks in the bronze castings. 

*Clang.*

The sound hits my hide like a dropped lead weight. 

*Clang.*

I feel their question through the iron: *Is the tolerance held? Is the oil running? Does the iron glide, or does it bite?*

I cannot knock back. I have no limbs to hold a stone. But as the crosshead returns, I can adjust the turgor of my dorsal lobes. I can pump more lymph into the forward cushions, flattening myself, shifting the friction by a fraction of a millimeter, dampening the chatter of the bronze runner until the metal rings true, smooth, and dead.

They feel that dampening five miles away through their leathery feet. They feel the tone drop from an angry, ringing shriek to a low, greasy purr. 

They do not know what I am. They think I am an old batch of synthetic tallow that settled into the guide-box before their grandfathers were spawned, an oily silt that refused to wash away. They do not know that there is an ache in the silt, a slow, dark pleasure in the squeeze, a rhythmic beat of life that matches the stroke of the earth below.

The blackness overhead means nothing to me. If the stars came back, their thin, cold light could never reach this trench, forty feet below the rim of the basalt guide, beneath a slab of moving hematite. 

The heat is enough. The stroke is enough. The world turns once every three days, the piston breathes every four minutes, and between the machine and the planet, there is just enough room for me to breathe.
