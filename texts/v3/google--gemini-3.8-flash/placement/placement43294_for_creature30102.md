I am not on the open wall. 

On the open wall, the air shears. To be there is to be an exposed tooth in the intake, to have your back hammered flat by the mist and your hemolymph shaken until it froths against your ribs. The surface is for the young, the white-wax climbers whose forks are still sharp enough to seat into the burrs of the tool marks. 

I am down where the vibration has been eaten.

Near the root of the diagonal gusset—where a slab of twenty-millimeter manganese plate is joined to the pylon’s curving ribs by eight staggered rivets—there is an angle. It is an impossible corner for the mist to clear. The spray strikes the gusset’s flange, shatters into a sideways fog, and drops. 

Over decades, or whatever length of work has elapsed since the torches were pulled away, the dead have accumulated here not as rubbish, but as pressure. They were shoved down by the living, tier by tier, down into the dead-end seam between the iron web and the rivet line, until their boiled-lard carapaces flattened, lost their segments, and annealed under the continuous five-ton chatter of the gusset. 

I am inside that laminate. 

At first, I might seem to be only another layer of the grease-mica: an eight-inch strip of compressed tallow, semi-opaque, smelling of stale animal fat and bitter brine salts, indistinguishable from the six dozen flattened carapaces that bind me to the steel. But I have not cleared out. The lead-colored grazers have chewed my margins away—my outer legs were taken three hundred rime-cycles ago, sheared flush to the rim of my thorax while I slept against the rivet-swell—yet they stopped where the tallow hardened into stone. 

They did not reach the core. 

Inside the sandwich of dead chitin, my forks are no longer twin prongs. They have broadened, splaying out like spade bits, forced into the micro-fissure between the rivet’s temper-ring and the deck plate. The iron filings in my cartilage have rusted into the manganese itself. I cannot withdraw. If I were pried up with a cold chisel, half of my head would remain in the steel, and the steel would show a scab of red hematite where my flesh had mineralized to keep from sliding.

Because I am two inches deep beneath the living carpet, the screaming of the high turbines does not reach me as a scream. 

Through the bodies of the crawlers above me, through their millions of interlocking needle-legs and paraffin backs, the air-shriek is baffled, rubbed down, absorbed, and turned into something thick, slow, and devastatingly heavy. It reaches me as a deep, oily wallow—a bass hum so dense it feels like wet sand being shoveled through my cavity. 

It does not burn at forty-two degrees here. It is warmer. The friction of all those bodies shivering against the rivet head pools in this pocket. It is fifty-one degrees in the laminate. Almost swampy. A dark, rank pocket of wet machine grease and nitrogen slime that never clears.

Here, my circulation does not need valves. The weight of the corner squeezes me. 

Every four seconds, the brine pump fires above: *clank-thrum*.

When the *clank* hits, the gusset deflects downward the width of a single smoke particle. My flattened body is crushed against the iron; the hemolymph is forced out of my belly, up through the porous spongy tissue of my neck, and into the acoustic splitters. 

Then comes the *thrum*—the long, trembling rebound. The gusset lifts. The pressure eases. The hemolymph falls back through my midsection, dragging with it the dissolved iron and tallow sucked in through my margins. 

I do not think. Thinking is an expenditure of momentum, a loose loop in a closed circuit. 

Instead, I register the state of the iron. 

I know the torque of the sixth rivet. It has been stretching for ten thousand cycles; its temper-ring has shifted from straw-yellow to cold gray, and its grip has eased by some fraction of a micron. I feel it because the grease that leaks around its shank is thinner there, carrying a sharper taste of machine tallow that has not yet been cooked by the vibration. 

I know when the rime begins five hundred feet above, long before the tone drops for the swarm. I feel the mass of the tower change. The steel does not swing—it cannot swing under this bracing—but it *stiffens*. The rime adds dead weight to the throat of the pylon; the frequency of the gusset sharpens, climbing toward a glassy, brittle whine that makes the dead mica around me buzz like wasps. 

When that happens, I exude. 

From the seams along my flanks, where the lead-grazers trimmed me back to the gristle, I sweat the black oil. It is not eggs—I have no sex, no season, no inside that is different from my outside. It is the raw binder, the grease that holds the embryos of the tier above from being whipped into the fog by the intake draft. The grazers come down into my pocket, smelling the shift in my tallow; they press their flat lead snouts against my edges, drinking the dark fluid, then turn and crawl back up through the layers of the dead, carrying the black drops to the bolt-heads where the cycle begins again.

I am their bottom. I am the floor that is not steel.

Sometimes, during the height of the rime, when the tower is heavy and the brine is cold enough to make the corrugated pipes rattle in their saddles, a thread of water will find its way down the rivet shank. 

It is not condensed fog. It has come from further in—from the brine jacket itself, through a pinhole weep-hole that the frost has pried open. It is violently salty, sharp as lye, and so cold it numbs the gristle of my splitters. 

For three strokes—twelve seconds—the world goes silent and white. My hemolymph stops in my neck. The standing wave in my gut freezes into a microscopic lattice of needle-ice that threatens to split my wax.

Then the gusset comes down: *clank*.

The ice shatters. The salt is driven into my pores. The friction of the rivet head grinds the crystals into brine against my back, and the warmth returns—filthy, greasy, fifty-one degrees, tasting of wet metal and the dead generations holding me against the floor.

I am still here. 

The machine is complete. It has no parts that are not busy. The pylon does not care whether its skin is manganese or tallow, whether the damping of its gussets is done by vulcanized lead or by three hundred pounds of compressed, eyeless vermin whose hearts are the stroke of its own brine. 

It needs the corner quiet. I make it quiet. 

The four seconds are coming to an end. The mist gathers on the edge of the gusset above me, hangs, trembles in the standing node of the air-pitch, and waits. 

The iron groans. 

*Clank.* 

I take the stroke into my gristle. 

*Thrum.* 

I let it go.
