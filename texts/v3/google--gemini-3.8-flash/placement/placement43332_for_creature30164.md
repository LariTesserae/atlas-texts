At first, there is only the angle. 

In nature, water rounds everything, carving scoops and bowls and erratic hollows. But here, even after ten millennia of slow dissolution, the universe still has corners. To exist here is to understand that the world was once struck with an edge.

I would not be in the open drift. The middle water is too vast, too thin, too untethered; the drowners can keep that pale suspension, turning their blind, eight-foot barrels through the convective rise without ever knowing where the ceiling ends and the floor begins. Nor would I be in the runnels of the floor, smothered in the grease of the tallow-worms, drinking straight from the volcanic scald. 

I would be in the seam.

Where two megaliths meet—where an eighty-ton lintel rests upon the shoulder of a four-pace pillar—there is a hairline fissure. When the builders hauled these stones up the mountain and bedded them with pounded bitumen and wet clay, they left a gap no thicker than a thumb. Over thousands of years, the water has eaten out the clay, eaten out the lime, leaving only the black, rubbery tar-crust and an empty, horizontal shelf between two crushing infinities of basalt.

That is where I would be. 

I am not soft, though I am not made of rock. My body has the shape of a bronze chisel that was left in the joint and forgotten until it began to breathe. 

I am flattened, broad, and segmented—a creature like an ancient, overgrown chiton or a trilobite whose ancestral eyes have long since fused into solid bone. My dorsal plates are not chitin like the carpenters’, nor horn, but an aggregate shell: thick, layered laminations of calcium, sulfur, and powdered stone that have grown out of my own mantle. I have no need for limbs that run or scull. Along my margins are hundreds of blunt, muscular pads that grip the basalt with hydraulic tenacity, wedged so firmly into the horizontal seam that the weight of the lintel overhead does not crush me; rather, I hold the stones apart. 

I am a **shim**.

I do not move for decades. Movement is an indulgence of the light, an extravagance for things that must chase or flee. Here, the world comes to the joint. 

My senses are tuned entirely to the mechanics of the megalith. Through the dorsal plate pressed against the ceiling stone, and the ventral pads anchored to the pillar below, I feel the skeleton of the cistern. When the volcanic vein three miles beneath the mountain shoulder shifts its weight, a microscopic tremor travels up through the basalt flagstones, through the pillars, and passes through my marrow like a low, electric hum hours before the water warms by a single degree. 

I know the Bell’s cycle not by its bioluminescence—for I have no eyes to drink that faint, milky lantern—but by the stroke of its pulse against the stone. Every few minutes, when its great central dome contracts with that muffled, hydraulic *whump*, the water in my fissure jerks outward, then draws back in. It is a slow, rhythmic tide that washes over my gills, bringing the taste of the cistern into my mouth.

And the taste is dense. I taste the cold trace of the bitumen mortar that still clings to the deep interior of the crack: bitter, ancient, carrying the aromatic ghosts of pine-pitch and dead earth from whatever lowland forest the masons robbed to build this place. I taste the sweet, milky slough of the ivory sponges that crust the edges of my lintel, spreading their pale cushions right up to the boundary of my shell. I taste the passing of the drowners—the heavy, oily wake of a fish that has drifted twenty feet below, shedding its velvet blubber into the warm drift.

Sometimes, a carpenter comes to my stone. 

I feel the dry, rhythmic *pip-pip-pip* of its porcelain legs long before it arrives. It walks the perimeter of the pillar, measuring the chiseled chamfers with its caliper-claws, sweeping its seven-foot antennae across the rock face like blind whips. 

Eventually, the whip touches me. 

It does not draw back in fear; in this hall, nothing strikes. The antenna lingers on my margin, tasting the mineral encrustation of my plates, sweeping across the flat bevel of my head. The carpenter registers that the rock has ended and something alive has begun, but because I do not shift, because my chemistry is sixty percent the basalt it has known since it molted, it treats me simply as a ledge that breathes. It climbs onto my shell. 

I can feel the exact prick of its chalky claws upon my plates—six tiny, needle-sharp points of pressure traveling across my back. As it walks, its secondary mouthparts work furiously, scraping away the film of sulfur-bacteria and bryozoan spores that have settled upon my crest. It grooms me with surgical delicacy, cleaning my joints with the patience of an architect sweeping dust from an engraving. Then, with a brittle, muffled click, it steps from my shell onto the underside of the stepped arch, reaches out its reeds of claw to test the seam ahead, and drifts away into the dark.

I do not sleep, because sleep belongs to day and night, and the cistern has neither. There is only the cycle of convective roll: the water rising warm along the pillar's belly, licking my face, and rolling toward the ceiling where the sheath-leeches hang, then cooling against the mountain rock and falling down the far walls like a quiet curtain of lead.

I am an inhabitant of the builders' accuracy. Had their bronze tools been cruder, had they left the stones rough-hewn and wild, I could not exist; I need the straight line, the ninety-degree bed, the true and level plane that keeps the weight distributed evenly across my shell. 

I am the flesh that has grown into the mortar-gap, the memory of an empire's plumblines translated into living bone. The civilization that cut the mountain died when the air was young, but here, in the black, blood-warm broth, their joinery is still occupied. The stones have settled upon me, and they are heavy, and they are warm, and they are enough.
