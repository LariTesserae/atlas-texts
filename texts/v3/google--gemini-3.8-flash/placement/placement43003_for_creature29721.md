At first, it is difficult to know if there is a self here at all, or merely a location where the mountain’s weight becomes conscious of its own surfaces.

If you search for yourself with the instincts of the lowlands—looking for a heartbeat that taps out seconds, an eye that blinks against grit, a hunger that gnaws—you find nothing. There is no tremor in the chest. There is no frantic intake of breath, no sudden draft of air drying the throat. For a long time, the only presence is an immense, unhurried pressure: the downward drag of gravity through dense bone, met evenly from below by the flat, unbroken basalt of a terrace floor.

It begins not as a mind, but as a texture. 

I am down where the water is thickest, where it spreads over the dark flagstones in that silent, unbroken skin. My belly and the broad flat of my chest are pressed directly against the stone, so completely flush with it that the water does not flow under me or around me; it simply rises over the slope of my ribs and washes across my spine as though I were merely another stepped rise in the pavement.

For a long while, I could be a lintel that was dropped before it was squared. I could be a bench of river slate, or the residue of an ancient sill. 

Then comes the sensation of the gills—the slow, rhythmic percolation along my flanks. It is not breathing as animals know it; there is no rising and falling, no ribcage expanding against the sky. It is capillary. The high, warm vapor of the plateau, laden with the taste of wet stone and pulverized basalt, slides into those deep lateral folds of its own accord. It leaves behind its dissolved silt, its cool mineral salts, its damp nourishment, and seeps back out into the runnels. My blood does not race; it moves with the slow, heavy viscosity of cold linseed oil, turning once through the broad limbs in the time it takes the sun to clear three paces of terrace wall.

I do not have an inside that is separate from this place. The moisture that coats the retaining wall ten yards away is the same moisture currently sliding across my shoulders. 

I discover my hands before I discover my eyes.

They are resting outstretched on the bed of the canal, fingers spread. They do not feel like limbs; they feel like wedges. The skin of the palms is thick, yellowish-charcoal, and so densely calloused that it has taken on the microscopic negative impression of the basalt underneath it. Through them, the mountain is speaking, though the voice is not sound. 

It is a long, low vibration—a physical hum that lives in the basalt. 

Two miles to the east, along the rift bench, an enormous mass is settling. I feel it enter the heels of my hands: a deep, shuddering note, clean and single, like the ring of a dropped anvil heard through fifty fathoms of water. A slab has shifted four millimeters against its bed-joint. The alignment is complete. The sound passes through my forearms, vibrates softly in the heavy bridge of my collarbones, and travels down through the soles of my wide, flat feet back into the terrace floor, returning to the rock. 

I know the city this way. I know where the water is flowing too quickly, wearing an abrasive channel in the third sector; I know where a terrace wall has taken the weight of the spongy moss and swelled by half a hair’s breadth. 

Slowly, without haste, the horizontal will to move gathers in my joints.

It does not come from a sudden thought. It comes because the season—or the century—has tilted just enough that the film of water across this particular flagstone has broken into three thin ribbons instead of remaining a single, mirror-like sheet. A drop has formed. I felt the tiny, turbulent impact of its fall against the curb three paces downstream. It was a disturbance. A roughness.

I bring my limbs under me. The motion is hydraulic, silent, terrifyingly heavy. The club moss beneath my knees compresses, weeping clear water into the gutter, then waits to rise again. 

My head rises just high enough to see the avenue stretching away into the pale, warm vapor. My eyes have no lids to shut; they are covered with a thick, clear membrane that remains perpetually wet under the ceiling of mist. I see the world without glare: the long, disciplined lines of the basalt pavilions, the dark, waxy crowns of the leathery ferns in their sunken courts, the broad avenues that lead toward the sheared cliffs where the rock looks cut by wire.

Beside me in the shallow water lies my companion stone.

It is a nodule of diorite, round as an ostrich egg, greenish-gray, heavy as lead. It has no sharp edges; centuries of my own palms have rubbed it until its grain matches the hollows between my fingers. 

I do not stand—there is no need to present a tall face to the sky, which holds nothing that needs watching. I remain low, crouched on broad hams, balanced on four wide points like a living trestle. I take the diorite nodule between my hands. 

I place it against the lip of the flagstone where the water caught. 

Lift four inches. Drop. 
Lift four inches. Drop.

The sound does not echo. The heavy, warm air swallows it, turning it into a dull, flat *thud* that enters the stone and travels outward across the plateau, informing the others—wherever they lie in the meadows or the canals—that the work is continuing here. 

I will remain at this joint until the water ceases to break. Perhaps until the sun shifts behind the mist; perhaps until the ferns in the courtyard drop their sap three more times. My broad tongue will taste the exudate when the time comes; my flank-slits will drink the vapor; my skin will remain black and slick under the unblinking daylight.

I am not a visitor here. I am not an exile from a louder, softer world. 

I am the friction that keeps the stone from returning to wilderness. I am the weight that holds the terrace true. I am the silence, given two broad hands and an endless afternoon to smooth the edge of things.
