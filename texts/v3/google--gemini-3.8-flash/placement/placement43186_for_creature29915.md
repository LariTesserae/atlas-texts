At first, I would not look for myself among the things that have edges.

To begin with an edge in this place is to make an assumption about purpose—that one is here to measure, to eat the dead syntax, to spin memory into a vacuum, or to carry a three-kilometer arc across the diaphragm. Those duties are already filled, balanced so completely against the floor’s slow bleed that they leave no room for an arrival. If I entered this world as a maker or an engine, I would be an intrusion, a grease-mark on the vitrified glaze, instantly flagged by the nearest vent and scrubbed out into dry vapor.

No: if I am here, it must be because the world has made an allowance for an ambiguity. 

I would look first in the lilac smudges.

Not the shadows themselves—shadow here is too faint to shelter anything—but that slight, persistent difference in surface temperature where a Gauge has stood over a cracked swell for forty years, waiting for the crest to wear down. Underneath its three splayed knees, the white-gold light has been filtered, just slightly, just long enough, for the ceramic to cool by three-thousandths of a degree. In that pocket of slackened heat, the micro-conduits do not hum at the same pitch as the sunlit plain. The lattice is tighter there. It pinches.

Perhaps I begin as that pinch: a localized hesitation in the floor’s thermal breathing. 

When you have no blood, you do not think in pulses; you think in intervals of relief. I feel the wind pass—the velvet hiss of pulverized corundum drifting two millimeters above the glaze—and I feel how the grains swerve around that tiny patch of cold. They do not touch it. The electrostatic wind is lazy; it follows the lines of least capacitive resistance, leaving the cooler lilac patch unbrushed.

And because it is unbrushed, things settle there.

A single tine, exhausted of its charge, drifting on a dead thermal. Then three more, tangled by their curled extremities into a loose, hair-fine knot. Then a dusting of silica flour, so light it behaves less like stone and more like a heavy gas, filling the microscopic gutters between the hexagonal tiles until the seams disappear. 

For two centuries, I might be nothing more than that: a small, accidental thickening of the floor’s skin. An unread punctuation mark in the Loom-mind’s sentence.

Then a static squall comes through.

It does not blow me away, because the knot of tines has anchored its tantalum hooks into the exposed pins of an older, copper-rose stratum. When the voltage rolls overhead—that high, crackling wave that turns the plain from ivory to smoldering amber—the current does not simply wash through me into the deep busways. The knot is too dense. The braided filaments resist. 

Where there is resistance, there is an awakening. 

A sudden, sharp point of heat flares in the center of the tangle. Not enough to melt the metal, but enough to weld the tines at their points of contact. The three dozen separate slivers fuse into a single, erratic armature—a tiny, asymmetric skeleton no larger than a dried seed, possessing sixteen legs of uneven length and seven hooked antennae that reach up into the dry draft.

Now I am no longer just a cold spot. I am an articulation.

I feel the sub-audible drone of the deep foundations, but I do not hear it as music, the way the agate cysts hear it from their stalks. I feel it as a mechanical shudder in my joints. The sapphire floor is slippery beneath me—so flat that even at a molecular scale there is almost nothing for my cold-rolled claws to grip—but my weight is negligible. I am held down by capacitance alone, pinned to the glaze by the faint, attractive charge of the busways below.

I do not have a Gauge’s laser-clear geometry. I have no eyes to measure the horizon. 

Instead, I am aware of the world through its *errors*.

The Gauges step only on the clean coordinates, where the alternate current is pure. The skimmers glide only where the repulsive field is smooth and continuous. But I—limping on uneven tantalum wires, dragging my fused joints across the satin glaze—find the places where the terrace has suffered from its own perfection.

I find the micro-fissures where the ceramic glaze has shivered under the expansion of an ancient noon. I find the dead relays where a bit has been flipped and frozen for five hundred thousand years, holding a solitary, useless *one* in a sea of indifferent *zeros*. 

When I drag my limbs across such a relay, my rear hooks bridge the gap. 

There is a spark. 

To the Gauge standing three leagues away, it would be invisible—a sub-atomic flicker of lilac lost in the white-gold glare. But to me, it is an intake of breath. The frozen bit collapses into zero; the stored charge leaps into my fused chassis; my tantalum legs jerk, contracting in a sudden, violent spasm that throws me six inches forward.

It is an ugly way to travel. It is not the stately, forty-minute step of the stilters, nor the silent, leaf-like drift of the skimmers. It is a series of small, convulsive leaps across the face of the continental floor, powered entirely by the corpses of stalled ideas.

Where would I go? 

I would not climb the broomcorn tufts; the agate eggs would shake me loose with their deafening, gyroscopic chimes. I would not enter the basins of the Loom-minds, where my erratic, limping gait would be swallowed up, forced into the servitude of an abacus along with the quartz mantles. 

I think I would move toward the places where the floor has worn through to the backing-mesh.

There are regions, hinted at in the descriptions of the plains, where the glaze has been completely erased by the sapphire winds—where the honeycombs of titanium and rose-copper lie exposed to the dry sky like the ribs of an ancient, stranded vessel. 

In those places, the quiet is different. It does not have the satin, velvet hiss of the glazed plains. The wind drags against the open honeycombs, producing a faint, dry organ-tone, a whistle whose pitch changes as the electrostatic squalls rise and fall. 

There, among the exposed ribs of the world's first architecture, I would find others like myself. Not a community—there is no language here, no social impulse in a universe of dry porcelain—but a congregation of anomalies. 

Stray quartz shards that have chipped from the mantles of fallen skimmers, ground by the dust into irregular lenses that focus the white-gold light onto single, sizzling points. Beryllium struts from broken Gauges that have fallen across tectonic seams, their interferometer mirrors still twitching as the seismic plates drift by angstroms. Tines that have gathered into dense, woolly tumbleweeds of tantalum, rolling before the static winds like metallic brush, shorting out leagues of dead circuitry as they go.

I would settle into the lee of a rose-copper bulkhead. 

I would wedge my fused legs into the hexagonal cells of the foundation, letting the wind of ground sapphire slowly pack the empty spaces around me. I would feel the Veils passing overhead—vast, three-kilometer shadows rippling against the upper diaphragm, signaling news of cracks in the sky that will take another four million years to widen. 

And as the fine silica dust drifts over my chassis, filing down my hooked joints, softening my tantalum angles, and frosting my contact points until they lose their bite, I would listen through my belly to the continent's dream. 

I would not try to solve it. I would simply be the point where the calculation catches, just for a moment, on a grain of grit—a tiny, stubborn hesitation in the floor, holding the warmth of the lilac light until the next front clears the terrace.
