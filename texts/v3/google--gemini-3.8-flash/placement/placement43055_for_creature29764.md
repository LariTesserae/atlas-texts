To begin, there is only the refusal of a boundary. 

There is no heat inside to be kept from leaking out, and no heat outside to press inward; the blubber is packed so dense with mica flakes that the tissue simply does not register the concept of a gradient. You do not feel cold. You feel *density*. You feel the sheer, unlubricated tonnage of the brine sliding over your flank like heavy oil, saturated with salts and metals that taste of crushed pennies and wet battery plates whenever the spiracles draw it in.

You are not standing. You are pinned—not by malice, but by the casual, blind architecture of the crush. 

At first, you might think you are one of the great bulls, three meters of pale tallow inching up a tier, your blunt webbed claws clicking against the fullerene, your ribs vibrating as a neighbor’s flank shears past yours with the wet, tearing sound of rubber dragged over slate. You feel the frantic, microscopic scratch of crawlers scouring your grease vents, their carborundum claws clicking like clockwork beetles across your back, and you feel the upward draft carrying the sting of ammonia from the abyss.

But you are not moving. You have not moved for three cycles of the strut-resonance.

You are down in the narrow, non-Euclidean crotch of Tier Four, where a cantilevered drainage gutter meets a shear-pylon at an angle so acute that only the wedge of a skull could fit into it. You came here heavy, your belly low with the weight of leathery, spherical pods, seeking the dead-space where the deafening roar of the herd narrows into a single, whistling draft. You shoved your blunt snout into the corner, seeking the geometry, because the geometry is the only thing that does not give way when a hundred bodies lean against your hips.

And the geometry took you.

The virgin fullerene does not weather; it shaves. As the blind traffic shuffled behind you, nudging, mounting, pressing forward in their slow, hydraulic panic to climb against the brine, their weight drove you deeper into the join. The edge—fresh from whatever high-order print cast it—was sharp enough to part hairless hide without pain. 

It did not hurt. The blood does not spill quickly when it is thick with violet copper-complexes and cold as sea-trench water. The razor-seam of the corner simply sliced through the greased tallow of your snout, through the mica-sheeted fascia, until it met the thick, cartilaginous shield between your blind nasal pits. 

And there, it wedged into the bone.

Now, you do not need eyes, and you do not need the blind clacking of your jaw. The fullerene has become an extension of your cranium. 

The seven-hertz thrum that keeps the plateau’s water from freezing does not arrive at your ears through the thick air; it enters the apex of your skull directly from the foundational strut. It runs through your jaw, down your spine, rattling the heavy copper platelets in your violet blood until your whole body becomes a tuning fork anchored to the chassis of the city. 

You feel the migration not as a crowd, but as a distribution of loads across a beam. 

Through the bone-contact, you can feel forty thousand tons of fat dragging across Tier Two, three kilometers to the north, their claws scratching the graphene plates in rhythmic waves that match the subduction hum. You can feel the flutter of the anchors overhead—the delicate, keratinous tension of their pedestals as the convective updraft from the abyss tries to rip them from the expansion joints. You can feel the scrapers far below in the hydraulic channels, their diamond-toothed suckers rasped against the floorplates, shedding faint electrical sparks that propagate through the frame like microscopic pinpricks against your palate.

You have already dropped the pods. You felt them slip into the gutter beneath your belly days ago—or weeks ago, time measured only by the swelling and slackening of the brine sheets. You felt them roll away, bouncing down the stepped terraces like heavy shot, clattering toward the electromagnetic curing tanks at the base of the plateau. They are gone, hardening in the black torrents.

You did not follow them. You did not back out.

Behind you, the herd has found you convenient. You are no longer a body trying to climb; you are a baffle. Your back, pale and slick with alkaline tallow, has become a rounded curb against which the ascending bulls turn their shoulders. Thousands of them grind against your hips as they make the bend toward the upper skyway. Crawlers have colonized your flanks in stationary colonies, living generation after generation on the perpetual film of grease your dermal glands keep weeping into the friction. Their feeding palps work constantly against your skin, polishing the leather until it gleams dull like wet soapstone.

Higher up, caught in the eddy of your stalled mass, an anchor has taken root upon your left shoulder blade. Its vulcanized shell has etched chemically into your thick hide, mistaking your mineral-dense blubber for the stable composite of the city wall. Its translucent feeding filaments hang down from your flank, drifting in the turbulent slipstream of the passing herd, catching the exhaled plumes of ammonia and spit that puff from the spiracles of the passing giants. 

You do not mind the weight. You do not mind the anchor. Its root-pedestal hums with the same seven-hertz pulse that vibrates your snout.

You are changing. In the cold and the dark, where rot cannot exist, biology does not decay; it mineralizes under pressure. The fullerene edge in your skull is leaching subtle field-currents into your nervous system, replacing the slow, organic impulses of the abyssal salamander with the swift, inductive logic of the platform itself. 

You no longer breathe in rhythms of panic or hunger. You vent your spiracles only when the drainage ribbon overhead—suspended in mid-air by the null-gradient—trembles, collapses, and falls in a heavy, meter-thick sheet across your neck, washing away the salt crusts and feeding the copper in your veins.

You are the point where the reef becomes aware of its own load. 

When the tectonic grind below changes pitch—when the wet basalt shifts against the wet iron miles down in the trench, altering the frequency of the foundational hum—you feel the micro-strain in the strut before the rest of the herd senses it. You feel the fullerene flex by a fraction of a micron. Through the corner wedged in your face, you taste the torque of the plateau leaning into the mist.

And in response, deep in your chest, in that hollow, cavernous lung designed to process the weight of unheated clouds, you let out a low, involuntary pulse—a chest-rattling octave that passes from your ribs into the graphene floor, telling the hundreds of blind, heavy things scraping past your flank precisely where to set their claws.
