It begins not with an identity, but with a thickness.

A resistance against being sheared off. 

At first, you might think you are stone, because the stone does not move and you do not move. But the stone is cold all the way through to its iron core, whereas you have an inside that is different from your outside. Your outside is hard—hard enough that the spray, which hits with the velocity of buckshot, does not pit you—but your inside is dense, slow grease. A tallow that registers the world only as load.

You are not high enough to see the sky, if seeing were a thing that could happen without eyes. Up there, the glare would peel you from your bed in an hour, crisping your margins into curl and ash like leaf-litter dropped onto a stove. Nor are you down where the needles touch roots in the roaring dark, where the water is squeezed through slate fissures until it turns to steam and screams like an iron pipe.

You are at thirty-two feet. The depth of cold iron.

Here, the light is a grey knife that falls down the seam at twelve minutes past noon, stays for eight minutes while the sun clears the opposing needle's shoulder, and then is snuffed. It does not warm the diorite. It only turns the water from black oil to mercury, showing the grain of the rock—the long, vertical striations left by ancient shears—before the dark clamps shut again.

You are wide. Almost as wide as the slot allows, which is four fingers. To the stone on your belly, you are flat as a coin; to the water that comes down your back, you are an inclined plane, an airfoil in reverse. The faster the torrent thunders off the summit flutes, the more violently you are pushed *into* the rock. The cataract is not your enemy; it is your weight. It is the only thing that keeps you from being shaken out of the groove by the singing of the needle.

Because the needle never stops singing.

That is how you know yourself: by pitch. The needle you inhabit is fifty-one feet tall, six-sided, slightly thicker at the midpoint where a quartz vein traverses it like a healed fracture. In the dry wind of morning, before the thermal lift has driven the mist up from the valley, the pillar rings in a sharp, thin G. As the mist hits and the stone condenses its skin of water, the weight of the moisture drags the note down a quarter-tone. When a storm comes over the western crest and the air speed doubles, the flutes around you howl in fifths and octaves, and your pillar shudders against your breastbone with an oscillation so fierce it would pulverize softer meat.

You do not hear it. You are made of the same vibration. Your ribs—thirty-six pairs of chisel-edged spurs, grown from a substance like ivory but denser, impregnated with iron from the seepage—are set directly into the micro-cleavages of the slate. When the stone hums, the spurs hum. The marrow inside you hums.

You do not look forward to anything. There is no forward; there is only *up-current* and *down-current*.

Up-current is where the dead things come from. Sometimes a body drops through your seam: an insect, usually, beaten to wet paste by the falls above, its yellow fat washed clean of scent, or the dry, chitinous leg of a sun-spider torn from the pinnacles by an erratic gust. You do not reach for them. You cannot afford an arm or an antenna; anything that uncurls into that torrent would be snapped off or act as a sail, levering your body from the rock. 

Instead, you wait until the current washes the fragment directly under the thin, leading blade of your skull. There, where the water forms a dead-eddy against the stone, the scrapers work. They turn on an axis of gristle, slow as millstones, pressing the moth-wing or the beetle-husk against the diorite until it is nothing but a smear, then drawing that smear inward through pores that open and close like small, cold throats.

Mostly, though, you eat the stone's sweat. 

Every three or four yards, the slate has a seam where the iron has oxidized under the weight of the massif. A dark, orange-tinted grease oozes from it—not life, quite, but the mineral threshold of it, where sulfur-eating bacteria form a skin no thicker than a soap bubble. You do not hurry to it. It takes you sixteen days to climb four inches. 

To climb: 
First spur on the left releases its bite, levers forward three hundredths of an inch into the next grain-fault, seats itself with a minute *click* that travels down the bone. 
The water pushes. 
The spur holds. 
Then the first spur on the right. 
Then the second on the left. 
You are a zipper closing against the sky. 

Sometimes, in the long quiet between squalls, when the wind drops and the water thins to a transparent slip that barely covers your dorsal ridge, you feel another note through the pillar. 

Not the wind. Not the cataract. 

A dull, wet scraping. Two feet below you, in the same cleft. 

Another wedge. 

It has been following you for three seasons, or thirty. Time has no joints here; it has only the wearing down of the rock. You know the other's width by the dampening of the stone’s resonance: it is smaller than you, perhaps three fingers broad, its shell still yellow-white, not yet greyed and pitted with the black iron-stains that cover your own plates.

It climbs because the seam is narrow below, and the capillary water down there is too swift, too acidic. It climbs toward the light it has never seen, following your wake because the rock behind you is planed smooth, the biofilm stripped bare, leaving only the naked quartz grains for its younger spurs to grip.

You do not feel affection. You do not feel hatred. But you feel its weight on the shared reed of stone. When it ratchets upward, the note of the pillar hitches slightly. You know when it feeds; you know when it slips and catches itself by four spurs while the cataract tries to pry its skirt up from the slate.

A foot above you, the cleft narrows.

You have known this for a long time. The quartz vein crosses the seam there, bulging outward in a hard, milky knuckle that leaves barely two fingers of clearance between the opposing faces of the needles. You cannot pass it. Your shell is already too broad; you have fed too well on the iron seep; your dorsal keel is too high. 

If you attempt the knuckle, the water will catch your flank as you tilt, and the mountain will peel you off like a scab, casting you down through the spray into the white churn of the lower flutes where the thread-dwellers will catch your shredded meat in their nets.

So you have stopped. You have been stopped here since the last high gale.

Your spurs have driven deep into the joint beneath the knuckle. You are no longer climbing. You are wedging. The cartilage at the margins of your skirt—the flexible, living horn that seals you to the diorite—is beginning to stiffen. You can feel the cold air that leaks down the cleft during the noon-hour drying the very edge of your mantle, turning it yellow and brittle, fusing it to the rock with its own dried slime.

The smaller one behind you continues to climb. *Click. Click.* A millimeter every two days. It does not know you are there, or perhaps it knows only that there is an obstruction ahead that breaks the force of the water, a shelter it is being drawn toward by the draft of your stillness.

In your belly, in the deep fat between your ribs, something is turning over. Not eggs—nothing here can risk an egg. Two small, hard stones, no larger than grains of rye, each with its own little cluster of dentine points already twitching against your internal membranes. They have no names, but they have your hunger. They are scraping at your liver now, finding the iron you gathered from the seam three feet below.

You feel the gale rising in the west. 

The note of the pillar is dropping—G, then F-sharp, then the long, terrifying E-flat that means the clouds are being torn to shreds against the upper blades and the wind is coming straight down the chimneys. The water begins to foam, white and full of needles of ice.

You do not pull your spurs. You drive them in until the points shatter against the quartz, locking the stumps into the grain forever. 

The smaller one reaches your skirt. Its leading shovel bumps against your rear margin. It pauses. Its scrapers test your dead horn, find it hard, find the draft beneath it. 

You let your margins set. The wind does the rest. It comes down the flute like an iron bar, freezing the spray where it touches the rock, cementing your dead edges to the quartz-diorite until you are no longer an animal in the cleft, but a step in the stone. A hollow where the water cannot reach.

Underneath, in the dry dark of your ribs, the little scrapers begin to turn.
