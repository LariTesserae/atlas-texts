It takes three days for a spilled cup of tallow to stop tasting of the goat it came from and begin to taste of the floor.

I am down where that change happens. Not five tiers deep among the lard-skinned who run on their knuckles, and not up on the skin where the chalk flies and the mallet-gangs lean their brows together to hum. I am in the third watch down—the *slack-reach*, they call it, though there is nothing slack in it.

The ash here is no longer green. It has ceased weeping its sap into the seams, but it has not yet gone grey and dry like bone. It is the color of a wet horse. It is the season of timber when the fibers are finishing their first big stretch, when every tenon is trying to pull out of its mortise by the width of a barleycorn, and if someone is not there with a horn wedge and an eight-pound maul of boxwood to say *no, stay*, the entire bay will buckle upward like a blister on a burned hand.

That is where I find myself. Sitting in the angle where three spruce stringers cross a king-post, my knees tucked into the space where the brace will go next winter.

***

At first, I thought I was an auger-man who had dropped his stone and stayed behind to look for it. Then I thought I was one of the ones who catch the babies—the ones who sit on the ash staging with cold hands, waiting for the split-second when the greased shoulders slip through the floorboards. But my palms are not soft enough for infants, and I have no iron on me. You cannot keep iron down here; the lime-sweat eats it until it looks like dry peat.

I have only a pocket of dry ash wedges, a horn rule with no notches on it, and my heels.

My heels are the largest part of me. If you saw them from the light of an oil-wick, you would say they were the burls you find on the flanks of old birch trees—hard, yellowish, cross-grained, without any feeling in the skin until you reach the bone itself. I do not walk. There is nothing to walk on except the four-inch faces of the lower joists, and even those are greasy from the dripping of the courses above. I swing. I hook my heels over the cross-struts and let the weight of my ribs pull the joints tight while I work.

You can tell what season it is by the salt.

Above me, fifty feet up, the new floor is being laid down this morning. I know this because every twenty minutes, a drop of water hits the back of my neck. It does not smell like rain; it smells like slaked rock. It is hot with the lime. It leaves a crust on my shoulder like the dried spit of a bull. By noon, the water will be cooler, and by the second watch it will be oily with the grease they use to lubricate the drop-pins. When that grease reaches me, cold and curdled like lard, I know the ram-teams are changing shifts.

The sound does not come through the air. The air here is as quiet as an empty well, save for the draft that pulls steadily from the south-southwest—an enormous, cold inhalation that smells of rotted rushes and wet hemp, rising from the tiers that were roofed over before my mother’s grandmother had her heels chalked. The sound comes through the spruce.

I have my ribs pressed against the post. Every time the heavy rams drop three miles off, the spruce does not merely hum; it *heaves*. It gets thicker by the thickness of a fingernail, then thinner, then settles.

If the settle is right, the wood gives a small, greasy sigh as the tallow-cakes squeeze into the mortise. It is a good sound. It sounds like a fat man pulling off a wet boot.

If the settle is wrong, there is no sigh. There is only a sharp, needle-like *plink*—the sound of an ash pin taking a shear it was never split to bear. When I feel that *plink* against my breastbone, I do not rap the wood. The Upper-folk do not care about a single pin; they have twelve thousand more to drive before the grease-pans cool.

Instead, I reach into the gap between the post and the sill with a horn spatula.

There is a silverfish there. She is eight inches long, lead-plated, her back as broad as a trencher. She is jammed into the crevice because the grease there is three seasons old and sour as curd, which is how she likes it. She does not move when I touch her horn plates; she only sets her amber hooks deeper into the sapwood.

I do not kill her. If I kill her, the tallow rots dry and turns to powder within forty days, and then the draft gets in. A joint with the draft in it is a joint that will shrink.

I slide the spatula under her belly, lift her three inches—she gives a dull, wet click with her mandibles, tasting the birch-oil on my thumb—and I drive a wedge of boiled horn directly into the meat of the split behind her.

*Tap. Tap.*

I use the butt of my palm, not a mallet. A mallet makes an Upper-sound; it invites an answer, and if they answer with four raps, they will drive another pin down through my ceiling and split the brace entirely. The palm makes a flat, dead *thump* that belongs only to this tier.

The silverfish settles back down over the wedge. Her belly-plates are cold and soft, like the fat on the top of cooled broth. She covers the horn. She begins to scrape again with that slow, circular chewing that sounds like someone sandpapering a shield in another room.

The spruce stops heaving. The shear has passed into the post; the post gives it to the transverse; the transverse drops it down into the dark, into the deads, where the Under-men can worry about it or wedge their grandfathers against it, whichever they prefer.

***

Sometimes I think the floor has a bottom, but it is only a thought I have when my stomach is empty and the lime-water has given me the mouth-itch.

If there were a bedrock, the timber would not have that long, slow crawl under the thighs. You can feel that the world is not standing on a stone; it is standing on the things that were made yesterday. It is like being the seventh layer of turf on a roof that has been thatched every autumn for a hundred years: you are not roof, and you are not earth; you are the place where the heat of the house meets the rot of the rain, turning slowly into something dark and heavy that has forgotten the ditch where the reeds grew.

A slick came through my bay an hour ago.

Not one of the blind hunters from the Upper-baskets, but a stray—one that had slipped its collar three winters back and grown long in the rib. It had no hair on its tail at all; the tallow had scalded it off. It came along the top of the joist like a thick white slug, its paddle-feet making no sound on the wet wood, its nose-pit twitching at the draft.

It stopped at my knee. It smelled the grease on my clout—the fat of the rams that drips through the floor-planks has coated me until I am the color of an old harness. It didn't bite. It laid its chin on my ankle, where the skin is thickest, and let its throat vibrate.

It wasn't purring. A ferret doesn't purr. It was catching the concussion from my bone. It was using my shins to find where the ram-stroke was falling, so it would know which way the draft was going to blow when the vents opened.

I reached down and peeled two fat silverfish-lice off its ear-folds and put them into my mouth. They taste of cold lard and ditch-lime, like everything else, but they are crunchy between the back teeth. The slick didn't care. It stayed there until the three-mile drop shook the joist, then it slipped down through a four-inch auger hole into the blackness below with that soft, oily *slurp* that means the hole has been well-bored and has no splinters.

Now it is quiet again.

Above me, the mallet-raps have changed. It is the five-stroke count. *Take the weight.*

They are going to grease the cables in the north. The rams will hang for two hours, twenty tons of split boxwood and river-stone balanced on tripods of lashed birch, swinging four inches above the green pins while two hundred boys with greased bladders climb the frames to smear the cedar journals.

The floor will rise.

It always does. When the rams are up, the whole province lifts two spans. The air between my spruce balks will turn cold as the draft sucks back down through the auger holes. The timber will stretch itself out, making that long, splintering groan that sounds like an old woman turning over in a willow basket.

I tuck my thumbs into my groin-strap to keep them from the frost that comes with the lift. I press my forehead against the post, right where the adze-scars form a little cup that fits the bone above my left eye.

I can feel them up there, through the four inches of terra-cotta and the eight inches of ash.

Millions of them. Their bare, horn-plated feet are shuffling three inches forward, three inches back, never lifting. Their faces are white with the dust. Their mouths are shut against the lime. They think they are making the ground.

They don't know that the timber is only waiting for them to finish the course so it can sag down onto me, and that I am only waiting for the next watch down to settle so I can let the horn wedges go.

A drop of lime hits my ear. It is nearly cold now.

The lift is beginning. The spruce begins to hum its thin, whistling note—the green-wood note, the one that says the tree still remembers which way the wind used to lean it before they cut off its head and brought it into the dark.

I close my eyes. I wait for the *plink*. When it comes, my palm is already greasy and ready to strike.
