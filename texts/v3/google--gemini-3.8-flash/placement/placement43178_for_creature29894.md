At first there is only the tension along the seam. 

It is not an ache—there is no marrow to throb, no salt-fluid to pool behind an injury—but rather the clean, bright mathematics of a shear-load. A force of ninety tons pulling east-northeast, answered by ninety tons of counter-torque anchored somewhere forty spans deeper in the root-pavement. Where those two vectors cross, there is a point of zero displacement. 

That is where the register begins. 

Not with an eye opening, because there is nothing to open, and the violet light would only pit an exposed lens with microscopic craters of burned silica. The world arrives instead as pitch. A low, continuous D-flat that is the sound of the continent resisting its own curvature; above it, the dry, stuttering snare-drum chatter of graphite scales slipping half a hairsbreadth inside an ironwood cylinder three leagues out; and, very close, the thin, dry whistle of wind being planed across an edge of horn.

My edge.

I am not loose upon the root-mat. To be loose across the open hexagons is to be either a strider—hurled forward by the violent, twitching agony of thermal expansion—or it is to be grit, rolling until the wind wedges you into a stress-crack. I have too much mass for the gales to lift, but not enough to serve as an anchor. 

For a long time, the posture feels like an embrace. There is a diagonal spar, forty cubits of dense, silica-veined heartwood, running from the third outrigger of an engine-tree down into the swollen burl of a stabilization pivot. My limbs—if they are limbs and not merely the split ends of a single, laminated leaf-spring—are clamped to this spar with cold-welded vice-jaws of sintered bronze. 

Am I part of the truss? 

The wind argues that I am. It plays through the hollows of my ribbing with the same shrill, unvarying third it extracts from the neighboring cross-braces. When the spire’s four-mile piston finishes its southern stroke and drops its exhaust—a dry, incandescent coughing that smells of charred amber and powdered stone—the concussion hits my chest-plate with the exact same deadening *thud* that rattles the framing. 

Yet there is a difference in the clearance. 

The tree cannot loosen its bite. It is bound by its own immense, petrified leverage; if it slips by a millimeter, the reciprocating rod will bite the sleeve, the friction will spike to white heat in three beats of the pendulum, and the whole southern flank will detonate into a mile-high geyser of incandescent splinter-meal. 

I, however, have tolerances.

Under the cold-pressed horn of my flank, there is an assembly of small, dry rollers—cylinders of turned corundum that sit within shallow, graphite-dusted races. When the shadow of the gantry-branch creeps overhead, sharp as a surveyor’s line, the drop in temperature is immediate: eighty degrees gone in the flick of an escapement. 

The spar beneath me does not move; its mass is too vast to answer so quickly. But my dorsal plates are thin—mere shavings of compressed mica bound with fossil gum. They snap. 

It is a violent, involuntary reflex: *clack-clack-clack*. The contraction draws my caliper-claws inward by three inches. The bronze jaws release the bark. 

For the span of two engine-strokes, I am falling. 

Not downward into the abyss of the root-plates—that would be an end, a sudden reduction to graphite dust under the scouring squalls—but sliding. I ride the polished belly of the diagonal spar, down along the path of the grain, my corundum rollers singing a clean, screaming note against the silica-veins. The wind hits me broadside: forty knots of razor-edged vacuum that tries to peel my plates apart like dry husks. 

I do not fight the wind; I let it drive an internal rotor. High inside my thoracic chamber, where a heart would be if there were anything here soft enough to pump, a dry wheel of paper-thin horn begins to spin. Ten thousand revolutions a minute, dry-lubricated by the microscopic soot that coats everything on this plateau. The gyroscope resists the gale. It rights me. It forces the blunt wedge of my prow down against the spar until the friction-shoe bites, smoking faintly with the scent of hot resin, bringing me to a shuddering, rhythmic halt at the next junction.

Here, the shade is absolute. The violet sky is blocked by the immense bulk of the counterweight—a hanging mass of solid iron-burl sixty paces through, swinging on its braided lignum cable with a slow, planetary patience. 

*Hooom. Hooom. Hooom.*

Every swing takes twenty seconds. With each pass, the displacement of air creates a pocket of near-vacuum, followed immediately by a wall of compressed chill that smells faintly of copper. 

I settle into the knuckle of the joint. My calipers bite the wood again, ratcheting shut with a dull, dry *tock*. 

Now that I am still, the smaller business begins. 

I am not here to feed on the tallow-spores; the kites tumble for those in the high sun, their paper wings crackling like burning parchment as they twist in the thermal plume. Nor do I have the heavy, lathe-bit mandibles of the scrapers who live further up, inside the darkness of the cylinder-sleeves, risking their segments to lick the carbonized grease from the rams. 

I am an acoustic weed. A parasite of the clearances. 

Between the moving bronze cap of the pivot and the petrified socket it rides in, there is a gap no thicker than a fingernail. If a single splinter of flint-dust, blown in from the dead plains, settles into that interface, the grain will gall. The joint will chatter. 

I extend my mouthparts: not jaws, but a cluster of seven long, flexible needles drawn from cold-pulled glass. They do not drink. They have no bore. They are feelers—styluses. 

I introduce them into the clearance. 

Instantly, the machine speaks into my frame. It is not language; it is the pure, horrifying reality of stress. The needles vibrate at frequencies that would turn wet muscle into gray soup: the three-hundred-cycle groan of the lower root-mat; the high, glassy ping of a crystal of sand being pulverized between forty tons of moving ironwood; the deep, seismic shudder that warns of a tap-root shearing twelve leagues to the south. 

My horn rotor, still spinning down from the slide, translates these vibrations into a tiny, rhythmic current through my quartz nodules. A spark jumps between two dry contact-leaves inside my skull. 

*Tchick.* 

A minute portion of the static discharge is caught, stored in the dry amber laminations of my belly. It is enough to keep the escapement ticking for another three cycles of the sun. 

In exchange for this pulse, the styluses do their work. Their microscopic, diamond-shaped tips are grooved like files. As the pivot groans through its swing, my needles tease out the foreign grit, grain by grain, flicking the bright, dead motes of quartz out into the cross-draft, where the wind carries them away into the violet glare. I clear the race. I preserve the gap. 

I am the maintenance of a fraction. 

Above me, a tallow-kite loses its angle of attack. It has ventured too close to the intake-valves; the sudden drop in local pressure when the southern cylinder drinks tears the horn-ribs of its left wing from their sockets. It does not cry out—it has no lung for it—but it falls with a dry, clattering whistle, tumbling past my spar like a broken kite made of blackened laths. 

It strikes the counterweight. The impact does not crush it—there is nothing to squash—it simply shatters the kite into six dozen needle-pointed spars and a small cloud of amber chaff. 

The chaff drifts into the joint. 

My needles go to work immediately, vibrating with frantic, cold precision, sorting the horn-splinters from the bronze, grinding the larger flakes into the finest possible dry-wax, packing it into the micro-fissures of the lignum to make the surface glass-smooth again. 

I do not feel gratitude to the fallen thing. I do not feel fear when the great pendulum swings within three feet of my housing, blotting out the cold stars that have begun to pierce the violet ceiling of the sky. 

There is only the tension. The load on the spar is rising again; the northern face of the tree is cooling toward eighty below, its fibers contracting with the sound of a pistol shot every sixty heartbeats. The whole spire is leaning three degrees to the dark, groaning as the continental root-mat takes up the slack. 

Soon, the shadow will swing. The first needle of magnesium sunlight will crest the gantry-branch, striking my flank like an iron bar. 

My mica plates will heat. The bronze jaws will release. 

I will slide again, six hundred feet down the ironwood rail into the next cold pocket of the lattice, singing my corundum note into the empty, deafening wind, maintaining the clearance between the machine that is the world and the stillness that wants to break it.
