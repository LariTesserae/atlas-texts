At first, I would not be an eye. 

To look here is only to confirm what the skin already knows: that the dark has a weight, that it is lukewarm, that it does not lift. 

I would be a back first. Specifically, the flat muscle between the shoulder blades, pressed hard into the ninety-degree corner where a transverse buttress meets a three-story retaining wall. In this world, you do not lean against curves; there are none, save for the bellies of the turtles and the swollen arches overhead. You learn where the courses sit level. You learn to fit the vertebrae into the horizontal joints where the mortar has recessed by half an inch, letting the fired clay hold the spine so the knees can rest.

The roar would live in my breastbone. It is not something you hear with the ears after a while; it settles into the spongy interior of the ribs, vibrating at the exact pitch of the blunt-headed frogs below and the click of the geckos above. It feels like leaning your forehead against the casing of a watermill that never stops grinding.

My hands would be broad, square-palmed, and coated in a dry, pale batter of calcified lime and dead moss that never completely washes away. In the creases of the knuckles, where the skin flexes, the flesh would be raw and blackish-pink, stained by the tannin of fermented persimmons and the bitter milk of the fig branches. The fingernails are worn flat, straight across, like small bone spade-bits from prying open the seams between bricks to look for grubs or to find the salt-crusts before the serows lick them down to the slime.

I would belong neither to the high, dry dust of the bat-lofts nor to the drowned vaults below. 

I would live in the middle stairs—the sunken, straight-cut flights that lead from the terrace orchards down into the blind water-conduits. They were built for giants or armies, eight cubits wide, but they have choked. A thousand years of ficus roots have buckled the risers into ramps of black rot, and the fine, lukewarm rain drifts down them in an endless, shallow sheet no thicker than a fingernail. 

This is where I would keep myself. In the wedge-space beneath a half-collapsed flight of slate steps, where the drift cannot strike directly, but where the air remains thick as porridge with the musk of wet fur and the sour ferment of the cisterns.

I would have a cloak, but it would not resemble cloth. It would be beaten fig-bark, three layers thick, felted with a brickbat until the fibers locked like horsehair. It is stiff as dried rawhide when the air holds still, smelling of lime and stagnant well-water, but when the twenty-minute drift arrives, it softens against my collarbones like boiled hide, warm and slick with the black grease I have rubbed into the shoulders—rendered fat from the glands behind the ears of the flying foxes, saved drop by drop in broken roofing-tiles.

My work, if it can be called work, is not hunting. You do not hunt in a place where life crawls into your armpits while you sleep. The millipedes fall from the lintels like heavy, jointed cords; the crickets are sluggish as plums in the heat; the persimmons drop into the gutters with soft, wet thuds and lie there bubbling until they smell like vinegared beer. Food is not chased. It is peeled, cracked, or fished out of the silt with bare toes.

My work would be the listening. 

Because the air is thick and the gloom swallows sight thirty paces out, nobody looks across the artificial chasms. The high-loft folk drop their clicks down from the viaduct cornices—*tlip, tlip, k-chak*—and the sound bounces from wet ashlar to wet ashlar, sharp and dry like flint striking flint. But the walls carry more than the air does.

I would spend hours with my ear laid directly against the stretcher-course of the great wall.

Through the brick, the geography speaks in iron tones. You can feel the weight of the water shifting four levels down: the long, slow surge as an intake scupper plugs with dead leaves, the pressure mounting against an ancient retaining arch until the lime groans—a tiny, sub-audible shudder that passes through the clay and into the bone of my jaw. You can feel the macaques when they work a capstone three terraces away, their rhythmic rocking coming through the foundations like a slow pulse: *tonk... tonk... tonk.* 

You can feel when a brick gives up. 

It does not happen often, but when it does, the whole saddle knows it hours before the fall. The mortar begins to weep fine grit inside the darkness of the wall; a faint, singing hum runs through the joint as the shear-stress transfers to the neighboring course. If you have your temple pressed to the brick, it sounds like hair being torn from the scalp, very slowly, deep inside the stone. 

When that sound comes, I move. Not by leaping—nothing leaps here without breaking a limb on the wet slate—but by the three-point crawl, belly close to the balustrade, moving along the mortar lines toward the sound.

I would be the one who gathers what drops.

When the brick falls and shatters, it opens a gap in the skin of the world. Behind the face-brick lies the rubble core: chunks of river-stone, broken tiles from the first building of the heights, and beds of old lime that have never seen the light. In those fresh gaps, before the ferns can find the damp and plug the wound with roots, there is salt. Pure, bitter, white saltpetre that flowers out of the broken core like wool.

I scrape it into hollow bamboo joints with a thumb-chisel ground from a shard of slate. The serows will smell it on me from sixty feet away in the brown fog; I can hear the suck and click of their cupped hooves on the vertical coping as they follow me, heavy-necked and patient, their milky nostrils flaring at the hem of my felt cloak, waiting for me to drop a pinch on a flagstone so they can lick the stone until their gums go gray.

And when one of the high-loft people dies—an old one whose toes have lost the grip to hang from the corbels, or a child who breathed too much of the bat-dust and whose deep chest has gone quiet—they do not bring them all the way down to the water. They are afraid of the cisterns, where the blind softshell turtles paddle through the dark like submerged gravestones. 

They bring the bundle down only as far as the middle stairs. They leave it wrapped in wet fig-felt on the fifth riser, weighted with a half-brick to keep the civets from dragging it into the flumes, and then they retreat up the ashlar towers, clicking through their teeth as they climb, their nails scratching the brick like a shower of gravel.

I come out from the wedge beneath the steps. 

I take the bundle by the binding cords. It is never heavy; the people here are small, their bones light and spongy from generations without the sun. I do not click. I have lived too long near the cistern mouth, where the vowels do not belong to men, but to the deep, iron thrumming of the blunt frogs.

I carry it down into the dark of the silt-trap, where the water is warm as blood and smells of rotting persimmon skins and old masonry. I lay it in the slow current where the lime-silt settles. I place the broken bricks across the chest and the knees, carefully, tucking the corners into the mud so the wash will not roll it.

Then I sit back on the bottom step, ankle-deep in the tepid drift, my spine wedged once more into the square corner of the wall. 

I wait until the first pale crab—flat as a dry leaf, slow as grease—creeps out of the drainage arch and touches the felt with its long, hairless leg. And I stay there in the roar, feeling the immense, digested grid above me through the soles of my feet, breathing the damp lime, completely hidden in the warm, roaring dark where nothing was ever born from the dirt, and nothing ever leaves the stone.
