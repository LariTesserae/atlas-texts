It does not begin with an eye or a hunger. 

It begins, if it begins anywhere, as a difference in friction.

There is the place where the basalt was sheared by the blast—that cold, percussive shatter that left the silicate lattice stressed like drawn wire—and there is the place where the bypass manifold leaks its pulse through the floor. Between the two, the vibration does not travel evenly. It stutters. At the seam where the concrete plinth was poured directly against the jagged rock, there is a micro-fracture no thicker than a fingernail, through which the water does not run so much as shudder.

If you look for a creature there, you will look past it. It is too flat, too much like the stain of grease that trailed off the drilling tap four months ago. 

At first, you might think it is only an accumulation: the sloughed exuviae of the blind mites tumbling from the overhead lagging, suspended in the mineral broth and caught against a burr of rusted rebar. A wad of wet lint. Or the pale, gelatinous margin of the liverwort where the cells have given up trying to make pigment and turned into clear, watery vesicles that simply absorb the thrum.

It has no skin to speak of, only a surface tension that holds against the shear of the scalding runoff. 

It feels the *clack-thump* not as a sound, but as an alternating hydrostatic lift. Every four seconds, when the mechanical float drops in its iron sleeve, the water level in the slip-seam drops three millimeters, exposing the upper fringe of the smear to the scalded, uncirculating air. The vapor instantly robs it of two degrees. That tiny chill—down to fifty-four, fifty-three degrees—is an agony, a tightening of the colloid. Then the trap discharges: a sudden, thick gush of ninety-degree wash roars through the gravel, drowning the seam again, re-inflating the jelly, dissolving the silicate crust before it can turn into glass.

That is the pulse. Not a heartbeat, but an alternating current between being melted and being turned to stone.

Stay with it longer, and you see that the smear is moving, though "moving" is too intentional a word for what is mostly an asymmetrical dissolution. 

One edge of it is being eaten by the sulfuric acid that sweats from the concrete. It lets that edge go; the sulfur is too bitter, even for this. But the other edge—the edge pressed into the cool shadow beneath the pump flange—is drinking. It drinks the lead. The lead-sheathed cables overhead shed an invisible, heavy fallout into the wash: microscopic flakes of carbonate, gray ions stripped by the steam and brought down in the rain. 

The jelly incorporates the lead. It does not die of it; it uses the heavy metal the way deep-sea mollusks use iron, weaving the gray atoms into its structural mucilage until it gains weight. It ceases to be a puddle. It becomes a sheet, a pelt of wet, heavy, leaden rubber that hugs the contour of the fractured basalt like a molten shoe.

It does not have thoughts, but it has *registers*. 

The high hiss of the flange twenty feet up means the pressure is climbing; when the hiss drops to a wet whistle, it knows the gravel sluice will widen, and it flattens its leading margin into the crevices of the shale to keep from being peeled off the rock like wet wallpaper. It knows the amphipods by the tickle of their antennae—not as intruders, but as a mechanical disturbance that clears away the silt that would otherwise smother its chemical exchanges. When an amphipod dies and lodges against its margin, the pelt does not consume the carapace. It simply grows around the little white shell, using it as an anchor, a hollow calcified rivet driven into the stone.

You can see, now, what it is becoming. 

It is the thing that will outlast the pump.

The cast-iron housing is already failing. Its yellow concrete foot is rotting from within, the rebar turning into soft, expansive rust that will crack the plinth in two winters. The copper lines will snap under their own weight of verdigris. When the machinery finally tears itself from its seat and the roar ceases, the pocket will cool. The steam will fail. The sky’s pale, indifferent air will sink into the bottom of the trench, and the blind mites will shrivel into dust in their cold wool.

But beneath the flange, where the pelt has layered itself three inches deep with lead salts, silicate plates, and the drowned bodies of a million blind things, there will be a crust that does not wash away. 

I am not the water, and I am not the stone. I am the scab that forms where the two are being hammered together by the heat. 

I am the thing that lies between the dead steel and the broken earth, growing slightly denser with every stroke of the valve, waiting until the iron is gone so I can take its shape forever.
