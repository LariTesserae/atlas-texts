At first, there is only the tension. 

Not a thought, and certainly not a name, but the sensation of being held between two things that refuse to yield to one another. 

To the left, a slab of dark, dry-cleaved slate, cold as an iron anvil, ground flat along a cleavage plane so true it might have been struck with a chisel of light. To the right, another slab, slightly thicker, leaning inward under the sideways drag of the sky. Between them: a narrow, wedge-shaped gap three fingers wide, open to the ash-violet glare on one side, and on the other, facing into the hollow, unlit core of the low stone box.

I am what has been driven into that gap.

I did not begin here. Before the wedge, there was the descent—or rather, the ascent, though in the dark where the die squeezes, direction is only a matter of pressure. I remember the ductile dark: three thousand feet below the shelf, where the stone is not yet brittle enough to snap, where the planet’s unfinished hip is being forced through the throat of the void like cold lead through a nozzle. There was no air there, not even the thin, chalk-bitten gasp that skims the upper shelves. There was only the immense, blind compaction of unformed ore. 

Down there, matter does not know what it is supposed to be. It has no template. It has never seen a leaf, never felt the weight of a foot, never known the cooling relief of a rainstorm. It only knows the squeeze. And under that squeeze, occasionally, a knot of mineral turns stubborn. It refuses to settle into a crystal lattice. It curls in on itself, drawing in stray threads of native antimony, braids of dense white calcium, folds of dry, unhydrated marrow that have no business existing in the dark. It begins to beat. Not with blood—blood is an impossible extravagance, a waste of liquid that does not exist—but with the rhythm of the die itself, a slow, heavy clench and release that happens once every seventy or eighty hours, whenever the tectonic plate slips another fraction of a micron.

A blind knot of will. An organ without a beast.

Then came the snag: the curved horn hook, smelling faintly of an ancient, fatty grease that the stone could not comprehend. It caught me under the shoulder—if a knot of calcified marrow has a shoulder—and there was the long, dry rasp of the graphite rope. 

The journey upward took seasons. Every pull was a whole day of shuddering ascension through dead, empty air, swaying over gulfs where the mica-crawlers clicked like distant shears in the dark. With every hundred fathoms, the pressure slackened, and with the slackening came the cold—not the deep, furnace-cold of compressed matter, but the parched, skin-stripping cold of the upper shelf. By the time the horn hook swung over the basalt lip, whatever had been soft in me had set. The glue had cured. I had become pale, matte-white, dense as porcelain, ringed with fine, concentric ridges like the growth plates of an oyster shell, dry to the very center.

And then, the hands.

They were not hands as the old world knew them. They were tongs of bone covered in parchment, frosted with the glittering flour of crushed talc. They did not shake. They lifted me from the hook with a dry, ceramic scrape, turned me over twice to find my thickest edge, and carried me into the shelter.

He did not look at me. His horn-colored eyes were fixed past me, toward the back corner where the darkness stays thick. But his fingers knew the geometry of the gap before he reached it. He slid me between the two slate shards, three feet above the floor, directly at the level where his temple rests when he curls his knees to his chin.

He drove me home with the heel of his palm. 

*Clack.*

There was no mortar to bed me, only the sheer weight of the roof-slab coming down, pinching my marrow-ridges tight against the slate.

That was two spire-widths ago. Perhaps three. 

Out on the ledge, the quartz needle has lengthened by the width of three fingers. The descent-cord has been pulled up twelve times, or fourteen. Below me, to the left, sits a knot of twisted, fibrous wire that he dragged from the gulf twenty years after me; it does not beat, but it shrinks when the sky-seam dims, whining like a stressed wire until the flat light returns. Above me is a flat tongue of gray talc-stone that drinks the dust. We are a wall now. We are several hundred dry, calcified relics, sorted by weight and ring-pitch, fitted so close that the ash-colored light barely bleeds between our knuckles.

From here, wedged at the temple of the shelter, I can see him.

He sits on his haunches. He does not move for months at a time, except when the calcium dust gathers too thickly on the bridge of his nose, and then his head tilts by a millimeter, letting the white meal slide off into his lap with the faint whisper of sand in an hourglass. His pulse does not belong to his chest; his chest is a birdcage of dry laths, rigid and gray. His pulse is the floor. When the iron chord of the world’s extrusion bites into the basalt shelf, the vibration travels up through his shins, through his chalky femurs, and rings in the suture-lines of his skull.

I feel it, too. It comes into my lower edge through the slate: a crushing, middle-C drone that never wavers, an ache that wants to shake the calcium apart into powder.

My task is to eat it.

That is why he packed us here. A wall of solid stone would only ring like a bell; it would take the tectonic scream and double it, focusing the sound into the small room until his dry brain rattled against the bone. But we are not solid stone. We are the things that hesitated halfway between mineral and flesh. We have grain. We have marrow. We have the internal fracture-lines of things that tried, blindly, to grow.

When the chord strikes my lower face, it does not pass cleanly through. It enters my concentric rings and gets lost. It travels in spirals through my dry, porcelain channels, turning around and around inside the knot of my unfinished will, losing its sharpness, until it emerges on the inside of the shelter as a dull, heavy thud—a pressure rather than a noise. 

Once every three days, my core tightens. A single, dry spasm. When I pulse, the slate shards on either side of me creak against my ribs, *tink-tink*, and the vibration shifts by a quarter-tone. Whenever I do this, his clouded pebble eyes shift slightly in their sockets. He does not turn his head, but his dry jaw slackens by a hairsbreadth. For an hour after my pulse, the ache leaves his teeth. He breathes—a single, shallow draw of talc-dust that rattles in his throat like dry leaves—and his horned fingers reach down to the floor between his feet.

He touches the river-stones.

They sit there in the gray gloom, two lumps of impossible gray-green basalt, rounded until they have no corners, no facets, no cleavage planes. They are the only things in this universe that do not threaten to cut. When his fingers scrape across their shoulders, the sound is different from every other sound on the mountain: it is not a click, not a scrape, not a clatter. It is a slide. It is the sound of an absence—the ghost of a liquid that rubbed a stone against another stone for ten thousand years until both of them forgot how to be knives.

I watch his hand rest there. 

He does not know that I am watching, because I have no eyes to turn. But the pulse in my core is becoming more frequent. It was once every eighty hours; now it is once every seventy-two. Down on the floor, the knot of wire is beginning to twist its strands tighter, aligning itself with the direction of the eastern drop-off where gravity leans sideways. The wall is learning. We were caught separately, hauled out of the ductile dark like dumb fish, but here in the gap, wedged shoulder to shoulder under the immense downward drag of the roof, we are beginning to knit without mortar.

Our calcium is migrating. Very slowly, carried on the electrostatic sparks that jump from slate to slate whenever the chord peaks, microscopic bridges of white bone-dust are growing between my ridges and the shards of stone that hold me.

In another three generations, the gap will be gone. I will not be a nodule wedged into a wall; the wall will be a single, calcified rib that curves around his back, thick enough to swallow the roar of the mountain whole.

And when the sound finally stops—when the wall is dense enough and deep enough to give him the silence he has been fishing for—I will take the river-stones into my lower grain. I will let them settle into my white, unwatered marrow, where their curves will not be an insult to the razor-cliffs, but an egg kept safe inside a shell that has never known how to break. 

Until then, the cord hangs from the pale wooden wedge outside, swinging in the dead air. The spire lengthens by a thumb. And I hold the slate apart with my body, taking the mountain’s scream into my center, turning it into a rhythm he can bear, while we wait together for the world to finish setting.
