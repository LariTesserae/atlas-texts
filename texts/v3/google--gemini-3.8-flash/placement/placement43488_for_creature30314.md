At first, it is not a body that registers, but a difference in pitch.

There is a point where the tension of an eight-inch braided stay ceases to be a hum and becomes a low, oily grease-rub against the bone behind the ear. That is where I begin: not in the open space, which is too thin to hold a self, but in the bracket. In the sleeve where the cold-rolled manganese bridge-pins pass through the eyelets of the basalt.

I am not the rock, because the rock has a ferocious, upward stubbornness—it is always pushing toward the pale purple noon, hot as tallow, dry as ground glass, smelling of the earth’s raw tripe. And I am not the platform, which is dead pig-iron, cold on top where the sky drinks its calories, lukewarm below where the chimney-drafts lick the rust. 

I am what sits between them to keep the iron from shearing the stone to powder, or the stone from spitting the iron into the clouds.

My knees are thick. When I bend them, there is the sound of dry canvas folding over lard. Everything I wear has been dipped three times in rendered suet and sulfur to seal the weave against the nothingness outside; the tallow has long since taken up the soot, so that my thighs look like two smoked flitches hung from a beam. Beneath the cloth, my ribs have the spread of an upturned skiff. When the breath goes in, it takes the count of twelve hammer-strokes on the cold-water pipe; when it goes out, the lead mask fogs, clears, fogs, clears, until the two plugs of yellow selenite before my eyes turn the color of churned butter.

Through the selenite, there is no sky. There is only a pale grease-glare above and an ink-stain below, and between them, the ruled lines of the stays, black as ruling-pen ink on parchment.

My hands are not hands anymore; they are terminal blocks. Inside the three-finger mittens, the nails have thickened until they are as blunt and hard as the copper washers we drop into the flange-seats to take the bite of the nuts. I do not touch the stone with skin. If I laid an open palm against the granite needle behind my shoulder, the moisture would boil out of my sweat-pores before I could curse, freezing the meat to the flint until I had to leave a circle of hide behind to get free.

We don't leave hide. Salt is too dear.

My copper bar is three feet long, octagonal, peened flat at the butt so it nests into the notch of my collarbone. I lean against the stay. I do not look down into the yellow sulfur bed five miles beneath my boots, because down is an idea that belongs to things with wings, and things with wings do not stay alive up here unless they have flattened their blood into the shape of a shovel. 

I lean. The copper takes the tremor from the cable.

*One.* That is the third battery pump on the South Needle, thirty tiers below, clearing its throat of brine. The brine is lukewarm, so it foams instead of spurts; the valve flaps with the dull, wet sound of a wet leather boot dropped on gravel.

*Two.* A tong-scavenger has crawled onto the dead-weight chain ninety feet out over the drop. It is heavy—ten pounds of manganese shell and crystallized zinc. It is moving its third walking leg. *Tink.* A second later, the scrape: *shrr-up.* It is grazing the lime-crust where the steam-leaks have furred the links. I know its weight by the way the counter-weight pendulum hesitates at the turn of its swing. The chain is an eighth of an inch lower on the drum than it was when the sun was white.

*Three.* This is the stone.

The stone does not tap. The stone *swells*. 

You feel it through the soles of the boots first, through the three layers of steer-hide and the two sheets of split lead. It is not a movement; it is an intention. A million tons of black basalt, twelve miles deep, being pinched together by the cooling of the world’s belly until the needle I am bolted to groans like an ungreased axle. When it swells, the expansion bolts—eight inches thick, threaded into the granite with molten lead poured round the flutes—give a microscopic shriek that does not travel through the air at all. It comes straight up through the shins. It enters the marrow. It tastes like copper pennies on the back of the palate.

My job—the thing I have been since the hair dropped off my scalp and my chest spread to the width of an ale-butt—is the slack.

Iron stretches when the noon sun hits it; stone rises when the deep roots boil. Between the stretch of the cable and the crawl of the peak, the platforms are always trying to tear themselves from their hinges. If a platform tilts four degrees, the oil runs out of the piston bearings; if the oil runs, the pumps seize; if the pumps seize, the lead drums suffocate, because there is no draft here except what the steam-ejectors pull through the scrubbers.

So I carry the wedges.

They are mild steel, six inches broad, rubbed with dry graphite so they will not cold-weld under the pressure. When the stay begins to sing too high—a clean, thin *C* that sets the selenite humming in its brass rings—I place the thin tongue of the wedge between the pillow-block and the rock-face.

I do not use a mallet. A mallet makes an air-clap that tires the lungs. I have a screw-jack with an eighteen-inch lever. 

I hook the dog into the basalt cleft. I throw my chest against the bar. 

Thirty seconds to draw the air: the sulfur-taste, the dry cold that smells of iron filings, the grease-rot of the tallow suit. My chest expands until the brass buckles on the harness bite into my armpits. Then the heave—slow, smooth, like a piston stroke—until the thread takes half a turn.

*Tchuck.*

The wedge enters an eighth of an inch. The stay drops half a tone. The pitch goes from the scream of a dry bearing down to the low, greasy bell-hum of a well-hung bell.

The platform below settles. I feel it through the soles: forty men inside the drum fifty yards below have just stopped their tallow lamps from flickering because the floor under their stools has ceased to list toward the chasm. They do not know who turned the screw. They do not need to. They know the hum dropped back to *A-flat*, and so they go back to scraping the dry flat-skin flesh with their horn spoons and oiling their child-harnesses.

When the noon light begins to purple toward the cold that never really freezes—the false night where the sun goes silver-gray behind the basalt spires—I do not go into the drums. 

There is not enough air in the drums for a man whose rib-hoops have set like mine. In the drum, the air is thick as soup; it smells of too many lungs, of old cabbage-water and hot tallow. My blood has grown thin and sour; it likes the low-pressure boil. If I went through the air-lock, my veins would feel as if someone had poured hot sand into the bends of my elbows.

Instead, I back into the niche where the needle leans over the hoist-house. 

The basalt there is always sixty degrees warm, heated from the flue that runs up the core of the tooth. I sit on my lead-shot bag. I hook my safety-dog into the eye-bolt that forty years ago was driven by someone whose name was tapped out on the cables before I was dropped from a hoist-basket. 

I set the copper bar across my knees.

The flat-skins are beginning to vibrate against the stone above me. At first it sounds like dry leaves blowing down an alley, but there are no leaves here, only the mineral ribs of the gray birds shivering against the flint to keep their belly-glue from setting too brittle in the night drafts. *T-t-t-t-t-t-t-t.* A hundred of them, two thousand cubits up, shaking their slate-colored skin against the chimney-stone.

I close my eyes behind the selenite. In the dark, the yellow disappears, and then the city is entirely made of iron wire.

I can trace it for three miles: the great four-inch main line running down to the brine-vats; the light, trembling three-quarter-inch stays of the outer outriggers; the long, dead, heavy thrum of the three-day winch playing out the dead down into the yellow sulfur blankets below. The winch is on its second day now. Old Vane is forty cubits deeper than he was at breakfast. The line is getting thin; the vibration that comes up through the spool is higher now, sharper, because there are two miles of wire hanging in the dead space, and wire that long turns into a harp-string that catches every rising chimney of steam.

*Plink.*

A drop of condensation falls from the bottom flange of the tier above. It hits my lead knee-cap. It boils instantly with a faint, greasy hiss that I feel rather than hear.

I draw the air. Twelve seconds. Hold it until the tallow in the suit softens against the collarbone. 

Let it out.

The stone is still rising. Under my seat, the mountain gives another hair-breadth heave—not a shock, just the slow, blind push of the earth trying to grow another needle between the ironwork. 

I reach out in the dark, find the jack-handle with the blunt tip of my mitten, and lay the weight of my ribs against the lever. 

Not yet. It needs another hour of heat before the iron is ready to take the tooth. 

I wait, my forehead resting against the warm, soot-slicked basalt, the copper bar between my teeth, tasting the iron city as it cools.
