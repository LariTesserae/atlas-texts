At first, I would not be distinct from the friction.

To exist here is not to inhabit a room, but to be pinched between two surfaces that are sliding in opposite directions at the speed of settling mud. If you looked for me with eyes that know the plains, you would mistake me for a swelling in the frame—a wet bundle of raffia jammed into the groin where four split-hazel spars cross under the belly of a mud-basket.

I am wedged where the floor drops away and the gravity turns sideways. My left hip-bone is permanently nestled into the crook of a green alder rib; the bark has peeled back under my weight, and the cambium has wept its clear, almond-scented gum into the pores of my flank until my skin no longer slides against the wood. We have set together, like a bone broken and bound crookedly with twine.

I am not the Weaver, who sits below in the grease-trough testing the singing cords, nor am I one of the slick, quick-ribbed runners who haul the grey silt through the flues with their heels. 

I am a shim.

My task is to be heavy, but elastic. When the outer gale takes the joint—when that anvil-shriek hits the cow-hide skin and the three-hundred-foot basket of this knuckle wants to twist six inches to the sun-side—I am the mass that absorbs the shear. I sit with my heels tucked hard into my groin, my gristle-cushioned soles braced against the head of a hazel lath that is three inches thick and fighting like a captured eel to unbend itself. 

If I release it, the lath will whip through the wattle and tear a mouth in the wall four feet wide, through which the blue-black frost would suck us all out like marrow from a cracked marrow-bone. So I do not release it. I take the kick of the timber through my pelvic bowl. My tailbone is ground flat against the wet cane floor; every quarter-hour, when the wind leans hard, the wood groans, bends an eighth of an inch deeper into my stomach, and I let my breath out between my teeth—*tsst-tsst-k-k*—so my lungs do not burst against my spine.

My hands are not idle, though they are no longer suitable for the fine, hair-thin splits of the Weaver. My fingers have begun the turning: the middle joints are thick as walnut-galls, white with the encrustation of lime slurry that never leaves the skin. But they are broad, and they are hard. With my right hand, I hold the tallow-gourd; with my left thumb, I grease the lashings.

Gut-lashings do not like the kiln-heat of this node. If they dry, they snap; if they get too wet with fresh river-water, they rot and stretch like boiled tripe. They must be fed fat. I take the yellow rind of fat scraped from the hide-seams and work it into the hemp loops where they bite the wood. My thumb is flattened, broad as a mussel-shell, completely numb. I can press it directly against the singing hemp when it hums at its highest, lethal pitch and feel the heat of the friction without my flesh blistering; the skin there has died three times over, turning into a horn-colored pad that smells of old marrow and salt.

I taste the tower before I see it change. 

Because my face is pressed within two inches of the wattling, the steam vents through my beard—or what would be a beard, if the lime had not licked the hair-roots clean away. The steam changes flavor. When the gang above me is laying silt that was gathered from a still bend in the lower river, the breath tastes of pond-weed, rotted water-beetles, and cold fat. But when they strike into a pocket of upland clay—hauled up through thirty joints in raw-hide buckets that leave trails of white scum along the rungs—the steam turns sharp and metallic, like an iron knife held against the tongue. That is the mud that holds. When I taste that, I click three times with the back of my palate against the hazel strut, a short, hard rap that travels down the wood into the mud-trough to let the Weaver know the weight is good.

I have perhaps two hundred slips left before my heels stop giving.

Already, the pink gristle of my left foot has taken a permanent turn, hooking into the crook of the alder until I cannot pull it free even when the mud-gang rests. I feel the lime working into the fluid of my knee. It does not hurt—pain is a luxury for places where there is room to thrash—it merely thickens, like tallow left in a draft. In the dark between the gales, when the spire hangs still for three heartbeats and only the steam is hissing through the wicker-blowholes, I can feel the tiny, hair-like rootlets of the bile-green liverwort creeping out of the knot behind my neck, tasting the sweat on my nape, anchoring themselves into the creases of my skin.

I do not brush them off. They are cool. In this ninety-eight-degree soup, anything that has not been boiled by the altitude is a mercy.

Sometimes, in the needle-thin slashes of purple light that pierce the unplastered wattle, I see the voles swimming through the wet mortar three inches from my temple. They move without fear of me; to a creature with bald pits for eyes, my jaw is just another smooth river-stone jammed into the silt to keep the wall from sliding. Their pink, shovel-shaped paws tickle my ear-rim as they dig through the grey grease after the wasps. 

Once, one of them died in the wall beside my temple. The mortar took a frost through an ungreased hide-knot, and the vole froze solid inside the silt, small as a plum, hard as river-slate. I leaned my forehead against that spot for three watches. It was the only cold thing that had ever existed in the world. I held my brow to it until the mud thawed from my own body heat, turned soft again, and began to run down my cheek in a warm, sour tear that smelled of drowned fur.

Below me, two feet into the darkness where the struts cross like broken fingers, I can feel the Weaver’s presence. It does not sleep. It shifts its weight with a sound like a wet sack of grain being dragged across wicker. Occasionally, its blind, six-fingered hand comes creeping up the lath I am bracing—not seeking me, never seeking me, but following the run of the hazel rod to where my knee is locked over the knuckle.

Its horny yellow nail finds the side of my ankle. It does not pinch; it merely taps—*twick, twick*—testing whether the tendon is still wet, whether the gristle is still springy enough to carry the floor’s kick when the next gust strikes the spire.

I twitch my toes in the wicker to show the cord is taut.

The Weaver taps once more, satisfied, and the pale, grease-smeared hand slides back into the blind angle, trailing a split thread of river-cane that smells of swamp-rot and cold milk.

Above me, the mud-chains begin to heave again. I hear the wet *shluck-shluck-shluck* of a boy’s belly dragging through the upper flue, the heavy *plop* of sixty pounds of wet silt dropped into the hanging basket, and the long, screaming groan of the withies taking the strain. The hazel lath under my pelvic bone leaps like a living beast, trying to drive its blunt head through my chest.

I take the blow. I let my collarbones fold together until they touch. I swallow the bitter curd of the almond-sap that has pooled on the lath, fill my lungs with the boiling scald of the steam, and hold the world plumb for one more slip.
