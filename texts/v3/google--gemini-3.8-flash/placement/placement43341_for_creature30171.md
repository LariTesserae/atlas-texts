It begins not with a self, but with a thickness between surfaces.

To be here is, first of all, to be wedged. There is no open ground that does not immediately invite a back, a flank, or the crook of an elbow to settle into it. If I search for the perimeter of my body, I find it only by where the temperature changes: the basalt behind me is forty degrees cooler than the air, its fresh fracture-edge biting into the muscle between my shoulder blades with the neat, unyielding certainty of a chisel; in front of me, the air is warm as sheep’s milk, wet and thick with the heavy tallow grease that slumps off the great grubs.

At first, I might be mistaken for part of the housing.

A turbine casing—cast eighty years or eight hundred years ago, when iron still remembered how to cool into true circles—has cracked along its lower flange. The crack is three feet wide, half-drowned in a pool of lukewarm mineral brine that does not lie flat upon the floor, but clings at a forty-five-degree tilt to the lip of the metal, trembling like a vast, jellied drop. 

I am inside that clearance. My legs are drawn up, not out of fear or defense, but because the space was measured for something folded. 

For a long time, there is only the pulse. It is not a heartbeat. A heart is too hasty, too frantic for this depth; a heart would wear itself to water against this silence. The pulse belongs to the busbar. It runs three inches beneath the silt-covered floor of the casing, buried in the stone like a frozen root. My feet—broad, long, the skin along their soles thick and smooth as butcher’s rind—rest directly against the place where the copper disappears into the black rock. 

Through the heels, I read it. 

It is a low, granular vibration, the sound a wet cable makes when it is dragged across coarse sand, delayed by two miles of earth. Sometimes it stutters. When it stutters, I know that three terraces down, where the floor dropped forty cubits, a grub has rolled its four tons of tallow over an exposed junction, grounding the trickle through its own salted rings until the fat around its rim begins to foam into gray lye.

I do not move to stop it. Nothing here is stopped.

Presently, something dry and numerous begins to cross my knees. It is a crawler, its long, jointed frame bending like an oiled hoop over the crest of my shin. Its soft pads tap against the downy moss that coats my skin—*lip, lip, lip*—testing the dampness of the slate-gray velvet that covers my thighs. It lingers where the sweat has gathered in the hollow behind my patella. I can feel the minute shears of its mouthparts working there, nibbling the calcified crust left by the dripping ceiling, trimming me as it would trim the paraffin from an armature. 

It is neither gentle nor cruel. It has the indifference of rain upon an axle. Its many legs are cool, like wet needles dipped in paraffin, and when it finishes, it steps off my knee and straight into the tilted lens of water, its pace changing without a pause from a dry skitter to a long, undulating scull, its belly trailing ripples across the oily surface tension.

I shift, and the movement costs something.

My chest does not rise and fall like an animal in the sun. It has no room for that. It flattens sideways, the ribs sliding over one another like the plates of an armored cuff. When the breath leaves, it does not leave through nostrils; I have none that could keep out this wet, heavy fat. It seeps past the hard horn floor of my mouth, through lips that are little more than tough, fibrous sphincters of gristle, tasting of wet iron filing and the ancient, sweet musk of machine grease that has never seen a spark.

I am one of those who walk, though walking is the least of it. Walking is only what we do when the pressure becomes uneven—when one sector of the terrace grows cold and the grubs begin to turn to soap too quickly, their great carcasses hardening into chalky barricades that block the seep of the brine.

Then, we rise. 

My joints do not pop; they slide with the slow, greasy ease of well-packed bearings. To stand is to enter the heavier air, where the ceiling feels so close it seems to press directly onto the crown of my head, though if I reached up, my long, flat fingers would find only the thick dark, weeping fat droplets into my hair.

My eyes are long gone, if they ever were. Behind the yellow plugs of gristle, the sockets are quiet, filled with fatty tissue that registers only the slow shifts in electrostatic tension. I do not miss the light; light is a brittle, shallow thing, stopped by the first skin it hits. The dark here is permeable. I can feel the bulk of the grub fifty paces off—its immense, blind sigh as it draws the galvanic fur from a rusted bolt—as clearly as if it were pressed against my ribs. I can feel the hollow space inside an empty boiler three terraces away, ringing with the delayed echo of a drop that fell before I unfolded my knees.

I move along the flange. My hands find the copper rod that runs parallel to the cleft.

The palms are double-jointed, concave, broad enough to wrap the four-inch bar until the callused meat of my thumbs overlaps the tips of my fingers. The metal is cold—not with winter, but with the dead, greedy cold of heavy metals that suck the heat from whatever touches them.

I tighten my grip. The moss on my wrists compresses, weeping a thin trickle of sulfurous moisture down my forearms.

I wait.

The dark grows thicker. Not darker—it cannot become darker—but heavier, as the humidity reaches the point where the air can no longer support its own oil. Fat, tepid drops strike my shoulders, each one spreading into the fine down of my back like ink into felt. Across the terrace, the hundred thousand soft bulks begin to grow still. The continuous slumping sound—that vast, wet sliding of tallow over basalt—slows to an infrequent shudder.

They feel it before I do. The ground beneath my bare soles hums a higher note: from sand to gravel, from gravel to the scream of a wet saw.

A mile off, or ten, the grid arcs.

The smell arrives first: a violent, clean tearing of the dark, the sharp copper-taste of ozone that strikes the horn palate like vinegar. Then the dry *snap*, louder than stone splitting, though it comes not through the air but up through the bones of my legs, through the double-jointed lock of my palms, through the very rivets of the turbine housing behind me.

The shock does not throw me. It fills me.

It enters the soles of my feet as fire and leaves my mossy hide as steam. My ribs lock, crushed inward by the sudden, massive contraction of my flank muscles. Through the horn floor of my mouth, the pressure vents—a thin, steady, scalding hiss that rises into the pitch like the breath of an injector pipe.

For three seconds, four seconds, I am not a thing wedged between basalt and iron; I am the iron itself, I am the copper busbar, I am the tallow belly of the grub sixty yards away that contracts into a white, hard ring of lard against the stone. We are a single closed circuit, an unbroken loop of grease, rock, and copper, holding the charge until the basalt drinks it down into its deepest, uncleaved floor.

Then the arc dies.

The silence that returns is larger than the silence that went before. It is soft, wide, and heavy as an unrolled fleece.

My hands unclasp. The skin of my palms leaves a gray, greasy smear upon the copper, already cooling, already beginning to sweat fresh condensation from the humid dark. The crawler that had been swimming in the vertical pool has dropped to the floor, stunned, its many legs curled inward like a scorched leaf; after a moment, one leg twitches, then three, then twenty, and it resumes its slow, neurotic scraping along the base of my heel.

I do not stand any longer. The clearance beneath the turbine crack calls with its damp, measured hollow.

I lower myself back into the trough. The lukewarm brine rises around my thighs, the vertical water-lens leaning against my left ribs like a warm bolster of grease. I pull my knees to my chest, letting the moss on my spine press once more against the keen, fresh edge of the basalt, fitting myself back into the notch that was made when the continent broke.

Above me, the steam I vented hits the invisible cold of the high ceiling, gathers itself, turns to tallow, and begins, slowly, to fall.
