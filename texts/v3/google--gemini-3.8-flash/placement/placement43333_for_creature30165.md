At first, there is only the difference between my left side and my right. 

My left side is cold, washed by the aerosol draft that curls up from the floorless gulf. My right side is warm, close to the pale flank of an outrigger beam that has been drinking sun since dawn. 

I am not standing on the grand causeways where the bands run forty cubits wide and true as a plumb line. Those plains of blond heartwood are for the swift gliders, the ones whose pads are greased with pure mucus and whose courses are set for miles without a touch to the steering-tiller. 

I am down where the wood has been forced to make a corner.

Even in an empire of north-and-south, water must eventually turn. And where water turns, the xylem screams.

I find myself wedged in the throat of an elbow—a massive, torque-stressed knee where a flume of gourd-wood diverts three hundred tons of condensation a second toward a western siphon. The grain here was not allowed to grow straight; it was bent while green, bound with helical tension-straps of cured wicker until the fibers grew compressed, angry, and dense as horn. 

I am one of the narrow ones, but I am not young. The white birch-matte of my ribs has begun to take on a faint, oily yellowing at the hinges, like tallow left in the sun. My legs are folded double, my shins tucked flush against the underside of my thighs, the joints locked into their shallow grooves so that I take up no more air than a discarded spar. 

My chin is pressed to the seam. 

This is how I know what I am: I am an ear that has been given hands.

Through the bone of my lower jaw, the elbow speaks to me. It does not speak of history, because there is no yesterday in wood that has never dropped a leaf. It speaks of *rate*. 

The note entering my left molar is a thin, dry whistle—a whistle that tastes like green rind and salt. It means the inner radius is starved; the sap is pulling away from the cambium, leaving a pocket of vacuum beneath the skin of the flume. If the vacuum holds, the timber will cup. If it cups, the seal will blow, and forty leagues of viaduct will snap its moorings and thrash like a severed tendon into the clouds.

My hands know what to do before my mind forms the shape of the work. 

I carry my plugs not in a bag—for there is nothing to weave that does not rot or fray—but pegged into the meat of my own forearms. They are slender, six-inch bodkins of dried iron-vine, kept bone-dry by the heat of my own skin. When I pull one from the sheath of my flesh, the small wound does not bleed red; it weeps a clear, thick resin that seals itself before the air can touch it. We are bred of the same sap we tend; our humors are only three degrees removed from the wash in the flumes.

I reach out over the roaring empty. My fingers are four, extraordinarily long, tipped with flat spades of horn that can find a fissure no wider than a hair. 

I find the whistling seam. 

The vibration is so fierce it numbs my knuckles instantly, turning the bone to cold lard. But I lean my weight—not much, perhaps fifty pounds of dry bone and sinew—onto the butt of the iron-vine peg. I work it into the mortise with a slow, grinding roll of my wrist. 

The timber bites it. 

The moment the dry vine touches the bitter sap running within the wall, it screams. A sound like silk tearing. Beneath my palm, the peg drinks. It swells. It does not merely fill the hole; it becomes an anchor, throwing out desperate, microscopic fibrils that knit directly into the cellular walls of the gourd-wood. The whistle drops an octave. It becomes a moan, then a hum, then dissolves entirely into the vast, pneumatic bass-note that fills the sky.

The seam is tight again. The elbow survives another noon.

I do not slide away. I cannot plane across this corner; the grain here is cross-cut, twisted like the braid of a girl’s hair, full of vicious little burs of fiber that would strip the skin from my skids if I tried to glide. To leave this place, I must climb—hand over hand, knuckle over knuckle, along the tension cables that tether the flume to the nearest fluted pylon, half a league above.

I look up along that cable. 

The leather-leaves are moored there, massive and still as beached boats, catching the mist on their upper faces and channeling it downward into the central siphon through clear, flexible stems. On the lowest leaf, three hundred yards above me, sits another one of my kind. 

He is older than I. His skin has entirely lost the birch-whiteness; it has the deep, greasy translucence of old amber, the color of pine rosin that has sat ten seasons in a cellar. He is not moving. He has not moved since the sun was at the three-quarter mark. 

I tap my teeth together. 

*Clack. Clack-clack. T-t-t-clack.*

The sound runs up the vine, through the iron fibers, clean and sharp. 

*How is the slack?* I ask him through the wood.

The reply takes a long time to come down. When it does, it is dull, muffled by the thickening density of his bones. 

*The pylon is drinking,* he taps back. His rhythm is slow, like water falling through oil. *The cradle wants my ribs.*

I look at him through my amber lids. The sun is behind him, turning his thin, lath-like silhouette into a dark notch against the blinding glare of the southern reaches. I can see the tendrils now—not because I can see them with my eyes across that gulf, but because the cable between us has changed its pitch. It no longer sings with the clean, wire-taut hum of hemp and vine. It has the heavy, deadened thud of a living branch. 

The pylon has found his feet. The white, thread-like roots have already worked through the skin of his heels, winding around the twin bones of his calves, cementing him to the mast. He is becoming a gusset. By tomorrow’s noon, his knees will be knots; by the next, his long arms will be braces supporting the leaf-cradle against the wind that comes off the lower sea.

There is no pity in me for him, and none in him for himself. In a world where nothing rots, to be incorporated is the only completion. To remain mobile too long is to become brittle, to catch upon the grain, to be splintered by the sheer velocity of the water we serve. 

I look down at my own wrist, at the row of small, puckered sockets where the vine-pegs rest against my tendons. 

The skin around the third peg is hard. It does not yield when I press it; it clicks like a fingernail. The amber is rising in me, too. It begins in the joints that bear the most vibration—the wrists, the jaw, the collarbone. Soon, my teeth will no longer drum clearly; they will fuse into a single plate of acoustic bone, and I will be silent.

I turn my face back to the elbow of the flume. 

The water roars inside it—warm, blind, gallons by the million, lifting the breath of the clouds up into the sun to keep this floor from curling into kindling. I lay my forehead against the timber once more. 

The wood is quiet here now. But five miles to the south, through the long, interlocking bands of heartwood, I can feel a new shudder. A taproot has slipped its stay. A cradle is rocking in the wind, empty, waiting for someone to crawl into its joints and make it heavy.

I unlock my knees. I set my pads into the north-south grain of the main spar, where the wood is smooth as tallow, and I let the wind take my shoulders. 

I do not walk. I do not run. 

I drop into the grain, and I slide.
