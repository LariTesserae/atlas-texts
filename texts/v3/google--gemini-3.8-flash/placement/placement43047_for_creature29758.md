I would not be on the mud. The mud is a stomach that never empties, and its digestion is too fast. 

Nor would I be among the catenary towers where the pelicans crowd the rusted stays until the wire sags like wet hemp. Up there, the heat has nowhere to go; it gathers against the low, milk-white wool of the sky until the iron burns through the calluses of anything that perches too long, and the guano dries so fast it turns to an airborne powder that coats the back of the throat with lime.

I find myself in the middle thickness. 

Twenty-four feet above the crust, where the twelve-inch steam mains run parallel on twin I-beams, bridging the gulf between the motor house and the open-hearth pits. It is a dead level, horizontal world. The pipes are lagged in rotting magnesia canvas that was painted black sixty years ago; the canvas has split along its spine, and out of the seam weeps a steady, tepid distillation that tastes of limestone and rain. 

At first, I might be only the dark shape settled into the saddle where two expansion loops meet—something curled out of the draft that smells of wet slag and ammonia. For a long time, there is no need to move enough to prove whether the shape has fur, or tallow-skin, or the greasy slate-colored feathers of a night heron that has forgotten how to fish the open channels. 

When the midge-clouds rise at four in the afternoon, like greasy smoke blown sideways through the trestles, you can see what I have gathered around myself.

A length of heavy braided copper ground-wire, its individual strands unravelled with an old iron file and woven into a low, stiff cradle between the pipe and the flange. It does not swing; it has the solid, unforgiving give of bridge cable. A square of corrugated zinc, wedge-jammed above my head into the web of an overhead crane runway, just wide enough to keep the falling lime-wash and the heavy, warm drops of the three-o'clock condensation from hitting my neck while I sleep. 

My feet are wrapped. That is the first thing that becomes clear: they are not claws, though the nails have grown thick and yellow as horn from decades of resting against sulfur-crusted plate. They are wrapped in three plies of bias-ply tractor tire, the tread worn bald, bound to the ankles with long, black ribbons cut from a forklift tube. The rubber has taken the shape of my heels, but it never softens. When I walk the tops of the eight-inch lines, my steps make no more sound than an owl shifting its grip on a dead branch—just the dull, rubbery *tuck* of dead vulcanized gum against the velvet of stable rust.

I have an eye that stays open longer than a man’s should, because there is almost nothing here that moves quickly enough to require a blink. 

The crabs down on the floor: they do not run; they drift like shale sliding down a low bank. When the tide backs up the brick culverts and the brine pools swell until they touch the lower flanges of the switchgear, I watch them from the loop. Millions of them. Their gray shells catch the flat, skyless light, turning the three-hundred-yard gap between the mills into a sheet of rippling lead. I know the sound they make when they find a dead goby that failed to bury itself before the sulfur seeped up through the sand—a tiny, dry tearing, like a thumb skimming the edges of a closed book.

I am not one of the men in the shacks, though I watch them through the cracked, wired glass of the motor-room monitors. They stay too low. They live thirty feet closer to the ammonia than I do, down where the air sits in the lungs like wet wool and the skin turns the color of cold lard. They spend their days tending their drop-traps, their minds narrowed down to the gray joints of the blue-claws and the length of pipe they use to stun the herons. 

I do not eat the big crabs. The meat in their claws is gray-veined and smells of the bitumen they scrape against in the dark sumps. 

Instead, I wait for the midges to settle on the wet lagging after their twilight flight. With the flat of a wooden slat—a piece of cedar tongue-and-groove pried forty years ago from an office ceiling that hadn't yet fallen—I scrape them off the warm pipes by the handful. They have no mouths; they have never eaten; they taste of nothing but clean grease and river fog. I pack them into cakes with a little of the sweet condensation from the twelve-inch flange, press them between two scraps of slate, and lay them on the south side of the steam header where the trapped iron-heat dries them into hard, black biscuits by noon the next day.

Sometimes I go down. Not to the floor—never to the floor—but down to the third landing of the blast-furnace skip-hoist, where the wild raccoons have their highway. 

We do not fight over the roosts. They are leaner than the ones that live upvalley near the orchards; their ribs show through their mahogany-stained coats like the stays of an old basket, and their hams are hard as dried beef from a lifetime of balance. When we cross on the narrow flange of a plate girder, we pass belly-to-belly, their cold noses twitching against my inner-tube gaiters, their yellow-green eyes flat and uninterested. We know the same things: which rivets have sheared beneath the guano-crust, which sections of galvanized pipe will hold a hundred pounds, and which copper grounding lines have been eaten through from within by the battery acid that drifted out of the substation forty years back.

My skin has stopped shedding. The humidity keeps it pasted down, smooth and gray as the belly of an eel, smelling faintly of the coal-tar pitch I use to seal the seams of my water-trough. 

I keep a trough. It is an eight-foot length of lead-sheathed telephone conduit, split down the middle with a cold chisel and hung from the underside of the crane-rail with loops of fence wire. Every morning, the fog that rolls off the unseen estuary hits the cold iron of the mill’s north wall and runs down the stiffeners in clear, hair-thin beads. The lead trough catches it before it hits the bird-lime on the lower deck. It yields three pints a day of water that doesn't burn the tongue. It tastes of zinc and old solder, but it is cold. In this valley, cold is rarer than salt.

Who am I? 

I am what the place could afford to keep of a man after it finished stripping away the things that couldn't survive the wet bell of the sky. 

I have no tools left that require oiling; oil gathers the pulverized slag and turns to grinding paste within a week. I have no fire; the men below have their greasy, tar-fed embers that blacken their faces and make their spittle thick with soot, but I have forgotten the need for hot food. When you live inside an oven that never drops below eighty degrees, heat is not something you make; it is something you endure until it becomes the pressure that holds your joints together.

I spend the middle hours of the day—when the sky turns the dull, blinding silver of an uncleaned mirror—watching the white-faced ibises on the high catenaries. 

They are weaving. They take the frayed strands of my old telephone lines, three miles of which hang in tangled swags from the gantry towers, and they work them into their platforms of dead wing-bones with the patience of blacksmiths. They do not drop things. If an ibis drops a four-foot length of wire, it does not fly down to the mud to fetch it; to touch the gray flats is to offer your shins to the fiddlers and your breast to the caustic brine. The wire falls into the gray-black carpet, the crabs part for three seconds, the wire disappears under ten thousand small, pale carapaces, and it is gone forever. The birds know this. They hold the wire with bill and foot, knotting it with a slow, clumsy precision that looks like an old man mending a net with broken fingers.

I have their patience, but not their wings. 

I have my sixty yards of steam header. I have my cradle of copper rope that never rusts, only turns that pale, velvety green that matches the mold on the north side of the brick stacks. I have the sound of the crabs—that vast, unceasing hiss that is the clock of the basin, telling me whether the estuary is pushing or pulling through the dark conduits under the floor.

When my knees finally lock—when the brine in my joints turns to the same lime that has petrified the catwalks—I will not drop into the canal. The men will not have to slide me into the outflow for the eels. 

I will simply pull my legs up into the copper cradle, between the two expansion loops where the iron never cools, and let the midges settle. The spiders will bridge the six-inch gap between my knees and the overhead gantry with their heavy, slag-gray silk. Within three seasons, the ibises will come down for my ribs to mend their floorings, working with that same heavy, silent care, unclipping the small bones one by one until the wire is clear again, clean and ready for the next thing that needs to keep its feet out of the mud.
