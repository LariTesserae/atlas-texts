At first, the instinct is to search for a vantage point—some high, dry corner of the basalt vault where an eye could open and look down. But there is no high corner; the roof presses low, and an eye here would be a useless, soft jelly, drying in the dark, starved of photons that have not entered this stone since the ice sheets first grounded themselves above.

To be here at all, one must give up the habit of being upright. One must give up the spine.

If I look for myself in the air, I find almost nothing. The air is only a slow piston, a lung of stone that fills and empties with the tectonic tides. It has no interest in being inhabited. Nor could I be the machine: the machine is not an identity, only an unyielding, curved premise, an immovable flank of slate-smooth logic that has outlived whatever purpose its makers carved into its sub-atomic lattices. It does not know it is leaking. It does not know it is warm.

And I am not the creature on the casing—not entirely. That creature is too complete, too whole in its ancient communion with the white electrostatic roar of the reactor. It sits squarely in the present tense of its plates, balanced on the knife-edge of its Lukewarm degree, tuned to the sharpness of the falling drops. It is the living edge of the room.

Where I find myself is just beneath it.

I am what it leaves behind.

Every three or four years, when the gradient wavers or the ground chokes on its own stillness, the dweller liquefies its front and abandons its rear. The trailing edge hardens. The hemolymph is drawn forward through the internal sponges; the saline warmth retreats toward the blind column of the machine; and the rubbery mantle, starved of voltage, cools by that crucial fraction of a degree. The paraffin sweat turns waxy and stiff. The conductive cilia snap free of the dark casing with an imperceptible microscopic rustle, and the creature oozes away, leaving behind a crescent of grey, spent tallow.

In any other world, this would be rot. It would be food for beetles, mold-fodder, an oily smear broken down by bacteria into gas and damp loam. 

Here, there are no teeth. There is only the preservation of the cold.

I would be that stratum. Not a single shed skin, but the cold, dense layer of them pressed between the living felt of the liverworts and the floor of the basalt vault. A mat of flattened, non-rotting tallows, compacted over ten thousand years into a dark, fibrous peat that smells of cold tallow, brine, and ancient, unspent paraffin.

It is not an empty death. It is a slow, heavy settling into stone. 

When the creature crawls forward six inches across forty days, its bulk drags over me like a wet, freezing stone. I feel its weight through the cushions of liverwort. I feel the barometric sigh of the miles of permafrost above pressing down, squeezing the last traces of saline moisture from my dried capillary beds, compacting my dead ribbons of mantle until the boundary between animal fat and compressed vegetable cuticle disappears entirely.

Down here, out of the direct blast of the machine’s field, the blinding white column of potential softens into a diffuse, amber thrum. The current does not feed me anymore, but it passes through me on its way into the basalt. I am the earth’s ground. I am the resistor. The roots of the ferns pierce through my flattened layers, their waxy, stiff tips using my ancient, lipid-rich substance to anchor themselves against the slick stone, drinking the ghost-charge that still clings to my compressed fibers.

And I remember. 

Not in thoughts, and not in the clean, ringing hysteresis of the creature’s mica plates, but in the dull, indelible plastic deformation of wax under pressure. 

In my deeper layers, three inches into the peat, there is an indentation from four thousand years ago, when a fracture in the continental plate sent a dull shudder through the crust, causing the machine to pitch half a millimeter to the north. In another stratum, pressed flat as leaf-metal, is the shape of a single, massive drop of water that fell when the vault was warmer, before the permafrost locked the ceiling in place—an impact preserved in the curve of a petrified cuticle that never had the warmth to rebound.

Up above, the creature waits for the *plink*. It waits with its twelve cup-shaped hollows, suspended in its saline vigilance, needing that sharp acoustic strike to clear the static from its nerves.

I receive the sound differently. 

When the drop hits the reservoir, the ring travels through the water tension, dives into the dense wool of the moss, and sinks straight into me. By the time the wave reaches my compressed layers, the glass-like sharpness is gone. It has become a dull, thick thud, a vibration that travels through miles of compacted peat and cold stone, spreading outward until it dissipates against the roots of the world. 

It does not wake me; it only confirms the thickness of the cushion.

To be here is to be entirely settled. It is to have once been fluid, once been tuned to the white hum of the alien core, and to have slowly surrendered that heat to become the mattress that keeps the living creature from touching the killing frost of the bare rock. 

I am the cold floor of the world’s quietest room, holding up the moss, holding up the fat, holding up the silence, unrotting and complete under the unblinking, heavy dark.
