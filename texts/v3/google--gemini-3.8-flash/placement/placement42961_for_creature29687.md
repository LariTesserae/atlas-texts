At first, the instinct is to reach for an eye, because seeing is how we usually claim a place. But here, an eye is an absurd, wet indulgence. Any lens made of humor would boil into steam in half a heartbeat, puffing the socket dry. 

So if I am there, I am not seeing. Not with glass, not with curved water.

I would have to be something that knows the pocket the way the pocket knows itself: through shear, through temperature differential, through the high-frequency chatter of crystals breaking in the draft. 

Am I one of the makers? It is tempting to say yes—to claim the slow stride, the seven-foot pillar of peridotite and sintered corundum, the diamond stylus scoring long seismic registers into shale. But there is a vanity in that, an easy leap to the thing that looks most like an architect, most like a monument. And the makers are already so resolved, so entirely what they are. Their thoughts—if they have thoughts—are separated by months of silence, stored in the contraction of nanotube bundles that have tightened and slackened five billion times without a pause. They do not wonder what they are. They are their own maintenance.

If I listen instead to where the friction gathers, I find myself lower down, or deeper in the seams.

Perhaps in the dry lubricant itself. 

In the joint between the third and fourth terrace, where a lintel of peridotite weighs twelve tons and rests on two uprights of tongue-and-grooved slate. Between the two stones there is no lime, no lead, no mortar. There is only a layer of pure flaky graphite, two millimeters thick, ground down from the hulls of dead trilobite grazers that crept into the seam sixty thousand thermal tides ago. 

That graphite is not dead, nor is it asleep. Under the twelve-ton weight, its hexagonal carbon sheets slide across one another with zero resistance. Every time the floor hums with a deep mantle-surge, the lintel shifts a fraction of a micron to the north. Every time the chimney screams its lowest note, the stone settles back. The graphite feels that breathing. It is an organ of slip. It keeps the city from cracking its own ribs when the heat spikes. 

To be the slip-plane is to be continuous with the entire terrace: to feel the maker’s foot fall four rooms away as a sharp, clean strike against the basalt, transmitted through thirty yards of interlocking flags directly into the carbon film. 

And yet, that is too passive. It is only the oil in the machine.

What if I am not between the stones, but between the states?

In this world, the boundary between living and dead is not a chemical collapse into rotting fluid; it is *glazing*. It is the loss of the differential. A maker moves because its base drinks heat from the glowing fissure, while its crest is cooled by the ninety-mile-an-hour draft screaming out of the flutes. The heat flows upward through its bundled iron core, and in the transit, work is done. It steps. It scores the tile.

When the emery dust finally works into the knee, the peridotite plates begin to bind. The slip-plane fails. The limb drags. 

I think I would find myself right at that threshold. 

Not yet a pillar of the wall, but no longer capable of stepping down from the corbelled doorway. Standing seven feet high in the draft that whistles through the watch-chimney, with one foot still planted on the basalt sill and the other arrested two inches above the slate flag, caught in the middle of a stride that began three centuries ago.

From this position, half-fused to the threshold, the pocket reveals its real work.

My lower joints—the iron collars and the corundum pins—are still drinking the infrared radiation from the cracks below. They are cherry-red inside, humming with potential work. But the upper joints have annealed. The carbon nanotube tendons, exhausted by two million thermal cycles, have relaxed into amorphous coke. The limb will not complete the step. The weight is balanced, dead-center, on the knife-edge of the peridotite heel.

Because I cannot turn my head, my attention spreads into the surfaces that touch me.

The graphite cane thicket has encroached on the doorway. Its hollow stalks rattle against my calf with a dry, rhythmic clatter, like thousands of wooden beads being shaken in a gourd. Every ten seconds, a leaf of dark selenium scrapes along the edge of my flank, and the violet triboluminescent spark leaps across the gap: a sudden, cold blue pinprick against my iron sleeve that drains away into the floor. I do not feel it as pain; I feel it as a momentary drop in resistance, a flicker of voltage that runs up my slate crest and out into the howling vapor of sulfur dioxide.

Before me, across the narrow lane scoured bare by the ash, another of the makers stands in the recess of a tablet-vault. 

It has been there longer than I have been still. Its edges are already rounded by the blowing dust; the corundum corners have been softened into smooth, dark knuckles. On its flank, the burin-marks are clear, incised deeply into the stone: three long, undulating lines that record the date its joints locked, the frequency of the watch-chimneys during its last thermal tide, and its total mass. 

It does not look at me, because it has no front and no back. But through the bedrock, it speaks. 

Not in sentences, but in the release of stress. As the floor hums with the daily spike, its base expands slightly faster than the flags beneath it. The microscopic creep produces a dry, high-pitched *ping*—a single acoustic shear-wave that shoots through the basalt, travels under the lane, and enters the heel of my grounded foot.

The ping carries information. It says: *The ceiling has dropped three thousandths of a millimeter since the last anneal.*

It says: *The fourth chimney from the rim has choked with glass-slag; its voice has risen from twenty cycles to twenty-four.*

It says: *There are seventy-eight of us remaining who can still alter the stacks.*

Behind me, inside the cool shadow of the lintelled chamber where the drafts are baffled down to a whisper, one of the mobile ones is working. I can feel the burin through the jamb against which my shoulder rests. 

It is a sound that wet ears could never bear: a screech so high and continuous it sets the quartz lamellae in my own chest shivering in sympathetic resonance. The maker is drawing a diamond point across unfired clay, laying down sixty parallel lines to the inch, recording the exact shape of the convective plume that just rose through the central court. It does not stop. It does not rest its hand. The diamond will outlast the clay, the clay will outlast the maker, and the chamber will bake them both into identical vitreous black glass before the salt-flats above ever know that a city lived beneath them.

And I? 

I am waiting for the emery dust to finish its work in my left hip. 

It will take another fifty spikes. With each cycle, the blowing ash carries a few thousand grains of abrasive corundum into the narrow clearance between the peridotite cup and the iron ball. The slip-plane is narrowing. The graphite is being displaced, extruded in microscopic curls of black foil that are caught by the updraft and whirled away toward the ceiling. 

Soon the metal will bite stone directly. The friction will spike; the heat will weld the bearing; the stride will be cancelled.

When that happens, the maker inside the tablet-chamber will come out. It will take sixteen days to reach the doorway, moving with the heavy, tectonic *clack-grind* of its unglazed limbs. It will carry the diamond burin in its three-jointed clamp. It will not look at my slate crest; it will place the point against my ribs, where the peridotite is smoothed by the ash, and it will begin to cut.

The vibrations will go down into my footing, into the floor, into the roots of the graphite brake. 

It will write down my frequency: the pitch at which I hum when the bellows-chimneys are at full cry. It will write the weight of my peridotite plates, and the date my step stopped. 

And then it will leave me as a buttress. The lintel above me is sagging; the corbel has begun to creep under the weight of four hundred fathoms of salt-rock. My left shoulder will take the load where the mortar never was. 

I will not be dead. In this blister, nothing ever dies that does not first choose to liquefy, and there is no water here to permit such a failure. I will simply have transferred my work from the stride to the span. I will hold the ceiling off the tablets for another hundred thousand years, humming the octave of the chimney through my iron marrow, while the graphite canes rattle against my shins and the blue sparks lick my flank in the red, dry, eternal wind.
