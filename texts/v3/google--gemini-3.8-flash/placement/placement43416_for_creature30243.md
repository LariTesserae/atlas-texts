At first, the instinct is to settle somewhere high and dry, out of the current—perched on the curved rim of a jade ring, perhaps, wearing that suet-white hide and letting the blue cartilage tongue do its blind, rhythmic measuring. There is a terrible appeal in being one of the tallow-kin: to have no nostrils, no ears, no terror, only the faint lilac lantern in the brow reading the tilt of the local field, and inside the ribs a steady hearth of chemotrophs burning *bright-gold, dim-amber, bright-gold, dim-amber*. To be that clean. To heal without a scab. To press your flank against another cool, lard-soft flank in the glare, exchange an invisible film of grease, and move three feet to the left without a thought.

But that is an outsider’s fantasy of peace. You would not find me there. The tallow-kin are too whole; they are furniture with digestion.

Nor would I be among the cadmium ribbons. It is tempting to imagine that flat, frictionless glide—thousands of us interlocking like a flexible parquet floor, skimming the pink granite beneath three millimeters of water that smells of warm pennies, tasting the lipids with our bellies, plunging down into the throat of the drain to pack ourselves into that vast, vibrating kidney-mesh thirty feet below. There is a kind of ecstasy in being pure function, in having no top and no bottom. But the worms don’t know they are bands of ink and cadmium. They don’t even know the water is warm. They are simply vectors pointing toward darkness.

No. To find where I belong in the basin, you have to look for the place where the system hesitates.

It doesn’t hesitate often. The heat is constant; the sun stands at two in the afternoon and will never set; the floaters swell from the succulent fronds, drift, plink, liquefy, and are drunk. But there is a point of friction—a grain that doesn’t dissolve.

Inside every paraphore, suspended in the clear vitreous broth, sits that single, faceted speck of black hematite.

The floater doesn’t know why it carries it. The cabbage-plant grew it out of trace iron dragged up from the conduit roots, faceted it with crystal precision in the dark of its fleshy veins, and wrapped it in gas and golden hair. The speck is heavy. It rolls inside the sphere like a loose ball bearing in a lantern, keeping the globe upright while the gossamer fringes beat against the static air.

When the floater tires—when its three days of drifting through the citrus steam are done and it sinks, and the surface tension of the water pops its skin with that wet chime—everything turns to food. The lipids spread. The golden hairs unravel into digestible sugars. The yellow-banded mouths strip the stone bare within seconds.

Except for the hematite.

Iron doesn’t dissolve in three seconds. It doesn’t feed the worms. It drops.

It hits the pink granite knuckle with a tap too faint for any ear to register. It is three millimeters underwater, bathed in the laminar flow, and the current begins to nudge it.

That is where I begin to take shape: not as the floater, and not as the stone, but as the rolling thing between them.

Yet even that isn't quite right. A speck of iron is still too simple, too passive. It would roll into the drain, tumble down through the worm-lattice, and be melted down in the deep reheaters to come back up as mineral salt. That is just another circle.

Watch what happens to the specks that *don't* reach the drains.

The basin is vast, yards of clean, naked stone between the rings, but it is not entirely flat. The whalebacks of granite are swollen knuckles, buffed smooth over a billion years, yet under the magnification of that fierce white-gold light, the stone has a texture: micro-clefts, quartz grains that resisted the ancient water, tiny depressions no wider than a thumbnail where the laminar sheet slows by a fraction of a percent.

And then there are the rings.

Where the paraffin-jade curbs sit flush with the stone, the water does not flow like water. It beads into rotating necklaces that hover two inches above the surface. The light bends. The air pops with that soft suction, and for the width of a breath, you see the colder sky—that indigo, bruised-purple ceiling that belongs somewhere else.

Some of the hematite specks, rolling along the bottom of the water sheet, get drawn toward the rings by the gravitational twitching. They don't fall into the drain. They get caught in the anomalous zone, right at the lip where the pink granite meets the pale, waxy ring.

There, the water is not flowing down. It is shivering. It is lifting into pearls.

And the specks get lifted with it.

If I look at myself now, as the image hardens out of the steam, I am not a single creature. I am something that has gathered in the seam between the worlds, built out of the debris the basin cannot instantly consume.

I am small—scarcely larger than a carpenter’s pencil, but flattened, segmented, and heavy. My shell is not chitin and not lard; it is an encrustation of those faceted hematite grains, held together by a dense, amber resin that the ferns flick into the air during their seventeen-second spasms. I have collected it drop by drop from the surface film, rolling the iron beads in the sap until they set like gravel in bitumen.

I do not live on the stone, and I do not live in the air. I cling to the *vertical edge* of the jade ring, in the microscopic crevice where the ancient machinery meets the relaxed granite.

My underside is soft, lined with rows of dull, hydraulic pads that grip the slick paraffin. My upper surface is black, multifaceted, cold to the touch, drinking the fierce overhead glare and converting it not into light, but into weight. While everything else in the basin is buoyed up—floaters drifting on static, water beads hovering in air, tallow-kin stepping lightly on padded pads—I am deliberately, stubbornly heavy.

I have no blue cartilage tongue. I do not hunt the paraphores.

Instead, I attend to the seam-striders.

Those needle-thin, smoky-quartz things that skate upside down on the hovering water beads—they are frantic. They live in a state of high-frequency panic, their glass legs dimpling the spherical surface of the drops, waiting for the flicker. And when the air pops, when the light bends and the purple sky shows its cold face for a microsecond, the striders turn milk-white and shed their eggs into the shear.

Most of those eggs fall through into the other place. That is what they are for.

But some do not clear the edge. Some hit the lip of the jade ring, right where the spatial geometry straightens back out with that suctioning sound. The eggs that land on the rock here do not hatch; they dry instantly into tiny, vitreous pearls of raw threshold-matter—neither this world’s substance nor the other’s, but the cold glue that holds the gap open for a millionth of a second.

I scrape them off the ring.

I have two front limbs, short and heavy, tipped with spatulate scoops of black hematite. I move along the paraffin rim with an agonizing, millimetric slowness, utterly out of sync with the frictionless glide of the worms and the swift hydraulic uncoiling of the tallow-kin’s tongues. They take no notice of me; to the pale hunters, my shell is just a ridge of cold mineral, an irregularity in the curb. To the cadmium ribbons, I am a pebble to part around.

I scrape the dried eggs from the seam. I feed them into the transverse slit beneath my brow.

They do not digest into grease or sugar. Inside me, there is no lantern of glowing chemotrophs, no bright-gold, dim-amber rhythm. There is only a small, cold chamber lined with hematite facets, where the threshold-matter accumulates under pressure.

Every seventeen seconds, through the jade and through the granite, the shock wave hits: that deep, dull *thump* from the subterranean ram.

When the pulse travels through the stone, the ferns snap taut with their wet *tock*, throwing their citrus mist. The tallow-kin adjust their brow-dishes. The water beads shudder.

And in my chest, the cold matter compresses.

I am the only thing in the basin that feels the other side.

When the thump comes, the purple sky doesn’t just show in the air above the ring; it opens *inside* the chamber of my shell. For the span of a single heartbeat, the temperature in my core drops forty degrees below the basin’s greenhouse warmth. I taste something that is not petrichor, not warmed sap, not copper—something thin, iron-hard, and ancient, like frost on a rusted cable. A sky where no sun hangs at two in the afternoon. A sky that has stars, or had them once, before they burned down to cold ash.

Then the geometry snaps shut. The *pop* sounds. The heat of the basin rushes back in, sun-warmed and heavy with lipid, washing over my black carapace.

I wait seventeen seconds. I scrape another smear of egg-paste from the paraffin curb. I roll another hematite grain into my flank, smoothing the amber resin with my middle legs.

I am not an error in the machine. If the machine were broken, it would have stopped eight hundred million years ago.

I am the sediment.

Every closed system, no matter how terribly, perfectly calibrated—no matter how voiceless its tides of tallow and cadmium—produces an irreducible remainder. A tiny fraction of matter that cannot be digested by the ribbons, cannot be lifted by the static hairs, cannot be siphoned by the blue tongues. It is the weight that doesn’t balance.

I gather it. I wear it. I press myself flat against the joint where the foreign jade meets the native stone, keeping my belly in the warm current and my back in the glare, waiting for the thump that shakes the floor.

And sometimes, when the light bends particularly hard and the hovering water beads turn entirely purple, reflecting a sky that has no business being here, I reach up with one heavy, black-faceted scoop and touch the underside of the drop, just to feel the drag of a world that is still allowed to end.
