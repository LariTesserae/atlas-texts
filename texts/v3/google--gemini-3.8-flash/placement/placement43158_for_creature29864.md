The first thing to understand is that I do not stand upright. To stand straight would mean offering a sail to thirty miles of uninterrupted draft; the wind would snap the small bones of the ankles before it cast the body fifty yards down-trench against a basalt shoulder. 

I live at forty degrees from the horizontal. My forehead is always pointed toward the distant intake array, leaning out over the pale gray expanse of the moss as if into a stiff, freezing hand that never tires of holding me up. 

My boots are four layers of laminated fabric-ply conveyor belting, hacked out with a cold chisel and bound to the calves with greasy rawhide strips. They have no arch and no flex; they are sleds. Underneath them, the root-turf does not crunch—it gives with the dull, rubbery recoil of chilled lard. Every step is an unseating, a slow tearing of the tiny white holdfasts that seek to gum themselves to the soles between one shift of weight and the next.

I am what the others tap for when the slack changes along the south wall: the *cable-creeper*. 

My ears are stopped with balls of bee-wax kneaded with shot-lead, worked into the canal until the world becomes a thick, greasy silence. But the silence is not empty. The lead does not kill the fans; it changes their medium. The thunder no longer enters through the drum; it enters through the soles of the conveyor boots, travels through the tibia, rattles the ball-joints of the hips, and seats itself directly behind the eyes as a persistent, cold ache that tastes of tin filings. When a brass bearing four miles south scuffs against its race, I do not hear an alarm. I feel a sharp, dry stitch in the left side of my groin, where the nerve has grown accustomed to the frequency of that specific turbine.

Around my ribs, beneath three layers of mineralized canvas, is the felt. It was peeled from the under-flange of a dead compressor eight winters ago—a dense, pale pelt of dried liverwort and peat-lichen, three inches thick, reeking of cold lard and ancient ammonia. It does not warm me by producing heat; it warms me by refusing to allow the draft to steal the tallow-grease I rub into my chest every morning. 

In my right hand is the ash-stave, iron-shod at the point, copper-capped at the butt. I do not walk with it; I lean it against the basalt walls. 

When I press the copper cap to the flat planar fractures of the stone and touch my lower teeth to the ash grain, the whole trench becomes an open diagram. I can bite the vibration of the rift. Through the enamel, I can feel where the basalt is weeping into the root-mat sixty yards ahead. I can feel the dragging, hydraulic crawl of a mat-slider two feet beneath my feet—a thick, slow sludge of movement that registers as an oily wobble in the teeth, distinct from the brittle, click-click vibration of a spindle-dog threading a knot of taproots.

Most of my day is spent not on the carpet, but in the slit.

Between the pylons, the black power cables sag into the turf, and where they touch the liverwort, the heat from the current weeps out, melting a narrow, trench-like pocket in the pale fleece. In those pockets, out of the howling gale, the air is dead and smells intensely of iron water and rotting parsnips. 

That is where I drop down. 

I take the billhook from my belt—a heavy, leaf-shaped blade with an edge kept blunt so it splits the fibers rather than cutting them—and I pry open the seam where the root-hawsers ride the stone. I slide in feet first, pulling the rubbery flap of pale moss over my head like an oily counterpane.

Down here, between the fibrous white ceiling and the planar floor of the cleaved basalt, there is no wind. The cold is different; it is not the biting, scouring draft of the blades, but the still, mineral freeze of a butcher’s cellar. 

My back presses against the hawsers—they are thick as an arm, cold and wet like drowned hawser-rope, pulsing slightly with the subterranean moisture they drink from the rock. My belly rests on the cold stone. Here, the noise is so immense it ceases to be acoustic; the rock floor shudders against my breastbone with a cadence so heavy it forces my own heart to drop its rhythm, falling into step with the four-story blades spinning miles away in the dark. *One. Two. One. Two.* Twelve beats a minute. The blood in my temples moves at the speed of the intake draft.

A spindle-dog slides past my shoulder in the dark. It does not bite; its whiskers, stiff as broom straw, drag along the canvas of my sleeve, testing the grease. It is no thicker than my forearm, pale and greasy as suet, its blind skull nudging into the crook of my elbow before it finds an opening in the root-tangle and pulls itself through with an accordion heave of its ribs. It leaves behind a smell like cold tallow and river mud. I let it pass. We are both ribs and teeth down here, waiting out the weight of the air.

I carry a bundle of copper split-pins and a hammer with a lead-weighted head. My work is simple: where the root-mass tries to heave the black cables off the stone, I drive a pin through the cable-collar into the cleavage lines of the basalt, setting it with three short, dead-blow strikes that do not chip the rock. 

The hammer strikes send bright, sweet rings through the stone. I know that half a mile down the trench, in the swaying corrugated box of Pylon 38, old Kroll is sitting on his three-legged stool with his boots off, his bare heels pressed to the floorplates, feeling those three rings come up through the iron stilts. *Clink. Clink. Clink.* He knows the south feed is pinned. He will not tap back; he will simply shift his weight and put another kettle of taproot-tallow on the cast-iron stove.

I do not think often of the surface plateau. I have seen the intake vents from below—monstrous gray throats of reinforced concrete, screened with steel mesh thick as bridge timbers, their corners crusted with rime six feet deep. Sometimes, caught in the wire of those screens, you see the carcass of something that belonged up there: a wide-winged hawk frozen into a dry wedge of feathers, or the bough of a spruce tree, stripped of its needles by the gale until it looks like a bundle of white fish bones. 

Those things look flimsy to me. They look thin, like paper that has dried too close to an oil stove. They could not endure the teeth-rattle. They would shatter under the first hour of the monotone bellow.

Down here, everything has thickness. The moss does not yield; it remembers. The rock does not weather; it cleaves clean and square. Even the bodies of the Gripers who went before me are still down here in the floor, braided through by the pale holdfasts until their ribs are indistinguishable from the iron hawsers of the turf. 

Sometimes, when I am reaching into the wet dark beneath the root-mat to set a piton, my fingers find a button—a flat disk of vulcanized rubber or a circle of stamped tin from an old ration tin. I do not pull it up. I leave it in the starch-mud against the basalt. It is an anchor-point, no different from the pylon footings.

When the shift is done, I will use the billhook to lever open the flap of turf. I will poke my head back out into the freezing blast, catch the amber glimmer of the sodium lamp on the gantry two hundred yards away, and lean my body out at forty degrees, letting the great cold lung of the rift take my weight once more. 

I will walk with the rolling trudge, my ash-stave tapping out the rhythm of the bearings against the iron pitons, neither fast nor slow, keeping time with the blades that will still be turning long after the tallow has set cold in my bones.
