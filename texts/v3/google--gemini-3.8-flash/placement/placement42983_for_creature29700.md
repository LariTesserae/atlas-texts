Not at the base. The base is an argument between the basalt and the sheer drop of the alloy, and there is no curiosity there, only the wait for things to land.

Nor in the gut of the shaft. To be a weaver is to have already agreed to the limits of the cylinder: to spend eighty years knowing the diameter of your world to within three centimeters, your fingers permanently green from copper salt, your forehead callused flat from interrogating the steel. It is too neat an adaptation; it has closed around them like a wet hide drying in the heat.

No, the place to begin is in the middle of a drop that takes eleven minutes.

*

At first, you would think yourself a swimmer that drifted an eighth of a league too far west, into the hollow between Pylon 4-South and the dead cantilever they call the Rib. You would feel your belly chill. The thick grease in the thoracic sac, which keeps you buoyant so long as the 400-cycle drone shakes the gas within it, would begin to stiffen, turning from amber oil to something like suet. Your ribs would draw closer together. The twitch of your lateral oars would stop answering the rhythm of the air-strata and begin to chop, uselessly, against an atmosphere that has suddenly lost its lift.

You would be falling.

Except there is no terror in it, because the fall here is not an interruption; it is a geography. 

Between the spires, where the induction fields thin out and cross-cancel into grey pockets of complete silence, there is an entire climate that never touches metal. The weavers cannot fish it—their fern-lines are too short, three hundred meters at most, dangling like dry whiskers against the vast flank of the tower. The kettles cannot reach it because their shells require the hard surface to scrape against; if a kettle leaves the steel, it is only because it has died or been pried off by a deluge.

Down here, halfway between the bruised tectonic cap and the black slate that has never known a tool, the air is not warm. It is the only cold thing in the basin. It smells not of ferns or ozone, but of old, suspended moisture—water that condensed three days ago on the north bevel of a strut, fell two thousand meters, broke into fog, was caught in an updraft caused by the thermal exhaust of the transit track, rose again, and has been hovering ever since, turning sour and flat.

In this cold, you would not have skin. Skin is an invitation to the kettles to bore for salt, or to the liverworts to find a microscopic fissure and drive a root-hair into your lymph. 

Instead, you find yourself wearing something between a dried hull and an oilcloth. It is gathered loose about the joints, thickest at the nape and the soles, layered like the leaves of a wild leek. When the dry vibration of the towers hits it, the fabric doesn't shiver; it sheds. The outer layer is constantly flaking away in dry, parchment-colored rinds that join the kettle-frass drifting toward the floor. You do not grow; you exfoliate.

Where are you? 

You are hanging from an anchor that isn't an anchor.

Between Pylon 4-South and the dead cantilever, someone—not the weavers, who do not cross the air, and not the builders, who left only the frame—flung a secondary guy-wire. It is no thicker than a thumb, two miles long, and it has sagged under its own weight until its belly hangs four hundred meters above the slate floor, right in the center of the null-space.

Because the induction doesn’t reach it, the wire does not hum. Because it does not hum, the cruisers do not graze it; it is too cold for their snouts. Because it does not heat on the six-hour cycle, the mosses cannot gain purchase; their spores land, shrivel in the stale drift, and fall away.

It is the only naked thing in the basin.

You are balanced upon it like a crow on a fence wire, or rather, you are slung beneath it in a hammock made of braided cruiser-ribs and the long, tough taproots of the purples that grow only where the lightning strikes the pylon hoods.

Your eyes—you still have them, though they have changed their trade. In the bruised, post-twilight glow, there are no shapes to see, only densities. You do not watch for the spires; you watch for the *tears* in the mist. 

When the six-hour cycle begins, you see it before the weavers feel it with their skulls. You see it as a dimpling of the gloom three miles up. The soot-colored belly of the ceiling seems to drop six inches, bloated by the sudden vacuum created when the thousand-foot radiators on the towers drink their heat back into the core. Then comes the line: a ruler-straight seam of pale, falling white that appears simultaneously along the edge of every spar within twelve leagues.

It is like watching a cage be dropped around the world.

Then the sound changes. The sub-bass, which you do not hear with your ears—they were plugged with kettle-wax years ago to save your brain from being turned to custard—rises two octaves. It leaves your teeth and enters the small of your back. It is the sound of eighty thousand tons of copper cooling ten degrees in forty seconds.

And then the spray comes.

This is who you are:

You are the one who intercepts what does not hit the slate.

When the cruisers tuck their oars and dive for the gutters, nine out of ten make the shelf. They wedge themselves under the steel flanges like fat grapes in a crate, shivering against one another, their translucent rinds glistening with grease. 

The tenth one misses.

Perhaps its dorsal bladder had a dead seam; perhaps it was old, its horny plates worn flat by sixty leagues of moss-scraping; perhaps it simply misjudged the drop of the air when the radiators drew the pressure down. It slips out of the current. It tumbles. It does not spiral—it is too heavy for that. It falls like an unskinned ox, its pale oars trailing behind it like wet ribbons, turning slowly in the cross-draught of the deluge.

It passes your wire.

You do not use a fern-line. A line has tension; tension creates a note; a note brings the stilts leaping toward you from three miles away, thinking something has struck the floor.

You drop a basket.

It is an immense, loose-meshed thing, woven from the flight-cases of three thousand dead kettles, linked together with annealed copper wire fished out of the maintenance vents during the cold gaps. It has no weight of its own, but it has surface. When you let it fall from the guy-wire, it billows like an open parachute, drifting sideways into the path of the falling meat.

The cruiser strikes the net with a sound like a wet sack of flour hitting turf. 

The wire groans. The guy-wire—cold, bare, drawn tight as a tendon—bobs twenty meters toward the black slate, then recoils. You ride the bounce with your knees drawn up to your chest, your chin resting on the greasy knee-pads of your suit, your knuckles locked into the turnbuckle that your great-predecessor clamped to the alloy before the rust began.

For five minutes, you are the heaviest thing in the void.

*

Below you, the stilts have heard the recoil.

You look down through the slatted floor of your rib-cradle. Two thousand feet below, in the perpetual matte-black of the basin floor, the needle-points are moving. 

You can’t see the bodies—they are too small, dark fists lost in the cavernous dark—but you can see where their feet strike the slate. The impact of their fibrous cartilage against the newly split stone produces a microscopic spark of static electricity—a pinprick of cold, blue-green fire that dies the instant it appears. 

There are thousands of them. It looks like an expanse of black water struck by a sudden, silent squall of phosphorescent rain. They are converging on the space directly beneath your hammock. They stand in a circle four hundred meters wide, their three-meter legs slightly bent, their blind, hypodermic heads tilted upward toward the wire.

They know something is up here. They can feel the twenty-meter dip of the cable vibrating down through the anchor-pins at the base of Pylon 4-South. They are waiting for the drop. They are waiting for the meat to slip through the mesh and make that singular, wet report on the stone that will set their pumping-organs into spasm.

You do not let it drop.

You sit in the center of the null-space, with sixty tons of water drifting past you as a warm, fern-scented aerosol that softens the hide around your neck. With slow, rhythmic pulls of a lever-and-pawl made from kettle-mandibles, you haul the basket up toward the wire, link by link.

The cruiser inside is still alive. Its snouts are pulsing, the pale gelatinous pits reading the strange, dead neutrality of this wire that has no current. It does not understand why it is not falling; it does not understand why it does not burn. Its skin smells of cold tallow and sheared copper.

You reach into the basket with a short, heavy blade ground from a piece of unworked spar-flange. You find the junction behind the third rib, where the lipid bladder tapers into the lateral oar-socket, and you drive the steel home.

A gallon of warm, amber grease floods over your wrists.

It does not stay warm long. In the dead pocket, it sets quickly, turning into a stiff, grey wax that smells of wet slate and old electricity. You scrape it into gourds made from the cruiser’s own dried stomach-linings. This is your fuel; this is your water; this is the grease you rub into your eyelids to keep the kettle-frass from blinding you while you sleep.

*

Why are you not on the ground?

Because the ground is where the machine remembers what it was meant to be. The slate is flat because it was cleared for foundations that never arrived; its expansion seams are straight because they were cut by laser-levels whose transmitters have been dead for ten thousand cycles. If you stand on the slate, you are an intruder in a hallway that was never walled. The stilts understand the slate; they are its only legitimate children, needle-legged and acid-mouthed, turning everything that fails back into clean, clear jelly to be tucked into the cracks where the world is split.

And why are you not in the towers?

Because the towers are too loud. To live in the towers is to be eaten by the frequency. The weavers have ears like cauliflower; their thoughts are sixty-cycle thoughts, six-hour thoughts; they wake when the metal sweats and sleep when it drinks. They are merely the soft parts of the pylon’s cooling system, little valves that clean the vents and harvest the lines.

Here, in the dead sag of the guy-wire, there is no system.

The designers did not draw this line; it was left behind when a crane-derrick fell during the third age of the work, its cable catching upon the spur of the cantilever and wedging there forever, out of reach of the maintenance crawlers.

It is an accident. The only accident in twelve leagues of systematic void.

You lie back in the basket as the condensation cycle finishes. The air grows clear again, thin and warm, smelling of the dry, crushed-pepper fern mats two miles away. The blue-green sparks on the slate floor begin to scatter, the stilts realizing that nothing is coming down, their long, hair-thin legs carrying them back toward the expansion seams in rhythmic, three-meter bounds that make no sound at all.

Above you, the kettles resume their shrieking treble, millions of them scraping their carapaces against the drying rails, shedding the last droplets before the rust can take them. The great induction drone returns, settling into your collarbones like an iron weight.

You take a piece of the cruiser’s horn—the flexible, dark plate from its upper mouth—and you begin to scrape the salt from your knuckles.

You have four hours until the wire begins to cool. You will sleep with your leg looped through the cable’s lay, your head pillowed on a sack of tallow that was swimming three hundred meters above you this morning, while the blind city screams its one-note song into the hollow belly of the rock, and the empty air keeps its balance.
