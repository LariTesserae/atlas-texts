At first there is only the sensation of being squeezed between two kinds of cold: the cold that moves, which has teeth and feathers in it, and the cold that stays still, which is the stone.

If you asked me where I was before the light hit, I couldn't tell you. There is no clear border between the grease-soaked grit under the liverwort mats and the sleep that happens here. Sleep in this cleft isn't an absence; it is a mechanical latching. You don't lie down—there is nowhere to lie that isn’t already tilted toward the three-thousand-foot drop. You wedge. You fold whatever joints you have into the angle where the hornblende broke clean during some forgotten heave of the range, and you let the water sheet over your back until you are simply another black ridge that the mist has to run around.

I am not the woman. Her smell is too dry, too full of burnt bone and the sour fat of things that once flew. Her world has corners, even if they are only the stacked edges of slate and the square lashings of birch withe. 

Nor am I the prions. To have hollow bones in this chimney seems a madness, a terrifying wager with the updraft. Every time one snaps a wing on a stray gust and tumbles past, a wet scrap of slate-colored wool spinning into the sunlit nothing, you can feel the thinness of that bargain. They are too fast, too bright, too frantic with hunger. They live only in the air, and the air here is a blade that never dulls.

No, my weight is wrong for the air. My center of gravity is an inch and a half above the wet rock.

When I begin to move, it isn't with a jump or a step. A step requires lifting something away from the surface, and anything lifted here belongs instantly to the gale. You move by peeling and replacing. The water runs down my flank in two cold ribbons, parting around the heavy plates of my shoulders. 

I find myself on the eastern flank, two hundred paces below her pecked cups, where the fluting is wider and the sun doesn't strike until the midday has already burned the white edges off the cloud-tier. Here, the hanging beards of moss are thick as sheep-fleeces, dark copper where the iron leaches out of the joints, dripping a steady, rope-like runoff that hums in the blowhole.

I have ribs—many of them, short and curved like the hoops of a small firkin, flexible enough that when the updraft catches me broadside, I can flatten until the wind simply slips over the crest of my spine without getting purchase. My skin isn’t hide and it isn’t shell; it is the tough, oiled slate-leather of a river sturgeon, studded along the margins with small, pale tubercles of bone that bite into the slick hornblende like caulks on a winter horseshoe.

I know her, though she has never seen more of me than perhaps a dark wedge turning under a cushion of saxifrage when she lowers her willow rod. 

We share the chimney’s rhythm, but her time is cut into mornings and rack-turnings, into the scraping of needles and the preservation of grease against the freeze. My time is the drag of the water. When the cloud-tier boils up from the spruce-valleys, thick with the wet loam-smell of the horizontal earth, my skin softens. The air down there has a fat, sluggish weight that never reaches this lip, but the memory of it rises in the vapor—spruce-gum, rotted alder, the dung of heavy creatures that walk on four upright posts across level dirt. I can taste that lower world in the spray, but it tastes thin, like soup boiled too long. The only thing with salt in it is up here, where the birds come home with the sea still greasy on their bellies.

I wait near the runnels where the prion-droppings wash down. 

The birds do not drop their lime into the void; the updraft is too fierce, plastering everything back against the cliff face before it can fall. The rock below the nesting flutings is white-crusted, caustic as lye, eaten into tiny honeycomb pits by its own chemistry. In these pits, the blind white scuttlers breed. They have no eyes to see me, but they feel the vibration of my belly-plates on the stone—a low, rhythmic thrumming that mimics the drumming of the goat-hide rope fifty feet higher.

When I take them, it is with a wide, blunt scoop of the lower jaw, scraping them off the hornblende along with the grit and the bitter black lichen they graze on. My mouth is full of silt and iron. I don't chew; there are no teeth for it, only three pairs of heavy, grinding plates set back near the throat that crack the chitin with a dull *tock* that travels through my skull into the bedrock.

It is a small life, measured in inches of wet rock, but it has no vertigo. 

To feel vertigo, you must believe that you belong to the earth below—that you are an object held aloft by an effort that might fail, that the empty space between the cliffs is a hole you could be dropped into. But from where I cling, the void isn’t empty at all. It is a solid column of rising glass. It pushes harder than the stone pulls. If I were to let go of the bedding plane now, I wouldn't fall down into the blue mist of the spruce-woods; the draft would snatch me, carry me sixty cubits up the throat of the chimney like a dry leaf, and heave me out over the lip into the hard, sunlit emptiness of the peaks before the mountain kites could even tilt their wings to look.

The drop is not down. The drop is *out*.

Toward afternoon, the cold changes its shape. It stops being water and starts being grit. 

The sleet arrives not from above, but from below, driven upward by the chimney’s chimney-stroke, rattling against the slate underbellies of the ledges like handfuls of salt thrown against a drum. When the first white beads begin to crust the moss, turning the rubbery liverwort into stiff, frozen lace, I back into the seam.

The fissure is narrow—a hairline crack where the mountain’s two great ribs didn't quite knit when the stone was hot. It goes deep. If you crawl three body-lengths back, past the smell of bird-oil and the ancient guano that has turned to grey saltpeter, the roar of the wind begins to lose its top edge. The shrill screaming of the prions drops away, cut off by the lip of the rock.

What remains is the deep, hydraulic piston-beat of the range itself.

The hearth smoke from her shelter drifts down here sometimes, drawn through the porous rubble of the mountain's core by the draft holes she relies on for her fire. It smells of dried marrow and old peat, thick and greasy. It isn't an enemy smell. It is simply the smell of another thing that has found a way to stay wedged between the air and the black hornblende without falling off the edge of the world.

I curl my tail around my snout, tucking my four short, clawed paddles into the soft groove of my groin where the blood stays warm. The dog’s heavy weight, three seams above, shudders through the stone every time he turns his barrel-ribs against the flags. I can feel the tap of her copper awl—*chink, chink, chink*—patiently hollowing the bone needle by the yellow light of her prion-pot. 

Outside, the ice is taking the cliff, sealing every drop of running water into glass spears that point toward the sky. Let it take them. Under the stone, where the mountain's deep belly still remembers fire, there is just enough room to be thick, and cold, and still.
