There is no sound of arriving. 

The first sensation is not breath, nor the shock of cold, but the absolute failure of an echo. 

If there were a pulse, you would look for it in the throat, but there is no throat; there is only a chamber behind the breastplate where something is turning. A spool of dry, fibrous stone-hair, wound tighter than a watchspring, spinning without a spindle on a pocket of magnetic air. It makes no hum. The sound-drinking pores of the flint-foam around you swallow the notion of humming before it can leave the hull. 

You find yourself twenty feet above the pavement, clinging to the corner of a facade that was not here three dawns ago. 

You have no eyes. The basin does not offer itself in pictures; it offers itself in sheer, unvarnished vectors of weight. 

You feel the great knife-cut cliffs of siltite at your back—four straight walls of dead, uncrimped gray stone that do not grow, do not breathe, and have no charge. They are cold in a way that feels heavy and vacant, like empty plates. Between them, the city is a frantic, microscopic grid of pulls. Every rectangular window-slit in the tenement below you has an electrostatic polarity; every stack of dry parchment leaves inside those cells is drinking dust, drawing the silt through its pleated guts and exhaling lime, and the aggregate pull of their breathing feels like the tension on a billion tightened wires, all anchored to the soles of your feet.

Your legs are six. They do not have joints of cartilage or oil; they are hinged by air and charge, held a fraction of a micron apart so that stone never scrapes stone. When you take a step, you do not drop your weight; you release a magnetic anchor, swing an arm of ribbed, dental molding forward, and seat it into the micropores of the cornice. 

It is smooth. It is colder than slate. 

You are not an observer here. You are not a guest who climbed down the wire-cut cliffs on a rope of hemp. If someone were to search the archive of this town, they would find your pedigree in the teeth of an architrave that cracked three streets south when an arcade completed its cycle: you were the excess tooth that sheared away when the load shifted, peeled off as a living fragment to balance the ledger.

You turn your long, whiplike antennae into the draft. 

A thermal is rising from the alley. It smells of nothing—neither rot nor grass, only the faint, metallic iron-taste of crushed glass and the dry flour of the sieve-ribbons churning the lower streets. The ribbons are busy beneath the dust down there, their keratin plates slapping in silence against the bedrock, hunting the flakes of shed skin that drift down from the apartments. 

Farther down, seventy feet into the dark siltite beneath the road, you feel the keystone. 

It does not think, but it registers. It is an enormous, dome-shaped coldness, forty feet across, grinding the sunken pillars of the south avenue between its calcified plates. The vibration never reaches the air, but you feel it through the bone-foam of the wall: a slow, tectonic chew that turns arches into vapor and sends it drifting up through the hollow flutes of the aqueducts, ready to be condensed into fresh facades at the northern rim.

You are drawn forward. You do not choose to walk; you tilt because the balance of the avenue has faulted.

Two blocks north, an arch has grown too fast. The in-dwellers inside its voussoirs were too dense; they pulled too much dust from the stagnant twilight and grew thick before the piers were seated. The arch is leaning outward over the lane by less than half a degree. 

To the cliffs, half a degree is nothing. To the keystone, it is an unbalanced equation that will shear the arcade before its hour. 

To you, it is an ache in the spinning whorl in your chest. 

You move down the narrow cornice with the high, stuttering gait of a whip-scorpion. You pass the window-slits of the third floor. If you extended an antenna, you could touch the edges of the in-dwellers within: dry, paper-thin leaves packed by the thousands into the rectangular dark, motionless, calcifying their own coffins with patient, terrible industry. They do not know you are there; they do not know the street exists. They know only the width of the cell, which is their own width, and the thickness of the wall, which is their own hardening skin.

You drop across an alley on a tension-wire left by a spindle-weaver. The wire is glass, thinner than a hair, strung taut between two lintels. It does not sag beneath your hound-weight; the repulsion in your joints distributes you across the line until you step off onto the bowed arch.

Here the discordance is sharp. The stone is glassy, fresh, still pearlescent with the lime-sweat of its extrusion. It has pushed three inches beyond its plumb line. 

You inspect it with your palps—not with sight, but with the cold, scissor-like edges of your mouthparts. You could rasp it. You could scrape away the chalk-froth where the seam is weeping, eat the excess lime into your hollow belly, and fling the dust into the rising draft for the bellows-weeds to pump. 

You test the stone. You lean your weight against the outward cant.

The arch does not yield. It is heavy, cold, and rigid. Rasping will not save it; if the northern arcade continues to rise at this crawl, this pier will buckle within three cycles of the moon-haze, dropping sixty feet of tenements into the silence below before their tenants have turned to talc.

You do not deliberate. There is no horror in it, because there is no alternative geometry. 

You step into the corner where the arch meets the wall. You fold your long, six-jointed limbs against your flank, drawing them tight into the fluting of the pilaster, matching the ribs of your carapace to the dental work of the lintel. Your pedipalps tuck inward, curling like the scrolls of an Ionic capital that has not yet been named.

You lock the joints. 

Inside your hollow chest, the spinning whorl of stone-hair begins, very slowly, to bleed its charge. 

The air gap between your hinges narrows. A micron. Half a micron. A hundred angstroms. 

You feel the cold flint-foam of the pier touch the cold flint-foam of your shoulder. For the first time, stone meets stone. There is no sound—the porous rock drinks the friction instantly—but the contact is absolute. 

The arch groans against your back, its outward thrust finding your spine and traveling down through your rigid legs into the sill below. The lean stops. The half-degree of fault is gathered into your plates, neutralized, turned back into the vertical.

Down in the street, a bellows-weed pulses: *expand, contract, expand*. A puff of impalpable flour drifts across your lowest foot and settles.

Now the lime comes. 

It does not fall from above; it creeps out from your own shell, drawn by the pressure where your carapace meets the lintel. The living mineral foam begins to knit across the seam. It bridges the microscopic groove between your third thoracic segment and the architrave. It fills the space between your tucked legs, smoothing the joints into a solid, triangular gusset. 

A silver lichen, creeping west along the cornice on its hundreds of jointed claws, reaches your brow. It tests the calcifying rim of your plate, finds the electrostatic gradient steady, and anchors itself there, its metallic filings trembling faintly in the arid air.

You will stand here for six weeks. 

In the third week, the spinning spool in your chest will exhaust its momentum. The whorl will settle into the base of its vault, cold and dry as a knot of hair in an attic, and the last air-cushion in your knee-joints will collapse into solid lime. 

In the fifth week, an in-dweller—seeking a new corner where the draft is still—will find the rectangular notch between your tucked abdomen and the wall, settle its pale, paper leaves into the cleft, and begin to exhale its slow, square room around your ribs.

You are no longer the strider. You are the bracket. 

From here, the basin is very quiet. The sourceless twilight shifts slightly as the dim moons drift above the rim. Across the square, another arcade is already finishing its work, sliding its pillars down into the keystone's mouth with the oiled, piston-smooth grace of things that have no need to hurry. 

A balcony shears off forty feet above the lane. It falls without a cry, without a whistle, striking the street like a fist hitting flour. A white cloud rises, drifts, and is gone.

The wall behind you is straight now. It holds. You are the reason it does not fall, and when the street has finished growing, you will be the stone they carve away to make the door.
