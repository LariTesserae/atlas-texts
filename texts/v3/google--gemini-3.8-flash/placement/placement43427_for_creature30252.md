I would not be on the sunward ridge, standing braced against the camber with a rake in my fists. That is work for the clean-jointed, whose shins still have the spring of green sapling-wood and whose lungs can drink the dry resin-dust without coughing curd. 

Nor would I be down in the interior flutes with the gall-monks, their skins bleached to the color of wet parchment, their fingers gummed into mantis-spurs, harvesting the dry brackets in the dark. That darkness is too thick; it smells of stale flour and the cold, breathless dung of long-dead borers.

I find myself wedged into an elbow.

It is a compression-burl, three leagues out along the underside of the Seventh Arch, where the timber twisted under some unfathomable torsional load twenty thousand centuries ago. Here, the growth rings do not run straight like roads; they buckle, curling into dense, corrugated whorls the size of amphitheaters. It sits directly on the border where the two climates of the span collide: above my head, three fathoms up, the lip of the wood is baked bone-white and screams with the passage of the martins; below my heels, the timber curves inward into the shadow-side, where the green-black moisture hangs and the traveling clouds come to scrape their bellies against the burls.

I am an old thing, small and heavy, curled into a dry larval pocket like an almond in its shell. 

My skin has long since ceased to be human skin in any tender sense. It is a rind. The smear—the boiled ox-beetle fat and bitter liverwort—has been worked into my hide so many thousands of times that it no longer washes out, even when the fleece-clouds drag through my lap. It has turned me the color of an old bridle dropped in a bog. My knees are permanently bent; the tendons behind them have shortened into cords of cured gut, locking me into an effortless, perpetual squat. If you laid me flat, I would spring back into a knot.

Around my waist is a broad belt of mantis-leather, dry and stiff as birch-bark, buckled with a carved rib-bone. From it, three short lanyards of twisted spider-silk link me to ironwood dowels driven four feet into the heartwood behind me. I do not untie them. I have not stood upright in four seasons. 

In my mouth, I carry the stone.

It is a gannet-pebble, no larger than a pigeon’s egg, gray-veined and heavy. A hunter brought it to me three generations ago, plucked from the gizzard of a long-winged diver that had fallen from the upper thermal with its skull split by an aerial serpent. It is the only stone within forty leagues. It tastes of nothing—a dead, mineral coldness that neither rots nor weeps resin. 

I do not keep it under my tongue for luck. I keep it between my back teeth, on the left side.

When I bite down on it, and press the butt of my left jaw against the petrified grain of the furrow, the deafening scream of the sky vanishes. The iron shearing of the martins, the four-winged drone of the ox-beetles, the dry, rhythmic sawing of the mantid swarms—the air swallows all of that, wasting it on the leagues of empty lavender below. 

Through the tooth, through the stone, through the heartwood, I hear the inside of the world.

The spans are not dead timber; they are an engine under tension. When the noon sun bakes the upper crests, the sunward wood expands by the thickness of a thumbnail across a league; the shadow-side stays cold and wet, and the timber groans against itself with a sound like a wet hide being twisted by four men. I hear that twist. I hear the water traveling through the grain—not sap, for there has been no sap since the beginning of things, but the cold weight of the cisterns draining down through the ancient internal rot-seams, a slow, hollow *tick... tick... tick* that tells me which knot-hole will be dry before the moon turns.

I hear the hunters above. They think their footfalls are silent because their soles are horn-thick and soft-split, but through the grain their steps are dull, leaden thuds, like wooden mauls dropped into flour. When they trip the gallows-rakes, the fulcrum sends a sharp, ringing *ping* down the growth rings that makes the fillings of my skull vibrate.

I am what they call a *Cleft-Sitter*, though they have shorter clicks for it in the throat: *the one who listens for the split*.

The wood does not rot easily, but it fractures. Sometimes an arch tires of its load. Ten miles to the west, deep in the blind core where no gall-monk has ever crawled, a fiber the thickness of a ship’s mast will part under the desiccated strain. To the men on the crest, it sounds like nothing—just another crack of thunder lost in the shrieking of twenty thousand birds. But to my jaw, it is a dry, snapping report that travels through thirty miles of solid cedar in the space of a heartbeat. 

When it comes, I do not shout. I reach out with a short mallet of beetle-horn and strike a tuned peg hammered into the rim of my furrow: *tok-tok, tok, tok-tok-tok*. 

The vibration climbs the growth ring like grease up a wick. A quarter-league above, a woman working the cambium-pot feels it through the calluses of her heels; she drops her push-knife, reaches for her children, and shifts them forty paces east before the shear-line opens and drops twelve fathoms of sun-bleached bark into the void.

They bring me things for this. 

They do not come down to my pocket—the rope-ladders are frail and the leeward wind has teeth—but they lower baskets on braided sinew lines from the upper platform. 

Inside the baskets, I find the liver of a young martinet, oily and purple, still warm enough to steam against the wood; little cakes of bracket-flour traded from the gall-monks, gray and dry as chalk, which I dissolve against the quartz stone in my cheek; and bladders of fresh, sweet cistern-water taken before the noon sun has had time to taint it with resin-scum. 

Sometimes there is an offering of smear—the yellow, thick blubber from behind an ox-beetle’s eyes, which is the finest grease there is. I rub it into my eyelids and between my toes, where the skin tends to fissure and bleed from the dry cloud-scour.

Beyond the people, there are the neighbors of the furrow.

A finger’s breadth from my left elbow, where the grain opens into an oblique fissure two inches wide, lives a solitary mantis. She is as long as my forearm, translucent as greased horn, with pale, milky eyes that cannot see light, only the heat of my shoulder. She does not hunt the air; she waits for the little blind ticks that creep out of the dry cambium at dusk. We do not touch, but we share the pocket. When the cloud-damp rises, she tucks her serrated arms beneath her breastplate and shivers, her chitin clicking softly against the heartwood. I know the rhythm of her shivering better than I know the face of my mother, whom the sky took when I had only three teeth.

Below me, thirty feet down the sheer drop of the burl, a blind weaver has slung its web. It is an old one; its belly is gray as sheep-fat and scarred from the kicks of falling divers. Sometimes, when a dead bird drops from the gallows-rakes and catches in its stays, the line hums with a low, musical note—a deep *thrummm* that enters the wood and passes straight through my pelvis. If the spider is slow to reach it, I know the beast is growing cold; soon it will fall, its eight legs curled tight like a fist, tumbling into the five-mile drop, and another will drag itself out of the damp rot-grooves to take the mooring.

I do not wonder what lies below the lavender mist. 

The young men with the rakes sometimes talk of it with their hands—clapping and clicking about stone floors, vast flats of silt, or cold seas where the water doesn't wait in knot-holes but lies flat and wide like an open hide. They are fools. If there were a floor, the things that fall would make a sound when they struck it. 

I have rested my jaw against this timber for forty seasons, listening through three leagues of petrified heartwood. I have heard the wind wear the knots smooth; I have heard the larvae dying in their tunnels; I have heard the roots of the arches—if they have roots—creaking against whatever props them in the deep, cold haze. 

There is no floor. There is only the wood, curving through the empty light until it forgets to curve, and the wind scouring it clean, and the birds falling forever without a thud.

The sun is dipping now toward the Western Arches. The pale wood is turning the color of dried apricot, then ash. The roar in the sky is changing key; the spine-winged martins are dropping into the cloud-banks to sleep, their screams thinning out until only the heavy, blubber-winged beetles are left to drone their iron chord across the gulfs.

The cloud is coming up. I smell it before I feel it—cold water, bitter liverwort, and the faint, sweet stink of rotting silk from the spider-stays below. 

I take the gannet-stone from between my teeth. I hold it in my palm for a moment, letting my spit cool on it in the rising draft. It is small. It is hard. It does not belong to this sky. 

Then I slide it back into the left cheek, settle my jaw against the groove of the thirty-thousandth growth ring, and close my eyes. Beneath my skull, the timber begins its evening contraction, whispering its long, dry secrets into the bone, while the wind takes the hair from my temples and blows it toward the nothing below.
