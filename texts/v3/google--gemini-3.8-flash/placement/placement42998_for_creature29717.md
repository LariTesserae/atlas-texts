I would not be out on the cedar poles, hanging like a dried bat over the three-hundred-yard drop. That belongs to those with light marrow and gristle in their knuckles. Nor would I be young enough to scramble the sheer masonry tiers during the three-hour lull, paring the yellow sulfur-briar before it turns to flint. 

If I am anywhere in this rift, I am seated three turns deep into the blind rock, where the white noon cannot throw even a splinter of its glare.

I find myself at the knuckle—the seam where the friable, sun-rotted limestone of the facade gives way to the cold, blue-white alabaster that has never seen a sky. My back is pressed flat against the stone, my shoulder blades settled into two shallow hollows worn smooth by thirty generations of bodies doing precisely this. 

My knees are drawn to my chin. My skin has the powdery, friction-free feel of a river pebble dried five years in an attic. I do not wear the selenite spectacles here; in this blackness, they are tucked into the hide pouch at my groin, wrapped in the soft hair of an owl-moth. Instead, my inner eyelids—the horn-colored membranes—are pulled half-shut, filtering nothing because there is nothing to see, but resting the pale amber irises from the memory of the light.

My work is not with the adze. My work is with the heat.

Between my bare feet sits the belly of a low earthenware retort, its rim sealed to a cedar goose-neck with a paste of wet talc and goat-spit. Underneath it, arranged in a tidy nest of chalk pebbles, are seven dry nodules of scale-mouse dung. They do not burn with a roar; they burn with a tiny, upright needle of clear azure fire that smells of old brass and hot vinegar. 

Inside the pot, the rock is weeping.

Every ten minutes, I reach into the darkness beside my left hip, take a fist-sized chunk of selenite—broken from the deep vein by the young carvers—and test its weight in my palm. You can tell how much water a crystal holds by its balance. A dry stone sits dead in the hand; a stone that still carries its ancient ocean has a slight, greasy coolness to the skin, an internal center of gravity that rolls toward the flesh. 

When the stone in the pot has finished sighing, I use two tongs of split juniper to lift the lid. A thick, bitter vapor rises, instantly coating my lips and nostrils in a film of sour chalk. I rake out the spent gypsum—now nothing more than a scalding, dead-white flour, so fine it does not fall so much as float—and drop in the new shard.

Then I wait for the copper pipe to tick.

*Tink.*

The drop falls from the cedar snout into a shallow bowl cut from green soapstone. It is heavy, oily water, so stiff with lime it barely makes a ripple. It takes three hours of azure flame to fill half a gourd. 

While the water cooks, I listen through my spine. 

I do not hear the wind with my ears; down here, forty spans behind the city’s face, the iron scream of the midday draft is converted by three million tons of rock into a slow, hydraulic pulse that throbs in the pelvic bone. It feels like being carried in the throat of an elk. It tells me everything happening in the hollow without my having to look upon the blinding terror of the light. 

When the downdraft begins to choke, I feel the stone expand. Limestone is an animal that breathes; under the violent chill of the midday gale it clenches, and when the air stalls at the sun’s zenith, the entire rift relaxes by the width of a fingernail. 

The vibration changes from a shriek to an ache.

*The slack-wind has come.*

Beside me, in the dry gravel of the floor, there is a rasp of tiny nails. A scale-mouse, plate-backed and dry as an almond shell, creeps against my ankle. It is warm from the fire. Its horn beak snips twice at the hem of my hide apron, searching for the salt my skin does not produce. I do not kick it; I reach down and feel along the margins of its belly plates for the hard, pea-sized nodules it has dropped during the wind-hours, rolling them into my palm like dried peas. A fair trade: my warmth for its blue fire.

Now, through the rock at my left ear, comes the tapping.

*Clack. Clack. Clack-clack.*

It is four tiers above, from the terrace of the cistern-carvers. The vibrations travel through the crystalline grain of the alabaster, sharp and dry: *The seam is widening toward the noon face. Bring the plaster.*

I rise. My hips make the sound of dry grass rubbing together. I scoop the boiling-hot white flour from the retort’s ash-lip into a sack of doubled linen, hoist it to my shoulder, and step toward the gray, milk-colored square that marks the tunnel mouth.

I do not go all the way out. I stop two paces back, in the vestibule where the air begins to smell of the scorched cinnamon of the sulfur-briars. 

I pull the selenite plates from my pouch and bind the gut-string tight behind my ears. Instantly, the world becomes a dream of milk and iron. 

Out on the narrow terrace, the light is an axe-stroke. I see the carvers, gray-white and angular, their hide aprons heavy with the dust of the lull, their adzes clicking against the lintels like the beaks of hundreds of starlings. They do not turn to look at me; to turn is to lose your footing on a ledge no wider than an adze-haft. 

I set the sack of hot plaster by the cedar lintel. An arm reaches around the jamb—corded, white-scurfed, the fingers ending in blunt, copper-stained nails—and drags the flour into the sun to mix with the bitter briar-water for sealing the new steps.

In exchange, a dry hand leaves a basket of parched locusts, charred and crushed with dried saffron pollen, and three more blocks of clear, cool selenite, freshly torn from the rib of the chasm.

We do not speak. To open the mouth here is to have the tongue dried to a scab before three words are out.

I crouch in the doorway, waiting. 

Far down in the belly of the cylinder, two hundred spans beneath our soles, the floor gives that long, dragging gasp. The furnace is clearing its throat. 

The air tastes suddenly of cold copper and lightning. The beard-grass twenty yards out begins its thin, paper-and-glass rattle. 

I watch through my milky crystals as the dwellers shake their aprons into the white air. It is magnificent, though none of them care to see it: a sudden, brilliant cataract of flour and yellow pollen falling into the throat of the void, hanging suspended for a single heartbeat in the dead air, before the hammer of the sky comes down.

*Whuff.*

The falling wall of wind catches the dust. The rift disappears in a blinding column of whirling pearl. The sound hits my breastbone like an iron spade, throwing me back on my haunches into the dark of the tunnel.

I draw the cedar pin. The door thuds shut against the white fury.

I take off the spectacles and lay them in the dust. The darkness returns, deep and smelling of stone-sweat and vinegar.

I sit back in the hollows my father’s fathers wore into the alabaster. I strike a scale-mouse pellet against a dry pyrites nodule. The blue flame leaps up, straight and steady, indifferent to the madness screaming fifty spans away. 

I drop the new selenite into the pot. 

I put my finger beneath the pipe to catch the first bitter drop of the stone’s blood, and I wait for the city to hum itself back to sleep.
