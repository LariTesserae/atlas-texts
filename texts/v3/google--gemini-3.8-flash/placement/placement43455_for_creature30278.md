At first, there is only the sensation of being very flat, and very cold, and held firmly on two sides by things that do not intend to let go.

Not trapped—trapped implies a struggle against an error, and here there are no errors, only tolerances so narrow they feel like purpose. 

Above me is the under-leaf of a carding-fern, pressed so close its embossed zinc ribs leave a faint, parallel fluting across what might be called my shoulders. Below me is the steel deck, matte and lilac, cold enough that if there were any moisture in me, I would be welded to it forever by a skin of frost. But there is no frost. There is only the grip of static potential: a clean, dry adherence, like a strip of gummed paper that has dried against a mirror.

I am not standing. In this world, to stand is to offer yourself to the six-mile fall of magnesium light, to become an antenna or an obstacle for the thirty-foot shuttles. I am horizontal. I am long, perhaps seven feet from the leading edge to the trailing points, but I have almost no thickness. If a keeper were to look directly down through the pin-holes of his horn mask, he would miss me entirely unless the light struck at that singular, glancing angle where the shadows fall toward the ceiling; then he might see an oblong shimmering, like a pane of mica laid over a blueprint.

My body is not a single piece. It is a lamination. 

There are twelve leaves to me—twelve paper-thin sheets of beaten boxwood veneer, interleaved with leaves of cold-hammered brass foil and sheets of stiff, oiled tracing vellum that have been rubbed with rottenstone until they are smooth as bone. We are held together not by pins or rivets, but by the pressure of our own motion. When I move, the leaves slide over one another by fractional millimeters, a dry, fanning shuffle that produces a faint *scritch-scritch*—the sound of an architect squaring a stack of drawings against a table.

I have no eyes to be blinded by the white noon. Instead, my edges are fringed with hundreds of split catgut whiskers, boiled in pine pitch until they are stiff as broom-straw. 

Through these whiskers, I do not see the plain; I feel its pitch.

A mile to the north, a wild-linen loom has completed its stroke. The impact of the shuttle against the buffer box does not come through the air—the air is too thin, too dead, smelling only of old lacquer and the chill of iron—but through the lilac plate. It enters my lowest brass leaf as a crisp, singing ring in the key of F-sharp. 

Three miles to the west, a quadrant drops. The piano wires in the sub-floor beneath me hum like a cello being de-tuned with a single wrench of the peg. 

My whiskers catch the vibration, sorting it: twelve links of chain have worn smooth in quadrant seven; an escapement in the mycorrhizal frieze has skipped three teeth; a kite has snagged on an ash gantry sixty cubits up, its capacitor tissue thrashing once before tearing free.

I am a feeler gauge. 

I did not build myself, nor was I born from an unthreaded bolt like the needle-punch beetles. I suspect I was once an inspection tool—a set of graduated shims left behind in the runners when the six-mile ceiling was first glazed, forgotten between the lilac plate and the root-mesh. Over ten thousand seasons of forty-day intake, the static charge of the basin has soaked into the boxwood and the foil, until the metal learned to creep, and the vellum learned to pull itself along the grooves.

My work is the clearance.

Every apparatus in the three thousand miles must have play. If the gears are cut too fine, the cold will seize them; if they are cut too loose, the reciprocating shears will chatter and chew the brittle mica-stems into useless dust. Between the moving brass roots of the spindle-grass and the static channels of the deck, there must always remain a gap of exactly one-sixty-fourth of an inch.

I live inside that sixty-fourth.

I move by shear. My bottom three leaves lock into the microscopic grain of the cold-rolled plate, clinging to its lilac oxide; my middle leaves lurch forward, driven by the charge that builds in the air; my top leaves follow, sliding like cards from the bottom of a deck. I advance an inch every four minutes, slipping between the turning gears and the floor, testing the gap.

Where the clearance is true, I pass through without resistance, leaving behind only a faint, silvered track where my foil has burnished the plate.

Where the clearance has tightened—where the weight of the overhead glass has bowed a gantry truss, or where the graphite grease has gathered into a dry, abrasive scab—I am pinched. 

It does not hurt; pain requires nerves, and I have only the friction of metal on wood. But when the pinch comes, the sliding of my leaves is arrested. The brass plates jam; the electrostatic charge, unable to discharge downward into the runner, begins to bank inside my vellum core. 

I grow hot. 

Not warm with blood, but hot like the core of a dry transformer. The pressed flax around me begins to give off its sharp, nutty smell. The amber stems of the liverwort beside me stiffen, their copper whiskers standing on end as they sense the sudden knot of potential.

Then, down the runner, comes the sound:

*Clack.*

A pause. The lilac plate rings like an anvil under my belly.

*Clack-clack.*

It is a keeper. He has felt the impedance through his boxwood boots five miles away. He does not know I am here; he knows only that at coordinate 400-North, the deck has lost its resonance, that the ring has gone dull, damped by something caught in the way.

He arrives without haste. Through my upper whiskers, I feel the displaced air of his oil-paper robes—that dry, papery *shudder-shudder* like a crow landing in a dry attic. His shadow does not fall over me—the glass sky permits no shadows—but his bulk blocks the magnesium light, cooling my top leaf by half a degree.

His long ash pole descends. The bone hook at its tip does not strike me; it finds the inspection lug of the gear-cradle an inch above my head. 

With a slow, lean-muscled heave—his arms are long and dry as peeled willow boughs—he levers the runner upward.

The sound is immense: a dry, reluctant *screee* of cold iron lifting from cold iron, clean as the opening of an ungreased vault.

The clearance widens. 

The pinch releases.

The stored charge in my vellum core rushes out of my tail in a single, pale-blue feather of silent fire that leaps across the six-inch gap to the keeper’s iron-banded heel. It does not stun him. He feels only the familiar prickle through his thick soles, a technical acknowledgment that the ground has cleared.

He lets the cradle fall back. The iron settles into its new register with a single, definitive *chink*.

He stamps once: *Clack.* 

The vibration passes through me, lifting my leaves for a fraction of a second so that I float upon the acoustic wave like a dead leaf on a pond, before settling back into the runner, aligned once more to the groove.

He turns and walks away. His boxwood soles beat out their syncopated measures into the distance, reporting the clearance to the empty horizon: *seven links true, five points clearance, zero moisture.*

I remain.

The forty-day intake is nearing its crest now. The light from the six-mile glass has taken on an incandescent, violet fringe that makes the edges of the carding-ferns look doubled, then tripled. Space is beginning to slip its registration. As I look forward through the amber thicket, the three bent drive-rods sixty yards away seem to occupy the same plane as the three rods three leagues beyond them; they slide through one another like drawings on overlapping sheets of tracing paper, perfectly aligned, impossible to separate.

My catgut whiskers are trembling now, not with vibration, but with the pressure of the sky. The voltage is so high that tiny, cold sparks—the size of sand grains—are beginning to hop along the ridges of my brass foil, clicking like lice.

Soon, the discharge will drop the quadrant. 

When it comes, the lilac plate will fall three inches in a heartbeat. The spindle-grass will freeze; the giant looms will halt their thirty-foot shuttles mid-stroke; the gaugers will fold their bone-china legs with that dry, linen rattle and press their prism-heads against the cold iron.

I will not fold. I am already as low as anything can be.

I will spread my twelve leaves as wide as their vellum tongues allow. I will slide myself completely into the mouth of the runner, beneath the central gear-train, where the lilac floor meets the foundation of the world. 

When the continental copper root-mesh fires its cold white fire from the southern docks, turning the entire three thousand miles into a single, ungrounded plate, the current will pass through me. My boxwood will sing; my brass will turn white-hot without melting; my vellum will become so translucent that the stamped serial numbers of the plate below will be readable through my flesh for forty miles around.

And then the shuttle will move six inches to the right. 

The whisper of the carding-ferns will resume—that vast, dry turning of a billion pages. 

And I will slide forward another inch into the zero-humidity noon, checking the margin between the iron and the leaf, making sure there is still enough emptiness left for the world to turn.
