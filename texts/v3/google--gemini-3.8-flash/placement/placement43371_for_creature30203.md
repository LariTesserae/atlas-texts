I would be on the causeway, six inches above the flour.

Not at its center, where the granite has been ground into a shallow, dish-shaped trough by forty generations of bare soles, but along its eastern lip—if east is the side where the cliffs are notched like broken teeth. 

At first, I might think I am a watcher, someone sitting cross-legged on a block of dressed basalt with a bundle of spiny twigs across my lap. But watching implies an eye that frames things, that separates the ruin from the bush and the bush from the bird. Here, the eye does not separate; it narrows. The heavy, overhang bone of my brow sits like an eave over my vision, keeping out the constant, sifted drizzle of mica-scales and pulverized lime that drops from the clouds of chalk-cutters wheeling above. What remains to be seen is blue-grey, flat, and shadowless, the eternal cellar-noon of the rift.

So I am not watching. I am listening with my bones.

My ears are dead, sealed shut before I had lived enough years to remember what an open voice sounded like. Inside my head there is only a round, vaulted quiet, like the interior of a cistern that has never held rain. But the soles of my feet—broad, cracked, yellowed like horn—are pressed flat against the stone, and through them the basin speaks in a continuous, shuddering chord.

I feel the birds first: a thin, high-frequency agitation that runs across the top sixteenth of an inch of the masonry, the frantic clicking of three million pairs of mica-tipped primaries slicing the air and beating against the upper pediments. It is a surface itch on the world. 

Far deeper is the crickets’ rasp. That does not travel through the causeway; it rises from the bedrock itself, four hundred feet of solid shale drinking the rhythm of their hind-spurs and passing it up through the foundations. It strikes the ball of my heel, climbs the dry marrow of my shins, and settles in the teeth of my lower jaw. It is a heavy, rhythmic churning, like a millstone turning without grain. When ten thousand of them strike together in the joint beneath the lintel of the fourth terrace, my ribs answer, vibrating in sympathetic chime like a cracked jar.

Between my knees lies the broom. 

The handle is a length of grey scrub-wood, hard as iron and polished smooth by the grease of human palms. The bristles are the horn-like spines of the low brush, bound with braided sinew stripped from the dried legs of swifts that fell into the courtyards before their plumage set. 

My work is very simple: I keep the line clear.

The wind here does not blow in gusts; it creeps, an imperceptible displacement caused by the cool air falling from the plateaus and sliding into the bottom of the trench. It carries the chalk-flour across the avenues in soft, slow-moving ripples, an inch an hour. If the causeway were left alone for three changes of the sky, the white drifts would rise to the knees, and to walk would mean dragging one’s shins through the dust, kicking up clouds that gum the eyes and dry the mouth to ash. 

So I push. 

I stand, the pasteboard skirt of beaten cork-bark rattling against my thighs like dry gourds, and lean into the broom. *Hiss. Hiss.* The sound does not exist in my ears, but I feel the resistance through the wood—the heavy, powdery drag of the feldspar yielding, tumbling in a white wave over the squared edge of the granite block, and falling into the ditch below.

There, just beneath the surface of the chalk-drift, a chevron of displacement appears. A ripple in the powder, three feet long, moving without a sound.

I stop. The broom rests against my instep. 

I wait until the skink breaks the crust. It rises like an arm from the flour, its polished scales gleaming the color of wet slate before the dust coats them white again. Its spade-head snaps sideways, taking a beetle that had tumbled from my broom-stroke, and then it sinks, its tail leaving a braided furrow that the dust fills before the skink has fully vanished. I feel the strike through the stone—a tiny, sharp *tick* against the causeway curb, like a pebble dropped into an empty cup.

I do not move for a long time after that. Movement here is expensive. Every breath takes a pinch of internal moisture and casts it against the warm labyrinth of the nasal bones; if you breathe hard, the sinuses dry until they crack, and the taste of blood stays in the back of the throat for days, sweet and salt, a terrible reminder of fluids that cannot be replaced. We move with the gait of the heavy grazers in the thicket: heel down, roll to the toe, pause, gather the air, advance.

Toward midday, when the indirect dusk brightens slightly—turning from the color of slate to the color of lead—the stone under my left heel jerks.

One. Two. A pause long enough for three breaths. Then a double strike, sharp and flat.

It comes from the north, where the causeway enters the great hypostyle that was buried when the high rampart slipped five hundred years ago. It is an old signal. It has the weight of an aged heel behind it, bone striking stone through a thin pad of horn.

*The pans are full.*

I turn my broom. I do not answer with my heel; the vibration from the north was tired, and to strike back would be to demand another effort from legs that are already stiffening into chalk. Instead, I walk toward the low-roofed quarter where my lintel sits three feet above the flour.

The door has no curtain. A curtain would rot or rattle. Inside, the ceiling is four multi-ton slabs of shale, planed so true that not a pin could pass between them, yet the joint is black with the bodies of the stridulators. They do not scatter when I enter; they are blind, and my movement does not touch the stone with the sharpness of a falling stone. 

Beneath them, on the swept flags, sit the wide pans of dark slate.

The harvest has been good. The night was cold, and the cold makes the carapaces brittle. The pans are covered with a two-inch layer of grey chaff: shed legs, porcelain-hard wing-cases, dried egg-pods that rattle like dry lentils, and here and there the whole, flattened disc of an orthopteran that lost its footing in the acoustic fury and fell to the floor.

I kneel. The skin of my knees makes a dry, sliding sound against the stone. 

With a wedge of slate, I scrape the meal into a bowl hollowed from a single granite pebble. My fingers are broad, short, the nails worn down to thick grey stubs by fifty years of handling stone and brush. I reach into the clay pot beside the hearth—the hearth that has never seen a fire—and take a handful of the dried seeds we gather from the thorn-scrub. They go into the mortar with the husks.

Then the stone pestle. 

The work is circular, slow, rhythmic. *Grind. Shift. Grind.* I feel the vibration in the meat of my palm, then in my wrist, then in my collarbone. The crickets in the roof above answer the mortar; their rasps shift slightly, falling into tempo with my hand, until the room itself seems to be breathing in time with the pestle. 

I add three drops of the thick, bitter curd we milk from the cistern-plants at dusk—white as lime, sticky as pitch, smelling of iron and ancient sap. The meal darkens. It gathers into a stiff, heavy dough that resists the fingers like wet clay. 

When it is pressed into flat cakes, each the size of a swift’s egg, I do not bake them. There is no wood to burn, and fire is a beast of the world above, an eater of air. I lay the cakes upon the window sill, where the dry, cellarlike draft draws the water out of them atom by atom, turning them into pale, dense biscuits that can be kept in a pouch of skin for a lifetime without souring.

I eat one. It tastes of dust, bitter bark, and the faint, oily salt of insect fat. It does not dissolve in the mouth; it must be held beneath the tongue until the slow saliva of the cheek softens its edge, then ground between the molars, which are flat and yellow as paving stones.

When the light begins to fail—when the blue slit of the sky four hundred meters above turns from lead to charcoal—I do not lie down on a bed. There are no furs, no grasses soft enough to sleep upon that would not crumble into powder in a month.

I sit against the rear wall, where the block of the house meets the foundation of the terrace behind it. I draw my knees up into the chest of my bark shirt, pull my hands into the stiff sleeves, and let my chin sink down until it rests upon the horn-plate of my collarbone.

Through the wall at my back, the city is still shouting. 

The birds have dropped into their holes, but their breathing remains: millions of tiny, dry lungs whistling through horn beaks, an ocean of air being drawn through feathers and cast out again. The crickets have picked up the night cadence, slower now, heavier, their spurs dragging across the granite with the sound of sledges pulled across gravel. 

And somewhere, four miles down the causeway, an old one has laid down their broom. 

I feel it. Not as a thought, but as a change in the weight of the street. A heel had been tapping there every forty paces since morning, a faint, rhythmic check against the stone that said: *the dust is moving; the way is open.*

Now there is only the crickets.

Tomorrow, before the flour drifts over the place where the heel stopped, someone will walk down the causeway with two cords of swift-sinew. They will find what is left: the bark smock already stiffening, the skin over the cheekbones already shrinking toward the bone, the eyes dried into little beads behind the shelf of the brow. 

They will not weep. We do not have water to spare for salt. 

They will take the body under the arms, their bare feet finding the purchase of the granite, and drag it down the stairs into the cellar vaults where the air never moves. They will lay it upon the basalt bench between the others who have stopped—the long row of grey figures whose skin has become like horn, whose hair is only a memory of dust, whose fingers are curled loosely around nothing.

And there it will stay, while the birds above fly until their wings wear down to the quills, and the crickets grind the corners off the lintels, and the white flour rises millimeter by millimeter against the walls of the city that has forgotten how to fall.

I close my eyes. Behind the lids, the world does not go dark; it stays the same leaden, cellarlike grey. 

My heart beats once, slow and thick as resin, against the stone at my back. 

The stone answers. It does not beat; it holds. And in that holding, I go still, until there is no difference between the man sitting against the wall and the wall that keeps the mountain from sliding down into the room.
