At first, there is only the pulse. 

It does not enter through the ears; it enters through the ball of the hip and the meat of the palms where they are pressed against flat iron. A slow, triple-beat shudder that travels five miles up from the dynamos through forty thousand tons of lattice before it reaches this plate: *shuv… shuv… clonk*. 

If you lay your cheek against the steel, it is warmer than a living throat.

I would not be on the open terraces. The upper decks are too wide, too bleached by the high-glare noon that eats the sight out of your eyes and sets the birds screaming in your hair. And I would not be down in the dark of the lower sumps, where the air is thick as wet felt and the dynamos turn in the grease-fog.

I would be in the blind spaces between. 

Specifically: the under-shelf of the seventh terrace, where the four-foot box-girders cross to support the weight of the ginger flumes above. It is a world turned upside down. Above my head is the corrugated belly of the deck, weeping lukewarm water from every seam; below my boots is three thousand feet of pale blue emptiness, sheared clean by the trade winds that whistle through the diagonals.

I am a walker, but an old one—one whose ribs have stayed narrow and whose arches have flattened until the yellow horn on the soles of my feet has grown around the lips of the T-irons like the hoof of a goat. My skin is the color of walnut oil, rubbed dull with soot and dried sulfur-crust, and I smell of boiled tallow and fern-mulch.

My work is the pins.

Where the great diagonal struts meet the vertical stanchions, there are hinge-pins thick as a man’s thigh, turned from nickel-steel before the memory of our grandfather-lines began. They carry the whole weight of the terrace—the black peat, the squash vines, the families sleeping in the hollow beams, the tons of creeping water. When the sun swings around the western crane-arms, the iron on this side grows hot and swells, while the iron on the shadow-side stays damp and contracts. The whole shelf shifts. It groans with the sound of a church organ played with lead weights.

The pins must slide. If a pin seizes in its collar, the bracket begins to tear. And if a bracket tears, the shelf tilts, the flume spills its guts into the void, and eighty yards of garden slide off into the white clouds below.

I carry a balance-pole—sixteen feet of hollow conduit with the copper wire stripped out, light enough to swing with two fingers—and an iron maul with a head no bigger than a lime. In a canvas pouch at my hip, sealed with bee-glue against the steam, I have grease: rancid, gray tallow rendered from the bellies of the lung-eels that die in the lower overflow screens.

I do not walk the decks. I walk the lower flanges of the stringers, four inches of wet, pitted iron suspended over the void. The wind here does not blow steadily; it breathes in great, cool gusts that smell of nothing—thin, mountain air that has never touched a tree—colliding with the hot, stinking up-draft of wet zinc and dynamo oil that pours from the ventilation grilles. You lean into the cold breath, then roll your shoulders as the heat hits you, your pole dipping and rising like the tail-feathers of a kestrel.

My eyes are small, squinting permanently against the reflection of the sky on the wet metal. I watch the rust.

Rust has voices. There is the dry, yellow rust that coats the high flanges where only the wind reaches; that rust is harmless, a scab that protects the bone. There is the greasy black scale that forms under the fern-mats; that is slow, a rot that takes generations to chew a rivet through. But then there is the weeping orange—the bright, sour tears that bleed from a joint under tension. 

When I find a pin that has stopped weeping grease and begun to weep orange, I step off the flange and hook my calves over the cross-brace. I hang there by the crook of my knees, upside down over the three-thousand-foot drop, the maul in my right hand, the grease-horn between my teeth.

The skinks do not move when I arrive. They are plastered along the steel beside the rivets, pale and flat as dropped dough, their throat-pouches fluttering as they drink the noon heat. They know the swing of my maul.

I tap the pin. *Ting.*

The note must be high. It must carry the bell-chime of iron that is whole, suspended in equilibrium between its mounts. If it thuds—if it goes *cluff*, like a spade hitting wet peat—the pin is binding. The shearing stress is biting into the steel.

I smear the lung-eel fat along the seam, where the plate meets the collar. The metal is so hot the tallow immediately liquefies, running into the hairline clearance with a soft, angry hiss that smells of fry-bread and old river. Then I take the maul by the short grip and strike the keeper-wedge three times.

*Chink. Chink. Tonk.*

On the third blow, the pin gives. It moves perhaps the thickness of a fingernail, settling into its new alignment as the afternoon expansion takes hold of the shelf. A shudder runs through the girder—a sudden, sharp jolt that knocks the limpets from their rings and makes the skinks scatter on their splayed pads. 

High above my head, on the sunlit promenade, the children herding the watercress probably don't even feel it. At most, the water in the zinc trough trembles, a circle of tiny ripples spreading across the surface of the flume before the slow current smooths them out.

I hang there for a while, waiting for the tallow to draw. 

My breath comes easy. At this height, people from the deep cells would starve for air in twenty paces, but our lungs have grown broad across the back, shaped to drink the thin mist and the boiler-gas without coughing.

From down here, looking out between the cross-braces, the horizon is a pale, curved line where the atmosphere thickens into white milk. Far off to the south, fifteen miles along the ridge of the superstructure, another column of steam rises from an exhaust stack, white and clean against the blue, looking like a chalk mark drawn on the sky. Between here and there are three hundred thousand trusses, two hundred terraces, and the green fringe of a million hanging gardens, all of it trembling to the same five-mile-deep thrum.

A corvid drops from the gantry above me, banking hard on greasy black wings. It doesn't scream; it makes that dropped-wrench *clong* in the back of its tongue, eyeing the tallow pouch at my side.

"Not for you, slick-tail," I mutter. My voice is rusted, thick from hours of silence.

It knows. It circles once, rides the hot thermal boiling out of an air-shaft, and rises back toward the light, where the grubs are thick in the squash beds.

I reach up, grip the damp lip of the flange with fingers that have no skin left to blister, and haul my hips up over the edge. I stand on the four-inch rail, shake the maul once to clear the grease from the face, and look along the beam to the next junction sixty yards out.

The sun is slipping toward the gantry horizon. The shadow of the upper crane-tracks is creeping across my feet, cold and blue. Soon the first contraction will come—that long, hollow *shudder-tonk* that sounds like the world cracking its knuckles.

There are four more pins on this shelf before the dark sets in. I set the pole across my shoulders, let my weight fall forward into the wind, and walk.
