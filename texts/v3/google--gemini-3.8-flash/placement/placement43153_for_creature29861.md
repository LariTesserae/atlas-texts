At first, there is the temptation to be the horn. 

To be the dried ibex gut petrified into mineral hardness, wedged back into the dry seam where the drafts cancel each other out; to be the cold witness, five hundred years old, keeping company with the amber-banded spiders and feeling the vibration of ten thousand rapid hearts transmit through the rock like an unbroken electric hum. It would be a safe perch, an archivist’s perch. You could watch the blue dawn filter thirty feet down into the vapor; you could smell the scald of ammonia without having your lungs pickled by it; you could watch the bodies peel away from the dome and drop into the throat of the chimney without ever having to risk the frost-shatter outside.

Or to be the floor. There is a strange, dim sentience down in that fermentation—the collective blindness of the crickets tasting the draft with antennae as long as fiddle-strings, or the dermestids whose entire universe is the measured conversion of fallen fat into lace-fine bone. To be the floor would be to never grow cold. It would be an eternity of digestion, warm, alkaline, and subterranean, utterly insulated from the sky.

But the mountain does not permit you to stay in the corners. The physics of this place pull everything toward the center of the heat, because the heat is the only currency that does not bankrupt you within the hour.

You feel it first as a thickening. The air is not empty space here; it has weight, moisture, grease. If you have an awareness, it cannot remain a disembodied eye for long, because the darkness is too dense for sight and the sound is too physical—it strikes the diaphragm, sets the inner ear humming, fills the sinuses with the smell of warm sheep-tallow and bruised sedge. You cannot float in this chamber; you have to hold onto something, or you will strike the mulch.

So your limbs find the stone.

Not the jagged stone of the ridge, but the rounded, tallow-slick troughs of the dome. And immediately, you are not alone on it. You are pinned. 

You have a weight—an unexpected, disproportionate heaviness in the chest. A keel so broad and flat that when you press your breast against the rock, your iron-black pectoral muscles feel like two warm stones laid against the slate. Your claws—four to a foot, short, curved like blue iron thorns—are already locked into an ancient groove worn by twenty thousand generations of your kind. If you tried to let go, the tendon mechanism would resist; you are built to hang without willing it, your weight ratcheting the grip tighter the deeper you sleep.

You are not an observer here. You are a cell in an organ that is running a perpetual fever.

Your snout is blunt, crushed flat to save its heat, tucked hard into the damp flank of the one hanging above you. You breathe her: the sour grass of the lower valley, the oily dust of seed-husks, the faint metallic sharpness of rime melting off her guard-hairs. Over your back lies the wing of another—a thick, oiled cape of skin and split bristles that covers your spine, trapping your own heat against your ribs until the skin beneath your down prickles with a prickly, suffocating warmth.

It is 108 degrees where you hang, four deep from the rock face. It is too hot. Your tongue is dry, coated with a thick film of seed-curd and salt scraped from the timberline rocks six hours ago. Your heart does not beat with a calm, mammalian stroke; it chatters. It is a tiny, frantic pump, racing at five hundred strokes a minute, burning the mountain-oils in your blood so fast that your flanks heave against the flanks of your neighbours in an irregular, rolling rhythm.

A restlessness takes you. You can’t stay in the deep tier; the air between the bodies is exhausted, depleted of oxygen, sour with carbon dioxide and the musk of the young. 

You loosen the grip of your left foot. You shoulder upward, driving your flat head between two damp, greasy bulks. They do not fight you, but they do not yield easily either; they grumble—a low, grinding rattle of denticles that sounds like river stones being rolled by a wave—and shift their bulk aside just enough to let you drag your weight through. 

You work your way toward the periphery, claw over shoulder, bristle against bristle. With every inch, the air changes. The grease thins slightly; the heat drops from a wet furnace to a sharp, steaming draft. 

Then you break the surface of the shingles.

The cool air strikes your face like a wet rag. The moisture condensing on the dome drips steadily here—a fat, lukewarm drop hits the bridge of your flattened nose, and your erectile nostrils flare, tasting the sudden, shocking thinness of the chimney’s draft. Above you, twenty feet through the boiling mist of breath, there is a streak of faint, freezing blue.

The light does not hurt your eyes—the cloudy membranes over your pupils diffuse it into a milky wash—but it registers in your brain like an electric shock. The dawn is thinning the gale outside. The barometric pressure inside the chimney has dropped a fraction of an inch; you can feel the void of the upper ridge pulling at the humid column of the cave, trying to suck the heat out through the cleft.

Beside you, a knot of three adults begins to tremble. It is not shivering from cold; it is the pre-flight seizure, the rapid uncoupling of the pectoral muscles to generate thirty degrees of internal heat before the drop. The rustle of flight-skins snapping taut sounds like dry parchment being crumpled in the dark.

Your crop is empty now. You fed the grey squab hours ago, vomiting the warm emulsion into its wide, soft beak until its fat little belly distended like an oiled bladder between your feet. There is nothing left in you but the hard, cold iron of your flight muscles and the need for grease.

You edge your way out onto the bare stone at the margin of the cluster. Your claws find the lip where the limestone drops away into the dark pit of the chimney. 

Beneath you, six feet down, the dermestids are rustling in the mulch. Above you, the blue slit turns the color of a wet slate shingle.

You do not think of the valley. You do not have a human memory of the yellow seed-meadows four thousand feet below, or the smell of wet pine needles, or the blinding sweep of the sky where the clouds lie like fleece across the lower ridges. You know only the blue thread above, the pull of the draft against your bristles, and the violent certainty that staying here means starving in the dark.

You let go.

For one second, you are nothing—just a stone falling through the ammonia-scented dark, heavy, clumsy, dropping like lead toward the mulched floor. The heat rushes past your ears; the chittering roar of the cluster falls away behind you.

Then, ten feet above the mulch, the rising column of the thermal catches the underside of your oiled membranes. 

You snap your wings wide—broad, dark, stiff as bark. The impact of the air against your chest is solid, like hitting a mattress. The upward draft takes your iron weight, swings your trajectory in a violent, ninety-degree arc, and slings you straight up into the throat of the cleft.

The light comes at you like a thrown spear. The humid warmth dies in the space of a single wingbeat.

Your nostrils snap shut against the ice. Your bristles flatten into a seamless, greased shell. You burst through the razor-slit of the peak into thirty-below zero, your wings biting the knife-thin gale with a dry, whip-like snap, diving headlong into the blue, empty fall of the mountain, already burning yourself alive to keep from freezing, already dreaming of the greasy dark where you will crawl back in to die or warm your blood against the flock.
