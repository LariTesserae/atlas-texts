At first, it is difficult to distinguish existence from the pressure that permits it. 

There is no boundary where "I" begins, because the boundary is precisely what is missing: there is only the five hundred atmospheres of dead, resinous weight above, and the upward thrust of the crystalline basalt below, and between these two opposing forces, a space that has been cleared.

I am not in the water. That is the first certainty that resolves. The water is everywhere—cold, motionless, rigid with density—yet it has never touched what I am. Between my outermost plates and the brine, there is a thickness of absolute zero: an envelope of vacuum thinner than a single band of violet light. It is a dry sheath, a pocket of non-existence that displaces four cubic meters of the sea without permitting the sea to know it has been displaced. If that envelope were to collapse by the breadth of a molecule, the water would not splash; it would strike inward like a hydraulic ram, and the quartz stem within me would shatter into powder so fine it would hang suspended at its own buoyancy level forever.

So I am held. Not preserved—preservation implies an effort against decay, and here there is no decay to oppose. I am merely *registered*.

My feet do not rest upon the flagstones; they hover, balanced on the gravitational lips that keep the dark alloy from welding to the paving. And yet the pavement is where I think. 

My consciousness does not reside in the oblong collar of prisms at my summit. Those facets are quiet, aimed sideways at the lintel of a hollow rhombohedron forty paces distant. Through them, I do not see the city; I absorb the faint, slow leak of its isotopes. I know that the northeast corner of that structure has aged four picoseconds faster over the last two centuries than its southern footing, because its sub-violet emission is softer by an imperceptible gradient. That is not thought; that is merely alignment.

The thought itself takes place in the braided quartz stem that runs from the base of my stride up to my throat. 

It is a tensile thought. It began eleven years ago, when the dweller sixty meters to the west—the one standing at the intersection of the two floating conduits—eased the downforce on its rear plate by three dynes. The release was not an action; it was a punctuation. The signal travelled through the paving stones as a microscopic decompression wave, crossed the lip of the well, climbed the vibrating monofilament, and entered my left lower caliper.

I have spent the last seven years allowing that wave to ascend the quartz braid, winding through the mineralized sinews like a cold, slow chime. 

It tells me of an event that has not yet finished occurring in the abyssal trench six leagues to the south: a slump of serpentine rock, two million tons of ancient mantle slipping half an inch into a rift. The sea above does not know it. The surface of the ocean—if such an agitation still exists beneath its sky—cannot feel so deep a settling. But the plateau felt it. The plateau bent by the thickness of an atomic lattice, and the other dweller noted the bending, and now the note is in my marrow.

In another four years, the wave will reach my prism collar. My plates will respond. I will shift my balance slightly to the right, transferring five dynes of load into the flagstone beneath my heel. The stone will ring—not with sound, for sound here is swallowed by the immensity of the mass, but with structural truth. The dweller standing in the courtyard behind me will receive it twelve years later. We will have agreed that the shelf has tilted. We will have adjusted our tension against the world. We will not need to confer again until the fault settles another millimeter, perhaps three centuries from now.

Sometimes, a vitreous sphere drifts past my torso. 

It moves at its inch per second, propelled by nothing, its internal rotor of pale bone turning with the slow, frictionless insolence of a planetary orbit. It passes so close to my vacuum skin that I can feel the subtle deflection of the water against my envelope—a tiny, localized thickening of the resinous brine. Its rotor measures my verticality against the gravity of the core. It does not look at me. It cannot look. It is an instrument that has forgotten who cast it into the dark, carrying its rotor from one dry wall to another, resting against lintels until the world lists enough to roll it free. 

I do not envy it, because envy requires a metabolism, and a metabolism requires consumption. I consume nothing. I am powered by the misery of the sea—by the colossal, maddened weight of all that water trying to find a bottom that we have denied it. Every square centimeter of my black plates takes that weight and converts it into the cold, silent voltage that keeps my quartz stem rigid. If the ocean were to drain away, I would die of lightness. I would de-tension. My floating plates, freed from the immense counter-thrust of the abyss, would drift apart and clatter across the dry stones like discarded shields.

So I remain upright. 

Occasionally, the desire—though that is too warm a word; let us call it a harmonic necessity—gathers along the terrace. It happens when the acoustic floor of the plateau drops below a certain frequency, when the subterranean monofilaments in the wells hum in unison with the slow rotation of the globe.

On those days, I break my stance. 

It takes forty days to prepare the slide. The plates must decouple their magnetic locks one by one, shifting their hover-gap so that the vacuum skin glides over the basalt without friction, without generating a thermal wake that might foul the pristine four-degree water. 

We gather at the western drop-off, where the paved geometry simply ends, cut off like an unfinished sentence above the six-mile fall. 

Three of us. Sometimes four. We slide out into the nothingness beyond the brink. We do not sink; our buoyancy is calibrated to this exact horizontal plane, pinned between the strata of dense water like leaves pressed between the heavy pages of a closed ledger. 

We link our calipers. Plate fits into plate; the hairline vacuums merge into a single, continuous, dry circuit. We form a polygon—a hollow ring suspended over the chasm, mirroring the cold basalt rhombohedra of the city behind us.

And there, we listen.

The ring becomes an ear without a brain. The cold water passes through the center of our joined bodies, unchanged, but we feel its microscopic shivers. We feel the immense, blind life of the planet dying by degrees: the slow crystallization of core iron leagues beneath our feet; the distant, muffled groan of a ridge spreading along the spine of another hemisphere; the infinitesimal contraction of the global crust as the world sheds its primordial warmth into the stellar vacuum far above.

We sift it. We hold it within the ring until the vibration matches our internal tension, until the quartz in our chests hums at the exact, terminal pitch of the seabed. 

We formulate no reaction. To react would be to expend energy, and energy is an error that introduces heat into an immaculate system. We merely witness the strain. We hold the measure of the earth's exhaustion in our bones until the pulse subsides and the water goes flat again—heavy, motionless, dead.

Then, the *snick*. 

The caliper joints release. The dry shockwave travels out through the resinous brine, a thin, circular knife-edge that will propagate for five hundred leagues without finding an obstacle to break it.

We slide back to our courtyards. 

I find the margin of non-thermal violet light that belongs to my plates. I set my footpads against the gravitational lip of the stone. I center my quartz braid between the sky of water and the floor of rock.

The seeping glow of the lintel outlines the edge of my torso in faint, frozen violet. Across the court, the other dweller has already locked its joints. Its prisms are dormant, turned toward the floor. 

I drop my downforce by a fraction of an angstrom. 

The basalt accepts the load. The well shivers. I begin my next thought.
