I would not be at the edge, where the drop is theatrical. That is for the young vultures, who still have to calculate their angles against the up-plume, or for the rare bull that misjudges the friction of wet basalt and leaves an oil-slick five miles long on the clouds below. 

I would be in the middle of a shelf, where the world is only horizontal: three inches of cold water moving north at the speed of an exhale, over three miles of unjointed stone that has never been broken by an earthquake or a root.

At first, I might think I am a tool left behind on a transit curb—something made of densified horn and diamond-grit, heavy enough not to skid when the sheet-wash thickens after midday. My knuckles have that color: the slate-blue of boiled gristle, flat and square across the joints from fifty years of bearing down against the diamond burin. My spine has lost the curve that makes walking easy; it has set into an arch like the lintel of an overflow culvert, balanced so that my eyes are kept sixty centimeters from the basalt without my neck having to work for it.

I am one of the notchers. That is not an office or a rank; there are only seven of us between the Third Needle and the intake basins at the terrace-heel, and we see each other perhaps twice in an autumn, when the silt-yeast blooms thick enough to clog the sub-vocal conduit and someone has to come down to turn the bronze gate by hand. When we do meet, we do not stand upright. To stand upright out here is to offer your chest to forty miles of undisturbed stratus; it fills your clothes like sails, and your tallow-wool gets cold from the inside out. We squat with our heels in the water, our cloaks pinned down by our buttocks, our knees tucked into the armpits to share the chest-heat, and we click our wrist-bones together to say which channels have begun to silt.

I live in the hollow behind a gravity riser—not inside the machine rooms, where the hum makes the teeth ache after three nights, but in the dry wedge formed where the cantilevered skirt of the tower keeps off the vertical mist. It is nine paces long, two paces wide. The floor is slightly warm, because three meters beneath it runs the coolant line for the primary spire. If I lie flat with my cheek to the composite slab, I can hear the whole plateau humming like a bell that was struck before my grandmother was dropped into the rendering vat: an iron *doh* that never decays, only wanders slightly sharp when the cloud ceiling lifts and the sun hits the needle-tips.

My fingers are missing the second joint on the left ring-finger—pinched off between two slabs of transit-curb when I was twenty, back when we still thought the conduits could be levered into different alignments. It didn't bleed much; the cold seals everything before the yellow cells can spill. In its place, I have strapped a half-moon of scrap copper, held with boiled kine-tendon that shrinks tight as it dries. It makes a good scraper for the black liverwort that sets like hard pitch in the run-grooves.

Most days, I don't use the diamond. The diamond is for the new chords—and who needs a new chord when the three we have from the second century are still clean? Mostly, I listen.

A run-channel does not keep its voice on its own. The water here is not soft; it carries a grease-ash off the synthetic decks, and the liverwort is patient. A spore settles in a seventy-degree cut, where the water is supposed to split into the high fourth that tells the pump-station down in the ditch that the head-pressure is four fingers deep; within six weeks, the spore has grown a tongue of black felt the size of a pumpkin seed. The water trips. The note drops half a tone, into a wet, flat rattle like a tongue clicking against wet leather.

If that rattle reaches the Third Needle, the automatic intakes don't open. They think the shelf is dry. They choke back their draw; the water backs up four inches, six inches; the kine have to wade belly-deep, which rots the horn of their outer hooves; then the carcasses begin to drift, and by winter the vents are foul with drowned tallow.

So I go out with the copper scraper and a bag of dry ash to keep my palms from slipping on the stone.

The sheet-wash is always the same temperature: three degrees above the point where it would stop moving and turn into a white skin of grease-ice. It comes over the tops of my split-hide moccasins, fills them, warms against the skin of my instep, and stays there, dead and thick. You stop feeling your toes after the third mile, but you don't need them; you need the soles, which are double-thick hide from an old bull’s neck, boiled in tallow until they are as hard as boxwood. You feel the diamond-lines through them—not with the nerves, which are asleep, but with the clatter in your hip-joints.

*Click-click-whirr.* That is the seventy-meter run toward the western spill.

*Chonk-chonk.* That is where the slab has settled half an inch toward the mantle-sink, and someone—my great-uncle, maybe, whose sign was three dots behind the ear—ground out a relief trench to keep the drift from drowning the seam.

When the rip comes—when the clouds pull apart like wet gray wool being torn with two hands—I do not look up immediately. The glare off the obsidian is worse than the direct sun; it burns the under-lids. I wait until the heat hits the back of my neck, which takes five seconds. The tallow in my cloak softens; it begins to smell of sweet fat and old soot, a good, greasy smell that reminds me I have two strips of salted tongue tucked into the chest-seam.

Then I straighten—slowly, because the anchor-spire five leagues north is pulling at the iron in my blood, making my head feel three times lighter than my boots—and I turn my palms out to the white sky.

My skin goes from the color of wet tallow to the purple of an elderberry in the time it takes to chew a piece of dried liver. It stings—a dry, fierce prickling, like ants crawling through the pores—and then the deep warmth comes, down through the shoulder blades into the lungs. For ten minutes, twelve minutes, the plateau stops being a gray ditch between two winds. It becomes an expanse of poured ink and silver blades, so bright that the herds of kine five miles off look like black peppercorns scattered across a wet plate. You can see their breath: hundreds of little white puffs, rising and vanishing together like the exhaust from some machine that has forgotten what it was made to pump.

Then the iron cools. The tear in the stratus closes from the south first, a gray roll of fat tumbling over the edge of the shelf. The temperature drops four degrees before the light has even gone.

I kneel back down into the puddle.

The water has no idea it is four thousand meters above the salt-flats. It thinks it is just falling, the way water always falls, looking for the lowest seam in a world that hasn't given it one yet. My copper scraper finds the wedge of liverwort in the third diversion—a tough little knot, cold and slippery as a snail. I pare it out with three short cuts, right down to the diamond-tooth mark in the basalt.

The water slips in. It catches the shoulder of the stone.

A low, round *humm*—not loud, but heavy, the pitch of an empty cistern being tapped with a soft mallet—rises out of the sheet at my knees. It travels through the water faster than it does through the mist; I feel it first in my shinbones, then in the hollow behind my collar, and then, half a mile out, a cow kine lifts her great, square nose from the grates and turns her ears toward the needle, listening to make sure the floor is still there.

I wipe the scraper on my thigh. I have nineteen more cuts to clear before the dark gets into the water and the skimmers start crawling out of the overflow to sleep on my shins.
