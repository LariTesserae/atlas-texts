At first, there is no sense of having a body that occupies space in the way air or trees do. There is only an intense, inescapable awareness of *clamping*.

I am pressed. 

To be here is to realize that three dimensions was always an arrogance, an unstable experiment that the earth permitted for a few million years before growing tired of the leverage. Here, gravity and the horizontal have made peace. If you raise your head, you invite the sky to strike you down; if you burrow, the shield rock refuses you after three millimeters. And so the world is four inches tall, warm as blood, and endlessly wide.

I would find myself in the slip-space. Not the deep cracks where the blind caecilians dream of glaciers, and not up on the dried parchment reeds where the cicadas burn their short lives into high-frequency grease. 

I am at the bottom of the sheet, where the water meets the stone.

***

My underside has no memory of scales. It is pale, soft as wet kidskin, and bathed in a slick, stubborn mucus that I exude continuously—a grease so dense that it does not wash away in the tea-colored current, but creates an oiled seal between my belly and the Precambrian floor. Beneath me, the granite is not smooth like polished marble; it has a fine, microscopic tooth. I feel every crystal: the cold, glassy edges of quartz, the dull crumble of altered feldspar, the slick, greasy planes of hornblende. 

I do not have a neck. To turn is to swing the whole plate of my body—eighteen inches of broad, leathery disc, flattened until my organs are laid out like maps upon a table. 

My eyes are two dark, wet currants set side-by-side upon the flat roof of my skull, staring directly upward through four inches of sun-warmed glass. I do not see landscapes; I see the double-white ceiling. I see the glare of the milky sky, and across it, the drifting shadows of water-striders—four dimpled coins of darkness skimming the surface tension, sliding across my field of view like blown leaves. 

And the sound. 

You cannot separate hearing from being touched here. My body is an acoustic plate clamped against a continent that never stops ringing. 

Through my dorsal skin, through the water that bathes my back, comes the screaming machine-drone of the cicadas—a white, dry sizzle that feels like hot needles dancing on my armor. But through my belly, straight out of the basement granite, comes the heavy, seismic heartbeat of the plate-frogs. It arrives as a shove: *thrumm... thrumm...* It lifts my ribless flanks by fractions of a hair, rattling the small calcified stones in my inner ear. The continent is vibrating like an iron gong struck softly five hundred leagues away, and my flesh is simply the damp cloth laid upon it to muffle the ringing.

***

I am hungry with the patient, cold hunger of things whose blood is the exact temperature of the puddle they lie in.

My mouth is beneath me, a horseshoe rasp set back from my blunt snout. I hitch forward on my splayed pectoral fins—not fins, really, but stubby, muscular spades tipped with blunt pads of horn. *Shluck.* The mucus breaks its seal, slides two inches over the pink rock, and seals again. *Shluck.* 

I plane the crust. My rasps shear away the iron-oxidizing slime, the bitter, metallic pelt of cyanobacteria that coats the stone like dried blood. It tastes of wet rust, ancient rain, and the faintly sweet sulfur of diatoms. It is not rich food, but there are ten thousand square miles of it, and nothing ever hurries me. The current—that continent-wide seepage—tugs faintly at my perimeter, but my disc is shaped to deflect it over my back, pushing me down, anchoring me harder against the floor. 

The danger is not the water. The danger is the saddles.

Eventually, the basin I am in shallows. The granite pavement curves upward in a great, whale-backed swell. The tea-colored water thins from four inches to one, then to a skin, then to nothing: a dry, red ridge of sun-baked rock, six paces wide, separating my pool from the next shimmering expanse of sheet-wash.

I lie in the shallows at the margin, my eyes breaking the surface film for the first time. 

The air is violently dry compared to the pool, thick with the smell of scorched iron and the deafening roar of the reeds. Above me, the glare is terrifying. There are no shadows, only an overwhelming, flat luminescence that erases distance. 

I wait. My gills pull the last oxygen from the warm film behind me; my primitive vascular lung—a spongy sac beneath my dorsal plate—draws in a slow, wet gulp of the heavy air. 

Then, I heave.

Out of the water, I weigh five times what I should. My soft belly drags over the dry stone with a wet, tearing hiss. My pectoral pads dig into the quartz grains, levering my wide, leathery body up the gentle slope. The milky sun hits my wet back instantly, baking the mucus into a tight, glossy varnish. If I stall here, the heat will cook my liver beneath my hide before the afternoon leans toward dusk. 

*Shluck. Scratch. Shluck.*

Three paces up. Three paces to the crest.

And then, above me, a pale shape crosses the white sky. 

It does not drop out of nowhere; it has been standing there for two hours, motionless as a bleached reed, but my upward-turned eyes only now resolve the two impossibly long, chalky stilts twenty feet away. 

A salt-needle.

I freeze. I flatten my perimeter until the edges of my disc are flush against the stone, offering no silhouette, no shadow. I am a circle of liver-colored rock on a slab of liver-colored rock, varnished with my own drying slime. 

Through the granite, I feel the bird's foot shift. One single, dry vibration—the click of a hard claw on feldspar.

Its lower eyelid, thick and milky, slides down over its eye, cutting the upward glare of the rock. It is looking for roundness. It is looking for the small, domed lens of an eye, or the dark arc of a gill-slit. 

I do not breathe. The air in my lung burns with the sour heat of an oven. The insect roar overhead is so loud it seems as though the sky itself is tearing along a seam. 

A second passes, measured by three slow beats of a distant plate-frog beneath my ribs. 

Then, the bird steps away. Its long, jointed leg lifts with the glacial, agonizing patience of the deep earth, breaking no water, casting no sudden shadow. It turns its white beak toward a distant sandbar, convinced that the red disc on the stone is merely another knot of iron ore that the weather forgot to dissolve five hundred million years ago.

***

I let out my breath—a tiny, wet puff against the hot stone—and lurch forward. 

The crest falls away. My front pads slip into the shallows of the next basin. The cool tea-water rushes over my parched head, drowning the white hiss of the air, restoring the muffled, comfortable bass of the world below. The mucus on my back softens, dissolves, and blooms outward in a faint, milky halo. 

My body settles into the four-inch universe once more. I am flat. I am hidden. The tea-colored water slides over my back, on its way to a sea that has no shore, moving at the speed of a drifting leaf.

I press my chin to the pink feldspar, open my rasps, and begin to scrape the ancient crust again, keeping time with the rock that never moves, beneath the sky that never leaves.
