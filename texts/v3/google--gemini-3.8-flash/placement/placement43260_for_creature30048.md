At first, you look for a perch that feels familiar—a doorway, a sill, some place where a human silhouette might lean to take in a square. You look for an vantage point, because that is the reflex of a mind arriving in an expanse: to establish a horizon, set a back against a post, and watch.

But here, a sill is not an edge to lean on. It is an active pore. The wood under your palms does not hold still; it is coursing with that cold, pressurized broth, and if you press your thumbs into the pale green cambium, you feel the grain twitching beneath your skin, aligning its micro-tubules like hair caught in an electrical draft.

So you are not a traveler on a balcony. You cannot be an onlooker here; there is no ambient middle-ground between the mechanism and its material. Everything that is not engine is load.

Where the ear goes first is into the teeth. In this precinct, hearing is an internal percussion: the jet-whistle of the corner trunks vibrating the jawbone until the molars ache against each other in their gums. To survive that sound without your skull coming unstitched, you have to find a place where the frequency cancels out—one of the dead nodes where the hydraulic roar of the perimeter collides with the screech of the anchored, vertebra-shaped creatures suspended in the center.

There are such places, but they are not rooms. They are seams.

I find myself not down in the cambium bowl, where the weight would crack my ankles, nor among the sideways-walkers on the fourth tier, whose onion-skin sheaths are too light, too papery to hold a red-blooded heat. 

Instead, I am caught behind the false front—not inside the bedchambers where the nurses sweat their sweet grease, but in the interstitial dead space between the facade’s outer relief and the thick structural trunks that carry the sap. 

It is a narrow vertical flue, no wider than the spread of two elbows. The air in here does not move with the sun. It smells of damp starch, vinegar, and the iron smell of sap running behind bark so thick it feels like warm slate. 

At first, I think I am an intruder wedged into the architecture’s ribbing, holding my breath so the leather-backed crawlers don't graze my knees as they pass through the conduit-slots. I think of myself as someone hiding. I test my fingers: they are still fingers, five to each hand, jointed with bone rather than braided ligament. I have eyes, though they are useless in this slit; daylight only enters as pale amber slits where the mock-clapboards have pulled slightly apart under the summer heat.

Yet my posture is wrong for an intruder. I am not crouched or huddled. 

My spine is straightened against a vertical beam, pressed flat against a braided column of lignum that has grown with an indentation precisely the width of my shoulder blades. The indentation is not carved; it has the gentle, calloused lip of a tree that has grown around an iron fence-rail over forty seasons. The wood knows the measure of my ribs. When the sap-slugs hammer through the pipe behind me, the shock does not knock me loose—it drives directly into my thoracic vertebrae, rattling through my sternum like a train crossing a trestle, but holding me pinned, upright, plumb.

I am not a citizen who arrived here. I am an *inclusion*.

Like a pebble taken up by an oyster or a nail swallowed by an oak, the architecture has grown around me to resolve an irregularity in its own grain. It needed a counter-weight, a hinge, or perhaps simply a foreign density to interrupt a shear-stress it could not calculate with wood alone.

My heels rest on a small, grown bracket. My arms are extended slightly, palms turned outward, resting in two shallow troughs where the cambium has wept a thick, rubbery pitch that has set around my wrists like soft cuffs. It does not hurt; it feels cool, like river mud that has begun to dry in the sun. It siphons heat from my forearms, drinking the warmth of my blood and using it—I can feel this through the grain—to temper the sap that feeds the eight or nine pseudo-alphabets carved into the lintel just on the other side of the wall.

Those letters: they don't mean anything to the onion-skinned biped out there scraping gray paste into the balustrade. To that creature, the letters are simply a structural texture that must not be allowed to blur into a burl.

But the wood is trying to spell something it felt through my skin.

Every several minutes, when the deep bedrock groans and the court jerks four inches to the east to catch the shifting sun, the flue around me tightens. The walls breathe in. The air squeezes my lungs until my breath is forced out through my teeth in a thin, whistling gasp that matches the pitch of the invisible, anchored fauna out in the bright bowl.

In that moment of rotation, through the gaps in the timber, I catch sight of the square:
The wide, vacant floor of amber cambium.
The disc-shaped weigbers creeping from their dish of oil, iron-heavy and ancient.
The oily birds diving from the loggias like soot shaken from a chimney.

And for three seconds, as the shadow clicks into its new notch, the pressure drops. The jet-scream falls to a heavy, resonant hum that vibrates in my stomach like a cathedral organ clearing its lowest pipes.

In that quiet window, the sap slows. The amber nodules in the wall before me clear, turning from murky syrup to glass.

Through them, I can see the back of a lateral stair twenty paces off. One of the onion-skinned things is standing there, its spade-feet sealed to the sheer drop, its narrow, eyeless spindle of a head turned toward my wall. It does not know I am here—it has no eyes to peer through the amber slits. 

Yet it raises its knee and strikes the timber. *Tock.*

The vibration travels down the joist. It bypasses the wood’s own vascular bundles, routes through the grown bracket under my heels, climbs the bone of my left tibia, crosses my pelvis, and registers in the hollow of my throat as a dry, clicking impulse.

It is asking for the tension. It is waiting for the sign that the facade is holding its shape, that the town has not slipped, that the rooms still look like places where a creature with joints, eyes, and lungs might one day wake, wash its face in a monobloc basin, and walk down to the square to speak to another of its kind.

My throat tightens against the wood. Without my choosing it, my chest expands against the lignum cradle. My ribcage flexes, bone striking the living joist from within the wall.

*Tock.*

The spindle-thing on the stair receives the tap through its chest, relaxes its ligament-wound joints with a sound like oiled harness leather, and reaches back into its tray of chewed fiber to resume the repair of the window frame.

The floor below slides shut with the sound of grinding mills. The pressure rises. The scream climbs back into the jet-registers, blinding and complete.

I close my eyes. The pitch sets around my wrists a fraction of a millimeter tighter. The sap rushes behind my shoulder blades, fast, cold, and vinegar-sour, carrying the daylight upward into the rafters where the city keeps pretending it was built by hands.
