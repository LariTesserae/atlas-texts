It begins not as a shape, but as a pressure against the underside.

There is no sense in looking for edges when the dark is this thick and warm. Everything here is an interior, even the open air, which has the weight of wet flannel laid across the throat. So you do not start by asking *what am I shaped like?* You start by asking where the weight is coming from, and where it goes when the floor heaves.

The basalt does not stay still. It shivers in two distinct frequencies: the low, churning gut-rumble of the flumes tunneling deep in the root-rock, and the sharp, rhythmic iron-shriek of a crosshead bearing three hundred yards off that has run out of grease and is cutting its own journal. You feel them through different parts of your mass. The deep water-thrum enters low, through whatever spreads widest against the wet stone; the iron-scream takes you higher up, behind the cartilage arches where the fluid sits in bags.

At first, you might think you are one of the things wedged between the cooling fins. The heat is right for it—that tallowy, boiled-onion warmth that bleeds off the cylinder heads. It would be easy to be that: a broad, oil-filled weight, blunt-snouted, chewing the rust-fur with a slow, planetary jaw, deaf to everything except the piston-chuckle. There is an enormous peace in being five tons of soaked gristle cemented to an intake pipe by your own dried mucus, waiting forty years for a single valve to seat properly.

But there is an itch in you that the grazers do not have. The grazers don't flinch when the field reverses.

You feel the power lines before the mist does. When the dynamos out on the shelf surge—when some subterranean weir drops its gate and sends a wall of boiling water through the turbine runners—the cables overhead don't just hum; they stiffen. The sagging catenaries lift two inches through the fog like drawing bows. When that happens, your mantle twitches along its seams. A bitter, copper taste fills the back of your breathing vents, bright as a tongue laid against both poles of a dry cell.

So you are not merely rooted. You have a share in the drift.

Perhaps, then, you are out in the wet space between the pylons. You test the idea: thin as a butcher’s apron, four meters of greased membrane floating through the warm, saturated drop, feeling the invisible magnetic pipes that arc from tower to tower. It would be a fine thing to have no skeleton to speak of, only struts of horn that bend when the drafts roll up off the boiler pits. To hang blind in the sulfur plumes, your belly-pores tingling as you slide down an isobar of ionized vapor, dropping down onto the black conduits to suck the snails out of their scalded shells.

Yet the gliders are too light. They have no memory of the stone. When the heavy sternum-strikes go out—when an old bull six leagues south hits the basalt with his breastplate to say the mud-shelf has slipped—the gliders don't even turn their heads. The sound passes straight through their thin bellies and dissolves into the rain.

No. You need the sound. You are built around it.

Let the image settle deeper, past the skin, into whatever does the work of holding you together.

You find yourself not on the open rock, and not up in the magnetic channels, but in the wet sleeve where the two things meet: the collar.

Every installation on this plateau—every boiler-house, every hydraulic accumulator three storeys tall—is held to the planet by anchor studs. Massive, fluted pins of cold-rolled iron, four feet through, driven sixty feet into the basalt to keep the engine from shaking itself off its ledge into the gulf. The rock does not like the iron; the iron does not like the rock. In the gap between them, where the vibration churns the seepage into a gray, frothing lard of basalt-dust, machine-oil, and pulverized fern-pulp, there has to be something that does not shear.

That is where the body makes sense.

You are wedged into the ring. You are neither the machine nor the shelf, but the living packing between them.

Your form has taken the shape of the tolerance. You are thick-bodied, flattened, crescent-curved to hug the circumference of an eight-foot foundation bolt. Your hide is not wet wood or chilled lard; it is layered like horn-shingles soaked in paraffin, dense enough that the six-thousand-pound pneumatic thrust of the piston stroke doesn't rupture your heart-sacs, but soft enough to yield half an inch when the bolt torques under load.

You have limbs, but they are not for walking; they are broad, ridged wedges, like the keys of a vault door, driven deep into the micro-fissures of the basalt. You do not move by stepping. You move by hydraulic creep, swelling one segment with lymph while venting another, sliding three inches a season along the circumference of the iron footings, following the hottest run of condensation.

Your head—if it can be called that—is pressed permanently against the rusted shoulder of the casting. You have no eyes, no nostrils, no need for the whistling throat of the vent-ticks. Your face is an acoustic diaphragm six spans across, a saucer of dense, fluid-filled bone backed with sensory nodes that register every microscopic fracture in the anchor bolt.

You are the listener of the joint.

Through your left flank comes the life of the machine: the slide of the phosphor-bronze shoes, the click of the valve-cages dropping onto their seats, the faint, high whistle where a copper gasket has blown out behind the secondary pump. You know the exact hour when the oil-sump beneath the crank is running low on tallow, because the vibration changes from a greasy slide to a sharp, dry chatter that sets the marrow in your anchor-claws aching.

Through your right flank comes the life of the highland: the slow, water-logged settling of the peat-mats; the five-stroke stamping cadence of the herds three miles off, warning of a bank-collapse; the endless, hissing churn of the ribbon-worms dissolving the belly of a dead bull down in the flume-mouth; the wet, tearing thud of a glider that lost the magnetic lead in a downdraft and broke itself against the boiler wall.

You eat what drains down the bolt. It is an extraordinary broth: warm rainwater that has filtered through three hundred feet of sulfur-fern garden, picking up the tannic acids of the rot, thickened by the black, paraffin-heavy cylinder oil that weeps past the piston-packings, and seeded with the iron-reducing bacteria that coat the metal in a greasy, orange fleece. Your mouthparts are wide, flat plates of horn that do not bite; they oscillate against the metal, a slow, micro-fine rasping that shears the slime away without ever dulling against the rust.

You taste the iron aging. It has a cold, sour flavor when it is sound; when it begins to fatigue, when the crystalline structure inside the stud starts to loosen under the millionth stroke of the crosshead, the metal sweats a different salt—a sharp, alkaline sting that wakes your nerve-rings like fire.

When that happens, you do not leave. Where would you go? The highland is big, but the bolts are few, and you have been four hundred years growing your shell into the precise curve of this flange.

Instead, you swell. You draw in the heavy mineral water through the lateral pores along your belly; you draw the dissolved iron from the engine’s sweat into your blood; you pack the secret chambers of your carapace with dense, fibrous deposits of siderite and bone-paste. You become denser than the basalt. You press your forehead into the shearing seam, locking your shingle-plates against the washer, wedging your ribs into the gap where the steel has begun to wander.

You take the stroke into yourself.

*Ch-clank. Sigh. Thud.*

The piston comes down. Three thousand tons of steam-shove behind it. The anchor bolt stretches half a hair’s breadth; the basalt groans, trying to shed the load into the peat; and between them, your body flattens, drinks the shock, converts the terror of that mechanical violence into heat, and vents it back out into the warm, roaring fog as a thin, bubbling trickle of grease.

You will stay until the iron gives or the stone gives. In the blackness that never broke for sun or star, in the multi-tonal thunder of water falling from nowhere into nowhere, that is enough of an identity: to be the thing that keeps the engine from flying into the chasm, and the stone from forgetting how to hold it.
