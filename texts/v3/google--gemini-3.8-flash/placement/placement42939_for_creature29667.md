At first, there is no reason to believe I have a shape.

There is only the temperature. Thirty-four degrees is not warmth; warmth is an argument between an inside and an outside, and here there is no dispute. The water enters the pores without meeting an opposing pressure; the lipid film on the surface of the cheek floats half a millimeter off the tissue, suspended between blood-heat and process-heat, so that if there is a boundary, it belongs to the water as much as to the skin.

For a long time, the world is only an orientation toward weight.

Down is where the yellow comes from. It is a thick, sulfurous cast, rising from the floor wells, that smells—though there is no nose open to smell it—of hot bronze and mineral wool. Up is where the white arrives, diluted through sixty feet of jade-tinted broth, broken into millions of tiny, stationary sparks by the oxygen mist that clings to the leaf margins.

I am not swimming. Swimming is an animal panic, an attempt to throw the fluid behind oneself in order to arrive somewhere else. In this casement, there is nowhere else. Every aisle is eighteen inches wide; every aisle is bounded on the left by an insulated steam collector and on the right by a wall of five-ply, rubbery fern fronds whose edges are notched like circular saws.

My hand finds a flange.

It does so without my having decided to move an arm. The fingers simply settle into the space behind the bolt circle because the bolt circle was turned with a four-inch clearance from the web of the I-beam, and four inches is the exact width of my palm across the metacarpals. My knuckles fit the gap between the washers. When the fingers close, they do not grip; they seat, like a brass key sliding into an oiled mortise.

The metal is cold, but only in the mind. In the flesh, it is thirty-four degrees, exactly like the water, exactly like the bone. What distinguishes the steel from the broth is not temperature, but grain. The steel has a frequency.

If I lay my brow against the flanged lip, the sixty-cycle pulse of the lower impeller does not enter through the skin. It does not need to. The front of my skull is broad, thickened with a ridge of dense, non-porous bone that has no marrow space, shaped like the nose of a punt. It meets the milled flat of the steel with a dull, wet contact, and the entire forty-thousand-ton installation rings through the roof of my mouth.

*One.*
*Two.*
*Three.*

The rhythm is too slow for a heart. It is the revolution of an iron wheel thirty paces across, turning through grease that has never seen an open sky.

Through the bone, I can tell that the fourth bypass on the western tier is choked. It is not an image in my head; it is a small, dry hitch in the vibration, a harmonic like the sound of a fingernail drawn across a wet comb, occurring every six seconds when the secondary lobe sweeps past the intake bell. A strap-leaf has grown through the perforated brass guard. Its parenchymal starch has swelled under the sodium floodlights until it is three inches thick, wedging itself into the slot where the bypass should float free on its spring pack.

I lever myself backward.

It is not a kick. It is a slow, pelvic draw, a contraction of the deep transversus muscles that pulls my thighs toward my ribs without disturbing the water by an inch. My feet, long and flat-arched, find the lower lip of a galvanized cable tray. The heels hook into the punched ventilation slots with the soft, hydraulic click of greased rubber.

Now I can see my forearm as it clears the foliage.

It is white, but not the white of chalk. It is the white of lard that has been boiled in tallow and allowed to set in a cold pan. A faint, greenish iridescence tracks along the radial tendon—not scales, but the thin sheen of the turbine oil that hangs near the ceiling, collected along my hairless skin as I drifted through the upper aisles an hour ago. There are no veins visible. The blood here runs deep, protected beneath two inches of dense, non-vascular blubber that keeps the demineralized water from drinking my salt.

The leaf is where the skull said it would be.

It has forced its jade tongue through the brass mesh, swelling as it drank the zinc ions until the brass wire is cutting into its rubbery skin. It does not bleed sap; it oozes a clear, viscous gel that smells of wet flour and electrical varnish.

I reach out. My thumb—broad-tipped, flattened like a spatula—slips under the strangling leaf.

I do not cut it. I do not have a blade. I have only the dental plates: two continuous, enameled bars set directly into the jawbones, upper and lower, curved to the radius of an eight-inch pipe.

I draw the leaf between them.

The starch is cold against the palate, though the thermometer would say it is thirty-four. It tastes of flat iron and synthetic nitrogen, with that sharp, alkaline prickle at the back of the throat that comes from the sacrificial anodes. I bite down with the steady, hydraulic pressure of the jaw until the parenchymal cells burst with a series of tiny, muffled pops—like the sound of grease hitting a warm plate under three fathoms of oil.

I draw the strap backward through my teeth, stripping the meat from the structural fiber in a single, three-foot ribbon. The yellow froth of crushed plant-tissue hangs around my face in a cloud, suspended without sinking, until my cheeks expand and draw it into the gullet with a wet, swallowing heave.

It is not food. There is no joy in it, no salt to make the tongue water, no rot to make it sweet. It is simply mass. The casement requires mass to be removed from the intake bells so the pressure can remain seventy-two pounds per square inch; my body requires mass to replenish the lipid mantle that the demineralized water strips from my flank, molecule by molecule, day after day. We are the same balance sheet.

When the leaf is clear, the hitch in the iron ceases. The sixty-cycle pulse returns to its smooth, tubular drone, vibrating through the balls of my feet where they rest against the cable tray.

Above me, twenty meters through the green corridors, the dead space waits.

It has been two hours—perhaps three. My cartilaginous ribs are beginning to draw inward, flattening toward the spine as the nitrogen in the cavern of my chest dissolves into the deep tissues. The buoyancy is shifting. I am becoming heavy; if I let go of the cable tray, I will drift down toward the thirty-meter floor, down into the violet twilight where the water is thick with discarded anode dust and the bronze drainage bells yawn three feet wide.

I release the tray.

I do not turn around. I simply tilt my chin toward the high skylights, straighten the long, flexible pillar of my spine, and allow the remaining gas in my lung-sacs to carry me upward through the chimney of the pipes.

The foliage parts before my head. The rubbery spade-leaves slide over my greased scalp with a sound like wet leather rubbing against wet leather. They do not tear. They yield, let me pass through their narrow, submerged aisles, and slap together behind my heels with a dull *whump* that displaces a hundred tiny bubbles of pure oxygen.

The light changes. The amber drops away, snuffed out by the dense, interlocking curtains of fern. The daylight takes me.

It is white, sharp, and terrifyingly cold, though the water remains thirty-four. It comes down through three inches of laminated quartz, straight from the high mountain sky where the air is too thin to hold heat. It shows every scratch on the gray paint of the transformers; it shows the stamped serial numbers—*44-A-HEX-09*—sharp as the day the dies hit the steel.

My head breaks the meniscus.

There is no splash. The oil on my skin meets the oil on the water's skin, and they weld together with a faint, iridescent shiver.

The air in the twelve-inch gap between the water and the ceiling plates is yellow-gray, rank with the sour, chemical reek of pure nitrogen and the ozone that drifts off the transformer bushings. It is four degrees colder than the water. It strikes my wet scalp like a flat iron laid against bare meat.

I tilt my face straight up into the ceiling rivets.

They are huge, dome-headed, four inches across, weeping a steady rain of distilled condensation that drips into my open, lidless eyes. The drops have no salt. They do not sting; they feel like nothing at all, water meeting water on a surface that has no nerve endings left to report the difference.

My nasal valve, high on the bridge of the brow where the forehead meets the hairless crown, opens.

*Chock.*

It is the only sharp sound in the world: the release of a neoprene seal that has been seated against five fathoms of head-pressure.

I breathe out.

A grey, humid geyser of spent nitrogen and warm tallow-fume hits the steel ceiling plate two inches above my nose, mushrooming against the cold gray primer and condensing instantly into fat, oily tears that run down the rivet lines.

Then I drink.

The nitrogen enters the chest not as air, but as an expansion of volume. The cartilaginous strakes of my ribs spread wide, levering the sternum forward until my chest fills the entire twelve-inch space between the water and the ceiling, my back pressed against the warm fluid, my breastbone scraping the wet, red-leaded plate of the roof.

I hold it.

There is no thought of what is above the three-inch steel. If there is a mountain, it is only a variation in the load-bearing calculations of the foundation girders; if there is a sky, it is only the source of the white light that makes the strap-leaves grow too fast along the western bypass.

My nasal valve snaps shut.

*Tock.*

The seal is made. My chest is a boiler again.

I let my heels rise. The water takes my face first—warm, slick, familiar as the inside of a cheek. The amber light from the floor wells catches my throat, turning it the color of beeswax.

Down here, fifty feet below the rivets, another shape is drifting along the primary steam header.

It is longer than I am, perhaps by the length of a hand. Its skin is gray-white, identical to mine, iridescent with the same thin film of grease. It is moving with that slow, six-inch pelvic draw, its broad webbed fingers trailing lightly over the zinc-plated nuts of the high-pressure flanges, feeling for the flutter that means a seat has begun to wire-draw.

We do not look at each other's faces. Faces are for animals that must distinguish between their young and their food.

As we cross in the narrow aisle between the transformer bank and the third fern-hedge, our ribs touch.

Our skins, slick with the thirty-four-degree oil, slide together without friction. There is a brief, soft drag—the sensation of two wet bladders passing in a pipe—and then the small, hydraulic suction as our flanks separate, leaving a track of disturbed micro-bubbles that drift slowly down toward the bronze floor wells.

Its tail-fin brushes my ankle: three quick, mechanical taps, rhythmic as a telegraph.

*Clear.*
*Clear.*
*Clear.*

I do not tap back. The sixty-cycle pulse is already inside my jaw, telling me that the second deck is drawing four hundred gallons a minute through the copper strainers, that the moss on the turbine skirt has reached its optimum density, that the engine is balance, and that the water is eighty feet deep, thirty-four degrees warm, and will never end.

I hook my long fingers into the next brass sleeve. I draw my body forward. The jade leaf touches my lip, cold and starch-thick, and I open my mouth to take its measure.
