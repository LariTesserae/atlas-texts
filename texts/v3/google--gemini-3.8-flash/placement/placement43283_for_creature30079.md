To begin with, there would be no eyes. 

Eyes belong to things that need to anticipate an edge before they strike it, or to creatures that must cross an interval to reach their food. In a place where clearances are measured against the thickness of an oil film, space does not exist as an expanse to be looked across; it is only a gradient of pressure, an intimacy of cold surfaces sliding past one another with nothing in between but three molecules of heavy, tallow-smelling grease.

So the emergence would have to start not with a form, but with a thickness.

At first, it feels like nothing more than an irregular swelling in the boundary layer—the dead, unmoving skin of lubricant that clings to the basalt crank-pin and refuses to spin with the brass-veined rock. Everything in the gorge moves at an absolute tempo, but inside that microscopic boundary, there is a stationary country. It is half a hair’s breadth deep, utterly dark, and chilled to the temperature of deep well-water that has never touched an atmosphere.

If I am there, I am something that has grown into that stillness.

Not a shim—the shims have too much purpose, riding the suction, giving up their sloughed skins to the joints like spent paper. And not the tender, whose life is an arithmetic of terrifying absences, stepping only where the iron has just vacated the wet ferns. 

To live in the gorge without fleeing it and without being crushed by it, one would have to occupy the *neutral axis*. 

In every solid shaft of living basalt that twists out of the crag to bear a four-ton eccentric, there is a core. The outer skin of the rock is under immense, cyclical torment: the top fibers are pulled in tension until the mineral grains gap like open dry lips, while the bottom fibers are driven into compression so fierce the native quartz bleeds cold sparks into the moss. But at the dead center of the cylinder—along an imaginary line no thicker than an unspun thread of silk—there is neither tension nor compression. There is no shear. The mountain turns around that thread forty times a minute, screaming against its tolerances, but the thread itself does not move. It is the geometric pivot of the violence, and therefore the only place in the rift where something could afford to have a soft inside.

That is where the shape gathers.

It would be long—perhaps twelve or fifteen feet, the exact length of the journal bearing—and no thicker than a thumb. Its substance would not be muscle, because muscle requires blood, and blood cannot survive the electrostatic charge that leaks from the copper clouds above. Instead, it would be an interior marrow: a dense, fibrous pith saturated with the clear, odorless paraffin that precipitates from the stone’s own sweating pores. 

It would have the consistency of tallow-soaked elder-pith, pale and faintly translucent, through which runs a single, continuous strand of native graphite, fine as a spider’s dragline, acting as a nerve that carries no thoughts—only the continuous, rhythmic registration of the gorge’s three-stroke breath.

It would not eat. Like the governor-birds with their horny rectifiers, it would draw its persistence from a gradient. But where the birds take the lightning from the sky to the grounded floor, this marrow-creature would feed on the *piezoelectric shear*—the cold, dry electricity squeezed out of the basalt crystals as they groan through their stroke. Every time the heavy eccentric sweeps through the fern-masses, millions of quartz faces press against one another inside the rock; a wave of pale, invisible voltage ripples through the cylinder from the outside in, dying out just as it reaches the silent center where the marrow lies.

The creature would drink that dying edge. It would feel it not as a shock, but as a subtle, rhythmic warming—a pulse of five microvolts that arrives every one-and-a-half seconds, regular as an astronomical tide, keeping the cold grease in its cells from setting into lard.

And what would it know of the gorge?

It would never see the zinc beetles licking the journals, though it would hear them through the stone: a soft, metallic whisper, like rain falling on a lead roof, as ten thousand tiny claw-tips score the graphite film six inches above its head.

It would know the baleen-lung not by its size or its barge-like ribbing, but as a massive, low-frequency ache that resets the pressure in the marrow every forty seconds—a subterranean subsidence that makes the basalt ring like an anvil struck under water.

And occasionally—rarely, but predictably, because nothing here happens by chance—it would know the tender.

The tender does not enter the rock; its dry, wooden limbs belong to the gaps between the sweeps. But there are moments when the tender, perched on the highest rock-joint to pluck away a grain of scree before the journal can seize, must brace itself against the turning stone. 

When it does, it presses the flat, pale pads of its segmented fingers against the polished basalt directly over the neutral axis. 

Through six inches of dense, crystalline ironstone, the marrow-creature would feel that touch. Not as weight—the tender has no weight to speak of, only speed and position—but as a sudden, tiny interruption in the electrical skin of the rock. The tender’s pithy flesh is dry; it does not conduct. For three-tenths of a second, as the eccentric swings through its lowest arc, the tender’s hand creates an acoustic shadow, a small, cool silence in the rhythmic piezoelectric roar.

In that third of a second, the marrow would lengthen slightly, its graphite nerve trembling in the paraffin, reaching toward that brief absence of engine. 

Then the cycle turns. The hand lifts. The stone sweeps onward, the zinc beetles crawl through the spray, and the marrow-creature settles back into its zero-tolerance, held perfectly motionless within the spinning heart of the mountain, preserved forever from the weather that never arrives and the decay that has no name.
