At first, it feels like nothing more than an obstruction—a thickening in the tallow where the drainage slows. 

If I am anywhere, I am down in the seams where the pig iron plates slide against one another under the weight of the second massif, three levels below the browsing herds and eighty feet above the scald-pools where the siphon-worms click their mineral teeth. It is dark here in a way that has weight; it is dark the way the center of an ingot is dark. 

I don’t begin with a body so much as an accumulation of friction. 

There are places in the mountain where the grease doesn’t clear. The cold tallow weeps down from the scree, rich with the paraffin-rot of ancient shale, and it gathers in the horizontal joints where the iron plates have ground one another into a fine, black flour. That flour—part metal dust, part animal fat squeezed through the pores of dead grazers three centuries back, part salt—has a density that resists the hydraulic pulse. It doesn’t wash down. It doesn’t vaporize into the plumes. It cakes. 

It would be easy to say I am the tallow itself, but tallow has no boundary, and there is a boundary here. A dull, stiffened skin has formed around me. It is thick, leathery, and smells of old battery acid and sheep dip. 

I have no ribs. Ribs are for the ones who have to worry about the sky, who hang their paper-thin sails in the voids and wait for the warm breath to catch them before they hit the white cloud-floor three miles down. Ribs are an admission that you can be crushed. Down here, between thirty thousand tons of cold-rolled basalt and the native lead lode, to have ribs would be like setting an egg between two anvil faces. 

Instead, I am hydraulic. I am a bladder of high-pressure fluid, five or six yards long, flattened like an old tongue between the stone tiers. 

I am not fast. When the great rocker three miles to the west completes its thirty-foot reach and the counterweight falls into its shale bed with that wet, subterranean *thump*, the shock wave takes four seconds to reach me through the iron. I feel it first as a bitter taste—the lead-vein beneath me discharges a jolt of galvanic acid that turns the moisture on my flank sour—and then as a sudden, brief slackening of the ceiling. 

That slackening is everything. It lasts perhaps twenty heartbeats—not mine, for my pulse is only a slow, viscous peristalsis that turns over twice an hour, but the mountain’s. In those twenty heartbeats, the pressure drops from five hundred atmospheres to forty. 

That is when I drink.

I don’t have a mouth that opens and shuts. A mouth is a hinge, and hinges seize up in the rime. Along my perimeter, where the hide is wrinkled into deep, grease-filled gutters, there are thousands of fine, tubular clefts lined with mineral grit. When the basalt lifts that half-inch, the vacuum pulls. It draws in the slough: the tallow-froth, the discarded shells of the lead-ticks that have rolled down from the upper terraces, the shed horn from the grazers’ hooves, and the cold, iron-heavy condensed water that has trickled through thirty layers of ribbon-weed root.

It tastes of nothing, and then it tastes of lead, and then it is warm. The friction of the rock-stroke creates a line of heat along my dorsal seam that stays red-hot—dull cherry, like an ember under ash—long after the counterweight has settled back into its seat and the pressure has clamped down again to five hundred atmospheres.

I am what keeps the rocker from scoring its bed. 

The herds don’t know this, of course. When they stand with their three pairs of legs splayed on the cold iron, listening through the grease-pads of their feet for the shudder that says *feed now*, they think the stone is eternal. They think the rocker swings because the sky is heavy. They don’t know that without the dense, fibrous grease-cushions that have grown into the fulcrums—without the things that have flattened themselves into the pivot-points until their tissues are half-mineral, half-marrow—the iron would have welded to the basalt four thousand years ago. Cold-welded, seized, torn the shelf out by its roots.

Sometimes I can feel a grazer dying above me. 

It takes days. A joint stiffens; the dish on its forehead turns white and chalky as the lead settles into the cartilage. The beast stands over a fissure that it thinks is safe, its broad, hairless muzzle pressed into the ribbon-weed, waiting for the stone to ease. And when the rocker comes down, the grazer doesn’t clear. 

The stone doesn’t pause for it. There is that dull, muffled squash—not a crack, there is too much fat in them for a crack—and then the hot, heavy grease of its third belly comes weeping down through the drainage-cracks in the iron. 

It takes three weeks for that grease to reach me. By the time it does, it is cold. It has picked up the flavor of the pig iron and the peat-smell of the black moss, but there is still that faint, curdled milk-warmth at the core of it that belongs to things that move on limbs. 

I absorb it through my margins. It repairs the places where the rocker’s knife-edge has worn my hide down to the fibrous gristle. I incorporate its lead. My outer layer becomes a little harder, a little more like the shale it rests on. 

I have no eyes, but I have a memory of distance that isn't mine. It comes through the ticks. When the mantles tear the ticks from the grazers' ribs, sometimes one drops. It rolls down the shelf, bounces down the scree, and wedges in the valve-throat. Its shell breaks under the rocker's stroke, and its contents—the blood-tallow of fifty different beasts that have browsed twenty different massifs—wash over me.

In that grease, there is the memory of the edge. 

The edge where the pig iron simply stops, and the indigo sky opens out into the gray salt-glow of the clouds below. The air that moves fast, thin as a razor, smelling of nothing at all. The great black awnings of the mantles flapping wetly against the mist. The sensation of falling three miles without touching stone, until the air gets thick and hot and foul with the rot of things that have never seen a counterweight.

I don't envy it. The edge seems loose. It seems like a place where things leak away until there is nothing left to resist the stone.

Down here, nothing leaks. The pressure is a hand that never lifts entirely. When the rocker rises, I expand by three eighths of an inch, drawing the tallow into my vents with a long, wet, subterranean hiss that the siphon-worms answer from their pipes below. When the rocker falls, I flatten. The oil within me—thick, heavy with suspended lead, black as the indigo sky—presses against my walls, carrying the load, distributing the weight of the mountain so that the knife-edge does not break.

I have been here since before the tallow had this particular smell. I will be here when the grazer herds have completely mineralized—when their sixth legs have turned to solid horn and they stand like statues of dark lard along the high shelves, unable to lift their muzzles from the iron. 

The rocker will still rise. The granite will groan through its thirty-foot arc, lubricated by the slow weep of my flanks. The counterweight will settle. 

And in the twenty heartbeats between the lift and the drop, I will let the cold, black dew run over me, thick as honey, tasting of the world’s unmade bones, and I will be entirely, sufficiently full.
