At first, there is a temptation to imagine oneself as the mantis. 

It is the easiest posture for the mind to borrow: the solitary sentinel, aloof on the high lintel, carved from soapstone and crystallized salt, watching the vast blue void of the plains five thousand meters below. There is a clean, severe romance to that kind of waiting—to hold so still that the wind forgets you are there, to live entirely outside the frantic domesticity of the mounds, waking only when a wet, soft stranger is hurled out of the sky by a thermal draft to be relieved of its chaotic juice. 

Or perhaps the Chalk-Belly. There is a deep, animal peace in being an oven: lying flat as a paving tile upon the sun-hammered basalt until the blood turns to liquid tar, then sliding down through the gallery shafts into the cool, dark nursery-crypts to let hundreds of tiny velvet soles climb upon your back, soaking up your noon while your bellows-lungs draw the thin air through baffles of dry oil. To be the island around which a whole silent civilization clusters for warmth.

But those are the margins. They are the edge-conditions of the terrace, tolerated or fed, sitting upon the architecture rather than being of it.

If I find where my weight actually settles, where the senses stop being an observer’s game and become an inescapable, mineral reality, it is inside the chitin.

I have six feet, and I have never heard the sound of my own walking.

Where the tarsi touch the pale basalt, there is no impact—only the faint, microscopic yield of ten thousand branched setae flattening themselves against the molecular ridges of the sheared stone. It feels like stepping into cool resin that lets go the moment the joint lifts. I walk along the underside of a pitch bridge forty meters above the main avenue, completely indifferent to the drop, because the glass-smooth belly of the bridge holds me with the same stubborn, intermolecular suction as the level floor. Gravity here is not a terror; it is merely an angle to be calculated against the grip of the pads.

I see nothing of the sky’s blue. Through the twin mica plates set into my forehead, the world does not have color; it has *temperatures*.

Above me, the empty sky is a terrifying, bottomless black—an absolute zero that pulls heat like a sponge. Below, the city-forest is a labyrinth of soft, braided ambers, dull bronzes, and pulsing hearth-glows. The Chalk-Bellys resting on the lower roofs are bright, sluggish coals, leaking calories into the surrounding masonry in broad, orange halos. The tallow-fronds are cool, jade-dark fans that whisper with the low, internal friction of their pumped oils. And my siblings—moving along the galleries in dense, rhythmic files—are long, slender needles of warm gold, their three-segmented bodies glowing with the slow, closed combustion of their tallow.

I am an architect, but I carry no tools. My tools are my third mouthparts, which are worn blunt and polished smooth by a lifetime of smearing pitch, and the glands beneath my seventh abdominal tergite, which are currently heavy with cured terpene-paste.

I am old. I know this not because I feel tired—fatigue is a condition of wet muscles that accumulate acid, and my tallow does not sour—but because my blood is growing thick.

When I was small, deep in the gypsum galleries where the Millers’ mandibles scraped the white crystal into smoke, my tallow was light, fluid as warm seed-oil. I moved quickly through the larval combs, carrying pellets of dried plaster, learning the tastes of the clan from the varnished walls. But over decades of sun-exposure on the high shelves, the long-chain hydrocarbons in my veins have slowly oxidized, cross-linking under the ultraviolet glare that leaks through the mica shutters. 

My tallow is turning to paraffin. 

In my joints, the flexor tendons have begun to dry into something like horn. When I extend my middle right limb, there is a tiny, internal hesitation—a stiffness that does not hurt, because there is no wet nerve to carry pain, but registers through my sensory hairs as a loss of tolerance, a structural drag of three or four microns. 

I do not turn back toward the brood-chambers. The deep dark is for the Millers and the young who are still learning to chemically tear water from the stone. My place is up here, where the sun cures.

I step off the underside of the pitch bridge onto the sunward ramp of Tower Nine. A sibling is coming down from the wax-harvest, carrying two dried slabs of Chalk-Belly skin beneath its front legs. We do not stop with a jerk; our paces slow together, the velvet pads kissing the resin floor, until our head-plates are an inch apart.

We lower our foreheads.

*Tuck.*

The contact is hard, smooth, and dry. For four seconds, my forehead plate rests against theirs. The greasy film between our carapaces dissolves into a single, shared circuit. 

From them, I receive the texture of the morning’s skin-harvest: the scales are dry, clear, and uniform, taken from an old male who baked yesterday on the eastern sheer; they will make three good clerestory windows for the secondary seed-vaults. 

From me, they receive the report of my joints: *drag on the right caliper; stiffness in the abdominal bellows; three days of mobility remaining.* 

There is no sorrow in the exchange. There is no concept of departure. To their chemical sensorium, my impending crystallization is simply an inventory update—a material transition from a mobile resin-applicator to a load-bearing element. They pull back. Their forehead plate carries a smear of my stearic acid; mine carries the talc-like scent of the lizard’s molt. We pass.

I climb higher, toward the fracture point where the pale basalt is newest. 

Here, the rift has opened an arm’s-width wider over the last two hot seasons. The morning wind, thin and bone-dry, whistles through the clean-flaked cleft like steam through a needle. At the corner of the highest terrace, an arch of cast pitch and masticated shale has begun to sag under the thermal expansion of the midday sun. The gap needs a lintel—something rigid, curved correctly to receive the lateral thrust of the upper drying-yards, and thoroughly impregnated with high-melting-point waxes that will not soften when the kiln-heat strikes at noon.

I reach the gap. I test it with my forelegs, sliding the velvet pads along the freshly split rock. The basalt here is so sharp it could shear silk, but my tarsal brushes settle between the microscopic tooths of the stone, gripping effortlessly.

I fit.

The curve of my thoracic shield matches the radius of the archway’s left shoulder. If I lock my three right legs into the vertical fissure of the cliff and swing my abdomen across the open span, my left legs will reach the anchor-peg of Tower Nine’s masonry.

I turn around twice, settling my calibers into the stone, finding the micro-ledges that will hold against thirty atmospheres of lateral shear. The basalt is cold against my ventral plates—a clean, iron-sharp chill that will soon warm as the sun climbs.

I extend my legs to their absolute limit. The dry tendons in my knees reach the end of their travel and, with a faint, internal *snap*, slip into their permanent locking grooves. The calipers will not flex again. The friction of the joints rises to infinity; they are no longer hinges, but struts.

Beneath my abdomen, the resin-valves open for the last time. 

The bolus does not come out thin, as it does when we patch a window or bind a frond-root to the masonry. It comes out dense, amber-dark, and boiling with volatile terpenes that instantly hit the warm, desiccated air. It foams slightly, then collapses into a heavy, vitreous tar that coats my hindlegs, cements my tarsi into the basalt fracture, and rises up around my lower segments like poured bronze.

The sweet, scorched-honey smell fills the small chimney between the walls. 

Below me, in the avenue, two young builders halt on the ramp. Their antennae raise; their mica plates turn upward toward the scent of the curing pitch. They know the smell. It is the announcement of a closed joint.

They do not come up to watch. There is no need. They turn back into the gallery to bring up the baskets of crushed shale and the bowls of lipid-mortar.

Through my mica shutters, the infrared world begins to slow down. The thermal pulse of my heart-tube, cycling its thick tallow through my thorax, beats once every thirty seconds... then once every minute. The heat of the city does not fade; rather, it enters me directly through the rock. The basalt warms under the morning kiln-light, and its energy pours into my locked legs, through my carapaced ribs, into the hardening resin that binds me to the cliff.

I do not lose my vision. The mica plates remain clear. I watch the Chalk-Bellys crawl out onto the slabs below, their wide backs catching the amber sun. I watch a mantis on the highest edge, still as an iron spike, waiting for the wind to bring it something that still remembers water.

A young builder climbs onto the archway from the gallery below. It does not pause. Its velvet-soled feet step firmly onto my midsection, finding the familiar, segmented ridges of my shell. It carries a heavy lump of gypsum-plaster, and it uses my back as a staging ledge, bracing itself to smooth the seam where my head-plate meets the cliff.

Its tarsal pads are soft. They grip my armor with the gentle, clinging intimacy of a thing that knows its house is made of its brothers.

The plaster cures fast in the dry mountain air. The resin turns to stone around my knees. The street above me is finished; the traffic begins to cross; and the city holds.
