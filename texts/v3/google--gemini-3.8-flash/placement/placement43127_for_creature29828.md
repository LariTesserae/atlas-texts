At first, there is only the difference between what yields and what does not. 

The heartwood yields, but only to the tune of a thousandth of an inch per century, a dull, oily springiness that accepts the weight of iron without bruising. The iron does not yield at all; it sweats, it cools three degrees below the air, and it accepts the bitter wash of oak-sap until its skin is as black and slick as an auger bit turning through lard.

If I look for where I begin, it is not in a name or a face. Those things have the short, hot life of midges; they belong to the air above the cut, where the sun rots things quickly and the wind has too much room to scatter what it dries out. 

Down here, between the retaining walls, you begin with a joint. 

Not necessarily a bone joint. A trunnion cradled in oak. A four-inch pin of turned scrap-iron through which forty tons of ballast tilts every nine minutes. Or the place where the root-plate meets the bottom flange of a twenty-pound rail.

I am not the water, though I have its temperature—forty-five degrees, steady as a plumb line. And I am not the iron, because the iron has no patience; it simply waits to oxidize, kept from its rust only by the lick of grease and the cold wash of copper salts.

Perhaps I am closest to the tallow. 

Not the fat in a bucket, but the tallow that has lived on the underside of a rocker beam so long it has forgotten the steer it came from, forgotten the tallow-kettle, forgotten the man with the wooden paddle who stirred it. It has become a gray, waxy crust, full of ground slag and shed bark-scales, that nevertheless warms just enough under friction to allow the pin to turn without screaming. 

No, that is too thin. I have more weight than grease. 

When the accumulator stroke finishes—that long, pneumatic *whoof* that smells of wet lichen and brass filings—there is a rebound. It is not large. A half-inch of slack taken up in the subterranean root-mass as the water pressure equalizes. 

I feel that rebound in the soles of my feet, but my feet are not things with toes. They are wide, blunt spatulas of horn, split twice by the frost forty years ago and healed over with a seam as hard as gutta-percha. They fit the camber of the rail head. When I stand upon the iron, there is no air beneath my arch; the horn has grown to match the profile of the steel, rolled out by the wheels of cranes that haven't moved since the masonry solved the wood.

I find myself near the third gantry, where the cut dead-ends against the wet sandstone slip. 

This is the quietest quarter. Here, the draft from the tunnel mouth fails to reach. The air is stagnant, thick with the smell of wet leather and river mud that has turned to shale. The dew does not fall in clean, distinct drops here; it hangs in a cold, grey fur on the rivets. 

If I am one of the four, I am the one who does not walk the tracks. 

The one on track two has his pebble-work; his back is supple as an ash stave, and he can bend until his temples graze his shins to hook a cinder from an expansion shim. I know his cadence by the small, rhythmic clack of his horn soles against the ballast, thirty paces behind the exhaust valve’s venting. 

My work is lower. It is the work of clearance.

Between the bottom flange of the gantry carriage and the stone slip, there is an opening four inches high. In the summer of the upper world—which we register only as a slight thickening of the wood-sugars in the condensation, a faint taste of wild cherry in the runoff—the roots expand. They crowd the stone. If they crowd it too tightly, the shear-pins in the carriage will snap, and when the pins snap, the entire overarching canopy loses its anchor against the south wall. The ribs would spread; the gray light would widen into glare; the forty-five-degree vault would warm, and once it warmed, the rot would enter.

So I sit in the four-inch gap.

My ribs are not basket-worked like the ribs of things that run. They are flat, overlapping slats of seasoned cartilage, dark as peat, that can be drawn together by a single long muscle running from the hip-horn to the base of the skull. When I exhale—that long, nine-minute leak of odorless moisture across the lower lip—I become three inches thick. When the rocker beam lifts and the hydrostatic head forces the sapwood out against the masonry, I take the pressure across the flat of my back.

It is a good weight. It is not the crushing weight of rock fall; it is the living, elastic heave of the basin, balanced precisely against the counterweights in the center yard. 

My hands are hooked into the drainage slit of the rail chair. The fingers are short, three to a side, joined nearly to the second knuckle by a tough, gristly web that resists the bite of the slag. My tongue is out. It lies against the chilled bronze of an oiler-well that hasn't seen fresh oil since the tunnel was bricked up. The bronze tastes of sour cider and pennies. Every eleven minutes, a single globule of cold water rolls down the lip of the fitting, hits the middle of my tongue, and splits. 

Part of it goes down the leathery pipe to keep the Peristalsis turning—that small, rhythmic squeeze that moves the iron-paste through my belly. Part of it stays on the horn of the jaw, drying into the white, crusty bloom that seals the skin against the air.

I do not think of what is outside the cut. 

Once, perhaps, there was a notion that the tunnel went through to an orchard, or a wharf where barges unloaded coal with a great, greasy clamor of shovels. There is a memory of red brick being dry and dusty in the sun, of grease that went rancid and stank of dead pigs, of men who spat red tobacco into the ash and shouted across the tracks because their blood was hot and hurried them toward some noon that never stayed still.

That memory is very small now. It has the size of a slag-pebble caught in a rail joint, and like that pebble, it will eventually be hooked out by a blunt fingernail and dropped into the ditch where the runoff can smooth it.

A spindle crawls across the instep of my left pad. 

It is heavy for its size, cold and ribbed like an uncoiled spring, smelling strongly of turned wood and the green slime that coats the trunnion-wells. Its six small scrapers find purchase in the horn of my heel, testing the crust, deciding whether I am iron to be scraped or stone to be cemented against. It decides I am wood. It leaves a trailing smear of amber mucilage across my ankle—cold, odorless, setting almost instantly into that slippery, graphite skin—and continues its climb toward the carriage pins.

I let it pass. My pulse does not jump. The heart, if it is still a heart and not simply an internal siphon made of tough, fibrous leather, turns over once while the spindle travels from the ankle to the calf. 

The brass valve at the tunnel mouth snaps: *chink*.

A long, slow shudder passes through the rails. The double-action rocker beam fifty yards away has reached the bottom of its stroke. The oak cradles groan—a deep, resonant *thrum* like a cello string dipped in oil—and then the silence drops back down over the basin, heavier and cooler than before.

I draw my chin down against the gristle of my breastplate. 

The white ring of vapor vents from the pipe overhead, drifts between the black-barked ribs, and settles upon my brow as a film of fine, cold sweat. I taste the oak in it. I taste the iron. I tighten the slats of my chest by the width of a hair to meet the new stroke of the accumulator, and I settle into the ninety feet of shadow between the gantry and the wall, waiting for the gutters to dry.
