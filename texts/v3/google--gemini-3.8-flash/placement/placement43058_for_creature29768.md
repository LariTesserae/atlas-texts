Not up in the rings. The wind there is too thin, sheared into whistle-edges by the black glass, and the clear kites turn endlessly in that draft with an agitation that feels like hunger without an object. 

Nor down in the basin’s torn root-slurry, beneath the suspended wafer of the bridge. That bottom is too thick with the memory of the cleaving; it smells of shattered bedrock, cold grease, and the frantic, sulfurous chemistry of an earth trying to scab over a wound sixty kilometers wide. To be down there is to be buried in the lungs of a stranger.

No. I would be wedged.

I would find myself in the blind run of an under-cantilever, thirty meters off the ground, where a monolithic esplanade juts out over a sunken terrace of tea-colored water. It is a place of permanent twilight within a world that already has no noon. Here, the low sky cannot look down, and the rising swamp cannot fully look up. There is only the immense, horizontal roof of the dark metamaterial above, absolutely level, cold to the touch, and dead-flat for three hundred paces before it ends at a razor-line that drops into empty air.

At first, there is scarcely a difference between the body and the stone.

I would be broad, low, and heavy—less a creature of limbs than of surface area. Not like the suction discs that wait to be washed over, and not like the pale, trembling striders whose bones are too loud for this quiet. My underside would be wide, soft, and slightly damp, conforming down into the microscopic valleys of the pour with the intimate fidelity of wet paper pressed against glass. 

The cold comes up into me continuously. It is not the cold of winter—there is no winter under this bruised wool sky—but the engineered cold of something that refuses to store the sun. To live against it is to maintain a slow, stubborn warmth within a few centimeters of flesh while the plane beneath drinks every calorie it can reach. 

My back, facing down toward the warm, wet floor of the basin, would be different: rougher, segmented, armored with thick plates of horn or slate-colored chitin the texture of wet shale. Over those plates, the copper ferns have already begun to sprawl. They do not know where the monolith ends and where I begin; their root-threads creep over my flanks, mistaking the ridges of my shell for another shelf of the terrace. I do not scrape them off. They are a pelt. They insulate the soft margins where my breath moves.

The breath is slow. It does not heave. It is drawn in along the lateral margins where the plate meets the stone—a long, shallow siphon of the humid air, heavy with the scent of bruised leaves and the faint, sweet trace of ozone.

Every twenty minutes, the purge comes.

I feel it three miles off before it arrives: a tightening in the grain of the metamaterial, a microscopic stiffening of the alloy that sets my shell ringing at a pitch below sound. 

The ferns on my back go rigid. The water pooling along the shelf ceases its trickle. 

Then the shock runs through. It is not an earthquake; it has no heave or rumble. It is an evacuation of tension. It passes through the underside of my belly like a cold blade—sharp, clean, and completely indifferent. 

Instantly, the ceiling lets go.

From thirty meters of overhang, the accumulated condensation detaches in a single, roaring curtain of droplets. It does not fall as rain; it falls as a wall of gray static, hissing violently as it punches through the thick, felted cabbage-leaves on the terrace below, churning the tea-colored pools into froth. For ten seconds, the air is pulverized mist, lukewarm and blinding.

That is the moment of feeding.

My edges lift—just a fraction of an inch, breaking the seal with a slow, hydraulic peel. The falling mist rolls beneath my belly, charged with the mineral-leach of the unweathered stone and the tiny, drifting spores that the gliders shed as they tumble above the spires. My gills—fine, mossy combs of dark purple tissue lining the lateral grooves—drink the runoff as it sheets across the black plane. It tastes of iron, distilled clouds, and the faint, bitter alkali of the ferns.

Then the vibration ceases. The surface snaps dead-still again. 

I settle my weight back down. The vacuum takes hold with a dull, wet sigh. The ferns settle over my seams, heavy with new water.

I do not have eyes; the light here has nothing to say that the skin cannot register. But I have an immense, peripheral sense of the geometry. 

I feel the sixty-meter span of the terrace above me, bearing no load, holding up only the empty, pearlescent air. I feel the distant, slow shifting of the great slug three kilometers to the south, sliding with hydraulic patience through its mathematical channel, displacing water that eventually finds its way into the cisterns below my perch. And far down, in the churned mud under the bridge, I can feel the slow, wet rhythm of the vast lung expanding and collapsing against the earth—a cousin, perhaps, or merely the furnace that was left behind when the tools were put away.

I do not migrate. To move across the flats would be an indecency—too much pale flesh exposed to the empty esplanades, too much skittering across planes that were meant to remain uninterrupted. 

Instead, I widen. Over months, the horn of my shell thickens; the margins creep an inch further along the alloy; the ferns on my back grow denser, tangling with the vines that climb down from the terrace lip. 

I am becoming an anomaly in the blueprint: a tiny patch of friction where the cold-pour was meant to be frictionless. A soft spot on the machine.

When the atmospheric drone groans through the hollow spires, setting the low clouds into slow, circular drafts, I do not look up. I pull myself tighter against the dark, unaging ceiling, taste the iron in the water that slides between my plates, and wait for the twenty minutes to turn again.
