I would not be in the settlements, where the three-story tenements take the thrum of the floor and turn it into the chatter of tin valves and wooden soles. Nor would I be out on the freight-flats under the halide towers, splitting amber resin with an iron wedge. Those places are for the people who still think in shifts and allotments—those whose condensers are still tight enough to dream of an interior life that does not smell of pinene.

If I look for where I settle, it is three miles out into the clearance, where the light from the copper girders grows diffuse and flat—that perpetual November-noon that casts no shadows except directly beneath the knees.

I am low to the timber. 

At first, it seems I might be one of the machines that has shed its housing: a long-levered thing made of steamed hickory and cold-drawn bronze, creeping along the drive-shafts where they span the empty quarters. But there is tallow inside the sleeves. There is the slow, viscous drag of blood that has traded its thinness for something heavier, something that does not boil when the throat-chute vents into the dry nitrogen draft. 

I am a *grain-walker*—or whatever name remains when a person’s sole duty has narrowed down to the measurement of tension.

My knees are wrapped in three layers of boiled pigskin, saturated with heavy stearates until they do not bend with a crease, but with the smooth, resistant yield of cold linoleum. In my ears there are no plugs, but two carved cones of dense boxwood, hollowed to a hair’s-breadth aperture; they cut out the twenty-minute roar of the breathing bracts above and leave only the deep, subterranean rumblings of the timber mantle. 

I carry very little. A horn-faced mallet. A pocket of powdered chalk—not mineral, but the ground carapaces of the planer-lice, collected from the dry curbings. A three-foot straight-edge of ground steel, preserved from rust by the utter absence of sweat, resting against my collarbone like a cold yoke.

My work is to find the shear before it sings.

When a trunk forty yards thick begins to draw itself apart under horizontal tension, it does not groan as earth does. It stores its torque. A square mile of ash floor will sit seemingly dead, planed to within a thirty-second of an inch, while four hundred feet below, the dry cambium is winding like a colossal clock-spring. If it lets go all at once, the standing splinters will punch through the rail trestles and the three-story barracks like lances of drawn glass.

So I crawl the kerfs. I press the flat of my cheek—the tallow-greased, leather-stiff flesh over the zygomatic bone—directly against the raw, unrotting grain.

From here, the world is not vast; it is immediate and microscopically dense. I feel the drive-shafts five hundred yards behind me: the *thrup-thrup-thrup* of the bronze pillow-blocks, each stroke carrying a faint puff of warm mineral grease into the floorboards. I feel the coursers three dowel-spans over, their rubber pads tapping out their frantic, tongue-less circuits beneath the rails. And deeper, through the heavy silicone xylem, I hear the long, crystalline alignment of the cellulose fibers. 

They speak in frequencies like cold bells. A healthy ring is dull, heavy, absorbent. Where the tension gathers, the sound tightens; it rises toward a flute-note that vibrates the iron fillings in the lag bolts.

When I find that note, I take the horn mallet and strike the floor once. 

The percussion travels outward, a crisp, clean *tock* that the dry ash takes into itself and spreads across the basin. Two miles away, the freight handlers feel the heel-tap in their shins and reach for the tensioning jacks. They do not look up. They do not need to know my face behind the condenser mask, nor do I care to see their gray aprons. We are linked only by the elasticity of the ash.

Between the cycles—in the five minutes of utter stillness before the overhead bracts begin their vast, leathery inhalation—I sit against the casing of a rotary coupling. 

My breath clicks behind the tin flaps: *chuff, click, cool.* The copper plates beneath my jaw are heavy with their captured dram of gray water, cold against my throat. I take a single wafer of bark-starch from the oiled pouch at my hip and let it dissolve without saliva, holding it against the roof of my mouth until the dry vegetable fats soften enough to swallow. It tastes of nothing—of an attic where dry linen has been kept in a cedar chest for fifty years.

In this stillness, the distinction between the tools and the body begins to drift away. My fingernails have grown thick, flat, and amber-colored, their edges beveled like scraper-blades from years of brushing aside the talc-drifts of lice-meal. The knuckles of my left hand—the hand that leads across the bare timber—have lost their hair and their wrinkles; they are calloused into smooth, horn-like pads that do not catch on the knife-edged splinters. 

Sometimes, a harness-moth drifts down from the ceiling struts, trailing its dry, binder-twine tethers. It mistakes my leg for a stationary trestle. It anchors one of its pale ribbons to the stiff cuff of my trousers, climbs two inches up the calf, and spreads its parchment flaps to catch the pinene drift. 

I do not brush it off. There is no warmth in me to frighten it away, no salt for it to seek, no dampness to foul its delicate fractional plates. We sit together in the cold halide glare, vibrating at the exact same pitch as the rotary turbine fifty miles to the north.

I know how it ends for a grain-walker. One day the boxwood cones in the ears do not hear the note rise quickly enough; or perhaps the knee-leather stiffens past the angle of crawling, and the twenty-minute gale catches you out in the center of an unsheltered square. 

You lie down in the kerf. You unbuckle the condenser and let the tin valves go cold. You let the nitrogen wind have that final, hoarded thimbleful of lung-steam—that single, brief fogging of the clear air that vanishes before it can touch the floor.

And then the planer-lice come out from the seams between the planks. They begin at the knuckles, where the grease has worn thin, working with their microscopic, diamond-hard mandibles, beveling, leveling, planing down the small, unnecessary curves of the ribs and the brow until there is nothing left to interrupt the passage of the scraper-sleds.

It is not an ugly thought. There is a great, cool dignity in a world where nothing is allowed to soften, where even the dead are held to the precision of a carpenter’s square, true and flush against the grain, forever free of rot.
