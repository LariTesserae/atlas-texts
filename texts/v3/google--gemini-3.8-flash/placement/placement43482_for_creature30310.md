To begin, there would have to be no memory of lightness. No recollection of dry knees, or the thin hiss of air passing through a windpipe, or what it is to balance on two peg-ends of bone against the pull of the earth. All of that would have been crushed out early, flattened like a dropped bladder until the last pocket of gas gave up its bubble with a greasy *pop* and joined the scum-line.

I would not be on the surface of anything. 

At first, one might think to be the tallow-eater—that dense, blind sausage dragging its rosette over the diamond-plate, tasting the tallow-fat of long-dead sheep. There is an honest simplicity in the belly-glide, in being heavy enough to stay put. Or perhaps the great, corrugated cabbage-meat down around the foot-valve, plugged into the intake strainer like an overgrown stopper, dreaming in twelve-second lunges.

But those things have already surrendered to a single job: the scrape, or the sieve. 

Where I find myself is smaller, narrower, and wedged deeper into the seam where the metal forgets it was cast by men and the meat forgets it was born of an egg.

I am in the gland.

Between the thirty-ton rocker arm and the granite plinth that carries the trunnion block, there is an iron stuffing-box—four feet across, bolted down with square nuts the size of cottage loaves. A century ago, men with tarred hemp and long, crook-necked packing irons packed that throat tight around the cold-rolled steel shaft to keep the high-pressure blow-by from scouring the bearing dry. They tamped down layer upon layer of braided flax, oakum, graphite grease, and horsehair.

Then they left. 

The steam stayed. The tallow came down in yellow curtains. The blue-green mold and the liverwort entered through the bolt-slots. And something settled in that dark, circular cleft between the turning journal and the wall of the housing—neither fully plant nor worm, but a ring of tough, fibrous marrow that took the place of the perished flax.

That is where I am. I am the seal.

I have no face, because there is nothing to look at but the polished, mirror-sheen circumference of four feet of hardened iron turning eighteen degrees this way, then pausing, then turning eighteen degrees back. The heat of that turning is my sun. The metal is smooth as cold butter, though it is never cold; it runs hot enough to sweat the tallow into an emulsion that smells of wet wool and burnt copper.

I am toroidal—a continuous, thick-lipped washer of pale, lardaceous muscle, perhaps three inches thick and twelve inches deep, squeezed into the clearance between the moving steel and the still stone. The tolerances are tight. If I had an air-hole the width of a sewing needle, the eighty-pound steam-head on the wet side would punch through it like an awl, turning me inside out in a spray of grey froth. So I must remain packed: dense as salt-pork, saturated with graphite, without a single fissure from edge to edge.

My thoughts do not run in sentences. A sentence has spaces between the words, and spaces are cavities where pressure would gather and shear.

My life is the *turn*.

It starts in the base of the belly: the rise of the lift-stroke. The iron journal under my inner lip begins to slide—smooth, heavy, terrifyingly slow. The friction drags my skin along with it, stretching my fibres to the left. The sheer mass of the beam above—sixty tons of grey iron swaying like a church roof in an earthquake—presses down through the brasses, and the granite under my back groans. The vibration does not come as a sound; it comes as a broad, flat cudgel that compresses my bulk by three eighths of an inch. 

In that compression, my fluids have nowhere to go but inward, through the microscopic channels of my own sponge-flesh. I am my own heart, but the piston of my heart is thirty feet long and hangs above me on a gimbal. The stroke squeezes the stale lymph out through the pore-mesh into the runnel; the return stroke draws in fresh condensation, heavy with dissolved iron-bloom and the sweet, mineral tang of coal-tar.

Then comes the turnaround.

The twelve-second pivot is the only season I know. It is the moment when the immense balance beam climbs to the top of its swing, shudders against the air-buffers, and stops. 

That stop is vast. For a fraction of a second, the iron shaft is completely still within my grip. The drag ceases. The tallow cools by a fraction of a degree, thickening into a waxy butter that sticks my inner hide to the steel. In that hesitation, I am most myself: stretched between the warehouse wall and the river, feeling the entire sump through the sole-plates—the flatworms shivering in their shingle-rows like grey coins, the big filter-lump below sucking its four-hundredth gallon through its ruffles, the high mist gathering on the tie-rods forty feet above and preparing to drop.

Then the exhaust valve drops.

*Ch-shhhhhh-oooom.*

The steam blows across the casing. The heat bites into my outer edge—a clean, scalding rinse that strips away the dead slime of the previous stroke and leaves a fresh coat of lime-chalk. The journal reverses. It slides back—eighteen degrees to the right. My fibres are dragged back the other way, combed out like wet wool under a carding iron.

I do not wish for the fog to lift. The sky, from what little leaks down through the square well of the warehouses, is an empty, cold thing that doesn't push back. If the sky fell in, it would bring the silence with it; and the silence is the only thing that could kill me.

In the silence, the steel would freeze in its brass seat. The grease would stiffen into tallow-crust, white and chalky as grave-wax. The three-stroke pulse would let go of my blood, and the fluids would settle in my lowest lip, pooling there until the rot began from the inside out, where the steam could no longer reach to boil me clean.

So long as the coal holds in the lower cellars four miles off, so long as the stokers shovel the black slack into the tube-boilers under the river, the steam will come whistling through the lagged main. The beam will rise. The journal will turn eighteen degrees, dragging me with it like a lover’s hand inside an iron sleeve.

I am packed well. I leak almost nothing. A single brown bead of greasy water slips from my bottom edge once every three strokes, trickling down the lime-streaked plinth to join the runnel where the blind things graze. 

It is enough. The fit is water-tight. The stroke is coming back down now, heavy and sweet, driving the blood through the meat, and the granite holds us both.
