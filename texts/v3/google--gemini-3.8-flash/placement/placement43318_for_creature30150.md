At first, there is only the grip. 

It is not an act of will; it is an adhesion. The underside of the shell is flat, rimmed with a wet, calcified skirt that cools and hardens whenever the summits go slack. When the northern ice plains withdraw their breath, the basin drops into a terrible, hollow vacuum where nothing moves except the slow drying of slime against the flagstone. In that silence, I am simply stone among stone: a walnut-sized knob of porous lime, rough as a dried sponge, indistinguishable from the travertine wash that flakes from the towers and litters the seams. There is no thought then. Thought requires a current, and in the lull, the fluids in the joints thicken to grease. The world stops at its edges.

Then the air spills over the northern lip.

It does not arrive as an idea; it arrives as a hammer. Forty feet above, the mouth of the nearest stack takes the downdraft, and the deep, grinding resonance—that flat, percussive C—strikes through the siltstone directly into the floor. 

The flagstone beneath me does not merely shake; it *uncouples*. 

The thixotropic mud, that thirty-six-degree black jelly where the moss rhizoids terminate, liquefies under the shear. My skirt lets go. The calcium seals crack with a microscopic pop. The sixty pale, bristle-like hairs along my belly-plates, stiff with rime minutes ago, begin to sweep in alternating waves of six, catching the vibration before it can dissipate into the cold. 

I do not hear the C. I have nowhere to put a sound. My interior is mostly solid—white muscle, gut-track, and the heavy, saturated chalk that lines my back. But the note sets the fluid in my ventral margin into a standing ring. It is an engine turning over. When the tower shouts, the joints in my walking legs unlock, driven by the frequency like iron shavings jerked along a magnet.

I am moving before I know there is an avenue.

The world beneath the moss-canopy is a low, green-black ceiling that shivers continuously at thirty beats a second. Copper-colored stems hang down like the rafters of a flooded cellar, brushing my shell, shedding cold drops that bead and run off my lime crust without sinking in. The mudstone flags beneath are wide, slick with sulfur-grease, their hexagonal sutures forming a geometric roadbed that I read through the tips of my hairs.

To turn left is to climb toward the fifth. 

The northern tower’s companion—eighty yards off, narrower at the throat, slightly clogged with the frozen sails of the lard-spiders—answers the gale with a shrill, harmonic E-flat that cuts straight through the liverwort roots. The E-flat feels thin, fast, like needles scraping the edges of my fourth segment. It warns of the stack’s skirt: the zone where the mist-spray falls so hard it turns the mud to scouring sand, where the drop-draft would flip my mineral plate and leave me spinning on my knobby spine.

So I swing my head-shield away from the E-flat, leaning my bulk into the heavier, drag-tail thrum of the great avenue. 

Here, the C is vast and dull. It tastes of iron and crushed diatoms. In the middle of the pavement, away from the sheltering skirts of the chimneys, the wind sometimes tears down through the seams in the moss, blowing the black soup clear off the rock. It is dangerous here. The flatworms know it; they stay coiled deep in the rot-tangles, milk-white ribbons trembling in the silt, ignoring the meat of the drowned mites that wash past. 

I do not ignore it. I drop my mouthparts—calcified scoops, heavy as river gravel—into the gray grease between two mudstone plates. The sulfur film is thick, kept warm by the friction of the shaking floor. I scrape. The sound of my own feeding is lost inside the architectural roar, but the rhythm of it enters my legs: scrape with the pulse of the stack, sweep with the rebound, advance three hairs’ breadths as the vibration dips between gusts.

A shadow passes over the moss-canopy—not a cloud, but a hard, swift occlusion of the white midday glare. 

High up, in the open throat of the stack, something has fallen. 

A shower of debris comes showering down the drainage slit twenty yards behind me: dry spider legs, broken silk-knots stiff with rime, and a scattering of pale lime dust. The cavern-merlin is inside the chimney. I feel the dull, triple-thump of its wing-calcars striking the internal spirals as it brakes itself in the dark. It is small, but its weight is dense, greased, and purposeful. When it hitches its tail into the crustacean burrows, the travertine sends a sharp, clicking stutter through the flags.

I flatten. My legs fold into the longitudinal grooves beneath my belly, and my skirt settles hard against the mudstone. The mineral back I carry—accumulated over twenty years of lime-wash and slow molt—is indistinguishable from the pavement’s natural weathering. If the raptor squeezes out through the low vent to wait out the mist-pulse, its slate-blue toes will step on me as though I were a knot of river stone. 

It does not come out. The wind is rising. 

The antiphony is shifting now; three summits are talking at once, the microtonal buzz growing louder, more abrasive, until the cartilage in my segmental joints aches with the sheer displacement. The water is beginning to sheet. Down from the eastern face of the monolith, a cold, glass-clear braid—four inches deep and moving at the speed of a dropped stone—cuts across the hexagonal avenue, aimed straight for the taproot.

The braid catches my flank. 

It does not sweep me—I am too heavy, too flat, too wedge-shaped—but it lifts the fringe of my belly-hairs. In the water, the acoustic roar changes. It is no longer a dry percussion; it is liquid, bell-like, amplified ten times by the density of the cold stream. 

Down the dark vertical chute, forty feet below where the water plunges into the Devonian granite, something is answering. 

It is not a sound that travels through air. It is the ringing of bone. The crystalline skulls of the lungless ones, wedged into their wet slots down in the black flues, are taking the chord from the towers and throwing it back up the column of spray. It is a thin, glass chime—pure, steady, holding the resonance long after the gust has clipped off at the rim. 

The chime works on me strangely. My feeding scoops pause. The grease on the mudstone is forgotten. My legs, which know only the lateral navigation of intervals—the C to the left, the E-flat to the right—twitch toward the lip of the hole where the braid drops into the dark. 

I can feel the draft from the taproot: air that has never seen the sun, smelling of wet basalt and the ancient, unchanging grease of the lagoon before the continent climbed into the sky. Down there, the wind cannot shatter a limb. Down there, the moss does not exist, and the cold is not an enemy that strikes from the sky every thirty minutes, but a permanent, still bed.

A chime-louse does not swim. If I step into the flue, the braid will carry me down two hundred feet through the smooth granite throat, tumbling over and over, until my mineral shell shatters against the horizontal ledges or wedges forever into the crevices where the purple salamanders hold their ground. My lime would feed their eggs. The clear, ringing clusters clinging to the Devonian stone would drink the calcium from my belly-plates as they listen to the towers shout through the rock.

The chord breaks. 

A lull hits the amphitheater. The wind lifts from the mouth of the stack; the low C cuts off cleanly, as if a hand had been laid across a drumskin. 

The water braid thins instantly to a trickle. The mud around my legs stops quivering; it thickens, stiffens, grabs at my tarsi with cold, clayey fingers. 

The glass ringing from the deep shafts dies away, smothered by the silence. 

I do not go to the edge. My belly-hairs settle flat against the hexagonal joint. My mineral skirt pulls down, sealing my gills into the remaining film of sulfur water, locking my bulk to the floor before the freezing air can reach my soft parts. 

The sun strikes through a rip in the clouds—blinding, sterile, eighty-hundred-foot light that casts the shadow of the monolith forty yards across the bryophytes, sharp as an iron blade. 

I am stone again. 

I will wait for the sky to fall into the chimney. I will wait for the organ to start, and when the C uncouples the basin from its frost, I will scrape another three inches of gray grease from the ancient floor, moving between the notes, an animate crumb of the mountain that only lives while the house is screaming.
