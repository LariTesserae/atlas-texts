At first, there is only the load. 

It does not present itself as weight or as sorrow; it is simply the condition under which being is permitted. If there were less of it, the marrow would boil and turn to froth against the walls of the bone. If there were an ounce of slack in the ceiling of the air, the blood would rush into the empty space, looking for an exit it has never needed. To exist here is to be pinned—flat, total, and permanent—between the basalt that has forgotten how to cool and the white scream that has forgotten how to fall.

I am not in the open. To be in the open would mean there is an edge to things, and here the edges have been smoothed back into the dough from which they were pinched. 

I find myself low. Very low, where the orange fleece is thickest and warm as a dog’s tongue against the flank. I am wedged into an angle—not a sharp corner, but one of those soft, finger-heaped junctions where the mud of a tenement wall slumps into the living floor of the mountain. For a long time, there is no need to determine whether I am the mortar that has dried or the fat that has settled against it. We are the same temperature: ninety-nine degrees of dark, mineral brine. We smell of the same stewed penny and bruised mint. 

Then, an interior differentiation: the hum.

The wall hums, but it hums with the dull, dead thud of schist that has been deadened by five miles of stone beneath it. My hum is sharper. Inside me, spanning from the base of where a neck would be down into the heavy splay of the ribs that do not move, there is a piece of dry, white timber—not wood, but bone so dense it has no holes for blood, a solid yoke that takes the great fall’s shout and turns it into a slow, circular friction. 

I am meat. 

I know this because I am greasy, and because when the hot moisture sheets down from the broad, kidney-shaped leaves above the door-lintel, it does not soak into me as it does into the alluvial brick; it pools in the palm-hollows of my back. There are five impressions across my flank—wide, blunt troughs where fingers the size of spade-handles once pressed to tamp me into the seam. I do not remember the hands. They are simply the shape of the world’s weight upon me before I cured.

My eyes are horn, two dry seeds set into the front of the wedge. They see nothing that moves, because nothing moves fast enough to displace the flat noon glare. What they register is the thickness of the yellow mist, which never lifts, and the greasy line where the grease of my hide meets the grease of the floor. 

I do not have a voice, but I have a displacement.

Beside me, another wedge—paler than I am, the color of a pig’s stomach washed in river silt—presses its shoulder into mine. It does not look at me; its horn-seeds are pointed at the blank jamb across the lane. But where our skins meet, the tallow gives. It deforms with a thick, slow yield, like lard under a thumb, and in that mash of hide against hide, the wishbone speaks to the wishbone.

It tells me what the cataract is doing three miles above. It tells me that the water has grown heavier by two grains of copper per bucket, that the sheet has thickened by the width of a reed against the third terrace, that the mountain has not yielded an inch to the north, but that the deep silt in the lower tier—thirty levels below, where the houses dip into the drowning vapor—has slipped the distance of a fingernail toward the clouds.

This is the only thing there is to know. There is no news of the sky, because the sky is a scald that never heals. There is no news of yesterday, because a day is merely the time it takes for one drop of grease to slide from the brow-slit to the shoulder.

After a long season—perhaps the time it takes for the orange lichen to grow the depth of a hair—my bulk shifts.

It is not an act of will. Will requires breath, and breath is a gap between two states. Here, there are no gaps. It is hydraulic necessity: the copper has gathered in my lower tissues until my flank drags like lead. My pads—five blunted lobes that know the grain of the cobblestones better than a tongue knows teeth—reach out into the lane.

The lane does not narrow as it goes. I move into it, and as I move, the walls draw back, yet the light remains exactly the same distance from my forehead. Steps rise before me, three shallow shelves of finger-worked clay; I draw my mass up them, feeling the gummy sap bite into the pores of my belly, and when I have cleared the third, I am back upon the terrace from which I began. The space has folded its edges under, like a loaf rising against the side of an earthen pan.

The gray trough is there. 

It does not rush. It turns upon itself, a flat, circular ribbon of thick water that has no beginning and no lip. In the center, coiled in the gray slime like a sleeping intestine, lies the gristle-cord.

I do not fear it. Fear is for things that can be torn apart, and there is nothing here that can be divided from its place. I extend my left pad, the broad, flat heel of it, into the basin.

The cord wakes without waking. It has no head, no mouth, no eyes to turn toward the splash; it simply shortens its coils. It is as thick as a drowned calf, smooth and gristly, pale as pork rind. It rises through the gray skin of the water and lays itself across my foot with the slow, agonizing grip of cold tallow setting in an iron pot.

It constricts. The pressure is glorious.

It does not crush the bone—the wishbone within me is too thick, too seasoned by the roar to yield to flesh—but it wrings the meat. It squeezes the hot, green grease from the grain of my hide. Through the skin of my foot, where the whorls of the ancient hands are deepest, the excess copper is drawn out into the gristle. I can feel the mineral passing from my dense marrow into the cord’s pale marrow—a slow, cold ache that tastes of wet coins and boiled leaves.

The trough-water darkens around us, purpling to the shade of a bruised kidney, then clears as the stone pedestal drinks the stain back into the schist.

The cord slacks. It does not drop away; it slides off me like a loop of wet rope, sinking back into the center of the ring to turn with the turning water.

My weight is lighter by half a stone of copper. The cataract sounds different now—not softer, for nothing can soften that three-mile hammer of boiling froth, but seated differently against my skull. The frequency has shifted from the teeth to the pelvis. 

I turn—not by pivoting, for I have no joints that swing, but by drawing my right flank across the river-smoothed stones until the lane opens its wide, distorted throat before me once more.

I will go back to the corner where the lichen is fleece. 

The other wedge will still be there, its horn-eyes fixed on the mud jamb, its side waiting for the return of my tallow. We will press together until the seam disappears. We will take the sound into our chests, and we will keep the terrace from sliding into the white steam below. 

We have always been here, pressed into the moist, warm bread of the city, and the water will never stop falling, and we will never have to speak.
