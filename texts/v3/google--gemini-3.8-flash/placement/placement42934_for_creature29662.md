At first, it is tempting to imagine being up near the teeth of the rift—perched where the lead-colored cedar crossbars meet the glare, tending the down-nests with sheep-horn spoons, close enough to the lip to smell the wild horse dung and dust blowing across the prairie above. 

Or perhaps down in the middle tiers, one of the soft-footed felt-makers, fingers bleached and pebbled like fish skin, scraping moss from the shale alleys with a flat blade of deer-rib to keep the foot-ways clear.

But the air in this place has a downward weight. It does not invite you to stay high. The heavy cold, stagnant and thick with the lime-fume of the seeps, pulls everything that has mass toward the lowest tufa basins. If you were folded into this chasm long enough, your marrow would settle toward your heels.

I would find myself down where the light stops bouncing—not in the black of an unworked cave, but in that pale, zinc-gray half-clarity forty cubits under the world, where the cellar-steps dip straight into the seep-pools. 

I would not be stone yet. But I would have begun the agreement.

My back would be wedged into the angle of a lower foundation tier, sitting upright in the dry-walled recess behind a shared cistern. Knees drawn to the breast, chin resting on the seasoned cross-peg of dwarf-willow, wrapped in four layers of oiled mouse-pelt that have long since stopped smelling of anything except cold well-bottoms and bitter parsnip brine. My spine would have reached that forty-year stillness: the lowest three vertebrae fused by the stone-water into a single solid knuckle, so that to look up at the swift-shaft, I do not bend my neck; I must tip the whole rib-basket back against the rock.

My hands, though, would still be loose. The mineral spares the hands longest, provided they stay wet.

My work would be the *governing of the drip*.

The snowmelt that seeps down through the forty cubits of karst does not know how to build a wall; left to itself, the calcium would crust into blunt, useless domes, choking the drain-troughs and turning the lower cellars into a clogged slag of yellow calcite. It would seal the ancestors into formless mounds, round as turnip-heaps, impossible to slide beneath a newly sagging lintel. 

Water has no square in its eye. It must be taught where to lay the spar.

I would sit in the cellar beside the running seep, my fingers submerged in the numbing, chalk-thick trickle. With small laths of split willow and dams of fat-kneaded clay, I would steer the mineral wash over the bodies of those who have been bound and brought down to cool. 

When a grandmother is brought to the shelf—knees folded tight, cedar-bark thongs biting into her shoulders—she is too soft, too round for masonry. My task is to lay the reed-troughs across her lap so the water pools only where the block must widen; to bank the riverwort along her flanks so the lime sheets down in flat, sheer courses; to pack tallow under her chin so that when the calcite finishes its three-year work, her skull has become a true-angled corner, clean enough to take the tongue-joint of an alley stair without rocking.

It is slow, quiet masonry, done without an adze. A grain of spar laid down by mid-afternoon; another by twilight. 

Between the drips, there is almost nothing to do but watch the beetles. One will climb over the felt of my knee—its jet carapace cold as a river pebble, its claws making that dry *tick-tick-tick* against the hide—testing the dampness of the willow sluice I have pinned against the wall. If it stays, I know the wood is wet enough not to rot. If it turns back toward the cedar tie-rods above, the seep has shifted an inch to the north, and I must reach into the black shale crack with a horn-scraper to clear the lime-skin that has begun to dam the lead.

The blind mice do not fear this posture. Because my pulse is slow and my feet do not leave the sill for three days together, they treat my shins as they treat the shale foundations. They climb into the folds of my smock to sleep against the sour heat of my groin, their cool, hairless snouts twitching as they feed on the mash-crusts I drop from my bowl. When one grows fat, I do not hunt it; I simply close a hand around its ribs, slide my thumb behind its ears until the neck clicks, and drop it into the grease-pot by my heel.

Sometimes, a click of the tongue will drift down from the fourth tier—a soft, palatal *tsik-chrr* dropping through the hoist-shaft like a dry leaf. 

It is a daughter asking if her mother’s block is level yet.

I do not shout back; to raise the voice here would shake the moisture from the moss ceilings and shower the lower basins with grit. Instead, I reach down with my thumb—broad-tipped, bleached white, calloused only by the scouring of lime-sand—and press the brow of the stone woman beneath the trickle. 

I feel the grain of the calcite. It is hard now, cool as iron, but the forehead still has the slight crown of a living skull; it will need another sixty days of snow-run to take the flat of the mason's level.

I take my bone pick and tap three times against the tufa sill: *wait, wait, wait.*

The reply comes down: the faint rustle of felt against stone as she steps back from the shaft, her slippers polishing the limestone threshold that was her grandfather’s lap, worn smooth by seventy years of bare heels.

In this depth, there is no midday glare, only the pale salt-wash reflected down from the higher walls, weak as tallow-light on ice. You do not think of the sun; you think of tolerances. You think of how much weight the arch of the water-gate can bear before the tongue-joint shears; you think of how many grains of mountain the spring has carried down since the swifts last laid; you feel the calcium stiffening the marrow of your own shins, millimeter by millimeter, climbing toward your hips like a slow, cold frost.

There is no horror in it. It is simply the city’s way of drawing you into its dry-stack. In ten years, perhaps twelve, my hands will stiffen like my knees; I will not be able to clear the willow sluices. Then someone younger—with soft, water-bleached thumbs and eyes that slit against the upper white—will fold my ribs against my knees, lash my wrists to my ankles with wet bark, and set me under the primary trickle where the seep enters the cellars.

They will lay the split reeds across my back so the lime sheets straight and plumb. They will pack tallow around my ears so the tongue-groove forms without a fissure. 

And after three years of the cold water singing its dead, unbreathing song over my forehead, I will be slid into the base of the south tenement, beneath the fourth stair, holding the weight of seven rooms and sixty living people upon my shoulder-blades, keeping the opposing cliffs three spade-blades apart, completely dry, completely silent, and never having seen the wind.
