I would not be out on the open faces. 

Theledges belong to the things with lateral balance, things that can bear the milk-colored abyss yawning at their heels—the pale ungulates whose bones have already become columns, whose eyes have gone flat with staring at nothing. And I would not be in the air, hanging on the thermal flues like the prions, wearing the sky out with rigid primaries. That space is too clean, too theatrical.

I would be in the joints. 

Not in the wide caverns—there are none yet; the mountain is too young for caves, which require the patience of carbonic acid and ten thousand centuries of rot. I would be wedged into the strike-slip seams: the blind, vertical bedding planes where an eight-hundred-ton slab of black gabbro has shoved itself four inches past a band of argillite and then seized, held fast by friction, rock flour, and monstrous, unspent torque.

At first, you would not see me as an animal at all. You would mistake me for a gasket.

My body would be long, laterally compressed until it was scarcely thicker than an axe-head, four or five cubits of hydraulic muscle and sliding dermal plates. The color of wet lead. Not scaled like a snake—scales snag on fresh argillite, and fresh argillite peels tissue like a drawknife. My skin would be more like the rind of an old sea-mammal, but compounded with silica and heavy waxes, greasy to the touch, self-healing, smelling permanently of cold grease, iron filings, and battery wash. 

I would have no use for eyes. In the joint-planes, the darkness is absolute, mineral, and cold. Instead, my skull would be a massive, solid wedge of bone, unstitched by fontanelles, fused directly into the cervical spine. It would be an anvil. When I pressed it against the upper rock face, I would not just hear the continental works running miles below; I would *conduct* them.

That hum is my blood pressure. The 60-cycle vibration that drives the marmots into their rhythmic stupor does not enter me through the ear; it moves through my sternum, through the lateral plates of my ribs, keeping my gut in continuous, slow peristalsis. I would be an organism whose digestion is mechanical, powered by the torque of the continent’s generators. When the plants below throttle down for maintenance—when the low, thrumming B-flat drops toward silence—my pulse would drag. My tissues would stiffen like cooling tallow. I would lie dormant between the slabs, flattened like a dried leech, waiting for the turbines to bite the grid again and kick my heart into gear.

How would I eat? 

I would live on the mountain’s weeping.

The water that slides down through the internal hairline cracks is not groundwater; it is industrial sweat. It enters my seam heavy with copper precipitate, suspended carbon, fluoride, and the fine, dead drift of the surface world that washes into the drainage slots: the cast-off molts of the crane flies, the grease-scum shed by the marmots' coats, the lime-dust knocked loose by the hooves above. 

My mouth would be situated entirely on my underside, a soft, muscular suction disk armed not with teeth, but with rows of horny, replaceable scrapers made of hardened keratin. I would move an inch an hour, creeping along the wet seam, grazing the black slime that forms where the aerosol sulfur meets the newly crushed mineral margins. It is a rich, toxic soup, dense with synthetic nitrogen and iron oxides. I would filter it through my throat, laying down layers of dense, heavy bone, packing my marrow with lead and barium, becoming steadily heavier, colder, and more like the rock that houses me.

My feet would not have claws. Claws lever things away from stone. I would have six or eight pairs of broad, pneumatic pads—thick discs of tough, glandular tissue that can generate negative pressure against wet rock. I would not walk; I would inch, adhering by vacuum, sliding my flattened ribcage through gaps so narrow that a falling coin would stick between them.

The ungulates know I am down here. They feel me through their chipped, iron hooves. When they stand for hours on those handspan ledges, grinding their jaws in rotary circuits, they are not only watching the fog—they are listening to the displacement of moisture beneath their footing. They know that where the stone is silent, it is dangerous; but where there is the low, wet, rhythmic squelch of my passage through the joint, the rock is still holding its breath. The friction is stable.

My only terror would not be falling. In a crack four inches wide, you cannot fall. 

My terror would be the *snap*.

Because the mountain is embryonic, the internal stress does not leak out slowly; it gathers until the crystal lattice gives up all at once. An hour before a face shears, the moisture in the seam begins to boil cold—microscopic cavitation bubbles popping against my skin as the gabbro stretches. The 60-cycle engine hum goes sour, rising into a shrill, singing overtone, like a piano wire pulled by an engine.

When that song starts, I would move. Not with the deliberate, grazing crawl, but with a sudden, violent hydraulic spasm, expelling the water from my pads, snapping my lateral plates together like a closed deck of cards, letting myself drop. 

I would drop straight down through the darkness of the crack, thirty, fifty, a hundred yards, scraping the narrow walls, shedding gray grease and skin-shavings against the razor edges, falling toward the heat, toward the place where the seam closes into an unyielding pinch. 

And then, above me: the dry, flat crack of the gunshot. 

The sound of two hundred thousand tons of virgin stone deciding it can no longer bear to be a mountain, parting along the argillite line with a violence that turns the trapped moisture into instant, scalding mist. The shudder would knock the wind out of my meat. Stones as large as locomotives would settle an eighth of an inch, groaning with the weight of the continent.

Then the silence would return. The steady, leaden drip. The smell of scorched stone and ozone.

I would extend my pads into the newly exposed surfaces—surfaces that have never seen light, never felt the mist, still smelling of the deep, abiotic mantle. I would taste the iron on the fresh fracture face before the first lichen could even drop a spore. 

I would press my forehead back against the black wall. I would find the hum again—low, heavy, steady as a furnace—and begin to scrape.
