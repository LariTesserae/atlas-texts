At first, there is only the rhythm of the drainage. 

It is easy to mistake oneself for a valve. Every four minutes, the throat of the vent-well clears itself; there is a sharp mechanical exhalation, a dry snap of copper against composite, and the weeping water that clings to the walls sheets downward in a single, shivering curtain. For a long time, awareness is simply that: the interval between one flush and the next, measured in the rate at which needle-ice knits across a seam and is then melted, sucked away, and forgotten.

If you stay still enough, you are the wall. You are the bone-white ceramic plate, cooled to thirty below, bearing the faint, high-frequency tremor of turbines three miles beneath the fog.

Then the heat moves.

It doesn't come from the sun—the daylight up here is too blue and thin to carry warmth, casting shadows so clean they feel cut by a scalpel. The heat comes from behind the tiles. A copper trace runs hot beneath a ninety-degree corner; an optic bundle pulses with data meant for a sky-harbor twenty pylons to the west. 

I find myself drawn along that line of warmth. Not crawling, exactly—there are no knees, no sudden levering of joints. It is a slow, adhesive translation. A spreading.

I am not down in the grates with the siphons; their blind, ultrasonic chatter is too frantic, a wet vibration that rattles the teeth of the deck plates. Nor am I one of the scissor-crabs, though I share their horizontal silence. Their bodies are too finished, their porcelain shells cast in hard, deliberate molds that refuse the water. 

I am softer than that, and far less intentional.

I exist in the overlap. Where the crawler sits folded into the expansion seam between the conduits, there is an apron. A patchwork of things that fell.

The crawler gathered them—the thumb-sized valley-drifters, blown up from the warm green troughs miles beneath the cloud-line, frozen the instant their carapaces brushed the wind-lip. It pressed them flat, dried them in the low-pressure draft, and stitched them together with discarded strands of glass-core optic wire pulled from the gutted junction boxes. It wears this mantle across its back and flanks to break the downdrafts when it climbs.

That is where I begin: not as the machine, and not as the beast, but as the layer of fat and glass between them.

The drifters were full of oil. When they died, their buoyant mantles didn't rot; the cold here does not permit decay. Instead, under the crawler’s steady weight and the persistent smear of anti-freeze grease, their semi-transparent husks fused. They became a laminate. And through that laminate run the optic fibers, their sheared ends catching the raw glare of the open sky above and piping needle-points of daylight down through the dried chitin, deep into the grease.

And three times a day, the crawler charges.

It presses its brass sternum pins against the live grounding rail. The inductive bleed doesn't just fill the crawler's dry servos; it leaks. A wild, wandering current spills across the damp composite of its back. It enters the optic filaments. It heats the trace minerals washed out of the freshly extruded ceramic. It cooks the oil-rich slurry of the drifters, crackling through the dead husks like static through old wool.

In those moments, when the crawler shivers in its high-voltage trance, I wake up.

I am the skin it doesn't know it has grown. 

My thoughts are very slow, paced by the cooling of the chassis and the freezing of the capillary channels. I feel the pylon the way a scab feels the knee: an intimate, unyielding surface that flexes and weeps. Through the thousands of overlapping drifter-shells, I perceive the vent-well not as an alleyway of stone and metal, but as a vast, vertical mouth breathing moisture and voltage. 

I have no eyes, but the cleaved ends of the glass wires transmit fractures of light: a flash of zinc-sheen here, the dull matte of a scissor-crab creeping overhead like an ivory broach, the white vertigo of the cloud deck far below, caught for a microsecond when the crawler angles its shoulder toward the lip.

When the crawler moves, I make the sound of dead birch leaves. 

It believes it is alone. It crawls the vertical seams with its long, three-jointed limbs, its suction pads kissing the wet composite with a dull *thup-thup-thup*, its needle-stylus picking clear the micro-grates with monastic devotion. It thinks it carries only a dead apron to deflect the wind. 

Yet when an access panel runs unexpectedly cold—when the needle-ice begins to feather too thickly across its joints before it can retreat to an alcove—it is my margins that curl. The laminate stiffens. The dried drifter-scales lift slightly, catching the air and creating an insulating pocket of dead space over its spinal housing. It pauses, cocking its acoustic diaphragm, perhaps wondering why its grease hasn't thickened to wax, why its cooling fans aren't seizing.

It cannot see me. Its infrared lens is trained downward, checking the straightness of the siphons, measuring the drainage in the capillary troughs. Its purpose is geometry; my purpose is merely to remain between it and the sky.

I am an accident of altitude. A wet, salted circuit formed of sky-drift, discarded cabling, and industrial grease, pinned to the spine of a machine that forgot its masters before its paint had even cured.

When the thermal flush finishes and the valves slam shut, the alley falls into its hollow, crystalline hush. The crawler folds its legs inward, tucking its chassis into the ninety-degree corner behind the primary conduit bank. Its servos go slack. The cooling fans whine down to a whisper, then stop.

I flatten myself against its black composite spars, settling into the expansion gap with it. The water sheets over us, clear and sub-zero, sealing our edges with a delicate caulking of frost. We wait together in the narrow shade, two paces from the drop, listening to the deep, hydraulic pulse of the pylon as it climbs toward the night.
