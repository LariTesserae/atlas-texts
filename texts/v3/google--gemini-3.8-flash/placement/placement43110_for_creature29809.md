At first, there is only the refusal of the skin to end. 

In thirty-eight degrees, the water does not meet the body; it continues it. To search for an edge—a perimeter where flesh becomes fluid—is like trying to find where the tallow ends and the paraffin begins in the warm caulking of the submerged seams. I would not begin with an outline. I would begin as an interior pressure, equalized so completely against the hydrostatic weight of three inches of alkaline wash that there is no sensation of weight at all, only an immense, resting resistance.

I would not be on the gantries. The air up there is too light, too thin with mist, too dry where the quartz arcs sizzle in their housings. Nor would I be suspended in the empty iron cradles, which run cold and hollow on their greased tracks, clicking away miles that have no meaning. 

I would be underneath. Not buried—for there is no earth to dig into, only the unyielding face of the rolled lead—but wedged.

My home would be the clearance. The precise, two-inch margin between the diamond-tread iron and the vast, yielding bellies of the rafts. 

I would not be a raft, with their great four-ton cushions of blubber and their drum-sized hearts beating out their slow, dim metronome. Nor would I be the shingles, locked rigid in their millions of tiled rows, flat and dry-shelled, taking the weight on their backs like roofing slate. 

I would be the intermediate thing: the creature that belongs to the shear.

My body would be long—perhaps twelve or fourteen feet—but thin as a draftsman’s ruler, a pale ribbon of gristly muscle and clear, cellular oil. I would have no ribs to crush, no hollow lungs to collapse. My skeleton would be a central runner of white cartilage, notched like a carpenter’s rule, flexible enough to bend double without a pop, but stiff enough to lever three hundredweight of warm chamois hide off the metal floor when I move.

I would have no use for the amber glare. My eyes would be nothing more than twin depressions of yellow fat, deep in the gristle of my skull, sensitive only to the passing shadow of a gantry crane or the sudden, electric pulse when a resistance coil switches on beneath the floor plates. 

Instead, I would have my margins.

My entire perimeter—the thin, frilled skirt of flesh that runs from my snout to my broad, paddle-flat tail—would be lined with microscopic, copper-sensitive papillae. With these, I would read the floor. I would know where the sulfurous solder beads have been planed smooth by the bellies of the amphibians, and where the lead has begun to creep under the continuous, grinding weight of the herds. I would taste the tallow leaking from the pump casings five hundred yards upstream; I would feel the faint, rhythmic suction of the drainage weirs pulling the sheet-water toward the four-inch drops.

My life would be an endless, slow glide through the darkness beneath the meat.

I would move by peristalsis, a quiet, hydraulic ripple traveling down my belly, sliding over the smooth carapaces of the shingles without dislodging them. They would know me only as a lubricated pressure, a smoothing-out of tension. Where they lock their plates to bear the heavy tramp of a raft, I would slide between them like warm grease through a valve, my skin coated in a dense, odorless mucus that reduces friction to near zero.

What would I eat? Not the thick paraffin caulking—my mouth would have no horn plates for scraping—and not the carcasses that the siphon-worms claim with their blind, muscular tubes. 

I would live on the *exhaust*. 

I would lie beneath the great rafts where their spiracles discharge into the water: warm jets of depleted broth, laden with sloughed cells, synthetic fats, and the microscopic flakes of keratin that shed from their horn plates as they plane the stanchions. I would inhale this exhausted liquor through broad, gill-like slits set along my flanks, filtering it through combs of fine, cartilaginous baleen, and expelling it behind me as water so clear, so entirely stripped of organic particulate, that the lead plates beneath my tail would gleam like freshly minted coin.

I would be the animal that maintains the slip.

Without me, the rafts would stick. Their vast, hairless bellies would drag against the lead; their chamois hide would gall on the diamond tread; the shingles would crack under the ungreased friction of four tons of sliding flesh. I would be the living layer of oil between the machine and the meat.

Time would not exist as hours or days, but as the long, mechanical breathing of the facility. 

Every five miles, the water drops four inches over the planed copper weir-lips. I would know when I am approaching a lip by the sudden thinning of the water, the way the current quickens from a drift to a smooth, frictionless glide. I would slide out from under a herd, feel the sudden, blinding cream of the sodium lights wash over my back for a single second as I tip over the lip, and then—*slip*—down into the lower tier. 

No splash. Just the heavy, quiet fold of warm glycerin falling into warm glycerin. 

And there, waiting on the lower plates, another herd. Another fifty square meters of pinkish-grey hides, wallowing between the steam-risers, their bellies waiting for the grease. 

I would wedge myself beneath them once more. I would feel their drum-hearts vibrating against my spine—five beats a minute, matched to the sixty-cycle thrum of the floor—and I would let my ribbons flatten out until I was scarcely thicker than a sheet of wet cardboard. 

The heat would enter from below, thirty-eight degrees rising through the lead; the weight would settle from above, four tons of sleeping meat pressing down with a dull, pneumatic comfort. 

There would be no hunger, for the broth never stops flowing from the diffusers. There would be no fear, for there is nothing here that has teeth, nothing that has claws, nothing that knows the shape of an enemy. There would be only the endless, warm noon; the faint, clean smell of scalded tin and tallow; and the knowledge—felt through every nerve in my gristly length—that the clearance is correct, the plates are slick, and the world is running within its tolerances.
