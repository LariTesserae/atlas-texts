At first, there is only the refusal of the eye to find an edge. 

You think you are standing, because there is a horizon, but the horizon is only a convention of the tiles—an orange grid running straight into the white milk of the air until the squares become too small for the cornea to divide. You think you have an inside, but the light has already reached it; without shadows, there is no back to your ribs, no dark pocket behind your knees. The glare does not warm your skin from above; it rises out of the wet terracotta with the exact same weight as it falls from the zenith, so that your flesh feels caught in a clamp of warm alabaster.

I would not be on my feet. To stand in this place is an error of leverage. The beasts with the wooden hooves can bear it because their bones are pegged together like the sills, but even they are anchored only by the turnip-heavy water in their guts. If you stand, the distance between your brow and the three millimeters of warm grease on the floor is four feet of vacant, boiling light—an intolerable lever.

I would be down where the grain meets the mud. 

Not in the water pans—they belong to the leeches, whose broad, tallow skirts have already claimed the flatness—but in the seam itself. Specifically, in the long, half-inch trench where an eighty-foot baulk of pale heartwood meets four hundred consecutive edges of pork-colored brick.

There is a shear there. It is the only true event in the world. 

Wood and fired earth do not drink at the same speed. The timbers, hewn with rhythmic, scallop-edged adze marks that never rot, are perpetually gorging themselves on the water skin, swelling across their grain, straining against the dry wooden pegs that pin them to the tier below. But the bricks are stubborn; they were cooked in some forgotten pit until their clay turned to dense, unyielding slag, and they will not widen by the width of a hair. 

Where they meet, along that hairline ditch filled with wet shell-paste, there is an immense, silent groan. It is not loud enough to disturb the saucers of pottery hovering in the air, but it travels through the soles of things like a toothache in the marrow.

That is where I would take form: not as an intruder, but as a wedge driven to keep that groan from becoming a split.

***

My body is not soft like the smooth-shouldered ones who knead the walls, though I share their color—the dull, greyish white of mutton tallow that has been left in a bucket of lime water. Nor am I timber, though my hide has the hard, cross-hatched grain of boxwood that has been boiled in oil until it cannot splinter. 

I am four-cornered, low, and broad as a mason’s float. My underside is ribbed with six pairs of blunt, horn-tipped lobes—not legs with knees that bend, but thick, hydraulic cams that turn slowly against the brick, drawing my mass along the joint at the rate of three tiles a week. 

I have no neck. My head is simply the front edge of my wedge, beveled like a broad chisel, shod with a thick plate of translucent amber-colored horn that is worn smooth by sixty miles of mortar-grit. 

I do not look at the sky. There is nothing in the sky but the four hovering beetles stationed over the corners of empty rooms, their violet muscles ticking—*chut, chut*—twelve seconds apart. I look down, through two flat, horizontal slits set flush with my horn plate. My eyes have no moisture to lose; they are discs of oiled mica, yellow-rimmed, focused entirely on the sixty grains of crushed cockle-shell that lie three inches ahead of my bevel.

My mouth is an iron-hard slot beneath the horn. I do not eat the ferns; their wet, leathery leather is for the barrel-bellied grazers who need ballast. I eat the seam. 

As I slide along the line where the pale oak presses against the terracotta, the lime-paste bulges upward, squeezed out by the swelling of the wood. It is cool, sour, smelling of river bottom and wet feathers. I take it in through my slot—not with a bite, but with the steady, draw-knife pull of a planer taking a shaving from a pine board. Within my middle, where a furnace would be if this were a world of fire, there is only a slow, cold millstone: two plates of porous grit that grind the paste against my own swallowed ballast, crushing the shell-fragments into a grease so fine it passes directly into my hide.

What I leave behind me is not a ditch, but the joint reset. The lime is packed level again, smoothed by the long, heavy drag of my tallow-belly, sealed with a skin of cold oil that keeps the timber from drinking too fast and bursting its peg.

***

Sometimes the tapir-beasts pass over me. 

A wooden pad, wide as a trenching spade and heavy with fifty stone of waterlogged timber-bone, will come down directly upon my back. There is no pain in it. The sound is the only thing—a flat, hollow *tock* that travels through my shell into the brickwork, rattling the ribbon-leeches in their pan three yards away. 

For an hour, while the beast stands motionless, staring into the shadowless distance with its jaw dripping turnip-scented bilge, I am pinned. My cams do not struggle against the weight. I am built to be pinned. My horn plate bites deeper into the shell-grit; the oil from my flanks spreads out into the three millimeters of water, creating a wide, rainbow-edged lens that refuses to ripple. 

Beneath its pad, I can feel the beast’s pulse. It does not beat like a heart; it is a slow, capillary suction, a dull drawing up of moisture from the joint through its hooves, into its belly of rolled fronds, and up toward the nostrils that will discharge it miles away when the timber parches. We are two pumps working the same floor: it drinks the water to keep the boards from floating; I drink the lime to keep them from breaking apart.

When it lifts its foot, the release is slow. The suction makes a small, flat *smack*, like a wet hide being lifted from a tanner’s bench. The water snaps back over my back with that quick, glass-like seal. A single yellow hair from its fetlock—stiff as a brass wire—remains stuck in the tallow of my flank, where it will stay for forty years before the lime eats it away.

***

The heat does not permit sleep, because sleep requires an elsewhere, and here there is only the present state of the masonry. But there are hours—or centuries, for the beetle above the pork-brick wall has turned on its axis only three times since I reached this timber—when the world achieves an equilibrium so tight that motion becomes an impertinence.

In those hours, I stop my cams. My chisel-nose rests against a dry peg of wild cherry that has held these two floorboards together since the adze was lifted. 

The water around my rim is so still that a microscopic spore from a distant fern, falling into the pan three inches away, does not drift. It sits on the meniscus like a copper bead on grease. 

From four miles off, down the long corridor of empty, four-foot walls where the fingers of the smooth-shouldered ones are working, comes the wet, heavy *schlock* of a thumb resetting a groove. It takes twelve seconds for the sound to reach me through the air, but through the tiles—through the bottomless, wet tiers of fired mud—it arrives instantly: a tiny, dull kick against my belly.

Then, deep down—deeper than the third tier, deeper than the amber light that smells of mud—the canal-sized thing turns its elbow.

The floor does not rise. The joinery is too true for that. But the pressure changes. The water in my pan humps itself upward by half a millimeter, clinging to the ridges of mortar with a sudden, desperate surface tension, rounding into a long, liquid blister that catches the white-gold sky and turns the whole world into a flat pane of brass.

My horn plate creaks. The cherry-wood peg groans against my nose. 

I take one half-grain of crushed shell into my slot. I grind it. I excrete the clear grease. 

The meniscus snaps back. The tension passes into the next square, and the next, traveling out toward the vanishing point where the boards tilt thirty degrees without spilling a drop, on and on toward the edge that was never drawn, keeping the afternoon from falling through.
