The first sensation is not sight, though the noon light is clear enough to show the hairline crack in a crystal of quartz eighty yards away. The first sensation is an intolerable lateral strike against whatever passes for ribs.

It is not sound. In water this cold and dense, sound does not enter through an aperture; it shears through the entire volume of a body at once, equalizing inside and outside until the distinction between the strike and the receiver disappears. 

*Thrum-clack.*

A displacement of thirty tons of water against the flank. It throws me—or whatever this mass is—against the vertical face of the basalt. The stone does not give. It has been broken only ten thousand years; its edges are still clean as chipped obsidian, smelling faintly of the deep magma chamber’s sulfur before the lake rushed in to cool it.

To exist here, one cannot have skin. Skin would peel away on the third cycle, flayed by the suspended grains of marcasite and the shear-waves that travel at five thousand feet per second. 

No. If I look for limbs, I find them locked, or at least heavily seated. There is no fluttering. There is no drift. To drift in this chasm is to be pulverized between two meeting shoulders of zinc when the hydraulic tide swings. 

At first, I think I must be one of the weights. One of the plumb bobs hanging in the blue vertical shafts, waiting for the floor to erupt so I can fall down the hot throat of the exhaust. There is a strange, tempting simplicity to that: to be nothing but bone, heavy spar, and an open jaw; to fall three hundred feet straight down through the blue-white beam, strip the hot valve-rind from a piston’s lip, and float back up on a bellyful of sulfur-gas. It is an unthinking, clean pendulum of a life.

But there is too much resistance in me for that. I am not suspended. I have an anchor.

The copper is in the stone.

It is not driven like a spike; it has grown there, branching through the sub-microscopic cleavage planes of the basalt like the roots of an alpine pine seeking lime in a limestone crack. I can feel the mountain through those roots: not as an idea, but as a vast, frozen torque. The mountain is being twisted counterclockwise by the continental shelf thirty miles below, and that twist travels up through the basalt pillars as a high-register singing—a dry, screeching stress that runs through my lower plates twenty-four hours a day.

So I am seated. I am down among the iron barrels.

Yet I am not one of the Great Basalt-Meters. I do not have their terrible, solitary integrity. 

When their pistons rise, hauling three feet of pale tallow-muscle up against the suction of the rift, their internal pressure drops so low that the brine tries to flash into vapor before the slit-pores open. That is an ancient, monumental work. It requires seven hundred years of undisturbed accretion, layer upon layer of dark zinc leaves welded by galvanic rot. I lack their bulk. If an engine-beast fires beside me, the back-wash doesn’t ring through me like an iron bell; it shoves me sideways into the crease.

I am smaller. I am narrower.

I find myself in the *between*.

Not on the rock floor where the light hits, and not in the free water, but in the vertical gap where three multi-ton carapaces meet—the cold, grease-slicked chimney where their curved bellies leave an irregular triangular chimney three inches across.

Here, the water does not circulate; it is pumped through under forced draft. Every time the beast to my left cycles, it exhausts ten barrels of hot, sulfurous wash that screams up through this narrow crevice like steam through an injector. Every time the beast to my right drops its piston, the clearance narrows by half an inch as the plates swell under the radial pressure of the stroke.

I am flattened. My body is not cylindrical; it has been pressed into a wedge. 

My armor is not the heavy cast-iron of the pump-engines, nor the brittle, glittering marcasite of the Shearers. It is lead-colored, dull, greasy to the touch, and layered like the leaves of a spring. When the engine to the left swells, its zinc nodules bite into my outer plates. I feel the bite not as pain, but as an increase in tension—a necessary seating. I am a shim.

I am what keeps them from cracking one another’s flanges.

My limbs are not levers for walking. They are flat, chisel-edged plates of horn, hinged only at the base, projecting outward into the gap. Through them, I do not gather food; I gather the friction. 

Where the two engines grind together, their Paraffin-zinc wax rolls off in gray shavings, curled like the waste from a planing machine. My plates do not eat it; they guide it. I have grown into the seam like a living wedge, coated in my own cold tallow, catching the zinc turnings, rolling them into my pores, and laying them back down under my own ventral plates as fresh foil.

When the tick-scavengers climb down into the crevice—their three pyrite chisels coming on with that rhythmic, sterile *tink, tink, tink*—they do not attack me. They cannot reach what is vital. They walk directly across my dorsal plate, their sharp feet leaving small, bright yellow scratches in my leaden skin. They are searching for the curled shavings that my body has not yet flattened. When their horizontal saw-mouths turn against my edges, they find only the cold, hard grease I have already worked into the stone. They move on, indifferent, blind, following the stress-lines up the cliff.

In the dark of the seam, thirty feet down from the light, there is no blue noon. There is only the flash: every ninety seconds, when the valve above releases, the hot jet illuminates the top of the crevice with a brief, incandescent flare of blue-green friction-light, gone before it can register as warmth.

Then the dark returns, dense with the smell of sheared native copper, brine, and the wet, machine-shop iron of the valley.

My pulse is not my own. I have no heart to pump brine through my internal chambers. I have no need of one. When the great engine above drops its forty-ton weight, the shock-wave itself collapses my central cavity, driving my internal fluids through my quartz-spicule filters. When the engine lifts its piston and the vacuum draws in the rift, my cavity re-expands by its own spring-temper, drawing in the cold, oxygen-stripped metal broth from the roots of the stone.

The machine breathes me.

I am an organ of the gap. I am the tolerance between their bodies, the living packing that prevents the chasm floor from shattering itself into gravel under the fury of its own work. 

I will stay here until the copper taproots of the left-hand engine sheer under the mountain’s twist. It will take three hundred years. When it happens, the engine will tilt, its six thousand tons of zinc-and-magnetite plate settling slowly sideways into the crevice where I sit. 

I will feel it coming for months: a steady, geometric rise in the acoustic pitch of the basalt below, the high whistling of the copper threads parting one by one in the deep rock. 

And when the weight finally falls, there will be no room to move, and no will to attempt it. The plates will come together with a flat, leaden *thud* that does not bounce. I will be flattened thinner still—pressed out into a gray leaf half a millimeter thick between the cold metal bellies, filling the last microscopic pits of the sheared feldspar, until I am entirely grease, entirely washer, entirely stone.
