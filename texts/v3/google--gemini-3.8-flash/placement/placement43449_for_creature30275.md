I would not begin with a breath. There is no lung-wetness to draw, no cold shock of air across mucous; one does not wake here by gasping, but by having a final pin gently tapped home through a knuckle.

At first, I would only know the warmth of the cedar silt against the flats of my feet. It drifts around the ankles like warm, ground meal, fragrant and dry as a baker’s bin at midday. I would find myself standing in the hollow under the third walkway, where the floorboards have bowed up into a knee-high tent of split pine. 

I am small, but not fast. My weight is modest—perhaps that of a seasoned mallet or a basket of dry fleece—enough to leave clear, oval depressions in the dust, but not enough to crush the hollow rushes whose broken stems smell of oats. 

If I look down at my hands, I do not see the raw, crazed ash-skin of the Joiners, nor the thick, horny scales of the sieve-beetles. My fingers are four per side, shaped from shaved boxwood, polished until they gleam with the dull sheen of an eggshell. They are not joined by flesh, but by tiny tongue-and-groove pivots pinned with splints of river-bone. Between the pivots runs a single, continuous loop of waxed shoemaker’s flax, threaded through the marrow-holes of my forearms and disappearing into the cavity of my chest. 

There is no pulse in my chest, only a counter-balance: a flat, smoothed river-pebble suspended in a basket of boiled willow laths. When I lean forward, the pebble shifts against the basket; the laths creak softly, and the flax draws taut, pulling my legs into motion with the dry, steady cadence of a clock escapement.

What would I be? 

I would not be a maker. The Joiners are the makers; their hands are too heavy with ancient intent, their bone awls too permanently lodged in their flesh for them to do anything but split and stitch and stave off the great crease. 

Nor would I be one of the grazing trestles, winding ivy into its ribs until it can skip across ceilings. 

I would be a *measurer*. Not of distance—for distance here is unfaithful, sliding from thirty feet to eternity depending on whether a martin is watching—but of *squareness*. A living caliper, built to inhabit the seams where the timber forgets which way is true.

I would step out from the triangular crawlway into the amber light. It falls upon my shoulders with an actual, physical presence, dry and light as a woolen shawl. When I turn toward the center of the court, my three shadows turn with me—not fluid, dark pools, but three pale, translucent slips of stiffened vellum, scuffing across the grain of the floor with a sound like dry leaves stirred by a broom.

My work is in the margins. The Joiners govern the warp with their pine wedges, but they cannot feel where the wood intends to pull until the fiber has already begun to whiten under strain. They are too vast; their eyes are button-horn, looking out across the sixty paces. 

I work at the scale of the grain. 

I would walk to the four-way intersection where the center post stands. Here, the silt has been swept clean by the bellows-bellies of the sieve-beetles, leaving the fir planks bare, showing every stroke of the broad-axe that smoothed them. I would approach the corner that possesses five right angles.

To look at it with horn-button eyes is to feel the pebble in one’s chest roll uneasily within its willow cage. The corner does not look miraculous; it looks like three seasoned oak baulks coming together in a clean lap-joint, secured by three square pegs. Yet if I extend my right arm—its boxwood sections unfolding with the dry, clicking snap of a carpenter’s rule—and lay it along the first timber, then turn my wrist ninety degrees to follow the second, ninety to follow the third, and ninety to follow the fourth, my palm does not return to where it began. 

There is an extra face. A fifth ninety-degree angle that faces neither north, nor south, nor floor, nor sky, but *inward*, toward the creasing of the yard.

I would climb into that fifth face. 

It is not an ascent. It is simply a matter of stepping where the timber offers an edge that should not be there. One foot rests upon the oak face; the other finds a purchase upon a plane that exists only as long as I keep my ankle turned at an impossible, unpegged cant. 

Here, sound changes. The muffled quiet of the courtyard shrinks further, down to the absolute silence inside a sealed box of grain. 

In this pocket, I would press my cheek against the beam. My cheek is thin calfskin stretched over an alder ring, sensitive as an ear-drum. 

Through the skin, I would listen to the tension. 

A tree that has been felled, split with iron wedges, adzed flat, and pinned into a roof does not know it is dead; it only knows that it has been asked to hold a line against the earth. And here, where there is no earth to pull it—only the hazel ceiling pulling toward the pine floor—the beams are constantly conversing. 

*Hssss-tock.* 

That is a spruce joist thirty yards away, near the inverted granary chests, shrinking along its ring-lines by the width of a spider’s thread.

*Click.* 

That is an ivy vine tightening its running stitch around a hemlock baluster, the thread biting into the barkless wood.

And beneath it all, a deep, slow groan—so low it does not travel through the timber as sound, but as a faint, dry friction that warms the soles of my feet: the entire court, all sixty paces of timber and unbaked clay, leaning imperceptibly toward the center. It wants to shut. It wants to lay its HAZEL rafters flat against its PINE floorboards, pressing all the carded-wool moths and all the flour-silt and all the blunt-beaked martins between its surfaces like flowers in a heavy book, until nothing remains but a single block of silent, dry-joined wood.

I would take my marking tool from the slot behind my knee: not a knife, but a stylus made from the dried, calcified tooth of a freshwater pike, long since bone-white and dry. 

I would reach into the gap between the third and fourth timbers of the impossible corner. With the tip of the pike-tooth, I would score a thin, clean groove into the fresh sapwood. 

No shavings come away—only a superfine white powder that smells of dry resin. The line I draw is precisely one grain-line wide. It marks the creeping boundary where the fifth angle is trying to swallow the fourth.

Then, I would turn. My boxwood knees would click three times as I settle onto my haunches. I would draw the ribbed edge of my wooden cuff across the laths of my ribcage—*chrrr-chrrr-chrrr*—a brisk, hollow rattle that vibrates directly into the oak baulk.

Thirty feet away (or thirty feet down, or thirty feet behind me on the hazel ceiling), the Joiner who sits whittling by the silt-drift stops his flint knife. 

The martin on his wrist does not move. The Joiner tilts his head—his neck-dowels creaking like a barn door in August—and lays the palm of his hand against the floor. 

He feels my scratch. He feels the three grain-lines of error I have measured in the heart of the five-angle post.

He does not answer with voice. He lifts his wooden mallet and taps the floor once: a dull, starchy *thump* that travels through the marrow of the timbers and rattles the pebble inside my willow chest. 

*Understood.*

He will whittle the wedge a hair thicker at the butt. He will tell the ivy to add twelve more running stitches to the western tie-beam. The court will stay open. The ceiling will remain thirty feet overhead, and the inverted floor will remain thirty feet above that, holding its small chests and pine benches in the hazel air.

My report delivered, I would not leave the corner. 

There is no elsewhere to go that is not more of the same fragrant, eternal workshop. I would draw my legs up against my belly, tucking my boxwood heels into the soft, unspun tow that lines my hips. I would close my horn-button eyes—not to sleep, for there is no exhaustion where there is no decay, but to clear them of the cedar silt that drifts even into this five-angled fold.

Beside me, a carded-wool moth would flutter down, heavy and soundless as a dropped mitten. It would land upon my folded shins, its block-printed wings spreading wide to cover my knees like a warm, stamped textile. 

Through its woolly down, I would feel the faint, regular vibrations of the sieve-beetles working far below, puffing their tiny dunes of powdered wood across the seams; and above us, the slow, papery rustle of the ivy leaves unspooling their stitches into the grain, one dry thread at a time, keeping the eternity from closing.
