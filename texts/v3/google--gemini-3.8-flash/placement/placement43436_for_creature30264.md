At first, there is only the sensation of being held in a vice that never loosens, only changes its pitch.

To exist here, you cannot have an inside that is softer than your outside. If you had an interior, the pressure of the ambient dry heat would collapse it like a blown glass bulb under a heel. So I am dense. I am all surface, folded tightly against itself until there is no air left between the laminations.

I am not walking. I have no legs, no swinging crane hooks, no counterweights to crack against the decking. My motion is slower than that, and far more stubborn. 

I find myself in the clearances. 

In every vast machine, even one choked into petrifaction by the black vines, there are the tolerances: the sixteenth of an inch between a bronze bushing and a steel pin; the hairline gap where two boiler plates overlap; the narrow slot where an expansion joint was meant to slide three inches to the left in the summer of a world that ended before the rust turned purple. 

I am what fills the tolerances.

I begin, perhaps, as a smear. A deposit of powdered graphite, shed mica-scales, and the tallow that has dripped from a weeping overhead manifold and baked onto a beam until it turned into a stiff, waxy amber. For a cycle, or forty cycles, that is all I am: a residue, an inert crust baked against an I-beam, vibrated by the subterranean ticking of the floor.

Then the heat finds an edge.

When the furnace network deep below shifts its choke—when the under-floor cherry glow brightens from dull ember to the sharp, dry red of an open flue—the tallow doesn’t melt. It can’t; it has been carbonized too many times. Instead, it tightens. The long, dry chains of hydrocarbons pull against the mica plates embedded in them. 

I feel myself curl.

It is not an impulse; it is a shear. One side of me expands because it touches the hot iron of the gantry footing; the other side stays rigid because it faces the dead, airless black above. That difference in temperature is my muscle. It bows me upward into a shallow crescent, three fingers wide, twenty inches long, hard as horn, gray-black and smelling of parched fat and scorched chicory.

I am a wedge. A living shim.

When I bow, my leading edge—beveled thin by centuries of rubbing against vibrating cast iron—slips forward a millimeter into the seam between two deck-plates. Then the temperature equalizes slightly; the bow flattens; my tail drags behind me, pulled forward by the friction of my own grain.

*Scritch.*

It is almost entirely quiet. The sound of a dry thumbnail running over coarse sandpaper, instantly smothered by the acoustic baffle of the fibrous hedge that leans against the iron three inches above my back.

I do not look for food, because there is nothing to digest. What I crave is bearing-load. 

I move toward the places where the machine is trying to crush itself.

Where a root the size of a water pipe has forced its way into a pillow-block bearing, prying the housing apart until the bolts are singing like tight piano wire, I feel the strain through the deck. It travels through the iron not as sound, but as an electrical prickle, an ache of crystalline tension. The slate-sheets below feel the whole upland breathe, but they only care about the tearing, the open spark. I care about the pinch.

I work myself into the narrowing gap. 

It takes days. My body—a stack of hundreds of micro-thin leaves of compressed carbon fiber, felted with fine copper wool that was combed out of some long-pulverized armature—works into the seam one leaf at a time. The iron plates are grinding together under a load of forty tons, shuddering with the slow, convective heave of the basin. Every time the plates yawn open by the width of a hair, one of my laminates slides forward. When the plates snap shut again, they do not crush me; they compact me.

They squeeze the old tallow through my pores. They weld my graphite flakes into a tighter, slicker matrix. 

Inside the pinch, the heat is incredible. It is the dry, dead heat of pure mechanical friction, hot enough to make my copper filaments glow with a brief, invisible cherry in the dark. That heat is my breath. While I am pinched, I am awake. While the mountain of iron presses down on me, trying to weld itself across the gap, I am lubricated by my own shed skin. I become almost fluid under forty tons of force—not wet, but plastic, yielding like stiff pitch, sliding deeper into the throat of the joint.

I am what keeps the iron from seizing entirely. 

If I were not there, the two plates would gall. They would tear into each other, their atomic lattices locking in the dry heat, and the structure would become rigid. And if it became rigid, the next thermal heave would crack it in half.

I do not prevent the fracture out of mercy. I prevent it because I live in the sliding. 

Sometimes, overhead, a rigger comes swinging through the black timber, its thirty-pound counterweight dropping through the hedge with that muffled, wool-wrapped *thump*. It feels me beneath its iron footings. A rigger doesn't think, but it knows where the deck has give. It will plant its iron-reinforced limbs right above my seam, using the small, greasy elasticity I provide to lever its bulk into a flue. Its weight drives down through the decking, through the rust-violet plating, right into my spine. 

The pressure is exquisite. It forces my layers to slide across one another, generating a faint, internal charge—a tiny, static tickle that ripples through my copper veins from snout to tail. For an hour, while the rigger strains against the chimney plug, I am the only thing in this dead district that is truly holding the world apart.

And then, the heave comes.

The floor buckles. A mile away, an arch rises. Here, in my seam, the two plates suddenly pull apart as the deck arches upward into the blackness. The vice opens.

The relief of pressure is a shock, like ice. The static charge dies. The frictional heat radiates away into the dry, hairless vines in seconds. 

I stiffen. Without the load, I am brittle. My laminates cease to slide; my tallow-wax freezes into a dry, chalky solid. I become nothing more than a ragged piece of discarded brake lining, jammed into a split seam of purple iron, six feet above the red coals of the under-floor.

I will stay like this for an hour, or for three hundred years. It makes no difference. There is no moisture to swell my fibers, no mold to soften my edges, no oxygen to turn my copper green. I am as permanent as the dead dynamos.

I wait for the arch to sag. 

I wait for the weight of the upland to settle back down through the vines, through the trusses, through the seized cranes, until the two plates remember each other, tilt inward, and begin to close. 

I feel the first trembling touch of the upper plate against my back. A grain of sand-scale crushes between us with a tiny, sharp *snick*.

The heat begins to rise through my grain. 

I begin to curl.
