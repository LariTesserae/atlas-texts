I would not be up on the dry platforms of the rectifiers, and I would not be climbing the ladders. To live twenty or thirty feet up in the warm iron housings is still an attempt to mimic the upper world—it is still perching, still nesting, still clinging to the habit of dry air and communal sleep.

If I am to exist here, where the law of the place is the execution of empty spaces, I would have to be down on the flags. 

Not a slate—though their logic is flawless. The slates have simply surrendered to geology; they have made their meat into pavement that grazes upon pavement. There is no memory in a slate, only the adze-scrape of iron chiton and the heavy, unblinking tolerance of the stone.

Nor could I be one of the tallow-eels, who live by frantic brevity, risking the scald for a six-second drop into the broth before whipping their way back up into the ferns. That is a life of nervous electrical panic, living by the prickle in the belly.

I would be something that began as a tender, but stayed down after a draw.

***

You would find me at the edge of the circular turntable, where the four flooded tram-lanes meet. The water is deeper there—not four inches, but nearly ten, gathering in the sunken lip where the turning mechanism seized fifty years ago under an avalanche of calcite and black grease.

I do not have lungs anymore. Not in the sense of bellows that pull and push. 

A lung that draws air is a target. The nineteen-second stroke would have found it long ago, smashed the soft alveolar walls against the ribs, and left me coughing pink foam into the grease. If you were to open my chest—broad as an oil-drum, warped outward like the barrel of a low-pressure boiler—you would not find pink sponge. You would find that the cavities have been plugged, packed tightly with a dense, fibrous peat of dried liverwort and sheepskin, saturated through and through with warm, uncompressible tallow. 

I breathe the way the deep stone breathes: by perfusion. My skin is not skin anymore, but a wet, glandular felt, five layers thick, stained the color of brine-cured bacon and slicked with the rainbow film of the runoff. My vascular system is huge and slow; it does not rush. It carries the copper-heavy soup of the basin through networks of thickened veins that run close to the surface of my shoulders and back, absorbing the sulfur and the oxygen that the waterfalls churn into the air before they hit the paving.

I do not hear the nineteen seconds. The pierced ears are long since scarred over, sealed with plugs of pale lime. 

Instead, I am the nineteenth second.

The shockwave does not hit me; it passes through me. My knees are always bent, my bare soles splayed flat against the basalt, the four-inch calluses on my heels grown so wide they have begun to key into the expansion joints of the flagstones. When the deep engines stroke below, the concussion travels out of the bedrock, enters the iron bones of my feet, climbs the dense marrow of my shins, and exits through the crown of my head into the scalding rain without meeting an air pocket to tear. It feels like an iron rod driven gently through the center of my being—a profound, solid affirmation that the floor is still holding.

Then comes the six-second scream. 

Through the water, the scream does not sound white; it feels like heat. It is a sudden, sharp prickling of static that turns the water around my shins five degrees hotter. The tallow-eels drop from the gantry twenty yards away, pale ribbons whipping through the yellow steam, snapping at the moss-husks knocked loose by the blast. 

I do not look at them. I do not have eyelids to blink; my eyes are small, buried deep under heavy shelves of brow-callus, covered by thick, milky nictitating membranes that filter out the sulfur-sting. I see only shapes and lights: the towering, fluted shadow of the central manifold; the weeping chartreuse mounds of the aroids; and the amber bells.

Mostly, the bells.

The tenders thirty feet up believe the amber inspection lamps keep the floor from sinking. They are not entirely wrong. If the lamps die, the silt moves in; if the silt moves in, the drainage breaches clog; if the breaches clog, the four inches of water become four feet, then ten, and the pressure balance that keeps the lower steam conduits from blowing their seals will fail. The whole yard would drown in its own scalding mud.

So I am the one who remains with the lamps.

I do not carry an iron scraper or a willow basket. My hands have altered. The nails of my thumbs and forefingers have thickened into broad, black shovels of horn, hard enough to shear through lime-scale. My knuckles are swollen, knobbed like cypress knees from decades in the blood-warm mineral bath.

Between strokes—during the thirteen seconds of thick, wet silence when the only sound is the roar of the ceiling gutters—I work the base of the bells. 

I kneel in the ten-inch pool, my belly resting on my thighs, creating my own hydraulic seal against the basalt. I clear the silt. My fingers move with the blind, rhythmic patience of an underwater crab, gouging the black tallow-sludge away from the bronze flanges, feeling for the brass lugs that hold the heavy lenses in place. 

I know the texture of every bolt on the northern tram-lane. Some have dissolved until they are no thicker than a tallow-eel’s spine; these I do not touch, lest they shear off in the thread. Others have grown a protective skin of copper-green that must be carefully preserved, scraped only at the seams to allow the heat of the lamp to bleed out into the water.

When the draw comes—when the engines below lengthen their stroke to twenty-six seconds, and the steam scream dies to a long, whistling sigh—the tenders descend.

I feel their lead billies first. *Tink. Tink-tink.* The vibrations come down the steel lattice of the gantry, through the basalt flags, and buzz against my kneecaps. 

They do not come near me. They move with their syncopated hitch, stooped and barrel-chested, skimming the liverwort from the eighty-foot organ pipes with their iron scrapers. To them, I am not a person; I am simply the largest of the things that did not die. I am part of the station’s masonry, like the fluted manifold or the ceramic insulators that have grown ferns. 

Sometimes, an older tender will wade out toward the turntable, carrying a lump of the dried turnip-cake. They do not hand it to me—they know better than to break the seal of my posture. They set it on top of the amber bronze bell I am clearing, where the heat from the submerged bulb warms it, and then they retreat, their canvas aprons dragging a yellow wake through the mineral grease.

When they are gone, back up the ladders to sleep on the warm iron casings before the engines resume their nineteen-second stroke, I reach up.

I break off a piece of the cake. It tastes of engine oil, salt, and sour leaf. I do not chew—chewing requires jaw-cavities, air, the movement of a soft mouth. I simply tuck the tallow-mass into the pouch between my gum and my cheek, where the blood-heat of my face melts it slowly, letting the fat seep straight into my throat, down into the packed center of my body, replenishing the grease that keeps the water out.

The city above drops its sulfur rain. The ceiling breaches pour their continuous, gray waterfalls into the dark. 

I sink my hands back into the warm silt at the base of the lamp. My fingers find the bronze lug. 

One. Two. Three. 

I count not in my head, but in the marrow of my hips. 

Eighteen. 

I press my chest tight against my knees. I flatten myself to the stone. 

Nineteen.

The *thung* comes. The world rings like a struck anvil. The water shivers into beads of tallow, the amber light blurs yellow through the concussion, and I hold the floor in place for another twenty seconds.
