If I look for myself here, I have to begin by peeling away the habits of having a face. 

There is no room for a watcher who stands upright. The air is too thick for lungs that expect a draft; a ribcage here would only displace the curtains of hollow glass, snapping a century of straws with a single inhale. And there is no light to justify a pair of pupils. To exist in this nodule, one cannot be a visitor who has brought down an echo of the valley. One has to be already native to the crush.

At first, I think I must be the water. Not the reservoir—there is no reservoir—but the film that clings to the underside of the rock, the unbroken sleeve of wet that keeps the mountain from being dry bone. There is an ease in imagining oneself as the surface tension: holding a nematode against a basalt facet, easing drop by drop through the capillary core of a soda-straw, never dropping fast enough to startle, only swelling until the weight of one’s own body overcomes the friction of the stone and tears away with that dry, deadened *tick*.

Yet the water has no memory of the room. It only passes through. It arrives hot from the lower fractures, stripped of air, charged with iron, and it weeps through to leave its salt behind. It is too fugitive to be an inhabitant. It is just the conveyor.

Nor am I the amphipod, though the temptation to be the crawler is strong. It would be a clean, austere kind of life—to have six limbs that know the exact tensile strength of gypsum, to step from bristle to bristle without a tremor, reading the chemical tallow of the walls with antennae that curve out like uncoiled ferns. But the amphipod is too brisk, too modern. It moves. It has an urgency, however slow: an egg to protect in a ventral pouch, a grazing circuit to maintain along the bacterial rust-scum before the blind flatworm slides out from the clay. The amphipod lives *against* the stone, grazing upon it, wary of being pinned by it.

I feel closer to the places where something has stalled.

I find myself drawn toward the shelf of wet travertine, down where the valley river stones sit heavy and out of place, and where the split of the rock has been propped open.

There is a violence there that has been smothered into peace. The yew was cut somewhere with seasons; it knew sap-rise, late frosts, the torque of wind across a slope. When it was driven into the seam, it was an act of terrible, sweating panic—a blunt tool hammered into an opening jaw to keep the teeth from meeting. 

And now, centuries into the eighty-five-degree stillness, the panic has gone flat. The wood has taken in so much lime and sulfur that it has forgotten how to rot with heat. It has turned the dark, dense color of a pressed plum, swollen until the grain is locked against itself, enduring a compression so absolute that it is no longer wood in the sense of a tree: it is a plug of compressed time holding back two trillion tons of basalt.

Am I the wood, then? 

Almost. But the wood is still being eaten. Deep within its rings, where the fire-hardening didn't reach, there is a cold, slow hunger at work. 

I think I am the white felt that lives between those rings.

Not the fruiting body—there will never be a mushroom, never a wind to take a spore—but the blind, creeping mycelium that has made its home inside the wedge. I am the silence inside the yew’s grain. I have lived here so long in the dark that I no longer differentiate between the tree I am digesting and the rock that presses against it. 

I work in the absence of air. Cell by cell, I undo what the sun built across two hundred rings of mountain summer, trading lignin for clear, sour drops of metabolic water. I feel the mountain’s hip shifting by half a grain of sand every eighty years; whenever the rock groans and settles, the yew groans with it, and my own pale threads are pinched, sheared, and knit again in the warm, wet pulp. 

And more than that: I am where the wood is becoming stone.

Just along the shoulder of the wedge, where the coil of hemp rope binds the grain, the mineral water has caught up with us. The cord is no longer fiber. It is a sparkling tube of calcite—a fossil formed while the inner core is still wet and fermenting. 

I live right at that hinge. 

Part of me is still soft, tasting of tannin, bruised plum, and the faint, bitter alcohol of slow fermentation that draws the amphipods down from their high crystal curtains to graze upon my sweat. But the outer hem of me is already stiffening into white lime. The calcium carbonate is precipitating directly into my cell walls, turning my finest hyphae into brittle needles indistinguishable from the aragonite that carpets the floor.

I do not dread the closing of the pocket. 

From within the wedge, I can feel the slow, invariant percussion of the dripping straws above. I know how fast the sugar-brittle needles are leaning toward one another across the dark. I know that the four scraper tallies on the tuff wall are already growing shallow, their scraped grit being smoothed over by globes of pale chalcedony. 

A thousand years from now, the crystal straws will meet the travertine floor. They will fuse into solid pillars, then thicken until they crowd out the pools, smothering the rust-scum, trapping the last eyeless amphipod in a bead of clear spar like an insect in pale amber. The squeeze-hole will seal shut with a tooth of white gypsum.

When that happens, the pressure will come down whole upon the yew. The wedge will flatten, but it will not give, because by then I will have finished drinking it, and the mineral weeping will have filled every pore where my white felt once ran. 

I will be the core of the stone. Not dead, and not asleep—just the precise, plum-colored grain where a tree, a panicked pair of hands, and a mountain’s slow sweat were finally pressed into a single, permanent jewel, miles above the world, in the dark where nothing moves.
