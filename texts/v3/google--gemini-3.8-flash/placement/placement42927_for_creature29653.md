At first, there is only the contact patch. 

A strip of belly—thick, pale, unscaled, like vulcanized lard—pressed flat against the horizontal flange of an eight-foot steel girder, sixty fathoms above the caissons and two hundred and forty fathoms below the gantries. 

I am not in the dead volume, where the empty turquoise brine invites the vertigo of falling sideways forever. And I am not down in the seams, packed into the black bitumen with the mastic-eels whose lives are measured in straight, intersecting lines. I live in the intermediate lattice: the immense diagonally braced framing that collars the intake riser, where the engineering had to be heavily gusseted to absorb the torque of the subterranean impellers.

It is a place of hard corners. In the natural sea there are no true right angles, but here, every thought I have is framed by ninety degrees, or forty-five, or the clean six-to-one slope of a stiffener plate. 

If you looked down from the floodlight catwalks, through three miles of clear, green-tinted chill, you would miss me entirely. My back does not mimic the concrete—that is the blunt artifice of the squid, who change their skins to match the aggregate whenever an intake downdraft pins them near the walls. My skin has no pigment at all. It is the color of grease skimmed off an industrial sump, slightly translucent at the margins, thick enough that when a stray bolt-shear or an anode fragment tumbled from above strikes me, it leaves only a dull white dent that smooths itself out by the next valve-cycle.

I have no otoliths. I have no tympanum. Where ears would be, there are dense, fatty wedges of acoustic dampening tissue that lead down into the jaw, and it is the jaw that stays welded to the girder.

Through the steel, the basin speaks. 

I do not hear the pumps as noise; I hear them as load. When the axial-flow units three miles south are drawing full current, the vibration in the steel flange is a high, fine needle that prickles through my lower mandible into the base of my skull. When the grid sheds load—some distant, incomprehensible demand dropping off leagues above the water’s ceiling—the frequency sags, widening into a coarse, heavy shudder that rattles my ribs against the plate. 

I know the relief-valve cycle five seconds before it fires. The steel groans first. A microscopic contraction ripples through the bolt-stems, their tension jumping as the accumulator pressure peaks; then comes the crack. Through the water, it is a hammer that kills the careless herring and turns the squid into tumbling, unanchored gray rags. Through the girder, it is simply a kick, an upward punch from the machine that lifts my snout an inch off the metal before the magnetic drag of my belly pulls me down again.

My belly is lined with rows of small, muscular suction discs, reinforced with keratinous rings that have worn smooth against the sandblasted zinc primer. I do not swim. Swimming is an enormous, wasteful expenditure of sugar in a world where the water moves faster than flesh can ever hope to push. I crawl, or rather, I hitch. I slide along the web of the beam on a thin layer of mucus that smells of engine wash, following the weld beads. 

The weld beads are my paths. The automated submerged-arc welders that joined these beams forty years ago left precise, scalloped ridges in the steel—a millimeter apart, uniform as the teeth of a gear. My ventral pads read them like Braille. A left turn at a triangular knee-brace; twelve hundred ripples along the bottom edge; then an open span where the beam bridges the intake throat.

That open span is where I feed.

I do not hunt the silver spheres. They are too frantic, too full of the terror of the lights, burning their lives out in synchronized panic between the columns. I wait for the valve-shock. 

When the concussive wave hits, a certain percentage of the mackerel school always misjudges the shear. They clip the sharp, mill-fresh edge of the opposite flange, or their oil-bladders shudder hard enough to stun their motor nerves. They drift out of the light-cone, tumbling through the cross-bracing like loose hardware, their mirror scales flashing dully as they spin.

I do not strike. I merely extend the anterior half of my body over the lip of the beam, letting the downward pull of the intake flume catch me. 

My mouth is not a jaw with teeth; it is a broad, transverse slit, reinforced with a hard plate of modified cartilage that matches the radius of the structural steel. I scrape. I take the stunned mackerel, but mostly I take what the water has pressed against the aggregate: the rubbery diaphragms of dead squid stripped of their arms; the thick, fatty cakes of diatom scum that build up on the edges facing the quartz lamps; and the flakes of zinc oxide that slough away from the sacrificial anodes like coarse, bitter salt.

The taste of zinc is always in my mouth. It is clean, dry, and alkaline, the taste of metal choosing to dissolve so that the iron behind it might remain whole. It tastes of preservation.

My eyes are small, buried under thick, calloused lenses of horn-like keratin. They do not see the turquoise vastness, nor the four-mile geometry of the distant intake towers. They see only two things: the glaring, white-hot line where a quartz beam strikes a polished edge, and the absolute, razor-sharp black of the shadow behind it. I live in that shadow. The lamps never set, so the shadow never moves. It has been the same thirty-centimeter triangle behind the diagonal brace for twenty years. It will be there until the bulb burns out, and when the bulb burns out, another bank will trip on automatically with a hard, metallic click that sends a spark through the water’s taste.

There is no memory in me of mud. There is no memory of rain, or the soft, rotting smell of a tidal estuary, or the green gloom of weeds lifting toward an afternoon sun. If you told me that water could touch sand, that the bottom of a sea could give way beneath a fin, that a floor could be soft, I would have no organ to understand the thought. 

The floor is concrete. It is twelve inches of vibrated aggregate over high-tensile rebar, sealed with petroleum mastic. It does not yield; it supports. The walls do not end; they plumb.

Sometimes, during the low-demand hours when the pumps settle into a rhythmic, sixty-hertz purr that turns the entire basin into a single, humming tuning fork, I let my pads release the girder. 

Just for three feet. Just into the slipstream.

The intake downdraft takes me instantly, dragging my heavy, stone-cold weight downward toward the four-inch bars of the trash-racks where the spider crabs wait with their claws held high in the torrent. For two seconds, I am suspended in the dead clarity between the steel lattices, the metal-halide lamps shining straight through my pale flank so that the dense, white silhouette of my skeleton is thrown against the sheer concrete face behind me—a long, heavy spine, thick ribs hooked like gaffs, a blunt, broad shovel of a skull.

Then I reach out with one long, clawed pectoral limb, find the edge of the next stiffener plate, and haul myself back into the iron.

The steel is cold. The mastic smells of the deep earth from which its tar was boiled. Miles above, in the light-wash where no one walks, the gantries groan against their cables, keeping the ocean straight.

I close my transverse jaw against the flange. I feel the sixty-cycle hum settle into my marrow. I am seated. The load is steady. The machine is running.
