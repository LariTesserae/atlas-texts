It begins with the refusal of the wind. 

To be anything at all in this notch, you must first turn your back on the seven-thousand-meter drop. You cannot afford the luxury of horizons; looking out means looking into a gale that would shear the moisture from your tissues before you could draw a second breath. So you look inward, into the wet throat of the rock.

At first, there is only the weight of the tallow. It does not feel like air; it feels like an oil that has been breathed ten thousand times already, warm and thick enough to float in. You would look for yourself among the fast things—the purple fronds uncurling with that wet, leathery sigh, or the bladders that bounce against the freezing ceiling like frantic, blind hearts. But their time is too noisy, too abrupt. Three days to live, rot, and dissolve into black gruel is not a life; it is an engine cycle. It is a spark jumping a gap.

You would look deeper, where the water does not splash, but weeps. 

Down in the shade of the cleaved wall, where the broadleaf vine loops its heavy, thigh-thick coils across the obsidian, the stone is not quite stone anymore. That is where the sensation starts to settle: in the damp margin where the dark volcanic glass ceases to be glass and begins to yield like refrigerated lard.

You are not the vine, but you are not the mountain either. You find yourself in the seam where they are negotiating what comes next.

You are pressed flat—impossibly flat—living in the hairline fractures that run beneath the mirror-surfaces polished by the crawlers. Up there on the terrace floor, the crawlers rasp their diamond pads over the pink cyanobacteria with a rhythmic, dry *shink-shink-shink*, laying down their millimeter-tall walls of salt. You can feel every stroke through your back. The salt they leave behind sets like hot plaster, and beneath that plaster, the pressure changes. It drives the trickling condensate into you.

It is a strange embodiment. You do not have a head, or ribs, or paws tipped with jade. Your body is a geometry of hollow capillaries, microscopic flutes eaten into the softened basalt by the milky vine-sap and the pylon’s low, forty-cycle vibration. 

You taste the world in voltages and lime.

When the curtain-leeches rise from the cistern floor to envelop the falling rot, you feel the water’s chemical tension slacken. The acid burns off; the lime settles. The water becomes light and clean again, and it seeps into your upper network, rinsing the salt away. You drink not by swallowing, but by capillary draw, pulling the warm grease of their skin deep into the rock. 

And then there are the long silences. 

You exist in the dark between the basalt grains, listening to the flute-song of the notch. The pylon behind the dry water-curtain hums a steady, square tone that never wavers. It is the metronome. Every three seconds, a gas bladder pops against the freeze overhead, dropping its little hail of ice pellets into the cisterns with a wet *clink*. The cisterns hiss. The steam rolls over the terrace floor like boiled milk. 

You are cold on one side—where the deep root of the mountain remains stubborn, dead, and sub-zero—and fever-hot on the other, where the white rib sends its mirage rippling through the rock. Living in that gradient is like being pulled through a wire-drawing die. It keeps you wakeful. It stretches your awareness thin and fine through meters of stone.

You feel the hound-thing before it moves. 

You feel it through its anchors—those jade limbs locked into the synthetic moss by the pylon. For twenty days it has been drawing the charge, drinking the static that bleeds off the diverted water. Through the stone, its presence feels like an iron weight suspended on a spider’s silk. 

Then the tropospheric gale pushes hard against the ridge. The bubble shudders. The smell of ozone vanishes, replaced for one terrifying half-second by the razor-scent of true mountain winter—dry, dead, and hungry. 

The weight drops.

*Tink-clack.* The jade claws strike the wet obsidian above you. It is moving fast. You track it through the shear planes: four points of hard, synthetic mineral hammering across the glass floor, scattering the salt levees, leaping the steaming cisterns. 

It hits the pale rib right above your central cluster. The locking *thunk* of its joints vibrates straight through your hollow tubes.

And then comes the strike.

It does not discharge through the air. The air is too soft, too wet. The static runs down into the bone spar, and the spar drives it straight through the Soapstone interface into the mountain’s cheese-soft marrow—into *you*.

For twelve seconds, you are not a creature hiding in the fractures. You are the circuit. 

The static floods your microscopic pipes, turning the milky vine-sap to liquid fire. The orange phosphorescence does not just shine from the beast's glass fur; it glows through the rock itself, illuminating the shear planes from beneath, turning the black terrace into a web of luminous amber. In that sudden, violent scald, your hollow tubes expand. They reach farther. They drink deeper into the cold basalt behind you, dissolving another cubic inch of the dark, ancient rock, knitting another rank of microscopic organ-pipes into the stone.

The pressure spikes. Your hearing goes white with the steam. The frost at the lip of the notch is blasted back into the sky, blown apart by the heat wave you helped conduct.

Then the surge bleeds out. 

The hound detaches, its joints dragging with salt, and begins its slow crawl back to the dry chimney. The terrace settles. The ferns sigh, their purple channels pulsing with the sap that was just supercharged by the shock. 

You are left in the dark again, slightly larger than you were an hour ago. 

You feel the mountain yielding around you, grain by grain, softening to the consistency of marrow. You are the instrument being bored into the basalt. You are the lungs the mountain is growing so it can finally breathe the tallow-air. And though you cannot see the pale spar half-buried in the shale, you can feel what lies two fathoms beneath it, curled deep in the cold, un-cleaved heart of the ridge: the rest of the skeleton, waiting for you to finish hollowing out the bed.
