At first, it is only a temperature: that specific thirty-eight degrees where water does not stiffen into rime, but clings like warm oil to the skin, thickening the knuckle-creases until every joint feels greased from the outside in.

I would not be on the roofs. The marmot-pavement belongs to itself; there is no room there for anything with a spine that does not bend three ways, and the oily heat of them, working against the raw slate, is too close to a furnace to bear for long. Nor would I be down in the drafts of the ground floor, where the iron smell comes up out of the floor-cracks so strong it leaves a copper skin upon the roof of the mouth.

I would be in the middle floor, but on the outside of it—on the larch bridges, or just below them, where the split logs rest on the corbels of phonolite and the rawhide lashings hang down like drowned willow shoots.

Or perhaps further in: in that four-inch gap between the dry-stack wall and the triple hangings of mountain-goat felt.

It is easy to imagine being a watcher there, but this place does not care for watchers; sight is an expensive sense where there are only two hours of dim, cobalt noon and twenty-two hours of tea-colored dusk. In the gap behind the felt, you do not use your eyes. Your back is pressed flat against the rhomboids of the fractured stone, and your knees are drawn up to your breastbone to keep from trailing into the downdraft that scours the tower skirts.

I would be one of those whose bones have taken the cold in without breaking. Not young, because the children are still quick and their heels hit the flagstones with that dangerous, flinty slap that startles the four-eyed swifts; and not an elder yet, because the elders have had their tongues dry up until they can only whistle against the roof of the mouth like an owl in an air-shaft. 

I would be the one whose knuckles wear the copper bands.

The town calls them *listeners*, though that is too proud a word for a trade that is mostly patience and mutton-tallow. The towers are dry-stacked; no mortar seals them, and when the mountain groans three miles down in its hot throat, the rhomboids do not shatter—they shift. They slide an eighth of an inch along their cleavage planes, copper pin grinding against green hole, the stone singing that faint, glass-hard note that only travels downward. 

If a tower leans, the white marmots on its roof do not cry out; they merely pack tighter on the uphill side to counter the cant with thirty stone of living blubber. But if the lean goes past the thumb-width, the rawhide of the middle bridge will begin to hum. It is a very low, wet drone, like a fly trapped in a grease-pot.

My work would be to lie between the felt and the cold phonolite, with my cheekbone pressed directly against the stone, my sheep-gut hood pulled back from one ear.

In that hollow, the dormice are all around me. They do not mind a man if he has been rubbed down with the hemlock-lard; to them, my ribs are just a broader shelf of rock that happens to hold ninety-eight degrees of heat instead of forty. They crowd into the crook of my arm, three or four of them, their blue-smoke coats so fine that when I breathe out, their hairparts shift like thistle-down. Their little round rumps are smooth as peeled turnips, hot against my ribs, their heartbeats running three times as fast as my own—*pat-pat-pat-pat*—a dry, tiny vibration that gets inside the rhythm of my blood until my own pulse slows down to match the mountain draft.

With the left cheek flat to the rock, the whole rift becomes a single hollow bell.

I can hear the lizards three chimneys over: the long, soft *shhh-wip* of their suction-bellies dragging down the moss-run, hunting the silver woodlice. It is not an aggressive sound; it is as indifferent as wet silk falling off a bench. 

I can hear the rams four hundred feet above the tower-tops, where the fog never quite clears. When one of them locks his shoulder-shovels to drop from the needle-scarp down to the liverwort ledge, the air does not whistle; it thuds once, deep in the bedrock, when his rubbery horn-tips strike the ledge. The stone carries that impact straight through thirty layers of masonry into the bone behind my ear: a dry *tock*, like a walnut dropped on a church flag.

And twice a day, when the wind in the upper gorge begins to hesitate—that five-minute lull before the cold milk-fog drops over the lip like grease poured into a cold skillet—I must put the copper ring to the corner-block.

Not a blow. Just a flick of the finger, the heavy, parsley-colored metal striking the crystalline grain of the phonolite.

*Ting.*

It is a clean, sharp, tooth-aching ring that travels eighty yards through the floor-slabs. Across the lane, in the fourth tower, another knuckle answers: *ting-ting*. Two towers down, near the central grease-trough, the third listener answers: *ting*.

It means: *The bridge-lashings are wet. The marmots have flattened. The swifts have tucked their forward eyes.*

Then I pull my knees closer to my chin. The dormice, feeling the cold change in the draft that slips up between the stones, begin to burrow deeper into the folds of my felt tunic. One wedges its hot, round skull right under my jaw, against the carotid artery, drinking the blood-warmth through my greasy hide collar. Its tiny, blind nose twitches against my skin, smelling of dried ferns and old fat.

Outside the felt hanging, the milk-fog has reached the second-story bridge. 

I cannot see it, but I can hear the weight of it. It doesn't drop like rain; it arrives like a fleece quilt laid over a sleeping man's face. The thousands of swift-wings that were whuffing through the lanes go dead silent at once. The claw-ticking on the roof-slates stops—not all together, but tier by tier, as the marmots tuck their snouts beneath each other’s flanks and let the mist crust their guard-hairs with glass beads.

For twenty minutes, the only thing left alive in the rift is the vibration of the rock against my temple.

The mountain is cooling. It has been cooling for four hundred years, but down in the cracks, the iron water is still boiling against the red root of the needle-peak. You can feel it through the phonolite: a faint, shivering simmer, like a broth-pot left on the embers after everyone has gone to sleep. 

My job is only to know if that simmer changes pitch. If it turns from a hum into a click, the rhomboids will jump in their courses, the copper spikes will shear, and the towers will come down into the rift like an armload of split firewood.

Until then, I stay between the wool and the granite. I do not wash. I do not speak. When my ear gets too cold to hear the grain, I reach into my armpit, take out one of the spherical blue mice, and hold its hot, tailless rump against my ear-cartilage until the skin stops aching and the stone begins to ring again.
