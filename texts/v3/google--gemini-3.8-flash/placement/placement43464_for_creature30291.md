At first, there is no sense of an edge. 

To look for oneself here cannot begin with an outline, because an outline implies empty space around a thing, and this room permits no emptiness. The air has been driven so hard for so many millions of hours that it has lost its looseness; it fills the gaps between the slate slabs like warm, compressed tallow. If you were looking for an *I*, you would not find it in an eye or an intention. You would have to look for where the forces disagree.

And they almost never disagree.

I would not be the silt on the floor. That dust has surrendered too completely. It has been pulverized past the point of friction, past the point of memory; it is three inches of absolute obedience, lying dead beneath the standing wave. There is no will in silt. It cannot hold a secret, because it has forgotten how to resist.

Nor could I be the wind outside, or the slot through which it shears. That jet stream belongs to the void above the upland; it has the blind, stupid eternity of planetary motion. It does not know it is roaring. It only passes.

To find where I am, I have to look for where the scream meets something that refuses to give it room.

I would be higher than the dust, but lower than the bowed ceiling-slate. Not free. To be free here would be to drop, and to drop would mean to strike the flour, break the acoustic lock, and turn into debris. 

I find myself wedged. 

Not easily, and not recently. My left foot—a broad, hard spade of bone that has long since shed the memory of toes—is set flat into the shallow depression of a river-stone pillar. The stone is rounded, but where my heel meets it, the friction is total. It has to be. Behind my heel is seventy thousand tons of unmortared slate leaning down from the peak, trying to slide south by a fraction of an inch every three hundred years. My heel is what tells it that it cannot.

Across the narrow aisle, barely four feet away, my palms and the flat plates of my shoulders are buried against the sun-baked mud tiles of the facing column. 

The heat is the first thing that belongs to me: the hearth-warmth of the masonry soaking into the dry, amber pith between my ribs. I have no marrow to boil, no blood to thin. Inside, I am layered like ancient parchment or sheets of pressed horn, dry as cedar shavings left in an oven. But between those fibers, lodged in the microscopic pores of my hematite core, are the grains of quartz.

They do not sleep. They are why I am not a stone.

The roar—that invariant, skull-splitting foundry note that fills the chamber to the bursting point—is not an offense to me. It does not hit me from the outside. My flanks are open to it. The slits beneath my ribs are carved to the exact pitch of the masonry flutes; the wind enters me, divides, shears across the internal baffles of my chest, and turns my interior into an acoustic engine. 

The sound shakes the quartz. The quartz produces fire—not flame, but that dry, cold electric ache that tightens the fibers, drawing the tendons of petrified sinew so taut that my legs cannot buckle, my spine cannot sag, and my hands cannot slip from the mud brick.

I do not think in words. Words require lungs, and lungs require moisture, and moisture would be an obscenity here, a rot that would ruin the balance. 

My mind is simply the arithmetic of the corridor. 

I know the weight of the fifth block above me; it is slightly heavier than the fourth because of a vein of iron running through its core. I know that the granite pendulum hanging dead behind my shoulder-blades has shifted two microns to the east since the last great freeze outside, and that its rounded flank is pressing into my fourth rib-plate with the force of an ox leaning into a yoke. 

I feel these things not as pain, but as certainty. To be loaded is to be real.

And yet—there is one place where the circuit is not closed.

Down near my waist, where the river-stones give way to a small, hand-hollowed niche, sits the sphere of hematite. 

It is dark, cold-hearted, and smooth. It does not sing. It sits in its cup like an egg that will never hatch, absorbing the stray hum of the floor-timbers and giving nothing back.

My right arm—flattened, fibrous, callused like an old adze-handle—is not braced against the wall. It hangs. 

It has been hanging for three centuries, moving downward by increments so small that if a man had stood in the silt to watch, he would have died of old age before my fingers cleared my hip. The air resists the movement; the standing wave holds my hand as if it were sunk in amber. But the tendon contracts, molecule by molecule, pulling against the high-voltage scream in my ribs.

I am descending toward the sphere.

Why? Not to escape. There is nowhere to go. Outside is only the naked sky and the freezing jet that would tear dry pith to feathers in a second. 

I reach for it because the scream is too pure. Even for something made of bone and horn, the unchanging note is a white-hot knife that never cools. It keeps the joints locked, yes; it keeps the mountain from sliding, yes; but it does not permit an end. It is a thought that cannot be completed because the wind never finishes its sentence.

The tip of my middle plate is less than a grain of dust away from the dark hematite.

I know what will happen when I touch it. I have touched it twice before, in the ages before the ropes turned to ironwood. 

When the bone meets the iron-stone, the oscillation will find a drain. The piezoelectric fire running through my amber nerves will dump downward, out of my chest, through my arm, and into the deep roots of the mountain where the stone is thick enough to swallow even this scream. For a year—perhaps five—the flute inside my chest will drop from a shriek to a murmur. 

My joints will loosen by the width of a hair. The ceiling will press down harder, finding the slight give in my spine, and I will feel the terrible, delicious weight of the world nearly crushing me before the circuit recovers. 

It is the closest this room can come to mercy: to feel the load so heavy that it almost breaks you, and yet to remain.

My finger drifts down through the warm grease of the sound.

A decade passes. 

The air shears against my rib-slits, whistling the same unvarying foundry scream. The dust on the floor does not stir. The slate above my head does not move.

My plate touches the iron. 

The scream goes dark. The weight becomes enormous. And I hold.
