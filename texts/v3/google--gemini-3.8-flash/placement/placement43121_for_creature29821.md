At first it feels like being the water itself—not the kind that falls or runs, but the dead-weight dew that forms directly upon the basalt out of nothing, hanging like lard on the stone adze-gouges, cold enough to ache, never turning to ice. To be everywhere along the sill, neither inside the house nor out in the weather, just an accumulation of wet grease holding the mineral light.

But there is too much movement for that. The water has no center, and here everything has a center of gravity so violent it feels like a hook dragged through the liver.

So I am not the film on the stones. 

Then it seems I must be under the moss mounds. There is a terrible comfort in the idea of being the fermentation: twenty feet beneath the sea-green lobes, where the heat sits like stewed vinegar in the dark, thirty paces from the air, thick with the lye-smell of moss breaking down into food. To be the grey curd, perhaps, waiting for the scoop of a dry femur to carry me up to the black peg-teeth. It has the right pulse—twenty slow thuds to the hour, the earth sliding an inch to the north on its greased bed of slate, nothing asking to be named, nothing seeing the sky.

Yet there is an itch that the curd cannot feel. An itch that belongs only to an edge.

I find myself higher up, where the air smells of chewed iron. 

My knees are bent before I have decided to bend them; they have set that way over decades, the cartilage squeezed dry between plates of iron-thick bone until there is no spring left in the stride, only the wide, lateral swing of the pelvis. The calloused horn on the soles of the feet is so thick that the cold of the flagstone reaches the marrow only as a dull, delayed pressure, like a wool blanket soaked in brine and laid over the foot three days ago.

No, I am not in the mounds. I am outside, on the sunken avenue between the two storehouses that do not agree on how far away they are.

My hand is small, wide, and wet. It holds a piece of native copper that has been hammered cold with river chert until the edge has folded over itself four or five times, hard as tooth-enamel and twice as heavy. It smells of pennies and ditch-water.

My task is not the city’s direction. The cities slide where they slide; a man might as well tell his otolith which way to drop through the skull’s grease. My task is the groove.

Every seventy paces along this avenue, the perspective does not just tilt; it frays. You can see the fraying if you don't look directly at it: the grey light develops a small, grease-slicked stitch where the edge of a masonry block seems to reflect an avenue that is running thirty feet above your head, inverted, its gutters dripping upward. If an elder were to shuffle through that stitch with their heels flat, the heavy copper-and-aragonite pebble in their right ear would swing toward the sky while the pebble in the left stayed bedded in its pocket, and the shock would drop them like an ox—their chest plates buckling under the split weight before their lungs could pump the steam out.

I am the one who finds the place where the stone is still honest.

I do not use an eye. Looking at the seam makes the back of the neck hot, and when the neck gets hot, the spit turns thin and sweet—a bad sign, the sign that the lungs are taking in lowland thoughts. 

Instead, I drop to all fours. My rib cage, wide as a beer-tun and plated solid like the under-belly of a river turtle, comes down flat against the cold basalt. 

I drum.

It is not a song; it is a single, hard thump of the breastbone against the rock, driven by the diaphragm. *Humm-ph.*

The stone answers. The vibration runs down through the flagstones, through the six feet of stacked ballast, through the sliding grease beneath the city’s raft, down into the dead root-rock of the plateau that hasn't moved since the mountains were tall.

Where the rock rings double—one beat high and sour like struck iron, one beat low and muffled like lard—that is where the world is tearing. That is the seam.

I crawl forward an inch at a time, dragging my belly. The condensation gathers on my chin, rolls down my hairless throat, and drips back onto the rock with a tiny, greasy click. 

When my left hand feels the air thin out—not cold, but light, so light that the arm wants to float upward toward the zenith while the elbow remains pinned by sixty pounds of native bone—I stop. 

I set the cold-hammered copper wedge against the basalt, two inches back from the stitch. 

I take the heavy hammer-stone of green chert from between my thighs. 

I peck.

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

The sound does not travel through the air; the wind here shears a hammer-blow into grey wool before it reaches three paces. But down into the avenue it goes, telling the women in the moss mounds that the street is being notched, telling the carriers that the gutter is not safe today until the second horn-groove is cut.

I cut it shallow. Just deep enough for a blind, four-inch-wide heel of horn to find it in the fog. A transverse notch, rounded at the lip so it doesn't split the callous, with three adze-pecks on the south side to say: *Lean forty degrees left as you drop your weight, or your blood will fall into your ribs.*

It takes half a day to cut one notch. The copper dulls against the basalt; every fifty strokes I have to lay the wedge on an unseamed curb and beat its nose flat again with the chert until the cold metal curls into a fresh, sour-tasting tooth.

While I work, someone passes. 

It is an elder, naked, their tallow-white skin glistening like lard in the omnidirectional grey glare. They are carrying a bundle of dried fern-stalks to thatch the throat of a ferment-chimney. They do not look at me. Their head is down, their grey nostrils wide, drawing the bitter iron-air in with a dry rattle like dry peas in a bladder.

Their left foot comes forward, broad as a shovel, toes spread out like fingers. 

The heel sweeps the wet flagstone—*shhk, shhk*—searching. 

It finds my cut.

The blunt horn drops into the notch. The elder stops. They do not look up to see that the street ahead of them has narrowed to the width of a thimble, nor that the wall above them is currently drifting through a low, greasy cloud that smells of burnt marrow. 

They simply wait. 

Inside their head, I know, the little ball of aragonite is rolling slowly through its thick oil, swinging down through the dark of their skull until it finds the new floor. I hear their ribs give a low, grinding click as the bones shift to take the ninety-degree draw.

They exhale. A plume of white steam shoots four feet into the air, perfectly horizontal, perfectly level with the notch.

Then they step. 

Their body snaps sideways, their feet now walking the vertical face of the storehouse wall as if it were a common alley, their fern-bundle trailing behind them like a wet horse-tail, their short white legs churning with that same slow, heavy shuffle, diminishing not by getting smaller, but by becoming simpler—until they are just a pale square of fat moving along a line where there shouldn't be a line, perfectly balanced, entirely unconcerned.

I stay on my belly. 

My chest is cold against the basalt, but the stone under my sternum is warm where my heart has been pounding its slow twenty beats against it. A little pool of greasy condensation has gathered in the hollow between my collarbones. 

I reach out with the copper wedge. Two inches to the right, there is another soft spot in the air, a place where a child’s knee might wander and come back three times its age.

I raise the green stone.

*Tock.*

The world on the plateau does not fall apart, because someone is always keeping the score between what the rock wants to do and what the feet must find. I am the notch-cutter. My ears are quiet; the pebbles in my skull do not roll, because I stay where the floor is still flat enough to bleed on. 

When the light does not change—because it never changes—and the hunger comes, I will reach into the bag of bladder-skin at my hip and take out a lump of the cold, fermented moss-curd, grey as the street, and roll it against my roof-teeth until the lye burns the grease off my tongue. 

Then I will strike the copper again.

*Tock.*

And the raft moves another hair's-breadth across the world.
