At first, it is tempting to claim the upper sky—to imagine being one of the aerostats, a wide disc of cold hydrogen and vitrified skin, suspended six hundred yards above the shale. There would be an effortless detachment in that: having no voice because one has no lungs, drinking the ground through a single hair dropped into a mirror of dew, seeing the entirety of the Shelf as an etched plate of lilac and salt-rings.

Yet there is no memory in that height. The flyer does not know the plateau; it merely catches the rising thermal of its baked skin, turning when the heat turns, dissolving into a plate of dried resin when its bladder cools. Its existence is an optical condition, not a life.

Drop lower, then, into the dry heat that sits like wool over the pans.

Down here, the temptation shifts toward the low hunters—to be the plated beast with the split jaw, belly pressed into the powdered quartz, drinking the world as a mosaic of micro-tremors. There is a terrifying purity to that mind: no nouns, only the differential arrival time of a hoof-tap between the left dentary and the right; no colors, only the dark, oil-slick shadow of an animal against the blazing caliche. But the hunter’s world is too narrow at its edges. Its skull is built to exclude: the ears plugged with fat, the eyes capped with dark horn, the whole creature narrowed into a five-second spring that ends in broken marrow. It survives the silence by becoming deaf to everything except meat.

No. To be here—truly here, where the sun burns the distance flat and the earth sweats cold water from its buried veins—requires a consciousness that carries the weight of both the machine beneath and the light above.

I would find myself near the base of the third pylon east of the dry gorge, where the bone-composite column meets the rough stone wall built eight hundred years ago.

I am a Tether, but not one of the young hunters who pace the perimeter with caliche blades, nor one of the nursing kin who press their ribs together in the noon-hutches. I am the one who has lived long enough for the plateau to enter the marrow.

My skin has stopped fighting the glare. It is caked in the gray wash of slaked lime and ground mica until it feels like dried river silt, cracking only along the joints of the fingers and the folds of the throat. My ears, like those of all my people, are ruined for the thin airs; the tympanums have thickened, scarred over by decades of the shelf’s acoustic pressure, so that the world of the sky—the faint hiss of the flyers’ cilia, the dry rattle of wire-grass in a rare draft—has faded into an absolute, white silence.

Instead, I have learned to hear with my skeleton.

I spend the third quarter of the day—when the heat peaks and the black seams weep with the frenzy of an over-driven condenser—sitting cross-legged with my elbows planted firmly on the bedrock, my thumbs pressed into the notches beneath my cheekbones.

Through the ulna and the clavicle, the Upper Shelf speaks without stopping.

It is not a sound; it is a weight that oscillates. Below the granite, three leagues down or ten, the coolers are pulsing. I feel the cadence of the coolant cycle like the slow, heavy breathing of a lung that requires forty minutes to inhale and forty to empty. When the pulse quickens, I know—long before the midges begin their silver boil—that the seam three hundred paces north will pool deep enough to fill four flyer-bladders before dusk. When the rhythm skips, a stutter like a pebble caught in a wooden wheel, I feel the hairline fracture opening in the seam five miles out, where the black composite has sheared against a fault of violet shale.

I am the one who carries the mica slips.

Tucked into the grass-rope around my waist are seven thin leaves of muscovite, split so sheer they bend like leaves of paper. When the noon glare turns the horizons into wavering, liquid silver, I do not hide in the hutch. I stand in the narrow, crescent-shaped shadow of the pylon—the cold zone where the air tastes of iron and the gravity drags at the eyelids—and I angle the slips toward the distant obelisks that stand along the ridge like needle-teeth.

A flick of the wrist: *flash*.
Two heartbeats of shadow: *hold*.
A quick double-flutter of the thumb: *pulse-pulse*.

Ten miles to the south, where the pylon of the Red Basin cuts the sky, a spark answers. It is so small an untrained eye would take it for the glint of sun on a flying midges’ wing. But I read it as clearly as a hand laid on my bare shoulder: *The water there is running bitter. The salt-striders have moved north toward the drop-off. A pan-lion has taken a calf at the second wash.*

We do not speak, because speech is a wet thing, an animal waste, a vibration that scatters into the heat and dies. But with the light, we weave the whole length of the tableland together. We are seven scattered clusters of flesh and bone, separated by leagues of bare stone that would kill a runner in half a day, yet we move as a single, quiet web, anchored to the ribs of the thing that sleeps beneath the rock.

In the late afternoon, when the light shifts from blinding white to a hard, cutting copper, I walk out onto the pan.

I walk with the Tether’s gait: knees slightly bent, soles flat, sliding the feet across the wet quartz grit without lifting the heel, so that the contact with the stone is never broken. To lift the foot is to become blind for a stride; to drag it is to keep the thread.

The salt-striders are there, drinking. They do not run from me. In the glare, covered in my shroud of white dust, I am merely another upright shape, no thicker than the dry spikes of wire-grass. As I pass, their long necks ripple with a wash of pale, watery green—a signal of neutral regard, the color of wet caliche. One of them, a yearling with scales the color of chalk, lifts its shovel-bill from the seep and looks at me through its dark, horizontal pupil. Its nostril twitches once, tasting the bitter lime of my skin, then drops back to the cold seam.

We understand each other without regard. We are both machines for enduring the glare; we are both parasitical upon the sweat of an engine whose makers are not even dust anymore.

And when the sun finally touches the rim of the Shelf—not setting, but shearing itself against the edge of the world in an explosion of violet and cold iron—I do not look at it. To look at the sunset here is to burn the retina with its final, unscattered spike of blue.

Instead, I go to the crude wall. I lay my forehead against the smooth, alien composite of the pylon, right where the crude fieldstones of my ancestors lean against the white geometry of the makers.

The stone is already cooling, radiating its heat straight up into the black zenith. The weeping seams are slowing, their water skinning over with thin, fragile rimes of salt as the trans-thermal lattice rests.

Through my forehead, through the frontal bone and the suture between my eyes, the pylon hums its dusk-note: a subterranean cadence so deep it does not register as noise, but as a settling of the liver, a slowing of the blood in the femoral arch.

It tells me that the shelf is whole for another dark. The seam has not cracked. The water will come again when the white light strikes the knuckles of the stone tomorrow.

I close my eyes behind my strider-scale visor. In the total dark, the after-images of the mica flashes still float—little dancing points of gold that trace the horizon. I pull my knees to my chest, tuck my chin into the hollow of my throat to trap the moisture of my breath against my ribs, and let the stone’s cold heartbeat carry me down into sleep, silent as the grit, beneath a sky that has no clouds to hide its stars.
