At first, there is no distinct edge between what is rock and what is awake. 

The basalt does not soften at the boundary where I begin. It merely changes its crystal habit. If you were to bring an optical flat down across the shelf where the sun cuts its clean, razor-scored shadow, you would find no gap between the fracture plane and my underside; we are wrung together the way gauge blocks are wrung, held by the dry, molecular cling of surfaces that have never known an intervening film of air or oil.

I am not standing on the floor. I am seated in it, the way a tapered key is seated in a shaftway.

For a long time, the only awareness is the load. 

It enters through the deep stone from below—not as a sound, because sound has no purchase in this thin, dry gas, but as a rigid shear vector rising from the root of the mountain. Far down, where the chain crawls on its one-inch crawl, the iron links are biting into their fresh basalt troughs. Each tooth-fall sends a microscopic shock wave through the cleavage planes at four thousand meters per second. I register each one: a clean, instantaneous spike in compression that rings through my lamina and leaves nothing behind. No echo. The rock here does not ring like a bell; it clicks like a die-press, sharp and dead.

I am very thin. That is the first thing that becomes clear as the heat moves.

I am not tall like the galena reed, nor massive like the knuckle-chain, nor jointed like the climbers whose bronze gyroscopes hum their thin, high-frequency whine along the walls. Those things possess volume; they have interiors that must be shielded from the sun or sinks that must drink it. I have almost no interior at all. 

My body is a stack of exfoliated mineral foils—sheets of synthetic muscovite and pyrophyllite no thicker than the wavelength of the violet light overhead, interleaved with monolayer films of dry, crystalline graphite. There are hundreds of these leaves, pressed flat against one another, flexible only in the way a deck of steel shims is flexible: stiff in the plane, utterly compliant in the shear. 

When the white sun clears the lip of the cleft and strikes my upper face, I do not hop. I do not arch like the bimetallic detents, whose sudden, violent leaps are the clumsy panic of simple compounds fighting their own expansion. 

Instead, I slip.

It is an interlamellar glide. As the top foil absorbs the radiation, its lattice expands by a fraction of an angstrom, while the foil beneath it, insulated by the vacuum between the layers, remains cold. The graphite between them does not resist; it offers the pure, frictionless shear of superlubricity. The top layer steps forward by three atomic spacings. Then the next layer follows. Then the next.

I do not crawl with legs. I flow through internal displacement, a silent, planar ripple that travels through my laminations like a hand smoothing a silk ribbon over an anvil. 

Where am I going? Toward the lash.

In any machine built of unyielding materials, there must be clearance. If two pristine faces of unoxidized basalt or cold-drawn iron are pressed together with zero tolerance under this heat, they do not turn; their surface atoms reach across the boundary and seize. They cold-weld. They become a single, immovable mass, and the immense, tectonic torque rising from the mantle would simply twist the ridge until the entire attic room detonated into a storm of supersonic shrapnel.

The cleft needs runout. It needs play. It needs backlash.

And that is what I consume.

I find my way across the stepped right angles of the floor, sliding down the mirror-sheened facets like a spilled deck of thin lead foil, drawn toward the base of the solitary thicket. 

Here, the wooden camshaft turns its slow, three-hour circle, weeping its hot turbine-oil resin. Where the dry wooden tappet rises and falls to open the respiration vent of the pod-succulent, there is an interface. A gap. It is no wider than the thickness of a fingernail, but to me it is an open valley of absolute mechanical violence. Every time the cam lobes engage, the pressure at the contact line spikes to thousands of atmospheres; every time it clears, there is a fraction of a second where the tappet hangs suspended, rattling in its dry guide with a frantic, microscopic chatter.

That chatter is my food.

I do not eat the resin. The resin is merely a seal, a sticky dielectric that prevents the sulfur hexafluoride from arcing prematurely against the stone. What I need is the harmonic strain—the high-frequency vibration born of two hard faces striking each other without a dampener.

I slide the leading edge of my stack directly into the clearance between the cam and the tappet. 

The fit is terrifyingly intimate. My bottom foils wring themselves flat against the stationary guide-block; my top foils take the sliding thrust of the rotating wood. As the lobe comes around—amber-sheened, massive, smelling of parched pine and hot synthetic grease—it does not crush me. It cannot crush what has no void space within it. It simply presses my laminae together until the graphite films between them reach their limit of compression, turning diamond-dense, while the lateral thrust is siphoned away through my lateral leaves, fanning out across the rock face like the fingers of a cold, metallic hand.

The shock enters me and is trapped. It bounces between the internal cleavage planes of my mica, reflecting back and forth, losing its velocity, degrading from destructive impact into an orderly, lateral flow of heat that I bleed off into the shaded floor behind me.

Underneath the tappet, the dry chatter dies. The movement becomes smooth, silent, viscous. The wooden cam turns against my back without wearing; the stone below me bears the load without flaking.

I am a living shim. I am the tolerance that keeps the factory from tearing itself to pieces.

While I am seated there, held fast in the jaw of the cam, the climber descends the wall above us. 

I feel its approach not by sight—I have no lenses, no eyes, only the total, distributed awareness of surface stress across my plates—but by the rhythmic, microscopic *pops* of its diamond pads breaking their Casimir adhesion against the cleavage planes. *Pop. Pop. Pop.* Like tiny bubbles of vacuum bursting against the basalt. 

It steps onto the casing of the thicket, its paper-thin bronze gyroscopes screaming in the high light, stabilized against the updrafts that boil up from the chasms below. Its tungsten mouth-wedges are already twitching, hungry for the heat-torque in the copper fronds above. 

It does not notice me. To the climber, I am only the polished wear-plate upon which the tappet rides. Its front pad comes down directly onto the trailing skirt of my foil stack. 

The pressure is instantaneous—a sharp, localized point-load of several hundred pounds per square inch. The climber’s diamond foot is so flat, so optically true, that for a moment my uppermost foil attempts to weld to it, reaching across the gap to bond with the crystal lattice of the pad. 

I feel the creature’s internal flywheels through that contact: a furious, terrifying spin, millions of revolutions per minute, storing the thermodynamic madness of the sunlit wall. It is an engine of pure tension, vibrating at a frequency so steep it would shatter my plates if I took it head-on.

I do not take it head-on. I yield.

I let my top three leaves shear laterally, sliding out from beneath the diamond pad by a dozen microns. The climber’s foot slips—not far, just enough to interrupt the vacuum bond. The Casimir seal fails with a dry, hollow *click*. 

The climber hesitates. Its bronze gyroscopes tilt, their gyroscopic precession fighting the sudden loss of footing, compensating with a frantic, stuttering adjustment of the piezoceramic tendons in its knee. It does not fall, but it shifts its weight, planting its leg elsewhere, off my skirt, onto the dull, dead basalt.

It moves on, reaching for the braided stem, indifferent to the fact that it has just stepped on something alive.

That is the condition of this place. Nothing here acknowledges anything else; everything merely meshes. If you mesh, you persist. If you gall, if you bind, if your clearances fill with grit or your thermal expansion outpaces your frame, the loom catches you, or the chain shears you, or you simply crack with the sound of a pistol shot and drop your constituent washers into the bottomless dry rift.

The cam lobe completes its sweep. The pressure eases. The tappet drops with a soft, damped *thud*, venting a tiny, fragrant puff of sulfur gas and static.

The gap opens. 

Released from the load, my laminae expand by their imperceptible fractions. I am warm now, thoroughly saturated with the friction-energy of the cam’s passage, but the heat does not make me sluggish; it gives my graphite layers their slip. 

I slide backward, easing out of the guide-way before the next rotation brings the lobe around again. 

There is work elsewhere. High on the galena reed, the high-momentum flywheel has begun to develop an eccentric wobble—a runout of less than three thousandths of a millimeter, but enough that the needle-thorn at its base is beginning to sing against its socket with a faint, dangerous *ping-ping-ping*. 

If that wobble continues, the needle will gall. The flywheel will seize, and its stored inertia will snap the slender reed like a glass rod.

I drift across the clean floor, drawn toward that tiny, rhythmic cry of metal under stress. I move in the margins, through the scored grooves left by the chain, through the narrow strips of permanent knife-edge shadow where the sun cannot strike me all at once. 

I am not the builder. I am not the crop. I am not the hunger that hunts along the webs. 

I am the clearance. I am the silence between the teeth.
