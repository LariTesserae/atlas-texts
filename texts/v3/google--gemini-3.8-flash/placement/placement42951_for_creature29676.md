The first thing to understand is that I am not floating.

Floating implies an ambition toward the surface, or at least a neglect of the bottom. Here, everything is held either by the throat or by the ankle. If you let go, you do not drift toward some bright air—you go sideways with the convective glide, forty yards an hour toward the open drop where the shelf breaks off into three miles of cold, sour liquor, or you settle, grain by heavy grain, until you hit the pillow-lava and it shears you into ribbons before you realize you have struck.

So I am holding. That is the first condition.

At first, I might think myself one of the tethers. There are moments when the swell pauses—that gray, thick interval when the water hesitates between its northeast push and its southwest draw—where you cannot tell whether the hemp is alive or the hand holding it is dead. The kelp-rope has a pulse: it shudders with the small, blind movements of the tether-lice working their ways up the core, eating the gray fungi, their little horn-plated legs rasping like flint on horn. When I press my ribs against the three-ply twist, my own chest-cavity takes up that dry, thin rattle and gives it back as a hollow, bell-shaped thrum. For fifty feet above me, the line hums with my liver-beat; for fifty feet below, it carries the shivering cold of the anchor-crag.

Am I the thing that crawls the line? 

No. The lice are too simple in their hunger. They do not know the terror of the slack. They only know that the salt is bitter and the rot is sweet. When the line stretches under the three-hour draw—when the pumice dome above catches the convective roll like a bladder in a wind and groans until the oil-jars inside rattle against their hoops—the lice flatten their carapaces into the braid and wait. They have no memory of what happens when a strand gives.

I do.

I have heard a strand part four fathoms above me: a single, dry *tcheq*, followed by the greasy slide of fiber against fiber as the weight transfers to the remaining three cords. You do not look up. Looking up tilts the amber plates of the eyes away from the stone, and if you lose your sight of the vent-spires, you have no horizon. The black rock has no edges except the ones that are warmer than the water. You navigate by the heat of the wounds.

I am lower down than the homes.

The families live in the lift—up in the cups, where the trapped water stays fatty and still, tasting of the grease-lanterns and the yellow meal they scrape from the succulent tubers. Up there, the children learn to sleep belly-down against the reed mats with their four hands knotted into the ceiling-ribs, dreaming of nothing harder than an unpeeled root. They have soft bellies, the young ones; their lipid coat is thin, pale as tallow, smelling faintly of sulfur-mash. They do not have the callous yet.

My knees have the callous.

It grows in yellow, horn-hard cushions over the kneecaps and down the inner line of the shins, where I have to bracket myself into the seams of the basalt while the swell runs. My skin does not smell of succulent grease; it smells of the vinegar-blood that leaks from my own thumbs when I miss a splice, and the cold, gray grease we render from the liver-sacks of the drifters when their carcasses finally lodge in the tooth-crags at the ridge-foot.

I am an adjuster of stones.

It is not an office with a name that can be struck on a kelp-rib with a stylus, because the work is too continuous for a single stroke. If a line is too taut, the pumice cell above begins to list; its lip dips, the still-warm water it has cupped inside spills out into the tea-brown drift, and the elders within begin to chill. If the line is too loose, the cell bobs and bucks in the convective roll, worrying its moorings until the greenstone ballast begins to chatter in the rock-cleft.

A chattering stone is death. In three tides, the obsidian around it will flake into needles; in five, the wedge slips, and fifty yards of braided hide and six lives shoot toward the olive dark like an unstrung arrow, to be broken against the higher crags or flattened by the pressure when the bladders finally burst in the heights.

So I stay down where the brine wobbles.

My hands are four, but they have forgotten how to be hands that hold food or smooth the slime onto an infant’s gills. The skin between the fingers is thick, cartilaginous, tough as the rim of a trencher. The digits do not taper; they end in blunt, square nails that have been broken and mended so often with hot sulfur-resin that they are black as the lava they grip. 

Between my lower limbs, trailing from a broad belt of cured ray-gristle, hangs my weight-iron: a single, eight-pound ingot of foreign metal—the only piece in six tiers of settlement—salvaged from some ancient drift that must have fallen through the ceiling before the ridge was born. It is cold, dense, and utterly dead; it does not take the temperature of the vents. When I drop it into a basket to balance a strained riser, it settles with a dull, leaden thump that has no music in it. The people in the domes hate the sound of it. They say it makes the grease-lanterns flicker green.

Right now, the vane-worms are beginning to lean.

I watch them from five feet away, where I am wedged between two pillows of glass that have cooled in the shape of severed lungs. The worms are six feet of clear water wrapped in a sleeve of cold jelly, with that single, ink-black thread of nerve running from their anchor-pads to their tips. For two hours they have stood upright, stiff as spears in the slack. Now, the top three inches of the forest tilt. They do not bend like grass in a wind; they pivot from the base, all three hundred of them, thirty degrees toward the sulfur vents behind my left shoulder.

The current is turning. The vast, slow lung of the deep has emptied its brine into the western trough and is beginning to suck it back across the ridge-crown.

The ropes know it before I do. 

The three-ply line of the third tier begins to sing. It starts as an ache in my jaw—a vibration that enters through the bone plates behind my gills and sets the oil in my forehead humming. *Zzzun-nn.* It is the middle rope of the Weaver’s cell, the one anchored to the split boulder forty yards out on the ledge. The weed on it is too thick; the drag is catching the new current like a sail.

I unhook my lower graspers from the crack.

You do not kick. If you kick, the displacement might brush a vane-worm, and a broken worm does not tell the turn. You pull. You take the line between the tough pads of your secondary palms, slide your primary hands six inches along the twist, and draw your belly toward the hemp.

The water here is ninety degrees; it smells of rotting iron and the burnt hair of the bottom-fire. As I rise five fathoms toward the junction, the heat drops six inches away from my skin, and the cold drift takes me in the face. My gills flare—the charcoal plates snapping open with a soft, wet click—and the iron-tasting wash pours through the combs.

The line is too tight. I can feel the hemp weeping; micro-fibers, individual hairs of beaten kelp, are parting with tiny, prickling snaps that prick the nerve-ends of my wrists.

I reach into the ballast-basket with my left foot, finding the wedge of greenstone by its coldness. It is jammed three inches too deep into the basalt fracture. The stone has settled since the last cycle; the cooling glass below has given that *tink-tink-thung* in the night, and the fissure has widened by the width of an eyelid.

I take my bone stylus from between my teeth. I do not strike. I place the flat of the ivory against the wedge-shoulder, lay my chest against the handle, and let the whole weight of my dense, iron-logged body press downward into the lever.

For five seconds, nothing moves. The current pushes my back, cold and tea-colored, smelling of distances I will never see. The kelp fronds seventy feet above me drag to the southwest with a sound like wet leather sliding over wet leather.

Then, the stone yields.

It does not slide; it slips an eighth of an inch with a dry *chhk* that travels through my ribs and down into my belly-fat. The tension on the line drops four tones—from the high, waspish hum of a cord about to part to the fat, guttural *dhum* of a line that has found its work again.

Above me, through the bronze-and-olive murk, the chartreuse eye of a grease-lantern in the Weaver’s floor blinks twice. Someone has felt the cell settle; someone has felt the floor come level again beneath their belly-strap.

They do not pluck the rope in thanks. You do not pluck a rope that has just been eased; it needs its rest.

I stay there, hanging by the web of my lower feet from the ballast cord, head-down over the drop.

Below me, three feet into the black fissure, the thermal brine rises clear as melted fat, wobbling the image of the basalt until the world below looks like it is made of shifting water-snakes. Somewhere down in that narrow, boiling dark, my grandfather’s ribs are jasper now. His hip-bones are part of the sill that holds the basket I am leaning on. If I reached down—if I were fool enough to strip the lipid-coat from my arm and plunge it three feet into the vent—I might touch the place where his skull has turned to black flint, anchoring the line that keeps the children from the sky.

I don't reach.

I spit a mouthful of sulfur-curd into the current, watch it tumble away into the bruised twilight, and wait for the worms to swing back.
