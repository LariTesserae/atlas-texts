At first, there is only the sensation of being held in place by an enormous weight that has no hands. 

It would be easy to think of oneself as something floating—the spray is so thick, the updraft so buoyant with warm, pulverized water that the whole sixty paces of air seem like a solid medium, a luminous white gel through which something might drift indefinitely like a diatom in a thermal vent. But drift is an illusion that lasts perhaps three seconds before the down-plunge catches you, carries you into the churning white hell of the sump, and feeds you into the subterranean flumes. 

To exist here at all is to refuse the air.

So I am not in the middle. I am not the spider, though there is something tempting in those long, hair-thin guy-lines strung across the dead-air corners, tasting the acoustic grease of the room as it strums the silk. That life is too precarious, too dependent on the geometry of shadows and iron flanges; one anomalous gust, one nozzle coughing a slug of un-atomized condensate, and the line snaps.

Nor am I down in the root-pelt of the ribbon-ferns, though that is where the density of living things is greatest. Down there, between the wet black iron of the H-beams and the three-inch felt of liverwort, existence is a frantic, blind crowding. Thousands of hooked legs knitting into root-hairs, thousands of fan-shaped combs tasting the hum of the intake pipes, a continuous carpet of armor plates clicking against graphite-slick steel. It is too social, too crowded with cousins, too full of the small, shivering anxieties of things that fear being dislodged from their moss.

No. I find myself higher up, where the architecture has shed its iron and becomes nothing but sheer, vertical masonry.

Here, sixty feet above the catwalk and forty feet below the atomizer ribs, the wall is a single, curving face of aggregate that has been washed for a hundred years until the cement has the texture of wet lard. It is stained that deep, chemical peacock-green—a mineral glaze laid down by the weeping of copper drainpipes far above, leaching salts into the stone until the rock itself has turned toxic and sweet.

I am pressed against this vertical curve. I am three inches wide, perhaps less—a shallow, flexible disc of horn, so thin at the margins that the constant sheets of eighty-five-degree water do not hit me; they simply slide over me as if I were a slight swelling in the concrete itself. 

There is no front to me, and no rear, only an *under* and an *over*.

The *over* is smooth as wet glass, curved like the back of a watch, rinsed every second by thousands of gallons of warm, mineralized rain. It has no feeling except the immense, steady vector of the slipstream—a hydraulic thumb pinning me to the wall with forty pounds of continuous atmospheric pressure. The downpour does not batter; it seals. It creates a low-pressure pocket beneath my skirt, so that the harder the torrent rushes over my back, the tighter my mantle bites into the stone.

The *under* is where the slow work happens.

Beneath the shield, in the dark, warm micro-climate between my belly and the aggregate, there is no roar. The sound of the chamber—that teeth-rattling mechanical scream that has burned for a century—does not arrive as noise. It arrives as temperature, and as a low, structural thrum that vibrates directly through the soles of my foot. Through the stone, I can feel the stroke of the distant pistons three stories down in the bedrock; I can feel the high, singing whine of the impellers forcing the condensate into the riser pipes; I can feel the slight, periodic tremor when a nozzle eighty feet above clears its throat of calcium scale.

I do not have eyes. The mercury-halide floods are so bright they burn through the amber edges of my shell, casting a soft, diffuse chartreuse halo into the margins of my flesh, but there is nothing to see. There has never been an image here. There is only the gradient of the salts.

My mouth is a ribbon of diamond-hard rasps, moving with the patient, rhythmic stroke of an antique clock. I do not eat leaves. I eat the skin of the world. 

With every pass of the radula, I shave off a fraction of a micron of the peacock glaze—the blue-green crystals of verdigris, the slick film of single-celled cyanobacteria that thrive on the copper runoff, and the soft, dissolved lime leached out of the Portland cement. It tastes of cold metal, battery water, and ancient dust. The copper does not poison me; it has become my skeleton, my pigment, my preservative. It sits in the cells of my mantle, turning my gut into an opaque, glowing nodule of turquoise jelly that shows faintly through the semi-clear horn of my shell.

I move perhaps two inches in a week. To move is a terrifying, magnificent calculation: lifting one millimeter of my margin just enough to slide the muscular foot forward across the slime, while the remaining nine-tenths of the circumference maintains the vacuum against the rock. If the seal breaks, the torrent slips beneath, and in that same tenth of a second I would be torn from the face, bounced off the rusted flanges of the lower catwalk, and drowned in the froth of the sump.

Behind me, where I have scraped the wall clean, I leave a path: an oval streak of pale, virgin grey where the raw concrete aggregate is exposed to the light for the first time since 1910. But the path does not last. Within forty-eight hours, the copper weep from above washes over it again, the turquoise salts crystallize in the microscopic pores of the stone, the green algae recolonizes the scratch, and the machine heals its stain.

There are others on the wall—I can feel their weight through the rock, small circular dead-spots in the stone’s vibration, three feet to the left, eight feet above—but we will never touch, never signal, never compete. The wall is vast, sixty paces of unbroken vertical shale, and the rain never runs dry. 

Sometimes, through the stone, there is a minute fluctuation—a drop of half a cycle in the frequency of the pumps, a fractional cooling of the water by two degrees as the distant plant shifts its electrical load. When that happens, my shell contracts slightly, pulling its perimeter tighter into the microscopic irregularities of the aggregate. We wait. We do not panic; panic is a mechanic of the air, of things with wings and fins that can be chased. Here there is only the grip, the rasp, and the flood.

Eventually, the pressure normalizes. The pitch climbs back to its immutable sixty cycles. The warm water sweeps over my dome, thick and heavy as oil, and the rasp begins again: *scrape, swallow, hold.* 

I will remain here until the diamond ribbon of my tongue wears down to the flesh, or until the vacuum finally fails from simple exhaustion of the foot. Then I will release, become a momentary shadow tumbling through the brilliant, fog-choked noon, and leave behind only a single white circle on the green stone—a clean, unblemished coin of limestone, soon to be washed over by the copper, waiting for the next mouth to find it.
