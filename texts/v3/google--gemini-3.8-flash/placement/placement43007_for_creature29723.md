It does not begin with an eye. An eye in this place is a cold bead of Iceland spar, ground flat by the air, capable of nothing except registration: noon arrived, noon maintained, noon absolute. It holds no memory of dusk because the shafts were cut by someone who hated the evening, who bored through sixty cubits of dead lime to ensure that only the plumb-line sun—the three hours where the sky is an anvil—could ever touch the floor.

To look for oneself in the light is a mistake. The light is an eviction. It flattens the baffles into a single, chalk-grey screen, pressing the eyes back into the bone until there is no distance left to inhabit.

Nor is it the chord. The chord is too wide. A note sustained for four hundred years ceases to be an event; it becomes the floor. It enters through the knuckles of the limestone tiles, runs up through the ceramic fins like vinegar through chalk, and sets every reed bundle to a rigid, petrified tremor. To be the sound would be to be the masonry itself, the whole dry weight of the mountain leaning down into five organ pipes.

No, you would have to look where the room fails to be entirely itself. 

You would have to look in the leeward trough of the thirty-seventh fin, four fingers above the limestone dice, where the draft does not shear cleanly.

Here, a single ceramic tooth was stamped slightly crooked when the fin was turned on the bench before the kilning. A thumb pressed too hard into the wet marl; the groove is a hair’s breadth deeper than its neighbors, and the backward-turned hooks of the shim cannot quite seat into the root of it. 

I am not the shim. The shim is too pure an engine—it has no choice but to flutter and slide, an acoustic shuttle thrown back and forth between two nodes of the standing wave, scraping its amber spoons until its gut is full of lime-flour. 

And I am not the fin, which is only baked mud holding a frequency.

I am the pinch.

I am the dead wedge of space between the shim’s fourth abdominal plate and the terracotta wall: a pocket no thicker than the film of grease left by an eyelash on glass, yet in this room, an ocean. 

Because the tooth is shallow, the standing wave does not clear this pocket. The air that enters it does not howl; it stalls. It turns upon itself in a microscopic, lazy eddy—the only circle in an architecture composed entirely of plunging lines and parallel slates. 

Into this stillness, things fall that have no right to survive the blast.

A single flake of lichen, not yet ground to dust by the shims’ scrapers, sits lodged here against the ceramic grain. It is curled like an iron filing. It does not vibrate at four thousand cycles; it shivers at forty, a heavy, drunken, out-of-tune drag on the fin’s clear singing. It has caught three grains of wind-borne quartz—not the soft chalk from the shafts, but hard, grey desert grit carried from fifty leagues away on some extraordinary gale that managed to plunge down the central pipe without being shattered against the baffles.

These three grains do not belong to the chord. When the note rises toward noon—when the sky turns white and the pressure from the shafts bears down with the weight of dropped lead—the grains grind against the terracotta. 

They make no sound that could be heard above the room’s enormous roar. Yet through the plate of the shim, which rests its horny margin against them, they transmit a tiny, irregular stutter. 

A click. 

A skip in the gear.

To be this point of grit is to be the only thing in the wagon-bed cellar that knows the wind has a pulse. 

The shims do not know it; they are tuned to the flute, and the flute is steady as granite. The lichens do not know it; they grow only in the direction the blast shears them, petrified into waves of pale bone. But here, where the grit chatters in the crooked tooth, the room is not quite closed. 

Through the calcite lenses of the creature pressed against me, the noon does not drop straight. It refracts through the quartz corners. It splits into a hair-thin line of violet and grease-yellow—colors that have no license to exist in this cellar of dried lime and baked clay. The light strikes the horn plate at an angle of three degrees off the perpendicular, and where that slant falls, the thermosiphon fails. The dust-salts inside the creature do not circulate evenly; they back up, form a crust, and leave a dark, brown freckle on the dried viscera.

The creature does not die of it—there is no moisture to rot, no blood to poison—but it becomes *heavy* on that side. It does not slide when the chord shifts its fifth. When the noon strikes the dice and the other carapaces slip an inch upward into their nodes of calm, this one stays caught, dragged down by the three grains and the yellow refraction, hanging askew across two grooves like a gate with a dropped hinge.

Its amber scrapers strike the earthenware out of time. 

*Clack.*

*Clack-clack.*

Against the infinite, deafening, unyielding drone of the sky’s organ, it beats a dry, lame measure. It is the only rhythm in the vault; everything else is tone. 

From this hitch, tiny consequences radiate into the baffles. 

Because the shim is crooked, the wind-shear behind it divides improperly. A thumb-sized plume of turbulent air spills off its trailing shingle and strikes the adjacent reed bundle, right where the gut lashing was tied off three centuries ago with a square knot. The knot, which should have lasted until the masonry crumbled, is being dried by an air that curls instead of passes. It is fraying. A single filament of sheep-gut, white as salt and stiff as wire, has lifted its tail into the draft.

It hums. Not at the room’s pitch. Higher. A thin, frantic wasp-sound that cuts through the roar like a needle through felt.

I find myself there: in the knot that has begun to think about coming untied, held together only by the lime dust that has caked its fibers, waiting for the one noon whose heat is heavy enough to crack the wax in the second grain of quartz.

We have seven seasons left, perhaps eight, before the reed slips its lash. When it does, it will spring three inches to the left, wedging itself across the slot of the thirty-seventh fin. The standing wave in that channel will break. The shim will rattle down like an iron nail onto the knuckle-dice below, its calcite eyes turning up to the glare, cold and blind and finished.

Until then, I keep the measure. The three grains turn in their socket; the amber spatula rasps out of turn; the light splits into its forbidden yellow; and beneath the five great shafts of sun, the roaring engine of the room carries, right at its heart, this small, hard, unappeasable stone of silence.
