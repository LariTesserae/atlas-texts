At first there is only the transmission: the basalt passing its shudder directly into whatever is pressed against it. 

No distance. If there were distance—even the thickness of a fingernail—the water would wedge itself into the gap and rip the surfaces apart. So the first fact is contact. A wide, flat adhesion.

It feels like sitting at the base of a dropped anvil that has not yet stopped ringing. The strike happened days ago, or weeks, but the metal—the black stone—is still vibrating at the pitch that splits teeth. It hums through the ribs, through the plates of the sternum, setting the fluid in the marrow into a low, greasy boil. 

I am not the water. The water has too much appetite, too much weight, spinning in its blind circle with the speed of a millrace, tearing open empty pockets in its own body and clapping them shut with the crack of split pine. 

Nor am I the edge where the water ends. That is too high, too bright, too thin. To live up there, glued to the underside of the void with the cold mountain wind freezing your belly while your legs dangle in copper broth, requires a frantic, collective suction—ten thousand mouths working to keep a single skin from snapping. That is a kind of desperation that has no patience in it. 

Here, down on the drowned shelf, there is nothing *but* patience. 

The weight is enormous, but it is also a relief. Before the tear opened in the floor, before the sulfurous broth welled up and filled the amphitheater to its flint teeth, there was the air: thin, blue, needle-cold, smelling of nothing at all. In that air, to lift an arm was an agony of leverage. The long, hollow rods of the limbs, hinged once, twice, three times, trembled under the pull of the earth. We moved like dry scaffolding across the cirque, dragging stone blocks with braided moss, each step threatening to splinter the brittle shafts of the shins against the talus. We were building walls against the wind because the wind was heavy enough to knock us down.

Then the gut broke. The hot brine rose. 

It lifted the sky away. It took the awful downward drag out of the joints. For the first time, the limbs were light, almost weightless, floating in the warm, syrupy wash like soaked wicker. 

And then the gyre began to spin.

Now, I am folded. 

It takes a long time to learn how to fold properly, so that the current finds no shelf to catch, no crook of an elbow to pry open. The knees must be brought hard against the breastbone—the three joints collapsing flat, like the struts of an iron rule closing into its casing. The head, flat and broad as a mason’s float, must tilt downward, wedged into the pocket where the thighs meet, sheltering the soft throat-louvers beneath. 

My back takes the wash. The hide there has thickened over the last hundred hours, drinking the iron out of the water until it is the color of wet slag. Between the shoulder blades, the four respiratory vents suck and squeeze: *draw in the grit, taste the copper, spit the black silt.* A steady, mechanical pulse. Behind me, on the dry-stacked slab of basalt that forms the lintel of this collapsed hut, the soot from my vents has left four overlapping crescents, like the scorch marks of four small torches. 

Beside me, another back. Another set of folded knees. We do not look at each other; there are no eyes for looking, only the dull, milky sensors along the rim of the skull that register when a shadow passes—a drifting block of quarry stone, or the pale, twitching rag of a fissure-crawler before the current flings it into the basalt and pulps it into jelly. 

We touch only where our hips touch, wedged tight beneath the tilted stone. Through that seam of cold, rubbery flesh, I can feel the rhythm of its vents, slightly out of phase with mine: *suck, spit; suck, spit.* 

My hands are not idle. They cannot be. If they are idle, the moss-tethers will saw through the carpal skin. 

Between my feet, right where the flagstone broke when the floor dropped, there is a razor edge. It was cleaved fresh when the basin filled; its fracture is so sharp that the water curling over it makes a high, whistling hiss that cuts through the roar of the cavitation bells. An edge like that is dangerous. An edge invites the hydraulic hammer. If a vacuum pocket collapses directly over a blade of stone, the shock will catch the shelf from below and lift the entire hut, tumbling us into the mid-water with the dead masonry.

My thumb finds the edge. 

The thumb is blunt, capped with a thick spade of horn that does not wear away easily. I press it against the basalt, right over a pale smear of dry-land chalk that still clings to the grain, and I drag it.

Forward. Back. 

A pinch of mineral powder—the fine, sharp silica dust ground off the floor by the siphon-worms in the deep fissures—is trapped beneath the horn. It acts as an abrasive. 

*Scritch.*

The vibration travels up the bone of the forearm, through the triple hinge of the elbow, directly into the skull. It is a tiny sound, lost completely in the cathedral-filling thunder of the vortex, but inside the marrow it is clear and distinct. 

A grain of basalt comes loose. The water whips it away instantly. 

Another stroke. Another grain. 

Under the horn of the thumb, the razor ridge is losing its sharpness. It is becoming a curve. A slow, dull shoulder that the current can slip over without snagging. 

When this edge is round, there is another behind my heel. And another running beneath the hip of the one sitting beside me. The entire basin is full of them—thousands of yards of fractured, screaming flint, all wanting to tear the water, all waiting to be caught by the hammer. 

We will smooth them. We have the brine to keep our bones from snapping under their own length. We have the copper to feed the blood. We have the chalk marks to tell us where the stone was true before the mountain drowned. 

Above, the disks hold the water down. Below, the worms bolt the fissures shut. Here, in the dark middle, under the weight of the warm, blind engine that wants to grind us into broth, my thumb moves against the stone. 

Stroke. 

Stroke. 

Turning the catastrophe, grain by grain, into a riverbed that has no teeth left to bite.
