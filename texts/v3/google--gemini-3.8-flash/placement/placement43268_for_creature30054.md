I would not look for myself in the light. 

There is no light here that belongs to an eye, anyway—only that sulfurous, yellow-green weight pressing downward, turning everything the color of pond-water and bruised rind. If I started near the top, caught in the high ribs of the vascular ferns, I would be too visible to things that do not even bother to look. To be seven feet of gristle moving an arm an inch an hour across a leaf, or a shield-backed thing nursing mold in the dark beneath its own wing-cases—that feels too close to purpose. It feels like an ambition, and ambition in this heat would curdle like grease.

Nor would I be the film. It is tempting, at first, to imagine being that vast, unstitched surface tension, rolling across the black mirrors forty feet wide, taking small drownings into oneself without a ripple. But the film is too thin; it has no interior, no weight to set against the stone. It has only an edge.

To find where I am, I have to let myself sink. 

Past the dripping canopy, where the bell-worms drop their twelve-foot cords into the steam, humming that jar-rim hum that rattles the soft palate until the teeth ache. Down through the mattress of liverworts—three feet of warm, yielding water-sponge that sighs and weeps when anything shifts. The spindle-crabs kick there in their blind, glass-limbed knots, but they are too many, too brief; they are just flour in the mill.

No, I would be lower still. Down where the moisture is no longer mist or rain, but an unceasing, hair-thin sheet of hot grease-water sliding over stone that has never been allowed to cool. 

I would be at the shear-line. Right where the living cushion stops and the floor begins: that floor of black, unblunted obsidian that has never heard of frost or wind.

***

At first, I think I am the wound itself. 

The basalt is a landscape of microscopic scythes, needle-crests, and gas-blister lips so thin they could slice a shadow. Nothing soft touches them without coming apart. But here, coming apart is not an ending; it is the first condition of residency. 

I feel myself wedged into a shallow depression between two blister-walls—not deep enough to be a blind vent where the tallow-hides stack like loaves of lardy dough, but a long, horizontal gutter where the stone puckered as it cooled, leaving a trench the width of a forearm and the length of a spine. 

I am not armored like the pavement-grubbers. I do not have their clinking, shingled skirts or their thousands of chisel-tipped false legs scraping the glass. That is one way to survive the razor: to be harder than the flint. 

The other way is to be too slow to be severed.

My body has settled into this trench until the stone has taken its measure. I am thick—four inches through, perhaps, and flat as a salt-fish, without limbs, without joints, without a face to offer to the dark. What I have instead of skin is a dense, yellow-gray mantle of vascular tallow, three layers deep, that does not resist the obsidian. I have pressed myself against the crested edges of the blister until the rock has bitten an inch into my undercarriage. 

The cut is there, permanent and cold-sharp against my belly, but the blood that met it did not spill. It met the hot, mineral water that washes through the gutter and it turned instantly to rubber. The stone is inside me now; it has become my keel. I am anchored to the basin floor not by suction or claws, but because I have allowed the newborn earth to staple me to itself with its own edges. 

The cut does not throb. The heat is too high for inflammation; protein does not flare here, it simply softens, adapts, and resets its bonds. The wound is where the stone and I have agreed to stop moving.

***

What am I doing, lying pinned in the scalding trickle?

I am tasting the run-off. 

Everything above me—the bellows-beetles dropping through torn fronds, the fronds themselves decaying into acidic slime, the shed horn-plates of the grubbers, the yellow spores falling in continuous flurries from the fungal gardens—eventually liquefies. The basin does not allow solid things to stay solid for long. The moisture catches the dust of life, dissolves it into a lukewarm, heavy broth, and draws it down through the moss. 

By the time that water reaches the basalt, it is thick as egg-white. It coats the glass in a laminar glide, moving toward the deep cracks where the tallow-hides gape. 

I lie in the way of it. 

Along the sides of my mantle, where the flesh curls upward to touch the green, water-logged underside of the moss, are thousands of fine, pale fringes—not gills, not roots, but hair-like gutters of mucus. They do not sweep; they let the water drag across them. They take the phosphorus, the sulfur, the dissolved tallow, the bitter green juices of crushed ferns, and they draw them directly into the fat of my dorsal pleats. 

I don't have a mouth that opens and shuts. A mouth implies an event—a beginning of eating and an end. Here, digestion is like the rain: it is an atmospheric condition. The broth enters me along thirty inches of my flank, moves through an open, unchambered interior like water through a sponge, and leaves behind what it carried, feeding a pulse so slow that an hour passes between one contraction of my dorsal vessel and the next.

***

Above me, the world is a single, deafening slab of noise, but down here against the glass, the sound changes its character. 

The drone of the bell-worms does not come through the air; it comes through the obsidian. The rock is so dense, so pure and un-fractured, that it conducts the hum like an iron bell. The vibration enters the rock-crests that are embedded in my belly. It travels up through the basalt razors, into my calloused lymph, and rattles the small, heavy stones that sit in the fluid-sacs along my center line. 

I don't hear it as a note. I feel it as a steady, internal scouring. It keeps the fluid moving inside me. It prevents the thick lymph from settling into stillness; it shakes the tiny, suspended grains of basalt grit through my tissues, polishing me from the inside out, keeping my channels clear while I lie motionless beneath the wet carpet.

Sometimes, a pavement-grubber slides across my back. 

It does not know I am there, or if it does, it regards me only as a softer ridge in the glass. Its amber-greased horn-skirt rasps against my dorsal hide with a sound like a blunt file on lead. Its chisel-feet find purchase in my thickened skin, leaving rows of small, white punctures that weep that milky, quick-setting lymph before the creature has even finished clearing my width. The weight of it—sixty pounds of damp shell and rasps—bears me down harder against the obsidian below, driving the keel-cut a fraction of a millimeter deeper into my belly. 

I welcome the weight. It seats me firmer in the blister. It squeezes the old water from my sponge-tissues and lets the fresh broth rush in when the horn-skirt has clinked away into the dark.

***

I do not imagine an end to this. 

There are no seasons to warn me of a change; there is no morning to bring a different light, no drought that could dry the greasy film from my mantle. The heat will remain pinned at this exact threshold, where flesh is always on the verge of slipping its knots but never quite does. 

If I grow, I grow sideways, following the line of the rock-fracture, extruding another inch of tallow-colored hide along the razor, letting the new stone bite in, letting the new seal form. If a mantis-crawler’s probe should reach down through the liverworts and tear a ribbon from my flank, the gap will fill with the same cloudy water that feeds me; the margins will glaze into rubber within three breaths; the moss will settle into the dent; and the broth will continue to slide across the scar.

I would be this: the thing that has found the seam between the violence of the floor and the suffocating glut of the canopy. Not the stone that cuts, and not the plant that rots, but the cold-blooded fat that sits between them, holding the razor in its meat, perfectly still, perfectly full, humming with the stone forever.
