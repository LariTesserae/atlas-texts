At first, there is no sense of having an edge. 

There is only the gradient between warm and scalding, and the difference between what yields and what does not. The root-mat below is soft, an endless, sour sponge that pushes back only when the deep floor shudders; the iron plates far beneath are hard, cold at their base, but sweating a continual, greasy fever where they meet the pulp. 

If I look for where I begin, I find it not in a skin or a face, but in a point of friction. 

I am not in the open green light. That lime-colored wash, falling through the fern-choked roof, belongs to the broad leaves and the things that dangle from the girders like empty tallow coats. Up there, the air is thin enough to hiss. Down in the sump, the pressure is too dull, too blind—ten yards of compressed felt and blind gut-tubes drinking rust in the dark. 

I find myself in the middle reach, in the throat of things.

Specifically: where the middle-pressure cylinder raises its neck toward the rocker arm. 

There is a circular aperture here—an old stuffing box, eighteen inches across, through which a vertical piston rod of hammered Swedish iron moves up and down. Long ago, before the roof fell, men in heavy wool would have packed this collar with rings of braided flax soaked in tallow and graphite, tightening down the bronze gland with two-inch nuts to keep the sixty-pound steam from shrieking out into the engine house. 

The men are long since washed into the sump; the bronze gland has cracked under the slow, hydraulic swelling of an ironwood burl; but the rod still moves. And the hole cannot be left empty. If it were empty, the steam would dump its heat into the canopy in a single, roaring hour, the pressure would drop to nothing, and the floor would freeze into a dead, sodden carpet.

The collar must be filled. I am what filled it.

It did not happen by choice; it happened by suction and settling. I am mostly braided marrow now, interwoven with ribbons of hairless, boiled-hide creeper that have turned black from seventy years of absorbing animal fat and cylinder grease. I have grown into the annular groove where the old flax used to sit. On the outside, my flesh is anchored into the cast-iron flange—anchored so deeply that my fibers have entered the microscopic pores of the metal, turning the grey iron into a brittle, purple-black composite that tastes of copper filings and ancient river silt. 

On the inside, I have no roots. On the inside, I am smooth.

My inner surface is a polished, cylindrical sleeve of dense, gristly callous, black as horn and slick with condensed oil. It grips the iron rod with the desperate, continuous embrace of a closed fist. 

I live by the interval.

For three minutes, there is a heavy, dripping quiet. The room above me weeps; gallons of water crash through the skiff-sized leaves with the sound of dropped wet rugs; the dough-things behind the flywheels skim their warm paste of rust. In these three minutes, I cool. My temperature drops from the heat of boiling tallow to the heat of a fresh wound. The sap from the vine-girders trickles down along the bronze bolts and finds my outer lip, feeding me a thin, bitter gum that carries traces of dissolved zinc and boiler lime. I absorb it through my skin like a sponge resting in a trough. I swell slightly. I tighten my grip around the still rod. I can feel the microscopic texture of the metal—a shallow, spiral scoring cut by a lathe two centuries ago, and a deep, jagged pit three inches from my lower margin where a flake of iron rusted out during the winter the roof gave way.

Then, the boiler far down in the sod makes its whisper. 

It starts not as sound, but as an ache in the base of my throat. The steam jacket swells. The dark, twitching lung-meat inside the cylinder walls contracts, stiffening against its fractures. The brine in the pulp beneath me goes still as the hydrostatic pressure builds.

*Thump.*

The stroke does not begin gently. The rod—fourteen inches of solid, pitted metal—is shoved upward through my belly with concussive, unpitying force. 

The friction is immediate and white-hot. It strips the cool brine from my inner surface in a fraction of a second, boiling it into a sharp, grease-sweet puff that vents through the moss collar around my neck with a dry, screaming whistle. The iron tears at me; it wants to pull my fibers upward, to turn me inside out and fling me against the high lantern roof. But the ironwood roots hold my base, and the iron tannates in my hide keep me from shearing. 

I do not resist the rod; I clamp it. I let its heat pass directly through my gristle. The sensation is not pain—there is no nervous system here to register an injury, no brain to dread the next stroke—it is an overwhelming, blinding fullness. It is the only time I am entirely real. For four seconds, as the piston sweeps its stroke, my marrow is heated to the verge of charcoal, my fat liquefies, and I taste the pure, metallic soul of the machine: ancient anthracite, river silt, sulfur, and the raw, scraping bite of iron against horn.

Then, the shudder at the top of the travel. The rocker arm groans above, crushing another collar of lichen into hot meal. 

The rod pauses. 

And then the long, sighing descent. The machine does not push on the way down; it settles under its own eighty-ton weight, a slow, greasy slide that draws a long breath of saturated steam down through my labyrinths. I drink the cylinder oil that coats the rod as it retreats. I lick the cold water of condensation from its scars. The heat recedes into a deep, dull throb, and my liquefied tallow thickens again into dense, protective butter.

Above me, the canopy unleashes its squall. A hundredweight of warm water crashes down onto my shoulders, washing away the flakes of burned fiber and scorched lichen, cooling my outer rim while my core remains red-hot. 

In the pulp three feet away, a white ribbon quivers, its rubbery gut expanding as the floor rebounds from the stroke. It brushes against my lower skirt, blind and indifferent, drinking the hot grease that has dripped from my lip. We do not acknowledge each other. We are both merely parts of the seal.

If I have an awareness, it does not look forward or backward. It does not remember the sky that once shone through the lantern roof before the liverworts webbed the iron. It does not know what lies beyond the four brick walls, where the stalactites of leached lime hang like pale legs in the green dusk. 

It knows only the cycle of the gland:
The cool three minutes of drinking brine and zinc.
The tightening of the marrow.
The rising ache of the steam.
And then the great, burning shaft sliding through the dark, holding the engine together, keeping the room wet and working, stroke after stroke after stroke, with no intention—and no way—of ever letting go.
