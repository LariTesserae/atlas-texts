At first, there is only the sensation of being jammed between two kinds of hardness: the ironstone behind, which does not yield a fraction of an inch to the small of the back, and the thick, yellow-lit air in front, which presses against the eyelids like a warm hand dipped in grease.

It would be easy to think myself the stone itself—the buttress of the second tier, perhaps, where the clay has split three times and been caulked with hair until it looks like an old wound healed over a grey knuckle. There is that much stillness in me. When the swiftlets make their five-hundred-throat drop into the white wool of the clouds, the small thrum in their hollow quill-bones vibrates through the rock and enters my calves before my ears take it in. I am anchored that low.

Yet there is a twitch in the heel. Stones do not have heels that itch where the caliche dust cakes between the cracked calluses.

So I am flesh, but flesh that has forgotten how to swell. 

For a long while, I think I must be down in the badger-runs—one of those low-slung, heavy-bellied things whose three toes are black with tallow, sliding along the river-cobbles until the skin between my hips grows hot and leaks that wild-sage smell. There is a comfort in that dark: no drop below, no amber ceiling above, only the greasy bellies of three generations of kin pressing close, groaning like porridge bubbling on dung-coals. My chest feels that wide. It does not lift and fall; it stays square, like a box made of green hide, taking in only as much of the bronze air as can pass through the narrow slit of the nostrils without rasping the throat.

But the light finds me. It is not down in the shingle.

The light sits on my forearms. It is the color of tallow that has been melted down three times in an unwashed skillet: flat, heavy, casting no edge. When I move a hand—and it takes the time it takes an earwig to cross three fingers for the command to go from the crown of the head down to the thumb—the skin does not show blue veins. The blood here is too thick for blue; it runs dark as elder-syrup, heavy with the iron that blows off the high teeth.

I am not scraping dew. My knees have the walnuts on them, yes—the grey knobs of bone where the horn trough used to bump against the shin thirty paces above the void—but the wrist has gone thick. It has lost that loose, snapping hinge that lets the horn blade glide under the moisture without disturbing the stone-meal. If I went out on the north rib before dawn now, I would turn the grey dew into fog with one clumsy jerk; I would come back with an empty trough and my shoulders white with mountain-flour, fit only to be spat upon by the boys who still have the narrow ribs and the wide, wet eyes.

No. I am on the fourth course of the bread-ovens, where the rock has a small hollow like an armpit that catches the leeward heat and keeps it until the middle of the third watch.

In my lap is an oval of porphyry, about the weight of a dried kid.

My thumbs know it better than my face knows water. It was rough when my mother’s sister had it; she ground the broom-seed on it until the center was as smooth as the inside of a badger’s ear, and then she gave it to me when her urine turned rust-red and she went up to the high nettles to swing. I have had it since the last buttress buckled under the goat-pen. I do not grind with it now; I hold it between the flats of my palms and I turn it.

Turn. A half-twist against the dry meat of the thumb.
Turn. The palm takes the curve, slick with the badger-grease I rubbed into the pores at the second bell of the swiftlets.

We are making a hearth-stone. It will take another two summers of turning before the porphyry is flat enough to bake the *palo-seco* dough without scorching the edges. There is no hurry. The oven has three courses of caliche left before the settling crushes the flue, and by then the stone will be ready, or I will be up among the *tijeretas*, and some girl with deep-set eyes and hair like grey broom will take it out of my lap while my limbs are still soft enough to bend.

From where I sit, three paces above the second threshing-floor, I can see the dogs.

They do not look at me. They are lying on their sides, their skin the color of an old liver left on a salt-block, their hairless bellies twitching when the sun-beetles click in the moss-mats. But every three or four breaths, the old dog—the one whose left ear was torn by a lilac-neck when the drought was four winters long—lifts his chin half an inch. He smells the ironstone above the third tier.

He is waiting for the seam.

I am waiting for it too, though I do not prick my ears. I feel it in my back teeth. When the cold air begins to gather down in the mountain’s old gut, the nerve in my left molar—the one worn down to the brown wood—gives a small, dry throb, like a cricket stepping on an ant.

It will come before the swiftlets go down. The bronze sky is already bruised around the rim, where the valleys are rotting with rain we will never see.

The dog twitches. His windpipe whistles—once, twice—a sound like dry cane rubbing in a wind.

Down in the lane below my knee, a girl looks up. She does not have a name I use with my tongue; she is the one who has the broad foot with the twisted middle toe, who carries the water-skins from the north faces. She has two bladders of badger-tallow slung over her hip, and her chest is working slow and even, the staves of her ribs never springing, just widening out like a basket being packed with wool. She looks at my hands to see if the stone has stopped turning.

It hasn't.

If I stop turning, the porphyry cools against the skin, and once the stone takes the skin-chill, it takes half a watch to get the grease to run slick again. I keep the wrist moving. *Scritch. Scritch.* The dust between the stone and my palm is fine as sifted ash; it has ground into the skin until my lifelines are grey as slate-seams.

The cold comes first as a smell.

It does not smell like water. Down-dwellers think cold smells like rain, but they are soft in the breast and their lungs leak water every time they shout. Cold here smells of old lime, crushed beetles, and the iron that has never seen the sun—the dead middle of the tooth before it is pulled.

The dogs get up on their toes. Their joints make a series of dry pops, like seed-pods bursting on the broom-bushes.

I do not look up at the seam. To crane the neck is to waste the grease behind the ears, and once the neck-sinews dry out, a person sits with their chin glued to their breastbone until the black juice is boiled. I keep my eyes on the stone in my lap. But I see the shadow of the summit shift—just a hair’s breadth, as the cold air rolls out of the spade-crack three hundred paces above us and tumbles down the parabola of the hollow.

The air hits my neck. It is so cold the grease on my collarbone sets into white lard with a tiny *crackle*.

For the length of three spindle-turns, the swiftlets do not drop. They cling to their holes in the sandstone, twenty thousand of them, their tails braced against the grit, their black beaks shut tight over their tongues. The scoop is so quiet you can hear the lice moving in the badger-gut bladders down in the store-tiers.

Then the old dog lets out his whistle—*whee-tchu*—and the mountain finishes its breath.

The bronze heat drops back down like a wet hide dropped over a chimney. The stagnant, sweet tallow-smell rushes back into the hollow, thicker than before, warmed by forty houses and three tiers of badgers and the long, greasy hair of the dead hanging above our heads in the nettles.

My thumbs move again. *Turn. Turn.*

The girl with the twisted toe goes into the low doorway beneath my platform, dragging her hide-sledge behind her. A single swiftlet falls from the rim, its black scythe-wings snapping shut, then open, clipping the thick amber air into two equal halves.

I am still here. My ribs are uncracked. The porphyry between my knees has taken the heat of my groin and is holding it against the ironstone.

In fifty years, or sixty, when the stone is flat enough to lay the loaves upon, some boy will lift it by the notch my right thumb is wearing into its edge. He will look at the smooth grey belly of it and he will not know my mother’s sister, nor the sound of my teeth clicking over the broom-bread, nor the white scar where the yoke bit into my shoulder when we brought the juniper up from the dead groves.

He will only know that the rock does not crack when the fire gets hot.

That is enough to leave behind in a place where the wind takes four hundred million years to round off a corner: a stone that does not crack, and an elbow’s width of grey grease rubbed so deep into the porphyry that even the mountain, when it settles its great teeth together at the end of the world, will have to swallow it whole.
