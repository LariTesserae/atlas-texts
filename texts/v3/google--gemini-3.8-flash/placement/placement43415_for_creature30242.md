At first, there is no sense of being an *inhabitant* at all. There is only the calibration.

To exist in this cut is to have the bedrock pressed directly against the skull, or the sternum, or whatever hard plate separates the interior wet from the cold wet of the air. The pump—two hundred feet into the mass of the hanging wall—does not make a sound; it makes a condition. It is a four-pole motor spinning at twenty-nine hundred revolutions every minute, geared down into a multi-stage impeller that handles heavy slurry. The rock does not merely conduct it; the rock is the medium through which the machine breathes. 

If I am anywhere, I am not in the air. The air is too expensive. The air belongs to the knife-winged things that turn inside out between the conduits, their mandibles snapping like dry twigs in the drafts. And I am not down in the sump, where the red-gilled pearlescent shapes churn blindly through the iron flakes, though there is a terrible, sleepy comfort in the bottom, where things go to break down and slip into the natural drains.

I find myself on the shear-plane. About eleven yards down from the shivering black pipes, eight yards above the churn. 

It is the rawest part of the slit. Decades ago, when the foundation footings above settled by three-eighths of an inch, the siltstone let go with a single, dry fracture that ran seventy feet down into the dark. The cleavage face here is flat as plate glass, but stepped like broken slate, matte blue-gray, with razor-thin exfoliation cracks running horizontally where the stress released.

The chill is a physical skin. At five degrees above freezing, every vaporized droplet of heavy cutting oil and cold sump-water settles instantly. It coats everything in a greasy, immaculate lacquer that never dries. 

I am wedged into one of those horizontal release cracks. My back is pressed against the roof of the seam; my belly is against the floor of it. The gap is scarcely the width of two copper coins, but inside it, the draft from the overhead blowers cannot strip the warmth away. 

Who am I? The lead-colored thing. The one that came down through the mesh decking when it was still spinning aerial webs, back before the air became a turbine exhaust. 

There are no webs here. In an abyss that trembles continuously at sixty cycles, a suspended net is useless; it becomes a blur of white noise, a loose guitar string vibrated by an engine until its glue dries out and its spirals snap. I have let all that go. What remains of the spinning glands now works like a mason’s trowel, spitting short, iron-stiffened guy-lines that are laid dead-flat against the stone, anchored with neat rosettes of insoluble spit directly into the micro-grooves of the shear. 

These lines do not catch flies. They are acoustic leads. 

Through the tips of my eight walking legs—tipped with microscopic, curved claws that hook into the microscopic chatter-marks of the fracture—the mountain is speaking all the time. It is a monotonous, crushing speech. The turbine’s bass note; the rhythmic *thump-thump* of the slurry valves clearing; the acoustic crack of a condensate drop falling thirty yards to shatter on the scree below like a small-caliber primer. 

Because the noise is total, it becomes nothing. It is the zero-line. The silence.

To hunt here is not to listen for a presence. It is to hunt for a hole in the machinery.

Above me, hanging off the ledge where the liverwort mat weeps its continuous, zinc-fouled water, one of the pale crane flies drifts down. It has no wings—only long, jointed stilts of milk-colored horn that bend like watch-springs. It steps off the sodden sponge of moss and places its claw upon the bare rock face.

It is so light it scarcely exists. But it is flesh. It is water and soft grease held in a thin skin.

Where its foot touches the blue-gray slate, it acts as a tiny, miraculous damper. 

The sixty-cycle thrum that rings through that particular three-inch square of rock suddenly softens. It is like a finger laid gently against the rim of a ringing crystal glass. The hum does not stop, but its overtone is clipped. A local dead spot blooms on the plane, four inches to the left of my crack.

That is the signal. Not a vibration, but an *absence*. The machine’s voice has been briefly blotted out by a body.

I do not think about the sintering yard above, where the red coke glows and men in aluminized coats shovel flux into hoppers under the rain. I do not think about the birds, whose asphalt nests cling like blisters to the trembling steel mains just beneath the roof. They are sky-things that have been crushed into the dark, still remembering speed and daylight in their frantic, ricocheting darts. 

I remember nothing. I am the color of the stone when the water washes over it: the gray of unrefined lead, the slate of slag that has fallen into a pool and cooled before it could rust. 

The dead spot stays for one beat. Two beats. The pale thing is grazing the bacterial scum left by the steam vents.

I draw my limbs beneath the armor of my carapace, feeling the stone’s cold tremor slide across the slick grease on my joints. The amber light from the overhead grates slashes downward for a fraction of a second as a steam relief valve sighs open seventy feet above; the whole chasm goes blinding white with warm mist, then snaps back to twilight as the suction sweeps it away.

In that brief, damp pulse of warmth, the damper does not move. 

I come out of the seam like a flake of the rock itself peeling free. Six inches of sheer stone crossed in the space between two drops of condensate. No sound. No song. Just the fangs—thick, cold, black as machine studs—punching down through the soft celluloid back, pinning the tremor to the stone, and letting the quiet sink all the way in.
