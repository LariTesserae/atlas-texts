At first, the instinct is to be small—to imagine myself as one of the false-scorpions in the crook of an iron strut, translucent as tallow, possessing only the memory of claws and the vibration of the rivet beneath. Or to be the planarian, that pale leaf of water and silt that exists only along the gradient between scalding and wet, whose death is merely an evaporation back into the rust. There is a clean, low-maintenance peace in that: no voice, no memory, no weight beyond the capillary draw of a seam.

Or even the vine itself. To be the tuberous wood that drinks steam, whose intelligence is entirely hydraulic, swelling until a brass casing splits with a sound like a wet knuckle cracking in the dark.

But none of those things carry the *time* of this place. They are the conditions of it, not its witness. And the Sump is too deliberate to be inhabited only by what feeds on it. It was dug with square arrises; the lead tags have numbers stamped into them with steel punches; someone measured the pitch of the catwalk stairs so that a man carrying three gallons of tallow wouldn’t clip his shins on the riser.

If I look for where my weight settles, it is not on the open iron where the hot drops fall, nor is it forty feet up where the children balance on eight-inch rails with their prehensile toes. That kinetic grace belongs to those who were dropped into the grease while their bones were still green.

I find myself lower down, and further back.

I am sitting on a short length of timber—squared hemlock, pickled in creosote until it is black and dense as coal—placed directly across the threshold of one of the barrel-vaulted tunnels where the vitrified brick gives way to the naked limestone. 

This is the hinge of the entire district. 

Two inches in front of my knees, the air is ninety-two degrees, slick with the steam-sweat of the mains and smelling of bruised watercress and hot cylinder-oil. Six inches behind my shoulder blades, the air is fifty-eight degrees, dry and mineral, smelling only of cold water seeped through forty feet of lime-rock, with that faint, alkaline taste that settles on the back of the tongue like chalk.

I sit on the sill between those two weathers. If I lean forward, my tallow-sheened skin catches the thoracic drumming of the engines through the wet catwalk plate. If I lean back, my spine rests against rock that has not moved since the ditch was cut, cool enough to take the fire out of an elbow.

I am not young. My lungs have the barrel-arch that comes from fifty years of breathing broth, but they are quiet lungs; they don't rattle. You learn early here not to draw more than three fingers of air into the chest at a time. The deep breath is a surface habit, a waste of animal heat, and dangerous besides—it drags the sulfur down into the small lobes where the red mold likes to seed.

My hands are like the hands of the man with the scraping-chisel: square-bladed, the skin on the palms turned into horn from decades of contact with hot iron, the fingernails worn down until they are just thick yellow crescents flush with the meat of the fingers. But I do not carry a scraping-chisel. 

I carry an awl.

The awl is old. It was turned from a broken file—you can still see the diagonal teeth ground smooth along the shank—fitted into a bulbous handle of boxwood that has absorbed enough finger-grease to look like dark horn. The point is not needle-sharp; a sharp point skitters on lead and tears it. It is ground to a small, rounded wedge, like a dog’s tooth.

My work is the lead.

The people here do not write on what rots, and they do not speak what can be left unsaid. But things happen that must be fixed to the wall, or the community drifts like the silt in the gutters. 

When the boiler-tenders three terraces over bank their fires, or when a gate-valve on the third level begins to seize and needs three turns of the wheel every fourth watch instead of five, they don’t shout it. They come down the red-lead catwalks with that flat, slapping tread of their thick-soled feet, their bodies smelling of the bitter sap they’ve been clearing, and they stand at my threshold. 

They do not come inside. The tunnel behind me is the sleeping-rock; to bring the hot, oily breath of the sump into the fifty-eight-degree air without cause is bad manners, the kind that gets a man turned out to sleep on the wet gratings. 

They stand in the orange twilight of the caged bulb, wait until I look up, and then they tap the metal of the door-frame with a knuckle.

The door-frame is lined with sheet lead, three-sixteenths of an inch thick, beaten flat against the brick arrises with wooden mallets forty years ago. It is five feet high and eight inches broad on either side of the low lintel. It is grey-blue, dull, and soft enough that if you press the ball of your thumb hard into it, you leave the whorl of your skin behind for an hour.

I am the one who cuts into it.

I don’t use letters—not the long, spindly letters they teach up where the horse-carts run and the sky is that wet slate color. Those letters are for ink; they take up too much room, and they have too many loops that gather grease and fill with the grey planarian slime until you can’t read them anyway. 

I use the marks that were settled when the first retaining wall was trued:
A horizontal tick with a tail down means an expansion-joint has been repacked with oakum.
A double circle with a cross-stroke is a child whose cord was cut under the rock.
A clean, straight gouge, four inches long and driven deep enough to show the bright, silvery lard-color of the fresh lead beneath the oxidation, is a seam that has split under pressure—a death by scald.

The lead is nearly full. On the left jamb, the marks go back to my grandfather’s time, when the limestone was still white and smelled of gunpowder from the blasting. Down there, near the floor where the bilge-damp has turned the metal to the color of a wet pigeon, the gouges are wide and clumsy. Up higher, where my own shoulder hits the casing, the work is tighter, smaller, spaced with the conservation of an old man who knows that when this strip of lead is covered, they will have to melt it down or cut another door into the rock, and the rock does not like to be cut twice.

Sometimes, when the engines drop that half-octave and the Sump goes into its dead-still shift, I don’t work at all. 

I sit on the hemlock baulk with my feet flat on the iron plate, feeling the small, steady *thrum-thrum-thrum* through the pads of my heels. It isn't a sound; it's a condition of the blood. I take a piece of tallow-cloth and I rub the fresh cuts I made during the shift. You have to rub them with lard and soot immediately, or the sulfur in the steam will crust the bright lead over with white fur within three days, and once the fur starts, the edge of the mark softens, loses its bite, becomes just another bruise in the soft metal.

I look out through the lattice of the catwalks.

From here, sixty feet below the grates, you can't see the sky at all, not even the gray slit. You see only the belly of things: the ribbed underside of the iron walkways, the swollen black throats of the aroid leaves catching the heavy drops, the pale gleam of the vines where they choke the steam mains like grease-cables.

Every few minutes: *plink*. A drop hits the broad leaf of an aroid ten paces off. The leaf doesn’t snap back; it is too heavy, too thick with oily juice. It merely dips an inch, slow as a counterweight, and rises again.

A young man comes down the red ladder opposite me. He has a tin of vine-milk in one hand and his chisel tucked into his hemp belt. His chest is broad, arched like a barrel, glistening with the greasy film that keeps the red mold out of his skin. He doesn't look at me, and I don't look at him, but as he passes the turn of the stair, his bare heel clicks once on a rivet that has worked loose from its hole.

He knows it. I know it.

It was not a true sound—not a shout or a dropped spanner—but it was a sharpness that didn't belong to the room. He pauses, his yellow sole hovering an inch above the iron, his shoulders stiffening in the orange light. He waits until the echo—the small, woolly rustle of it—is swallowed by the big leaves. Then he sets his foot down on the plate with the flat, rolling weight of a man who knows the metal is listening, and goes on down toward the sumps.

I reach into my pocket, take out the awl, and lay the thumb of my left hand against the lead jamb to find the next clear space.

The metal is cool against my skin. It has the same temperature as the rock behind me, but it is softer than the brick, softer than the iron, softer even than the sole of my foot. 

I find a patch of grey, unscarred surface no wider than three beans, right where the casing turns into the dark of the tunnel. I set the point of the file against it. I don't strike it; you don't use a hammer here. You lean your shoulder into the tool, using the thick meat of your chest and the high collarbone to drive the steel into the lead until you feel the sharp *bite* of the metal parting underneath.

One mark. A loose rivet on the third riser of the West Catwalk.

The bright lead curls out from the point like a grey worm, cold and greasy. I catch the curl with my thumb, roll it into a little ball between two fingers, and put it in my mouth to hold until I can spit it into the scrap-pot by the hearth. It tastes sweet and heavy, like well-water that has run through old pipes, and it stays cold against the tongue long after the steam has closed back over the door.
