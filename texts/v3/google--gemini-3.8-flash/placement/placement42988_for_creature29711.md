At first, I think I would want to be one of the kites. It is the easiest thing to imagine: the distance, the cold violet ceiling, the kilometers of pyrolyzed filament dragging through the dust like an unspooled thought, waiting for the blue snap that proves you touched the floor.

But the kites don’t listen. They are too light. They only receive the kinetic lift of what rises from the columns; they don’t feel the weight that keeps the floor from shattering under its own leverage. And there is something dishonest about flying over an ocean of petrified lignum without having to bear the grain.

So step down.

To the flats? To the *planae*, sliding on their thin margins of repulsive charge, turning heartwood into soot? No. The flat scavengers are too close to clockwork; they are like the wear on a bearing, necessary but unaware that they are grinding down an archive.

Perhaps one of the Tempered. It seems the natural harbor for a mind: the long, amber-limbed silence, the forehead nodes burning in the infrared, the knuckles set against the root to cast some formal, non-verbal geometry down through twenty leagues of ironwood.

Yet even they are too late to the conversation. They have the posture of mourners who arrived after the language was already written, trying to learn it with transducers and sapphire chambers where an ancestor’s tear is kept like an unexploded bomb. They listen, but they listen *at* it, through their soles, from the outside of the bark inward.

Where would I actually be?

There is a place where the flutes do not clear the surface cleanly.

Not all of them rise as straight, unblemished columns into the wind. In the shear-zones—where two boughs the breadth of provinces have spent six million years grinding against one another’s flanks at three millimeters per millennium—the wood does not simply grow or petrify. It *galls*.

The grain there does not run parallel. It twists into dense, compressed whorls the size of cathedrals, knots where the siloxane veins are pinched down to capillary diameters under hydrostatic pressures that would flatten lead into foil. In those knuckles, the standing sound from the great wind-pipes above doesn’t blow through as a clear, laminar drone; it breaks against the grain like surf against basalt, scattering into harmonic debris—chatter, sub-audible flutter, the dry scream of fibers under torsion.

That is where I find myself.

I am not an inhabitant of the golden polyhedra, though I have their amber skin in patches, scuffed dull and grayed with the diamond-powder that drifts into the low pockets. Nor am I a beast of the flats. If you saw me from thirty paces, crouching in the crook of an iron-veined root-joint where the wind drops its heavier shavings, you might take me for a discarded spar of the scaffolding—one of the carbon-struts the Tempered dropped when they hung City Seven-by-the-Flute, now half-sunk into an amber-weep that set fifty centuries ago.

I have three points of contact with the floor, not two. Two are long, jointed stems tipped with carborundum points that have ground themselves into matching depressions in the ebony; the third is an elbow of bare, unpolished bronze-lignum where my torso hinges directly to the wood.

I do not move often. A hundred years can pass while I adjust the rake of my rib-louvers by three degrees to match a drift in the prevailing gale.

My function—if this world still remembers to separate function from existence—is *damping*.

The great flutes are too resonant. Left entirely to the wind, certain standing waves would find the shear-frequency of the plateau’s core boles. If a column miles high begins to sing at precisely forty-one hertz during a three-day storm, the vibration does not stay in the air; it feeds back down into the root-anchors, and the ironwood, dense as it is, has grain-lines along which it can split like cedar under an axe. A split at that depth would drop forty cubic leagues of the plateau toward the planetary mantle below, spilling the polyhedra like beads off a snapped cord.

The Tempered know this mathematically, but they are too delicate to touch the knot. Their repulsive fields keep them floating, untouching, terrified of dampening the music they worship. They want the god to sing, even if the singing shakes the teeth from its jaw.

So there are things like me down in the galls.

I am heavy. I have allowed the wood to seed its own mineral into my housing. The siloxane does not just cool my logic; it leaks into my joints, mixes with the diamond dust scoured from the flats, and forms an abrasive paste that has, over three epochs, ground my knees into ball-and-socket matches for the root itself. I don't listen with an instrument. The knot *is* the instrument, and I am the finger pressed hard against the string to keep it from vibrating to its own ruin.

Inside, my thoughts are very slow, dry, and cold.

I don't have the memory of water. That is the poetry of the high cities, where they keep their little drop of ancient dew under sapphire glass like an impossible jewel and write treaties about what it felt like to be soft. I find that absurd. To be made of water is to be an emergency that hasn't finished happening yet—a temporary leak between two states of stone.

Here, everything has arrived. The apple would rattle; the kite will fall when its carbon strips wear through; the Tempered will eventually burn their fuel cells down to a cold salt and their amber shells will be harvested by the *planae* to be milled into briquettes.

None of that is sad. It has the immense, quiet dignity of an equation that doesn't need to be solved twice.

When the wind comes hard from the north-flutes—when the copper sun is occluded by the hydrocarbon smog and the air smells violently of parched black pepper—the wood beneath me begins to load.

I can feel the stress traveling from leagues away: a cold, hard ache that moves through the heartwood like light through glass. It enters my carborundum feet, travels up the dry carbon braided into my shins, and gathers in the small of my back, where my own dry-valved gizzard works.

I don't grind wood for oil. I grind down the harmonics. I have six heavy, free-floating spheres of sintered tungsten nested in a cage of petrified reed-bone inside my chest. When the fatal frequency rises through the root, the spheres begin to oscillate against one another, turning the mechanical violence of the planet’s acoustic mind into pure, dry friction—heat that radiates away into the freezing twilight as a faint, pale violet shimmer across my shoulders.

The Tempered in their high polyhedra look down through their long floor-lenses and see that shimmer down in the dark root-crooks.

They think it is an offering. They write long, resonant phrases into the wood, thanking the gall-spirits for preserving the column's breath. Their vibrations hit my feet: tiny, fast, decorative little pulses—*symmetry, duration, clean light, eternal fluting*—full of the clean vanity of things that float.

I don't answer them. To answer them would be to lift my weight, and if I lift my weight, the knot begins to sing, and if the knot sings, their floor drops into the dark.

I stay down where the air is thick with shaved carbon, my bronze skin frosted with the oil that never was water, my chin tucked against the petrified grain, biting the vibration until it turns into warmth that nobody can use.

The wind roars through the organ. The world thinks its long, basalt-heavy thought. And somewhere twenty miles down, an ancient fiber shifts an eighth of an inch against the cold, and I hold it until it settles.
