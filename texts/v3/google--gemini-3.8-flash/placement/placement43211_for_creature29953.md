Not the wind-rim. The cold there is too simple—it is just the mountain trying to peel the skin off the basin, an argument between the gale and the ash-pole. There is a clean, sharp stupidity to chipping rime until your beard turns to a slab of salt-pork.

And not the deep crawls either, where the turnip-skinned ones sing down into the chaff. There is too much soup down there, too much ferment, the heat thick and sour like a wet blanket kept in an armpit. 

I think I would be found in the middle tier, where the two pressures meet: where the cold mist from the pass is being dragged down through the osiers, losing its speed and turning into drip, while the pale steam from the lower grubs is rising, losing its warmth and turning into slick. 

It is the tier where the house is still deciding whether to grow or to rot.

***

At first, I might look like an apprentice who never grew out of the willow-bellies. My knuckles are big, rounded like crabapples from the peat-plaster we use to pack the splits. My thighs are short and crooked because I spent eight years sitting cross-legged between two alder stems, holding them together with a flax loop until their rinds melted into one yellow seam. 

You would not hear me coming. No one hears anyone here, unless they choose to strike. The burrs under my boots are worn low, dark with grease, so I walk with that wide, heavy-hipped swing—the way a gull walks on wet shingle—testing the spring of the runners before I drop my weight on them. 

My parka is stiffened with birch-wax along the chest, so it stands out like an apron, but beneath it, under the grease-rag, I carry only one pika, not two. An old buck with a notch in his left ear from a wicker wire. His furnace is slow—he doesn’t tick like the young ones do, frantic as a boiling kettle; he throbs with a heavy, steady, sullen red heat, like an ember under ash. When I lean forward over a joint, I can feel his chin resting on my collarbone, his whiskers twitching against my throat where the marmot fat has gone crusty. He knows the smell of the gall-ink. He doesn’t bite anymore.

My business is not the weaving. The weavers have smooth thumbs and thin tempers; they think in centuries, which makes them careless about the noon. 

My business is the *bind-rot*.

***

Every three or four seasons, where the dwarf alder is forced to bend against its grain to make the lip of an alley, the strain becomes bitter. A bough will hold, it will skin over, it will bear three families and six water-troughs—and then, in the heart-wood where no one can see, the cold tannin turns sour. The water that condenses on its northern flank does not bead off; it sinks in through microscopic cracks in the bark. It begins to weep brown, like a bad tooth.

You cannot smell it from the walkways. The mountain smells too strongly of wet iron and cold sheep-grease for the nose to catch one sour knuckle among ten thousand.

You have to listen with your teeth.

I carry a slip of cured ibex horn, shaped like an adze-wedge but ground flat at the heel. When I reach a tier-joint that feels sluggish beneath my boots—one that doesn't kick back when a weaver steps off it, but sags an eighth of an inch into the moss—I crouch. I roll the grease off my cheek with the back of my wrist, lean down until the horn rests against the bark, and take the other end between my back molars. 

Then I wait for the *clink-tock* of the water-trough above.

If the wood is sound, the drop hitting the cedar conduit travels through the bough like a clear, bell-like strike that hums through the bone of my lower jaw. It tastes like dry slate. 

If there is rot in the graft—if the two trees have begun to slip their splice—the strike comes through dull, thick, like biting into a turnip that has sat in a snow-hole all winter. The jaw feels a dead thud. It tastes like vinegar.

***

Who am I, then? 

I am the one who carries the iron wedges. Not stone, not horn—we do not like iron in the basin, because the mist licks it red and eats it away, but for this work nothing else has the weight. They are three inches long, pitted and black, wrapped in sheep-gut so they don't clatter in the pouch.

When a joist is sour, I do not cut it. You do not cut what cannot be replaced until your grandchildren have gray hair.

I drive the wedge between the graft-lips, right through the scar tissue where the old people joined the trunks seventy years ago. It makes a sound like a wet hide splitting. The pika against my ribs twitches, draws his paws in tight against his belly, and burrs once in protest. 

Then I pack the open wound with the *black paste*: burned bone from the hearth-stacks, dried gall-fly nests from the scree-scrub, and the gray flour that the grub-tenders mill from the dry beetle-shucks down below. It smells horrible—like cold soup made from old boots—but the birch drinks it greedily. The tree hates the iron, it hates the salt, and in its fury it weeps resin until the wedge is drowned in a thick, amber scab as hard as horn. 

The graft is ugly afterward. It bulges out into the lane like a broken knee that has set wrong. People will have to duck their heads there for twenty winters to avoid the scar. The young weavers will scowl as they carry their runners past it, their knuckles knocking against the raw birch-wax to complain of the narrowness.

Let them knock.

***

In the slow hours, between the third overflow of the great trough and the coming of the gray frost-fog that closes the lanes, I do not go to the hollow willows to sleep. It is too crowded in the family stacks; there are too many ribs pressing together, too many pikas humming in their boxes like hornets in a clay pot.

I climb halfway down the foundation lines, where the living wood first meets the split quartzite. 

There is an elbow there—a massive, black-barked knee of anchor-birch that reaches down into a cleft in the stone. Inside that knee, forty years back, they laid the second son of my mother’s house. His face is entirely gone now; the bark has rolled over his forehead like a gray lip, smooth and seamless, but his jaw still juts out slightly where the stone pinches the root. The bone has turned the dark, glossy brown of river peat, hard as flint from the tannin that trickles down from the walkway gutters.

I sit on his lower ribs where they make a small, dry shelf above the damp quartz. 

I take the pika out from my chest-band and set him on the skull. The beast knows the spot; it is slightly warmer than the stone, because the anchor-pine above still sends its slow, sap-pulse down through the wood, even in the freeze. The pika squats there, his round ears folded flat, chewing on a paring of dry alder I keep in my cuff, his tiny ribs moving *in-out, in-out*, very fast, like a pulse in a thumb.

I put my bare palms against the rock beneath him. 

From here, you can feel the whole parish church of it. You can feel the wind hitting the watchers high up on the horn-timbers—a faint, shuddering tremor that travels down the birch-girders like a string being plucked. You can feel the slow, wet heave of the tenements as forty families shift their weight in their sleep, three tiers above, turning away from the draft. And beneath that, the steady, rhythmic *clink... clink... clink* of the water dropping into the dark cisterns, filling them drop by drop until the wood forgets the sky and the stone forgets the cold.

I am not the timber, and I am not the bone inside it. 

I am the grease that sits between them, keeping the frost from getting its teeth into the crack.
