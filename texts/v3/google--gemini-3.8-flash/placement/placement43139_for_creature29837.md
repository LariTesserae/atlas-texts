At first, you would think yourself a peg.

A wedge of dense heartwood, perhaps, driven into an expansion fault by hands that worked fast against the freeze, pounded home with a chert maul until the mallet bounced and the grain around the split screamed in three notes at once. That is the first sensation: the four-sided compression, the vice of green timber that never stops tightening, the smell of vinegar-bitter gum rising like cold lard around your ribs until there is no air left to displace.

You would expect to drown in it. But the gum does not drown; it displaces the moisture in your calves first, then your thighs, creeping up through the porous marrow where the blood used to thin out in the high gales. 

So no—not a peg. A peg is dead wood, flint-black and dry. 

To find yourself here, you have to go into the fault while the skin is still warm enough to steam against the frost. You are what happens three seasons after the wedges are knocked out and the lips of the split come back together with the sound of an iron gate dropping into gravel.

***

I am seated, though there is no chair. The wood provides the posture. 

My spine is aligned with the vertical shear of a strut twenty leagues above the rot-floor, where the northwest wind splits around a knuckle the size of a citadel. My chin is pressed into my sternum by forty feet of living bark that has healed over the gash; my knees are drawn up to my throat, tucked between the layers of annual growth that do not grow in rings, but in sheets—vast, laminated leaves of green-white fiber that slide against one another under hydraulic surge like wet leather belts.

The Knot-bound call this being braced. They speak of it as an honor, or a duty, or a burial. They are mistaken about the sequence. They think you die, and then they wedge you in to keep the split from running. 

The truth is slower, and much more hydraulic.

The plant does not let bone stay bone. Bone is too soft; it has air in it, little caverns where the marrow used to shiver. When the sap finds those pockets, under five thousand pounds of pressure from the lower pumps, it does not crush them—it fills them. It drives the cold vinegar into every pore of the femur, into the spongy interior of the vertebrae, until the calcium turns to pale amber. You become heavy. You become dense as ballast. 

In the first year, you still hear with your ears. You hear the flute-whistle of the million-foot drops, the dry clatter of the leathery kites banking off the lee-side, the short, rhythmic *clack-clack-thump* of the camps signaling that a thermal is rising from the depths. You hear the hunters crawling over your own face—the prick of their recurved iron-chert claws biting into the outer rind just inches from your closed eyelids, dragging a gutted slug whose grease seeps down through the grain to touch your temple.

In the second year, the ears fill with gum. The hearing moves down into the collarbones. 

You no longer hear the wind. The wind is an exterior vanity, a thing for creatures with bellies and sails. You hear only the load. 

The load is magnificent. It has nothing to do with thought. It is the simple, terrifying mathematics of a billion tons of wet lignum trying to fall into the abyss and being prevented from doing so by the friction of its own knots. 

When a gale hits the upper canopy—that ceiling of dry storm-clouds three leagues higher—the whole lattice leans four paces to the east. You feel it begin fifty miles away: a minute hardening of the grain, an iron stiffness that travels down the spar like a chill down an arm. The fibers around my ribs bunch together. My sternum, petrified now, takes thirty tons of side-drift and transfers it down into my pelvis, through my heels, and into the runner-truss that dives straight down into the warm dark. 

I am an inclusion. A knot where the grain must twist to get around the stone-hard relic of what used to be a chest.

***

Sometimes the Listener lies directly above me.

She is young, this one. I know her by the weight of her cheek. She does not weigh more than a dried kite-carcass—the women of the crotch-camps are all sinew and hollow ribs, their bellies tucked under their lungs to keep from catching the draft. When she lays her skull against the bark, she is barely a thumb’s-breadth from the crown of my head, separated only by the green rind that grew two winters ago.

She taps. *Tock. Tock. Tock-tock.*

Her stone billet is green serpentine, ground smooth. She is asking the spar if it intends to split when the thermal drops at noon.

I cannot tap back. My arms are welded to my flanks; the gum has turned my fingers into five pale roots that have followed the tension-fissures around the circumference of the beam until my nails touched my own spine. But I can stiffen. 

When the deep hydraulic pulse rises from the floor—that vast, sour sigh of warm rot that expands the wood before the cold drop comes—I take the expansion into my teeth. My jaw is set. If the spar is going to hold, my teeth hold. The vibration passes through the enamel, through the amber-filled skull, through the two inches of bark, and strikes the thin plate of her temple.

She feels it. I feel her breath hitch—that quick, shallow gasp of a creature that knows it is balanced on a hair over nothing. She doesn't speak. She strikes two quick beats: *Heard.*

Then she scrambles away, her hooked toes clicking against the fibers as she hurries back to the inverted hull to tell them to tighten the hawsers.

***

There are hundreds of us in this knuckle alone. 

We do not talk, because the wood only speaks in the imperative: *Bear. Yield. Split. Stiffen.* But when the great frosts drop from the grey ceiling and the sap retreats inward toward the core to keep from bursting the walls, the wood shrinks. 

It shrinks until the seams open just a hair—a hairline fracture, invisible to the kites, but to us it is a sudden loosening of the skin. For an hour, between the third watch of the wind and the first grey light of the slate sky, the pressure drops from thirty tons to ten.

In that hour, the blood that is no longer blood moves a fraction of an inch through the petrified tubes of my arms. 

I remember the cold of having fingers that could drop things. 

It was a terrible way to live. The constant vigilance of the knees; the terror of the slick sole; the nightmare of horizontal ground that might tilt and slide you off into the white mists where you fall until you are nothing but a dried skin rattling in the downdraft. 

Down here, there is no falling. There is nowhere to fall to. The wood has me. It has taken the loose, soft shape of a man and corrected it. It has planed away the wasteful hollows, filled the lungs with stone-gum, and braced the fragile lattice of the ribs with twenty rings of white iron-grain.

The gale is rising again. I feel it through the soles of my feet first—a dry, shivering hum that means the clouds are scraping the upper knuckles. 

Above me, the little camp is shivering in its hide-skins, their yellow sap-lamps smoking in the fissures, their children tied fast to the heart-plugs, everyone praying to the wood not to twitch. 

They think they are holding on. They think their braided ropes of sinew and kite-gut are what keep their beds between the sky and the floor.

They don't know the density of the knot beneath their floorboards. They don't know how hard the teeth have to bite together, down in the dark where the sap smells of vinegar, to keep the mountain from coming apart.
