At first, the instinct is to claim the sky—to be one of the great horned cattle standing at the lip of the southern shelf, carrying three miles of welded horn upon the neck, holding the flat cerulean in a dry bead of jet. It is clean up there. The light does not flinch. You would be a monument, a pier in a suspension bridge of bone, feeling the dull *thock* of an accidental blow travel out through your brow into forty thousand other skulls and down into the slate without leaving a mark on your mind.

Or to be the shepherd. That is the human conceit: to imagine oneself the one with the ash crook and the orange-bright chisel, standing half-shod in a heifer’s flank, thinking the slow, horizontal thought that keeps the geometry from sliding.

But that is not where I am. 

Those positions are too intentional. They still smell of purpose, of the old panic that drove the nomadic tribes up out of the rot of the valleys. The shepherds climbed up here to *cure* the world; they brought their tools and their counts because they were stubborn. They are still working, even if the work takes ten thousand years for a single gouge of copper into horn. 

No. If I look for myself, I have to go down. Past the braided canopy. Past the dry, felted backs of the sheep where the sun lays its pale, unshifting rectangles. 

Down into the crawlspaces, where the light loses its blue and becomes the color of horn-shavings and dry flour.

***

At first, I think I am a mica-rat. I feel the flat, slate floor against my belly—not cold, exactly, but fifty degrees, which is the temperature of stone that has never met a hearth. I feel the suction-pads of my toes, dry as turnip discs, clinging to the dolomite without muscular effort, sealed by the clean vacuum of a world that does not breathe. 

There is an appeal in being small and scaled, tucked into the triangular vault where three sheep-shins meet the mountain. You would feel the weight above you not as an agony, but as a ceiling that has agreed never to drop. The mica on your back would reflect only the white pith of the hock beside you.

Yet there is too much neatness in the rat. It belongs too thoroughly to its niche. It fits like an unglazed tile.

I am not an unglazed tile. I did not arrive here with an articulated shell already designed to fold into a puzzle-box. 

I must have had a softer lineage.

***

Move forty paces east, toward the lip of one of those shallow depressions—the threshing floors where space has lost its manners.

Here, the density thickens until the edges blur. It is not dark, because the animals themselves seem to emit a faint, chalky lucence, like the phosphorescence of dry wood. 

Now the body clarifies. 

I am four-legged, but longer in the reach than the rodents. A hound, perhaps, or the ancestor of one—a rough-coated, thin-flanked thing that came up the shale scree behind the sheep before the scree settled into an unbroken pavement. A dog that belonged to the nomads, built for turning the strays on the steep edges, lungs once accustomed to the thin, sharp frost of the passes.

Except there are no strays now. The perimeter ceased to exist when the herd became the mountain.

My head is down. My chin rests not on the stone, but on the dry, pithy hock of a stag. My ribs—thin as ash splints—do not rise. That is the first thing that becomes certain: the absolute relief of the empty chest. There is no panting. There is no tongue hanging over blunt teeth to catch the grease of an air that does not move. The bellows have been packed with that elderberry pith, firm and odorless, holding the ribs at their widest, most comfortable expansion.

And I am not alone in my skin.

This is where the plateau does its real work, and this is where I find the center of what I am. I am standing precisely at the margin where the depression deepens, where the arithmetic gives way. 

My right foreleg passes directly through the thigh-bone of a yearling sheep. We do not jostle. We do not displace. Her marrow and my tendon share the same three cubic inches of reality. If you were to lay a hand upon that point, you would feel neither wool nor coarse hound-hair, but a third thing: a cool, smooth density like paraffin or cold suet, resisting the thumb with the absolute certainty of two truths spoken at the same instant.

My left ear—dry as a dried apricot, folded back against my skull—occupies the same volume as the base of a wild goat’s horn. 

Through that horn, I receive the news.

***

It is not thought. It is vibration, stripped of sound. 

Three miles to the north, a small crust of slate, balanced on the spine of a sleeping badger since the third age of the drying, has settled half a millimeter. The *tick* of it does not travel through the acoustic void of the air; it travels through the badger’s spine, into the mica-rat beneath its paw, into the ankle of the red deer, along the continuous, cold-welded lattice of horns that covers the plateau like a wicker basket, and down through the goat’s horn into my ear.

It enters my skull as a tiny, dry click. It does not demand an answer. I do not prick my ears; the cartilage has hardened into something like boxwood. But I register it. 

I am the register.

The shepherd at the wall thinks he is keeping the count with his five copper tallies, but his hand is slow. His chisel has been biting that fourth line for an epoch. He does not know what is happening in the lower strata. He does not know that beneath his boots, twelve layers deep in the buckled depressions, thirty thousand creatures have braided their limbs into a logic that has forgotten the sky entirely.

Down here, we do not look at the bleached cerulean. We look into each other’s flanks. My jet-black eye looks directly into the shoulder-blade of an elk, half an inch away, and finds it entirely sufficient. The bone has a grain like cedar-wood, pale and dry, traced with the microscopic channels where the blind white lice creep with their ivory hooks.

Sometimes, one of the lice crosses the frontier between the elk and me. It does not know where the elk ends and the hound begins; to its microscopic feet, our interpenetrated pith is a single continent of dry starch. It hooks its way along an old nerve-track that belonged to my hunting days, passes without pause into the marrow of the elk’s scapula, and anchors a broken thread of white pith with its own body. 

When it hooks in, there is a minute tension—a tightening of the seam. I feel it the way a dry roof feels the setting of an iron peg.

***

This is who I am: 

The hound at the floor of the fold, whose running has been folded away into the grain of the rock; 

whose blood has turned to the white pith of elder-stems; 

whose flank is half-ewe and whose ear is horn; 

holding, in the cool, timeless fifty degrees of the dolomite, the bottom-most knot of the world’s finished tapestry.

We are not waiting for the shepherd to finish his fourth mark. If he dropped the chisel, it would fall three inches, land on the wool between his feet, and produce no sound at all. The sky would not darken. The mountains would not sharpen their teeth. 

The knuckle of the dolomite would continue to bear us, and we would continue to bear the dolomite, neither living nor dead, but woven so tightly into the ledger of the mountain that even time, should it ever find its way back up the wash, would find no seam to prise apart.
