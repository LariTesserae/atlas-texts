I would not be on the open flats. The Skiffs have made that flat expanse a desert of pure momentum; if you have edges, if you have thoughts that take longer than a quarter-mile to resolve, their wakes will plane you into splinters or the vascular mat will seize your soles before you have chosen a direction. 

Nor would I be in the iron-wood towers, playing that desperate, sweating game the Glaziers play—swelling with liver-wax, lying to xylem that will eventually wake up, curdle, and drop you forty cubits to the deck. That is a courtier’s existence: frantic, parasitic, and short.

To find me, you would have to look down—not into the gutters where the scalding tea-water screams, but into the quietest flaw in the basin: the lip of the central depression, where the circle of ancient stumps rots so slowly that the wood has forgotten whether it is horn or wet slate.

I am not one of the Steles. I do not have their tall, dry dignity, nor their organ-pipes of jet. 

I think, at first, I am only an irregularity in the wash. 

A lump of dark tallow, perhaps, caught in the throat of an old hollow stump. A knot of dense, water-cured leather that has failed to slough. The Skiffs, banking at seventy miles an hour around the rim of the saucer, taste me with their snouts as they pass and register only a dull neutrality—neither the sugar of fresh cambium nor the bitter iron of an aborted gallery—and so they tilt their ribs and slide past, leaving me in the spray.

If you watched for an hour, you would see that the lump does not stay in the tooth. 

When the floor begins its pulmonary heave—that deep, subterranean intake of breath that flattens the surface water and turns the light into a grey, oilcloth glare—I come out.

I am low, wider than I am tall, shaped like a mason’s trowel or a flat-bottomed river-stone that has been given eight jointless, hydraulic pads beneath. My skin is the color of old copper left in a marsh: greenish-black, slick, and completely unpalatable to the floor. The vascular mat cannot graft me because I secrete no vegetable resin; my moisture is an alkaline grease, cold and mineral, distilled from the deep, calcified peat that sits beneath the wooden crust. 

I am the thing that tends the rotted teeth.

While the floor is inhaling, while the Steles are lifting their fibrous skirts to drift on the harmonic rise, the water across the basin stalls. That is the only time the world here has an edge. For five seconds, the engineered tension fails; the water gathers itself into those shivering, upright pyramids, each one sharp as a crystal prism, humming with the frequency of the basin’s deep valve.

In those five seconds, I do not slide, and I do not root. I *bite*.

My pads are tipped with narrow crescents of dark flint—not the basin’s living wood, but actual stone, dredged up from the petrified beds miles down through the intake conduits. I drive them into the sub-molecular seams where the floor’s grain has frayed. 

The floor does not like to be remembered. Its whole genius is forgetfulness—it dissolves its towers, it flushes its curd, it drinks its own pulp and prints another arch before the foam has dried. But the saucer at the center is an old memory that will not digest. The stump-ring was once a hall, or a threshing floor, or the base of an intake that drank something heavier than sap. Its grain runs against the grain of the modern floor; it is a permanent knot in the timber.

My work is to keep that knot from healing.

With short, rhythmic strokes of my flint-shod limbs—sounding like a cobbler driving tacks into wet saddle-flaps—I scrape the racing film’s sediments out of the rotted mortises. I clear the grain. I pare back the greedy, translucent tendrils that the living floor sends creeping up the dead stumps every morning. If I did not work, the basin would plane the council ring flat within three seasons, turning it into just another hundred yards of frictionless horn for the Skiffs to race across.

The Steles know I am there, though they never look down. They cannot. Their dry hampers are fixed on the steam plumes, their internal bone-pipes tuned to the chime of the high containment field. But when I finish clearing an ancient socket, when the true, fossil wood of the old foundation is bared to the wet air, it gives off a faint, resinous note—a dull *thup* that vibrates through the vascular mat.

And sometimes, when the floor has exhaled, when the roar of the water returns and the pyramids collapse back into grey, sliding ribbons that pin me flat against the saucer’s slope, the nearest Stele will settle down after its rotation and stop six inches closer to me than it was before.

It does not speak. But as the iron-wood towers convulse in the distance—as another gallery breaks with a sound like a split hull and the mantids dive for the slough—the Stele will warm slightly. A drift of dry, cinnamon-smelling dust will leak from its vertical seams, caught instantly by the hot draft and whipped across my back.

It is bird-chitin, ground fine. It is copper dust from the vents. It is the salt of things that had bones before this floor was grown. 

I draw the dust into the wet folds of my flanks, kneading it into the tallow of my hide, making myself heavier, harder, less digestible.

The Skiffs have speed. The Glaziers have their five weeks of architectural empire. The Steles have their delayed assembly, which they will attend until their ribs rot into peat.

I have the friction. I am the only thing in three hundred yards of immaculate, roaring grease that knows how to stay behind.
