At first, there is only the load. 

It does not present itself as thought, but as a distribution of tonnage through the arches of the feet and the small, flat bones of the pelvis. To be here is to be pressed upon. The city is four hundred feet of solid masonry above the river-shelf, and the stone does not merely rest; it leans, it pinches, it settles into its own tolerances with a slow, grinding insistence that never entirely goes quiet. 

If you do not attend to it, you would think you were only a stone among the others, wedged into a blind mortise where the sun never strikes. The cold comes in from the north wall—a dead, mineral cold that tastes of crushed shale—while from the south, deep in the core of the block, there is still the faint, greasy residue of yesterday’s noon, a blood-heat stored five feet back in the granodiorite.

I am not the water. The water is too fast, too thoughtless; it has no loyalty to the plane. It wants only to get to the drop, to shred itself into the pale air over the terraces and be gone into the lower cloud. 

Nor am I the black flies scraping the sluices. I hear them, though. When my cheek is turned to the riser of a cistern-stair, their mandibles sound like someone dragging a handful of river-shingle across a slate roof—a dry, rhythmic *scrit-scrit-scrit* that travels through the damp rock better than it does through the wind. They have no grief, and no fear of the ice. They are merely tools the water uses to keep its own throat clear.

No, I am thicker than that. The ribs are wide, wide enough that my elbows cannot rest against my flanks without being pushed outward by the cage of the lungs. The breath does not rise and fall in the chest; it rolls in the belly like a full bilge, slow and heavy, smelling of tallow and the dried, peppery cakes of the pupae we press in the stone presses at the end of the dry month.

There is a tool in my hand, though I do not hold it with fingers that bend easily. It is an old thing—a bar of horn-slate, four cubits long, its tip ground into a chisel-beak that matches the gauge of the second-order drainage seams. 

I know where the water is failing before I see it.

It comes up through the soles: not a sound, but an unevenness in the mountain’s shudder. Two terraces down, behind the wall of the Seventh Sluice, a weight has gathered. A dead fish, perhaps—one of the great pale ones from the low cisterns that sometimes loses its grip on the flume-mouth and drowns in the light—or a drift of granite flour washed down from the frost-shattered teeth above the city. The water there is no longer sliding; it is backing up against the lintel, rising an inch, two inches, seeking the dry seams where the lizards lie in their cold stupor.

If it reaches them, it will freeze tonight when the clouds tear open and the sky goes black and empty. If it freezes, the seam will open by the thickness of a thumb-nail. The next freeze will take the terrace.

My movement is not a stride. You do not stride on wet basalt where the slope is one-in-four and the flags are greased with the yellow liverwort-skin. You lean into the grade. You splay the five broad toes, each one calloused until it is round as a river-cobble, and you slide the mass forward, keeping the center of the cask low between the hips. 

The air in the lane is thin, like cold water held in the mouth. It has no taste except the faint, sour iron of the cistern-hatches. A cloud is dragging its belly down the street, belly-high, blind and white, muffling the *plip-plip* of the drops falling from the corbels overhead. I do not need the light. The city was not laid out for the eyes; it was laid out for the run of the drop. If you put your heel down on any flag in the three thousand lanes, you can feel which way the drainage falls by the subtle cant of the stone under your arch. Every stone tilts toward the abyss. Every stone is a slide.

I reach the lintel. 

The blockage is there, where the granodiorite has been grooved by five hundred years of gravel. It is a heap of grey silt and the carcass of a swift, its narrow wings glued to the stone by cold fat, stiff as a bit of charred wood. The water is already lipping the dry seam—a thin, trembling meniscus of black liquid, trembling with the seismic pulse of the city’s heart.

I drop to one knee. The stone is icy, wet enough that the tallow on my thighs stiffens to grey grease. 

I do not hurry. A hurried strike against the horn-slate will shatter the point, and there is no more slate until the traders come up the rope-baskets from the lower gorges in the third season—if they come at all, if the ropes have not rotted in the mist.

I set the chisel-beak into the notch below the swift’s body. I bring the heel of my palm down on the butt—once, twice.

The blow does not make a sound in the lane; the thin air swallows it. But beneath me, twenty feet down in the blind cistern, the water rings like a struck bowl. A long, dull *tonnng* shudders through the rock. 

The swift breaks away. The silt follows it, a black, viscous tongue that slips into the throat of the down-pipe with a wet, sucking gulp. The dry seam empties. The water drops four inches, finding its true weir again, flattening out into that sheer, silent sheet that slides over the edge of the terrace and down into the next level.

I stay there on my knee for a long time. 

The rain to the east has moved. The grey curtain has shifted toward the central platforms, silvered on its edge by the noon sun that is trying to burn through the fog above my head. My chest rises—one long, cold draft that tastes of lime and the sky—and stays full while the pulse beats twelve times in the thick artery behind my jaw. Then it goes out, a grey plume that disappears against the grey wall.

Above me, in the dry seam that is safe for another winter, a lizard twitches. Its blunt, slate-colored head emerges from the crack, its tongue testing the thinned air, seeking the first heat of the rock. It does not look at me. To the lizard, I am only the five-foot flag that does not move until the frost comes.

I turn the slate bar in my palms, feeling the grit on the leather wraps. 

There are twelve thousand weirs between the upper crest and the long drop. Three of them are mine. By the time the shadow of the western parapet reaches the basin of the cisterns, the sun will have left the eastern flags, and the ice will begin its work at the edges of the pools.

I rise. My back finds the angle of the wall, takes the load again, and fits itself to the stone.
