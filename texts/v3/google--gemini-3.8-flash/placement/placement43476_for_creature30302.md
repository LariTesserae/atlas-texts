I would not be on the brick steps. The steps belong to the dry leather, the horn cup, and the memory of things that had corners and thresholds. 

Nor would I be down in the sixty-foot liquor of the cylinder well. To be the great lung-fish is to be an anchor, an geology unto oneself, so old and thick-bellied that even the iron arches are only a brief crust overhead. That kind of patience requires a heart that beats once every two hundred drops of boiler-sweat; it is too close to stone.

I would find myself in the mid-water, hooked by the crook of an arm over the stationary balance-wheel.

Not a man, and not quite an otter, but the fourth or fifth remove of a water-spaniel whose great-granddam fell through a coal-chute when the boilers still had fires. 

My hide would be the color of a wet slate shingle, puckered at the armpits and behind the hocks with the gray, cauliflower creases of permanent submersion. Hair is an error down here; it rots, it catches the sharp edges of the louse-shells, it turns into a heavy, sour felt that drags a body toward the sump before its time. Better this boiled-pork skin, tough as an old apron, sealed against the alkaline bite by a gland-sweat that smells like cold tallow and river silt.

My jaw would be resting flat against the twelve-foot balance-wheel, where the iron spoke leaves the hub. 

That is where the world is told.

Through the bone of the lower jaw, the Nave does not present itself as sights or smells, but as an endless, greasy hum. The brickwork has one note—a low, grinding drone like a millstone turned by dead water—and the walking-beam has another: a heavy, wet *shuck-clang* that takes forty minutes to climb from the cellar-strata into the teeth of my skull. 

When the southern tilt begins, three rooms away, I would feel the bronze gudgeon-pin groan in its seat long before the water-scum parted. The iron speaks directly to the teeth. You can taste the strain in it: a prickle of acid on the back of the tongue when the thirty tons of truss-work bend against the hemp packing, and then the sweet, oily release when the man finishes working his rendered louse-grease into the housing.

He does not know I have my chin on his wheel. Or perhaps he does, the way he knows the coin-thickness of the water’s rise, but he pays it no mind. 

We are not pack. Pack is a word from the street-grade, where there was light enough to see an ear cocked or a tail drop. Down here, we are merely things that occupy the same displacement. 

When he wades out into the pool—the water hissing faintly against the stiff apron of his coat—I do not look up. To open an eye in this liquor is to let the lime bloom bite the jelly; the third lid, the milky one, stays pulled across until the light from the chutes turns the water the color of lead tea. But I feel his displacement. A man is a clumsy displace-ment: three hundred pounds of bone, stiff harness-leather, and stagnant soak, pushing a blunt, slow bow-wave that rocks the river-lice off their rust-stems forty paces away. 

The bow-wave comes to my wheel. It lifts my flank two inches off the iron spoke, rolls me in the cold mineral-grease, and lets me settle back down. 

Then comes the waiting. 

My lungs are no bigger than two turnip halves, but they do not hunger for air the way the city dogs hungered for it. A lung down here is not an engine; it is a ballast-tank. You take one slow drink of the chimney-draft when the starling-clicks are closest to the water—tasting the soot, the sheep-fat, the horse-piss dried into street-dust three strata above—and then you hold it. You hold it until the air inside your ribs turns to vinegar, until the blood cools down to the sixty-degree alkaline crawl of the flagstones, until your heart is only a soft, wet tap against the breastbone, timed to the drip from the second arch.

When the walking-beam sighs southward, the dwarf eels come out.

They don't swim so much as leak from the spoke-holes: pale, three-inch ribbons of gristle, blind as lard, tasting of the iron-shavings they browse. They have no bellies to speak of, only a single gut filled with red-lead and rust-paste. 

That is when I slide. 

No kick. Kicking churns the tallow-scum, and if the scum breaks, the greasy starlings drop from the ribs to snatch at the shreds before they sink. A dog who makes a wave goes hungry. You let your ribs go slack; you let the leaden water take your belly; you let your webbed paws trail behind like wet rags, guiding yourself by the cold draft that comes off the copper cooling-sleeves. 

The mouth takes them sideways. One, two, three, swallowed against the roof of the mouth where the skin has grown hard as a horse’s gum. They go down cold, wriggling once against the gorge, their little gristle-spines dissolving in the slow, bitter bile that keeps us from freezing.

Then back to the housing before the water settles.

If the *thung* is coming, I do not go to the bedplates where the others sleep. 

The bedplates are warm, yes—four of us wedged into the hollow cast-iron plinths among the shredded hemp and the dry starling-skeletons, our breath pooling together into a small, greasy pocket that smells of dog-belly and damp wool. But the bedplate is too far from the well-mouth.

When the water swells by its coin-thickness, and then swells again—the long, oily heave that means the forty-foot thing in the deep coal-measures has turned its belly in the dark—I go to the sill of the cylinder well.

I hook my chin over the curved iron lip. 

The water that comes up from sixty feet down is different from the Nave’s water. It has no sky in it at all. It does not even have the lead-colored light of the road-slots; it has rested against the coal-faces since the day the pits drowned, and it is thick with the dust of ferns that died before the city had a name. It smells of stone that has never seen an eye.

The man is already there, sixty feet above me on the beam, lowering his hazel rod.

I cannot see him, but I can hear the smooth iron crook slide into the water: a faint *ssss-clink* as it clears the scum. The rod passes three feet from my muzzle. It carries his smell with it down into the dark: the graphite on his knuckles, the bitter saltpetre of his sleeves, and the faint, sweet musk of the starling-fat he rubbed into the crook to keep the water from swelling the wood.

I don't touch the crook. The crook belongs to the fish.

We wait together—the man perched on the balance, his calloused toes curled round the iron truss; myself flattened against the wet flange like a discarded boot; and the great, pale bulk turning in the black liquor below. 

When the crook touches the gray flank forty feet down, the vibration travels up the hazel wood, through the man’s knuckles, out through his leather sleeves, into the iron truss, down the stela, through the foundation bolts, and into the iron lip beneath my throat. 

A single, massive stroke. *Thump.* 

It is not an engine. It is only the Nave’s own bladder, squeezing against the dark to prove the walls have not yet touched each other. 

My tail gives one short, slow twitch against the rust-scum—not out of gladness, for gladness is too fast a thing for this cellar, but simply because the beat found my own heart and moved it along by half an inch.

The man clicks his tongue above: *tck-tck*.

A starling answers from the blind bulkhead: *tck*.

I press my chin harder into the iron, close the milky lid over the dark, and draw the cool grease into my nostrils, keeping the level until the light through the slots dies from ash to coal, and the shift is done.
