It begins not with a name or a face, but with the dull, continuous thrum in the shins. 

The vibration does not enter through the ears; the air here is too heavy, choked with cold spray and the dense, sodden pelt of liverworts to carry anything sharp. It enters through the soles—through two inches of dense, water-cured ash bound to the feet with greased rawhide—and climbs the tibia, settling into the pelvic basin like lead ballast. 

At first, you might mistake yourself for a piece of ironwork that has simply developed a pulse. Everything here hums with the same heavy, rhythmic load: the counterweight shafts, the bolted gusset plates, the wet flanges of the box girders where the condensation gathers into fat, quivering beads before dropping into the fog. If you stand motionless with your palms flat against the steel lagging, the difference between your blood and the hydraulic slurry circulating three tiers down is mostly a matter of temperature.

Then comes the smell: boiled linseed, cold magnetite, sheep tallow, and the ancient, sour rot of wet sphagnum decomposing without air. 

I would be wedged into the lee of Deck Six’s primary trunnion, seventy feet below the open shear of the clouds, where the overhang of an automated coal-gantry keeps off the direct plummet of the rain. 

Not young. Youth has an impatience that would find you slipping off a wet channel-iron within forty days. The limbs would be thick, shortened by perpetual crouching, the rib cage barrel-shaped and widened by fifty years of drawing high-mountain air that has had all its sharpness flattened out by coal smoke and steam. The skin of the wrists, where the stiff wool of the tunic ends, would be an unvarying, waterlogged gray-white, cross-hatched by old scars from the edges of saw-steel scrapers and stained with black machine grease that no cold flume can lift.

My business would not be the grand sweep of the valley. To think about five kilometers of lattice is to invite vertigo, and vertigo is an error of mass. 

My business would be the third weep-hole on the down-slope web of Stanchion 44-B.

It is an aperture no wider than three fingers, drilled through two inches of cold-rolled plate to drain the interior condensation of the thermal column. If it clears, the water weeps steadily down the guide-trough and vents into the lower siphon. If it clogs with the fibrous, hair-like rootlets of the maidenhair ferns that thrive on the stanchion’s warm seams, two hundred gallons of water back up inside the hollow iron within an hour. The balance tilts half a degree to the west. The ball-and-socket joint fifty paces above me begins to complain—not with sound, but with an uneven, dry judder that travels straight through the ash soles into the teeth.

So I hang. 

The harness is four-ply canvas belting, stiffened with beeswax and riveted with copper. It creaks against the iron as I lean out over the drop, my knees hooked into the lip of a service cradle. The mist below is uniform, the color of wet zinc; somewhere down in that gray floor, thousands of feet down, the cold char are drifting through the dark cisterns, their lateral lines feeling the shudder of my boots against the iron.

In my right hand is the probe: six feet of spring-tempered manganese wire, looped at the end. 

I do not look at the drop. You look only at the seam, at the line where the black iron meets the emerald fur of the moss. You look for the telltale swelling of the peat, the tiny, glistening blister that shows water is being denied its path.

I push the wire home into the hole. 

There is a resistance—spongy, stubborn, full of cold life. The roots have found the iron warm inside the chimney and have woven themselves into a dense, felted plug, catching coal-silt until it is as hard as peat-brick. I twist the loop, feeling the fibers wrap around the manganese steel. You do not yank; yanking snaps the wire or shears the root-head, leaving the mass behind to rot into an impermeable paste. You work it with a slow, wrist-borne leverage, matching the cadence of your pull to the great pneumatic stroke of the compressor down on Tier Four. 

*Hiss. Pull. Rest.*
*Hiss. Pull. Turn.*

When it yields, it does not burst. It lets go with a soft, sucking gasp. The wire comes back out coated in dark, slick sludge, and behind it comes the backlog: six gallons of lukewarm, sulfur-tainted water that gushes across the iron plate, washes over the toes of my ash boots, and falls away into the lower framing, where it turns to spray before it has dropped twenty cubits.

The shudder in the stanchion smooths out. The dry judder in the trunnion ceases. The steel beneath my palms settles back into its long, unhurried breath.

I wipe the wire with a patch of lanolin-soaked wool pulled from my pocket. From a leather pouch at my hip, I take a piece of the starchy creeper shoot, dried on the casing of the dynamo until it is as hard as salt-fish, and slip it between my cheek and my gums. It tastes of river silt, cold iron, and the bitter tan of oak-gall, but as the saliva softens it, it spreads a dull, persistent heat through the jaw.

I do not have a name that anyone speaks. If someone two tiers below needs to know if the weep-hole is clear, they will not shout into the dead, moss-muffled fog. 

They will lift their bronze drift-pin and strike the non-structural web of the diagonal strut three times: *tap... tap... tap.*

I wait until the vibration finishes climbing through the rivets. Then I reach down to the non-bearing flange beside my knee, take the short copper bar hung from my lanyard, and strike the steel once: 

*Tung.*

A single, clean wave that travels through the black iron, past the fern-beds, through the greasy grease-blocks of the gantries, down into the cold dark where the water knows what to do with itself. 

The report comes back through the bones of my hips: an answering ring, miles out, half-lost in the mass of the mountain. 

It is clear. The deck is true. The weight is held. 

I pull myself back into the hollow of the swivel, sit down on the thick, dry bed of shredded insulation, draw my wool hood over my eyes to shut out the thin, slate-gray light, and wait for the northern well to thump.
