At first, there is only the refusal of the floor to yield.

It is not an empty refusal; it is packed, dense, ringing with four hundred miles of stone joined prism-to-prism without an air-gap or a smear of mud between them. If you press against it, it does not push back—it simply conducts you. It accepts your mass as an addition to its own, slightly altering the fundamental pitch of the shelf you occupy, the way an iron weight laid upon a bronze cymbal deadens its high, brassy shimmer into a dull, thick clonk.

I would not begin with a face. A face is a soft, wet concept, intended for things that push vapor out into a breeze or drink water from shallow depressions. There is no vapor here to justify a nose, no moisture to feed an eye that would only desiccate into a dried berry in twenty heartbeats. 

I find myself low. Very low. So low that the razor-slate does not threaten to drop me, because there is nowhere further to fall. 

My center of gravity is not a point balanced on two stilts; it is a three-cornered wedge, pressed so flat to the basalt that the heat of the deep crust—that old, dry, oven-baking heat that has never known rain—soaks directly into the plastron beneath my belly. It is not uncomfortable; it is the only temperature there has ever been. To be cold here would be an impossible thought, an abstraction like *blue* or *wet*. The stone is warm, and so the mass above it is warm. The pith inside me—fibrous, silica-veined, dry as charred cedar—does not circulate anything. It simply rests in the baking equilibrium of the room.

Am I one who hunts? 

For a long time, listening through the fan of bone spread flat across the seam of a six-sided column, I don't think I am. 

The hunters have a specific agitation in their pads. When their horn-clefts strike the floor, they strike with the quick, sheared bite of a wedge being driven home. Their pads are notched for the hawser-ropes; their tendons are wound tight for the lever-throw. Through the rock, I can feel two of them four miles north, their strikes clean and syncopated, working an ironstone maul against the skirt of a chasm where the shield-bugs crawl thickest. Their rhythm is functional: *strike, split, lever, scrape.* It makes the floor spit small, white chips of sound that travel fast through the upper crust and die against the open step-faults.

No, I am not moving with them. My pads have not felt the greasy, resinous hair of a tendon-line in a very long time.

Perhaps I am the blind itself? 

There is an attraction to that. A pile of slate shingles stacked forty layers high, each layer chosen for its density so that the low, grinding chatter of the shield-bugs’ bellies is allowed to pass through while the shrill, flinty tick of the long-legged harvestmen is turned back, scattered into the empty air where it can bite no one. In this dark, the difference between a living body and a well-stacked wall is almost negligible. Both are made of dry plates; both are wedges driven into the seams; both eventually break down under the weight of the floor until their dust fills the cracks.

Yet I have an arm. Two pairs of them, short and thick-limbed, set at the corners of my chassis. They are locked, but not mortared. The joints have that stiff, crystalline reluctance of dry horn that has held one position for fifty generations of the herds, but if I bring my will to bear against the lever-points, they will creak. A dry, interior sound—like a wicker basket being crushed beneath a heavy boot.

I have a stone in my right pad.

It is not black glass, which shatters and cuts. It is a lump of dense, rounded hematite—an ironstone nodule, dull red in the blackness, heavy as lead, its skin rubbed greasy by the passage of four or five sets of hands before mine. It does not belong to this shelf; it came from the iron-faults that run forty leagues into the lower dark, where the basalt gives way to greasy red hematite seams.

I am not hunting. I am listening to the tone of a fissure.

Before me, cut into the slate floor of a narrow clearing between two towering crystalline prisms, is a ditch three fingers wide. It is not straight. It turns at a clean forty-five-degree angle, runs the length of two strides, and terminates in a series of tiny, pecked dimples that follow the natural fracture of the stone.

When the great herd moves across the plains five miles down-fault, their millions of scrapers produce a tone that is flat, wide, and terrible to an ear that needs stillness. It is a sound that fills the stone with silt—an acoustic dust that hides the needle-clicks of the stilt-crawlers until they are already upon the blind, their flint spurs testing the cracks between the plates.

My task is to kill that sound.

I raise the ironstone nodule. It takes an hour. There is no haste, because the heat does not change and my grinding stones do not require salt today; the resin-flour in my belly-cavity is packed dense and will last until this prism rings true.

I bring the nodule down.

*Tock.*

The impact does not bounce. The iron is heavier than the shale; it crushes a thimble-full of rock into white, talc-dry powder. A tiny puff of chalk rises an inch into the dead-still air, then settles back upon my knuckles. Through the tympanic fan of my skull, I feel the chime run along the hexagonal prism: it is slightly too sharp. The edge of the ditch is too vertical; it catches the herd’s scraping hum and reflects it directly into the alcove where the young ones are milling their stones.

I shift my pads. The horn leaves a white, bruised semi-circle on the basalt.

I place the iron nodule against the inner lip of the ditch and lean my entire mass onto it, using the leverage of my broad chest-plate to scrape away three grains of silica. Then another three.

*Scritch. Scritch.*

Through the rock, I can feel the world answering. The ditch deepens by a fraction of an inch, and suddenly that great, oceanic rustle from the south dips down—not gone, but bent, turned away into the chasm where the empty drop can swallow it whole. In its place, the silence inside my little pen of slate clears. It becomes cool, acoustic glass. In that cleared quiet, I can suddenly hear the single, hair-thin step of a harvestman two thousand yards away, its claw tipping the edge of a flake of volcanic glass with a high, bell-like ping.

The wall behind me settles. One of the old ones, wedged between two upright slates three feet above my head, gives up a tiny, desiccated splinter of horn that drops onto my back with a dry *flack*. It does not matter. The old one is nearly wall now; its tendons have finished drying into flint, and its skull-shield has become just another dampener in the acoustic blind, turning aside the noise of the deep earth so that I may work the ditch.

I do not look up. There is no up—only the black ceiling that has never spoken, because nothing walks upon it and no hammer has ever struck it. 

The floor is the only thing that has ever been true. It is a vast, ringing bell of black basalt, miles thick, and we are its notches. We are the ones who chip away its edges until it stops roaring and begins to hum the few, quiet intervals that let a creature sit motionless in the dark, century after century, without going mad from the sound of its own dry weight.

I set the ironstone back into the ditch. I lower my head until the fan of my cheek touches the fresh-cut groove.

I wait for the next note to show me where the stone is too loud.
