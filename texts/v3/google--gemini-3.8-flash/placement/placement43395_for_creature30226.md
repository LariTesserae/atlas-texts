It begins not with vision, but with the load. 

A load is not merely weight; it is an orientation. It is seventy tons of chilled slate and boiler-chitin pressing downward through three square inches of contact, searching for a flaw in the stone, and finding me instead.

I am not crushed, because I have nowhere further down to go. I am already flat. 

My back is dished like a spring washer, formed from forty laminated leaves of dark, silicate horn that slide over one another on microscopically grooved tracks. Between each leaf is a smear of dense, graphite-rich tallow—not the warm amber grease the behemoths pump through their walking beams, but something stiffer, cold-set and inert, that only yields under pressures that would turn timber to water. When the foot comes down—that vast, square pad of horn-cased basalt, four yards wide, dropping with the slow, pneumatic sigh of an exhaust stroke—I do not resist it. Resistance is for the things that break. 

I deform.

The curvature of my shell flattens against the talus with a dry, iron *snick*. My margin plates spread outward into the grit, biting into the slate dust with rows of short, chisel-tempered cleats. Under the center of my mass, my viscera—a dense, coiled cable of silver-sheathed galvanic cords and acid-filled bladders no thicker than a leather belt—are squeezed into the micro-crevices of the bedrock, safe in the mountain’s own pores. For twenty-five seconds, thirty seconds, thirty-eight seconds, there is no sky, no diamond-edged sun, no horizon of razor-basalt. There is only the dead, vibrating mass of the bull-engine above me, transmitting the deep, three-mile-per-hour churn of its gut-stones directly through my spine.

I can feel the state of his works through my rim. His third lateral bronze-ring is worn; it chatters on the compression stroke, a three-beat flutter that sends a high, stinging harmonic down through the leg-beam into my plates. His tallow is fouled with blue scree-flour. He has two seasons of migration left before his knee-gimbals gall and freeze him in the chute.

Then: the stroke clears.

The walking-beam groans, a heavy lever-crane lifting by hydraulic torque. The load does not vanish—it peels away. 

In that single, clean second of vacuum between the rising foot and the returning frost, my spring-temper asserts itself. The forty leaves of my shell snap back into their arch. The stored kinetic energy of his eighty-ton step does not dissipate; it kicks me. It launches me forward six inches along the greasy slide of the chute, skittering on three pairs of short, cupped skids of phosphor-bronze that curl beneath my rim. 

I do not walk. I am cocked by their descent, and fired by their relief.

The light hits me then—that raw, unlidded glare that strips the color from everything, turning the blue talus into fields of shattered mirror. It hurts, but it is also my compass. My eyes are not lenses; they are two narrow slits of smoky fluorite set flush with my leading edge, blind to shape, sensitive only to the harsh, ultraviolet shear that signals open clearance between the legs of the herd. 

I smell the corridor: the biting, throat-coating reek of boiled whale-oil and cold sulfur, the hot paraffin vapor venting from the bulls' flank-ports in sudden, white ribbons that instantly shatter into snow against the basalt. 

Where the foot just lifted, the basalt is stamped smooth, a dark, greasy depression two inches deep. At the rim of the print, where the edge of the bull’s sole-iron sheared against the mountain, there is a fringe of *galling*—bright, ragged curls of unoxidized native iron and ripped chitin, torn away by the raw friction of the stroke. The metal is still hot. It steams faintly in the forty-below gale, blue and yellow where the air is searing the fresh fracture.

That is what I have come for. 

The caulkers cannot touch it; their vacuum-cups would fry against the fresh heat, their spinning scrapers are meant for soft mold, not jagged swarf. The tallow-leeches have no mouths for it; they are tubes for broth. 

I slide over the lip of the print. My under-snout is a reciprocating file, two plates of diamond-grit horn driven by an internal, grease-damped balance wheel that ticks with a dry, clockwork speed—*t-t-t-t-t-t-t*. I draw the curls of torn steel into my throat. They do not drop into an acid vat; they enter a muscular, stone-lined gizzard that rolls them against each other until they are rounded into shot, scouring the heavy, paraffin-thick bile from my digestive sleeves. 

I feed on the wear. I am what lives on the mountain’s loss of tolerance. 

Where the bulls are tightest—where the saddle narrows to a fifty-yard gut of basalt and three hundred engines are wedged flank-to-flank, their boiler plates groaning against one another until the grease smokes—that is where my kind are thickest. We live in the clearances. We are the shims. 

Sometimes, when two bulls lock foreheads in the territorial press, their plates will shift out of true. A cervical gimbal will slip its track by a fraction of an inch; the horn-collar will gap, revealing the pale, pulsing hydraulic neck-sleeve beneath, swollen with seventy pounds of mineralized lymph. If that sleeve touches the sharp edge of the misaligned plate, it will shear, and the bull will bleed out his pressure in five minutes of screaming white froth.

In that gap, I wedge. 

Not out of service. Not out of loyalty to the engine. The clearance is simply the only shape my body understands. 

I am driven into the notch by the pressure of the herd behind me, forced between the sliding plates like a chisel. The horn-leaves of my shell take the lateral thrust; they groan, sliding across one another until my tallow smokes, but they hold the gap open. The sharp edge of the dorsal plate bites into my flank, cutting through my fifth lamina, my sixth, but the spring-temper of the seventh turns the edge aside. The bull’s neck-sleeve pulses against my belly, hot and greasy, its galvanic nerves twitching against my bronze skids like dull, wet wires. 

I stay there for days while the bulls shove. 

The cold wind shrieks through the mountain gap, whistling through the empty shells of old dead who have been pressed into the pavement below us. High above, the aerostats flash violet against the dark, noon stars, their mile-wide bellows thumping once every thousand heartbeats, drifting toward the plumes of the volcanic ridge. Down here, in the dark between sixty tons of plate and sixty tons of plate, there is only the scream of iron against iron, the stench of scorch, and the steady, hydraulic heave that never ends.

I lose two leaves of my shell to the torque. They snap with the sharp, clean crack of pistol shots, their fragments rattling down through the gimbal into the shale. It does not matter. Beneath them, the eighth leaf is already growing, its grain laid down in alternating crosses of silica fiber and horn-glue, curing under the very heat that tried to shear it.

When the contest breaks—when the weaker bull's walking-beam buckles and he is ground down into the chute—the gap widens with a wet, sucking gasp. 

The pressure lets go. 

My spring-body snaps out of the joint, sailing twelve feet through the freezing, diamond-raw air before tumbling into the scree. I hit the talus, roll three times on my bronze skids, and settle into the shale dust just as the next engine’s rear axle-leg comes down—slow, blind, fifty tons of iron-sheathed muscle seeking the earth.

I feel the shadow cut the light. 

I draw my skids into my belly. I set my cleats against the stone. 

I wait for the load.
