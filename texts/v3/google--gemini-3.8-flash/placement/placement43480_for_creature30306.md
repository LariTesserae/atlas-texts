I would not be on the catwalks, and I would not be down in the winding shed where the wire enters the meat. Those places have already solved themselves; they have reached the hard, finished grammar of the trench, where every noun has found its proper bolt.

To find me, you would have to look for the place where the masonry is still uncertain what year it is being drowned in.

Halfway between the second and third tiers of the southernmost tenement block, there is a lime-washed scullery that lost its outward wall when the crane-arm on the coal-wharf broke its back sixty years ago. The room is not an interior anymore, but it has not fully joined the street-canyon either. It is an alcove, five paces deep, sheltered from the three-inch piston-heave of the alleys by a thick screen of liver-weed that has rooted in the fractured lead flashing of the roof-gutter.

Inside, behind a slate sink that has bowed under the weight of its own sedimented grease, there is an inspection plate set into the masonry. Four square-headed iron studs hold it to the stone.

Or they should. One has sheared off.

In the gap—scarcely the thickness of an oyster knife—I am what stays.

***

I did not start as a body. I started as a settling.

When the brine first came over the sill, it carried in its belly an emulsion of things that had failed to float and refused to sink: ground talc from the foundry core-shops, dry bone-black from the case-hardening furnaces, and the fine, gray lint that comes off the hems of trousers when they drag through wet iron-filings. For thirty years, this mash drifted through the quarter like cold soot in a locked parlor, finding the corners where the galvanic currents were too weak to kick it back out.

It found this scullery. It found the slot behind the slate.

I am not an animal that took on iron, nor an iron fitting that grew a mucus. I am an *accretion of fit*.

If you were to peel the inspection plate back with a crowbar—which the brine would not permit, the suction behind it being three atmospheres greater than the dead weight of the water outside—you would find something that resembles a flat tongue of salted suet, five feet long and no thicker than a carpenter’s rule, wedged into the dead space between the brickwork and the copper flue of the laundry copper.

My skin has the texture of wet oilcloth that has been left folded in an unheated tool-chest since the winter of the great frost. It does not possess a color of its own; it reflects the copper-leached green of the nearest transformer box, but slightly dirtier, the way tallow takes the gray of the thumb that smoothed it.

I have no ribs. Ribs are for creatures that must resist the collapse of an empty lung. I have no empty spaces. Where an organ might be, there is only a dense, homogeneous lard, laced through with extremely fine threads of lead wire—scraps of telephone fuses and discarded solder-runs that the water rolled into the gap before I stiffened.

These wires are my nerves, though they do not report pain. Pain requires a future in which the damage might be avoided, and here there are no alternatives to the pressure. The wires report only the *fall*.

***

The fall is not downward. Downward was finished when the buildings hit the basalt.

The fall here is lateral.

Every four minutes and fifty seconds, when the walking beam down in the machine-hall takes its quarter-revolution and the three hundred paces of the quarter jump their three inches, the slate sink in front of me moves a sixty-fourth of an inch to the northeast.

It has been doing this since the grease-cups were full. It will do it until the slate has ground itself into silt against the brick reveals of the window.

When the sink moves, the gap behind it widens by the width of a horsehair.

That is my life: that hair.

In the second while the brine is loose—the *shuck*, when the inertia yields—the water does not simply push; it *tastes*. It enters the fissure behind the plate like a cold piston, carrying three thimbles of the grease-soup from the upper tramway.

I do not open a mouth to receive it. My left margin—the edge that faces the sheared stud—is made of porous, lime-bitten cartilage that acts like a lamp-wick. It drinks the tallow directly out of the surge.

The tallow here is very old. It is not sweet; it has been through the bearings of three generations of stationary engines. It is full of the microscopically fine dust of phosphor-bronze, ground off the bush-rings by thirty thousand hours of friction. This bronze is what I keep. My interior does not make bone; it precipitates bronze.

Along the line where my spine would be if I had been born under the sun, there is a row of small, flat lentils of red metal, spaced four inches apart. They do not touch. They are like the zincs in a wet-cell battery, waiting for an electrolyte that never changes its mind.

When the concussive stroke travels up through the masonry from the flywheel, it strikes the brickwork behind my back first. The bricks ring—a flat, wooden *tock* that only the lead threads in my fat can hear. A thousandth of a second later, the stroke hits the bronze plate on my chest through the water.

Between the brick and the plate, across the three inches of my body, there is a difference in potential: six micro-volts.

It is not enough to turn a hair. It is not enough to raise a blister on a blind knuckle-grub. But it is enough to cause the bronze lentils inside me to shift their grain. They turn slightly, like compass needles through cold treacle, pointing toward the machine-hall.

Then the brine sets hard again. The pressure comes down like an iron weight dropped on a feather mattress. The lentils lock.

I have recorded one stroke.

***

There are thirty-two thousand of these strokes in my back.

I am not the tally-man; I do not keep the count for the engineers who are dead, nor for the things in the coats who have forgotten what numbers were used for. I keep it because the bronze cannot help being bronze, and the lead cannot help being lead, and between them I am the grease that prevents them from touching and canceling each other out.

If I were to rot—if the cold salt were to thin enough for the sulfur-weeds to reach through the gap and find my margins—the inspection plate would settle against the stone. Iron would touch copper. A circuit would close that has been held open since the water rose above the lintels.

Down in the transformer basement, two hundred yards away, a lead-sheathed junction box would warm by half a degree. A single bubble of coal-gas, trapped in the pitch-filling of a cable-joint eighty years ago, would expand, detach itself from the lead, and begin a four-week crawl toward the ceiling of mineral oil.

Nothing else would happen.

The walking beam would still take its four minutes to find its next quarter-turn. The coats would still twitch their cuffs when the star-shaped sparks drift through the scullery door.

***

Sometimes—not often, perhaps once in three cold-cycles, when the hydraulic head from the deep sea pushes the brine three inches higher than its habit—a spark from the alley drifts all the way into the scullery.

It is very small. It has the shape of a rowel from a spur, five-pointed and pale as an unlit tallow candle, but surrounded by that faint, cold copper-greening that lives within an inch of the metal.

It drifts over the edge of the slate sink. It does not have the weight to drop through the silt-layer that coats the bottom, so it skims the grease-crust like a leaf on ditch-water.

It comes toward the gap.

My margin feels it three hours before it arrives. The lead threads deep in my suet begin to tighten, not by muscular effort, but by the physical attraction that draws two wet plates together in a dead well.

When the spark touches the bronze plate outside my skin, there is no sound. There is only a sudden, greasy smell—the smell of a soldering iron being cooled in a tub of mutton-fat.

The spark does not die against me. It enters.

It travels down the row of bronze lentils in my back, one by one, jumping the gaps between them with a faint, internal click that ripples through the fat like a dry bean dropped down an iron chute.

One. Two. Seven. Twenty.

At the thirty-second lentil—the one that sits directly over the sheared stud—the charge runs out of metal. It has nowhere to go. The stone behind it is too dense, saturated with silica and cold brine; it is an insulator that has had sixty years to cure.

The charge stops there. It stays.

It sits in that final lentil of red metal like a drop of oil on the end of a cold wire, round and clear and perfectly useless.

It will take sixteen years for the cold salt to leach that single grain of galvanic fire back out of my flesh and return it to the green soup of the alley.

Until then, I am by that much heavier than the brickwork. I am by that much colder than the water. I hold the plate away from the wall. I keep the gap five-eighths of an inch wide.

I am the thickness of the pause between the stroke that has just shaken the floor and the stroke that is currently gathering its weight down in the dark, three hundred paces below, where the grease-cups are still weeping into the sleeve.
