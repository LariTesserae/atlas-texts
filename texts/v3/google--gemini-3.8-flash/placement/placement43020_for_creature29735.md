To be here would not begin with a body. It would begin with the cancellation of distance.

There is no horizon to draw an eye, because there are no eyes, and there is no perspective to organize a world into *near* and *far*. There is only the weight from above and the resistance from below. 

The light does not illuminate things so that they may be contemplated; it presses. It is a column of solid, unyielding white density, miles thick, descending from the selenite plate to settle upon whatever has the density to catch it. To exist beneath it is to feel a plumb line threaded directly through the center of the cranium, down between the shoulder-blades, through the pelvis, and into whatever surface supports you. If you were to lean even three degrees out of true, the radiance would catch the edge of the spar-plates in your skull like an iron lever, a shearing ache that forces you back into vertical alignment. You do not look around. You balance.

And because the temperature of the air is the temperature of the sap, and the temperature of the sap is the temperature of the lymph, there is no skin-sense of cold or warmth to tell you where you end. In a world where the floor is at fever-heat and the air is saturated to the point of liquid suspension, touch is not a gradient of heat; it is only a gradient of yield.

Some things yield with a heavy, wet elasticity: the calcified ribs of the wood, the broad tongue-leaves that crowd the shoulders, the warm marrow-pulp that hums beneath the moss.

Some things do not yield at all: the basalt.

That is where I find myself. Not deep in the chartreuse twilight where the thread-dips hang like spit between the wood and the glass-sharp slate; and not subsumed into the mattress, one of those pale arches whose ribcages now ferry the high-pressure sap toward the ceiling. 

I am at the boundary, where the yielding meets the unyielding.

I am seated on the outer edge of a low, dry-stone ring. The basalt beneath my haunches has no give; its crystalline edges are clean, fresh as broken flint, biting into the thick, waterlogged hide of my calves without cutting through. The stone is cold only by comparison to the fever of the wood—it does not generate its own warmth, so it drinks a fraction of mine, just enough to leave a ring of faint, dead numbness where my flesh meets the block. 

It is the only part of my body that is truly quiet. Everywhere else, the pressure is climbing.

I do not breathe. The air does not move in and out of me; there is no wind to draw it, no bellows in the chest. Instead, my sides are open. Along the flanks, between the heavy, slab-like ribs, the soft clefts are parting and closing on their own slow hydraulic cycle. When the broad frond to my left leans near, swollen with guttation, its leaf-teeth touch these clefts. The surface tension breaks with a sound like a seed pod snapping under water—*clup*—and the clear, mineral-dense water is drawn directly into the lymph, without the violent hurry of a swallow.

My blood does not rush. It has the viscosity of warmed tallow, rich in dissolved lime and vegetable gum. To move an arm requires a conscious redirection of hydrostatic pressure, a slow, hydraulic shunting from the core into the limb, like water being forced through the narrow neck of an earthen jar.

I have been lifting my left arm for what might be an hour.

It is not an act of labor, and it is not an act of fear. A new runner—a pale, succulent shoot, no thicker than a child’s wrist and covered in fine, calcified felt—has crept over the basalt rim. It did not jump; it has flowed, millimeter by millimeter, seeking the calcium in the stones, seeking the lime in my shins. Its tip is already turning upward, tasting the leaden verticality of the light.

If I leave it, by tomorrow the felt will have brushed the skin of my ankle. By the day after, the pore-valves along its bark will have mistaken the pores along my calf for its own tissue. The sap will bridge. The white lymph will curdle and knit. My domed skull will become an anchor for three more square yards of leather leaves, and the plumb line of the midday sun will press into me until I am simply another buttress supporting the floor of the sky.

There would be no pain in that. It is the natural drift of the trough, which hates a gap.

Yet I am lifting the arm.

My hand is heavy, square, the skin on the four flat pads as thick as cured hide. I do not swing it; I lean into it, wedging the thumb and the broad forefinger around the green neck of the shoot, right where it crosses the basalt's razor-lip.

I do not twist. I swell. 

I divert the interior head of pressure into the forearm until the calloused pads engorge, pinching the succulent wood against the stone edge until the fibers give way.

The snap comes: *thock*.

The sound is deadened instantly by the felted fronds around us, swallowed before it can travel ten paces. From the severed stem, a single dome of latex wells up—clear, thick, and quivering with surface tension, held together by the absolute stillness of the air.

It hangs there, an inch from my knee, catching the pure, perpendicular selenite glare until it shines like a dropped eye.

I do not pull my hand back. I lower my thumb into the center of the sphere.

The skin does not pierce the drop; the drop accepts the skin. The latex flattens, bridges across the calloused horn, and sinks into the spiracles of my hand, drawn upward by the steady, interior thirst of my bones. It tastes of crushed flint and bitter lime, hot as the blood it becomes.

When the stone is dry again, my arm settles back against my thigh. The basalt receives the weight.

I sit. Across the circle, another dome of pale elder-wood skin is curved beneath the light, its featureless face turned down toward the dark flags. It has not moved since the last shoot was broken. It may not move until the next one arrives.

Above us, the quartz lens holds the white sky rigid, unchanging, without the mercy of an afternoon. Below us, the thousands of tons of wet pulp tick and hum in their blind, vegetative midday.

I am not the tree, because I have the basalt. I am not the stone, because I have the thirst. I am the place where the stone is kept clean, sitting in the heavy, unblinking light, holding the noon at bay with the slow, wet breaking of a stem.
