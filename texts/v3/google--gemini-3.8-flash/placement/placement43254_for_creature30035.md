First, there is only the tension in the calves. 

The floor is never entirely level beneath the arches of the feet; it has the long, swelling crowns of cedar or yew, but seasoned by moisture until the grain has the greasy density of horn. If I stand anywhere near the western wall, my weight wants to slide inward toward the Mulch-Pits, where the air is five degrees warmer and smells of sour lard and ground iron. 

So the feet learn to hold. The yellow rind under the heels does not slip. It is thick enough that if a lead-wire staple were driven an eighth of an inch into the callus, it would find no blood.

I am wedged between the lower flange of the south cast-iron frame and the swelling buttress that has grown half-over it. It is the narrowest point of the deck. On the upstroke, there is space enough to turn my hips, though the iron of the A-frame sweats a thin, oily dew against the back of my tunic. On the downstroke, when the iron walking-beam comes down with its deliberate, crushing drop, the air between the frame and the wood is driven out with a sharp, flat *whuff* that blows the damp hair away from my temples.

To live here is not to be silent; it is to have the ears turned inward, entirely dead to the air, while the teeth serve as dials.

I keep a scrap of dried swift-breast wedged between the back molars on the left side. Without it, the counterweight’s landing would click the enamel together until the crowns shattered in their gums before two seasons were out. With the meat there, the strike—that five-second hammer—arrives as a blunt, round ache that travels from the wood into the heel-bone, up the tibia, through the locked pelvis, and terminates in the base of the skull as a dull, red flash behind the eyes. 

One. Two. Three. 

Between the strike and the rise, there is the work. 

I have three paces of clear wood along the bearing seat. My tool is an iron drift, beaten flat on an ancient anvil-stone that now sits three feet deep inside the root-wall, drowned in wood. It is six inches long, chisel-ended, wrapped at the grip with four layers of cured swift-gut that have turned stiff and grey like horn. 

On the third stride of the rising beam, I lay the chisel against the bronze pillow block. 

I do not look at it; the flat daylight from sixty feet above is too weak down in the shadow of the frame to show whether the bronze is scoring. Instead, I lay the flat of my lower jaw directly against the butt of the iron drift. 

Through the jaw, the machine explains itself. 

The grease has its own language in the bone. When the tallow is cool and thick, the vibration through the iron is long and slow—a greasy, heavy drag that feels like dragging a finger through wet flour. When grit gets in—the carbon dust thrown off the flywheel trough, or the shards of crushed carabus shell carried up by the wind of the crank—the jaw feels it as a hard, needle-like chatter, a dry *shiver* that sets the back teeth aching. 

If it shivers, I must scrape. 

I drop the weight back onto the heels, brace the small of the back against the cold, damp swell of the heartwood wall, and let the downstroke pass. 

*Tonk.*

The shudder hits the marrow. A mist of cold grease-sweat flecks my forearms. A beetle, dislodged from the flange above, drops into my lap; it is as broad as the spread of my four fingers, flat, wet-black, and smells like boiled hide. I do not brush it off with my hand; the edge of its shell is sharp enough to cut wet skin. I simply shift the angle of my thigh on the next rise, and the tilt of the leather-shingled garment dumps it cleanly into the runnel below.

Across the sixty-foot floor, past the long, glistening plane where the condensate sits in grey sheets, I can see the outline of the Elder who tends the intake screens. 

From here, through the fine drift of vapor from the high-pressure gland, he looks like a dark, five-sided stone bedded into the root-furrow. He does not move for ten strokes. He is watching the grate where the river water plunges out of the black lead pipes into the sump. Sometimes a log comes down from the mountain behind the wall, or a mass of mountain-grass that would choke the bronze vanes of the turbines behind the lead. When it happens, he will not shout. He will drop from his knees onto the flats of his palms.

The wood carries it better than iron. A hand-strike on the grain feels different than the beam’s *tonk*; it is higher, thinner, like the snap of green hemlock in winter. If his palms hit the floor twice, I will take the short scraper and go to him, timing the crossing: three paces while the nose of the beam tilts up toward the sky, freeze and sink into the knees as the iron counterweight falls, three paces more while the oil-slick belt whips back across its pulleys. 

We do not touch each other. The air is too wet for skin; skin against skin here is cold, like two lumps of fat left in a trough. We communicate through the clearance of our bodies. If I need his chisel, I set mine on the grain pointing north; if he has found a swift whose wing-bones are unbroken, he leaves it in the dry hollow beneath the south bearing-cap where the heat of the journal keeps the meat from spoiling for three strokes of the moon.

There is a woman who lives four furrows toward the east, where the root-plank rises into a kind of shelf before it reaches the vertical buttress. She is smaller than I am, but her shoulders are thicker, set forward like the shoulders of an ox. Her task is the bladders. All through the light-shift, while the sky above remains that single, pale, unmoving grey, she sits with her legs splayed wide, working the cold grease with her knuckles. 

She has a trough of soft lignum-shavings that she uses to dry her fingers. She takes the hard tallow that we chip from the journal faces, drops it into a vessel made of a dried horse-bladder—an ancient thing, mended forty times with sinew and lead-patch—and kneads it against the root-grain until the warmth of her thighs and her palms breaks the grain of the fat. She works powdered tannin into it, pinched from the dry underside of an ancient dead-knot three feet up the wall, until the tallow turns the color of river-silt. 

When the mixture is right, she does not carry it. She sets the bladder in a runnel that has a two-inch drop toward the west, gives it a single thrust with the sole of her broad, scarred foot, and lets it slide along the wet timber toward my frame. 

It arrives between the eighth and ninth stroke. I reach down, snatch it by its lead-wire neck during the beam’s rise, and hook it to the bronze stay beside my hip. 

Sometimes, when the pale daylight begins to narrow at the top of the pit—when the swifts begin to draw together into that dense, shrieking knot that looks from down here like an auger boring into the overcast—I stop the scraping for five strokes. 

I place both bare palms flat against the living wall, four feet above the deck, where the wood has not been planed by our knees. 

Here, the heartwood is alive. It does not feel like the dead planks of a ship. It is cool, but beneath the coolness there is a slow, vast, hydraulic surge that has nothing to do with the iron engine. It is the tree drinking. Millions of gallons of black water, drawn from the mountain roots five miles back in the dark, rising slowly through the bands of five-foot fiber to feed the invisible canopy that spreads somewhere above the clouds. 

Between the machine’s iron *tonk* and the wood’s cold, climbing blood, there is no difference in purpose. The beam would run without us; the roots would swell without the iron. But where they have met, they have ground out a space that is clean, hard, and grease-slicked. 

I take the chisel. I set the jaw to the iron drift again. 

The clearance is three-sixteenths of an inch. A swift has fallen into the Mulch-Pit; the flywheel rim has caught it, smeared its grey feathers with black coal-tar, and dragged it down into the trough where the worms graze. In six strokes, the rim will bring back only the quills. 

I sink my knees into the furrow. The counterweight lands. 

The marrow takes the blow, keeps it, and waits for the next.
