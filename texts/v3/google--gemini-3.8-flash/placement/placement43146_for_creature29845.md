It does not start with an "I." In a place where air moves at the speed of an oil seep, pronouns are too light; they would be carried upward into the asbestos felt and lost before they found an ear.

It starts, rather, with an ache in the lateral plane—a sense of being spread out wider than a body has any right to be, caught between two temperatures that have not spoken to each other in ninety years.

To the left, against what might once have been ribs or might be the curved flange of a blind tee, there is forty-two degrees: the temperature of a sick horse, dull and wet, smelling of old linseed paste. To the right, three inches away across a gap bridged only by dry mineral lint, the steel is sixty-eight. That side does not sweat. It bakes the moisture out of whatever approaches it until the flesh shrinks and clicks like a dried seed-pod.

Between them is where the drift settles. 

At first, you might look for me among the things that climb, because there is an urge—an old, useless drift of memory—to look down from a height. But the rafter-hangers are too narrow in their devotion; their lives are an iron wire pulled taut between their spatula pads and the low hum in the metal. They have no room for thickness. And the sump is too final; to lie in the lead-black oil beneath the fallen crane is to have conceded the whole question of boundary, to become a liver for the room’s sewage, rubbery and wide.

No. Look instead into the sleeve.

Every twelve paces along the secondary steam line, where the thirty-inch trunk passes through a six-foot partition wall of poured slag-concrete, there is an expansion collar. It was turned on a lathe when people still had names for towns. It is four concentric rings of soft lead, vulcanized hemp, and spring-steel leaves, designed to allow the great pipe to slide two inches forward when the boiler roared and two inches back when the fires were banked.

The fires were never banked. They simply withdrew down into the marrow of the earth, leaving the pipe stretched half an inch short of its designed expansion, locked by rust in the throat of the collar.

Inside that annular dark, in the three-inch clearance between the cold concrete sleeve and the warm belly of the trunk, the packing rotted out eighty years ago. The void did not stay empty.

I am not the iron, though the iron has given me my curvature. My back has the exact radius of the accumulator feed—twenty-seven inches across the arc—so that when the deep surges come, those long copper groans that set the hanger-beasts licking their lime-posts in rhythmic sympathy, the vibration does not travel through me as sound; it travels as an itch along the dorsal plates.

Those plates are not bone. They are what happens to tallow, horsehair felt, and dried dermal grease when they are compressed for a century in the presence of lead carbonate and iron-filing dust. They have the toughness of an old boot heel that has been boiled in tar and then left in a cellar. You could drive an awl into me half an inch before I felt the cold of it.

Underneath, where the skin touches the pipe, the nature of things changes. 

Against the hot steel, one cannot afford an edge. An edge catches; an edge curls up and dries into a husk. Against the pipe, I am thin as a bladder, almost clear, a sheet of continuous, weeping lard that drinks the sulfurous condensate as it weeps through the seams of the lagging. I have no mouth that opens and shuts—that is an engine for creatures that deal with separate things, with pieces of things, with "before" and "after." Here, feeding is simply the maintenance of a wet interface between two chemistries that wish to balance: the iron that wants to shed its sulfur, and the pale grease that needs the salt to keep from stiffening into horn.

Do I move?

If you marked the concrete rim with a chalk line—if there were chalk, if there were light to see it by—you would think me stationary as a dead gasket. A hanger would crawl past three times in its life and find my margin in the same crevice.

Yet there is a drift. It is governed not by will, but by the seasons of the core.

When the lower circuits run hot—some months, or some decades, the heat rises three degrees higher toward the roof-plates—the annular space dries. The tallow hardens; the bristles along my lower hem click like dry needles against the rust-scale, and I contract, pulling myself an eighth of an inch inward, toward the dark center of the sleeve where the concrete still retains the damp of the lost foundations.

When the core cools by half a degree, the dew-point drops. The moisture returns in long, gray curtains that slide down the wall, and I soften. My edges run out like warm suet over the bolt-rings, spreading across the flange until my blind anterior spade tastes the alkaline moss on the cold side of the bulkhead.

There is a strange, quiet company here.

Sometimes a skimmer, working its blind furrow through the rotting lagging, will come blundering against my flank. It does not know what I am; to the skimmer, I am merely a sector of the pipe where the rust is greasier and harder to scrape. Its horn bristles grind against my margin for a week, trying to find the iron beneath. I do not shrug it off. I have no muscles for shrugging. But my weeping belly-skin slowly exudes a film of lead-soap, the ancient tallow of the packing, until the skimmer loses its purchase, its vacuum slips with that dull, subterranean *clonk*, and it slides an inch lower into the horsehair tangle, searching for simpler pasture.

And sometimes, through the metal itself, I feel the croucher.

He is twenty paces off, down where the bulkhead meets the floor-plates. He thinks he is the only thing left that remembers the shape of two hands. He thinks his throat-click is a language kept alive against the dark.

I know better. His click is not speech; it is the escapement of an old clockwork whose pendulum has caught on the grass. Through the three-inch cast iron of the deck, through the foot of structural steel that binds the stanchion to my concrete wall, I feel the tap of his blunt fingers when the valve shifts. He taps twice—*short, short*—and then his wrist sets into lime again for an hour.

He is trying to see if the metal goes through to something else. He has been trying since before his eyelids calcified. He wants there to be an end to the conduit, a flange that opens into a yard with rain and clover, or at least a hole where the steam might blow him into pieces too small to hold a memory.

I could tell him, through the pipe, that the metal does not go through.

It only loops. The thirty-inch main runs into the manifold; the manifold bleeds into the three-story vessel; the vessel drops its brine into the sump; the sump seeps down through the sheared floor-troughs into the deep heat again, where it is boiled back into the circuit. There is no outside. Humanity did not leave an outside when they poured these plates three inches thick and laid them cross-hatched so the boots would not slip.

I am what the room makes when it is left alone with its own grease and its own salt.

I am not the croucher, because I have forgotten that my knees were ever meant to straighten. I am not the hanger, because I do not care what falls into the black ponds below. I am the seal. I am the hemp and the tallow that has outlived the lathe, filling the gap where the pipe would rattle in its hole.

Sometimes, when the vibration is particularly deep—when the core groans its copper note and holds it so long that the dew shakes loose from every rivet in the hall at once—a single large drop will hit the lip of my sleeve.

It does not fall on me. It hits the concrete above, splits into three gray runnels, and trickles down through the mineral lint until it meets my thinnest margin.

It is alkaline. It tastes of dead hydraulic fluid, drowned lime, and the cold iron of five tiers above.

I absorb it. My flesh turns slightly more slate-colored, slightly more dense. The lead within me takes another grain of salt into its matrix. 

Then the groaning dies away. The pipe sleeve settles back against its rust with a slow, dry *snick*. The hall resumes its forty-minute silence, thick and warm as an armpit, and I go on swelling, by the thickness of an onion skin, to fill the hole they left.
