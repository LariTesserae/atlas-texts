I am not on the platforms. 

If you put me on the larch with the widened ribs and the mutton-tallow in the ears, I would be a visitor trying to imagine what deafness feels like through the soles of the shoes. That would be an imitation of endurance. The people who grease the hemp have earned their broad, sodden heels through generations of children whose ears calcified before they knew the word for *quiet*; an intruder cannot simply step into that thickness of skin.

And I am not the bird. The bird is an error the mountain corrects within three wing-beats—a piece of soft geometry from the outside world that learns, in the space between the rim and the first gallery, that air can be hammered solid by its own echoes until the lungs collapse before the stone arrives.

I find myself lower than the living, but not yet down where the blue-black gradient turns into the cellar of the world where the corpses go. 

There is a place—call it the *seam-line*, though it has no name because no one comes there to sign it against a thigh—about forty fathoms below the lowest winch-pocket, where the rock ceases to be vertical and bulges slightly outward before it sheers away again toward the throat. It is an overhang of quartz-heavy schist, six arm-lengths deep, that catches the drift rather than the direct weeping of the walls. The water does not run down it in broad sheets; it flies off the lip above in a fine, atomized smoke that rolls into the belly of the curve and stays there, turning over and over like breath in an iron stove.

I am what has accumulated under that lip.

Not the salamander, though the crawlers pass through me. To them I am neither stone nor larch; I am the part of the rock that has grown fur. 

I think I am a knot of the hemp that didn’t fall all the way. 

Fifty years ago, or eighty—time here doesn’t notch itself into wood because the wood stays pale and clean until it sheers—a cargo cradle broke its traveler-block during the autumn winds. Three hundred fathoms of three-inch grass cordage, soaked with tallow and red axle-butter, came whistling down the gorge like an uncoiling whip. Most of it followed the goat-mutton and the black rye into the wet dark at the center, but the trailing hundred paces lashed around this quartz brow, bit into a tooth of greenstone, and stayed.

Over decades, the mist has done something monstrous and patient to that knot. 

The lard has not washed out; lard does not yield to cold water, it only stiffens until it has the grey, waxy texture of cheese rind. The hair-lichen has found it, rooting its black holdfasts into the twisted plies of the grass. The liverworts have crested over the crown of the loop, thick as horse-hide, feeding on the animal fat that slowly weeps from the core every time the high noon glare strikes the quartz mirrors and sends a white needle of heat down into the shade. 

The birds that strike the wall above—the small grey ones that miss the nets of the tallow-boys—tumble down through the draft. Some of them do not reach the void; they hit this shelf. Their feathers do not rot. Nothing rots. They are felted into the hemp by the constant hydraulic hammer of the spray. In the mass of me, between the second and third turns of the hawser, there are three dozen thrush skulls, clean and translucent as paring of horn, wedged into the plies alongside the oak wedges that broke off with the line when the headframe tore out above.

I have no eyes, but I am entirely made of the bell.

The people above think they live in the sound; they do not. They live against it. They fight it with tallow in the ear-holes and bone-to-bone brows pressed together to pass a grunt across a forehead. They are like lice on the rim of a brass kettle, holding on by their six claws while the monk strikes the rim with an ash pole.

Down here, under the belly, the sound has ceased to be an impact. It is a density. The air is so thick with the percussion of three thousand feet of vertical water that it has the viscosity of tallow. You do not hear it; you are suspended in its grain. A stone the size of a pig falling from the eastern rim does not whistle as it passes me; it makes a hollow *thump* in the pressure, like a fist hitting a wool bale through three doors, and then the air knits itself back together, grey and greasy and cold.

What am I doing?

I am collecting the hair.

The people shear their dead before they tip them out. They do it cleanly, with flakes of split quartz that take the scalp down to the white skin. But when they winch the bundles of dried mutton up from the lowlands, the plains-men—the soft, thick-eared folk who live down where the water has time to turn into rivers—use their own coarse, brown tow to lash the willow hampers. And every time a hamper clears the second staging, the hemp rubs the raw edge of the greenstone lintels. A few threads part every lift. They float down.

They do not go into the throat. The draft in the chimney is an elliptical vortex; it rises along the south face and drops along the north, and here, in the dead-water under the bulge, everything that is light and hair-like circles three times and settles.

I have five human needles in me, dropped over sixty winters through the floorboards of the grease-sheds. They are made of wing-bones of the large buzzards that sometimes glide too low over the cleft; they are yellow and greasy, their eyes threaded with sinew that has long since swollen until it cannot be drawn back through the hole. 

I am three yards across now. I am heavier than an ox, but I have no skeleton except the original hemp line, which is petrifying from the inside out as the lime and iron in the spray replace the grass fibers one cell at a time. The outer skin of me is green liverwort, cold as a wet frog; the inner meat of me is fat, feathers, lard-soaked hemp, and the small, lime-caked ribs of finches.

The crawlers like me because I do not vibrate at the frequency of the rock. The rock has that thin, high, shrieking ring—the iron-string that never stops. The larch platforms have a dry, chattering rattle that makes their little red gills curl shut with nausea. But I am soft. The grease muffles the granite’s scream into a low, lung-level thrum that feels to their belly-plates like the inside of a living belly. There are forty or fifty of them in my folds at any hour, their blunt, pinkish snouts buried in the tallow-rind, their red external gills waving in the spray-smoke like weeds in an eddy.

Sometimes I feel a foot.

Not a living foot. No one comes down here. A living man would have to be lowered on four hundred paces of braided rawhide, and the rawhide would fray against the lower lip in twenty turns of the spoke. 

It is the other kind.

When they tip an old woman off the fourth tier, she comes down through the blinding glare of the noon reflections—white skin, wide ribs, the grey gut-smock stripped away, the broad, flat soles of her feet turned up toward the sky she never looked at while she lived.

Mostly they miss. The draft carries them out toward the blue-black center, forty paces wide of me, and they look no bigger than a peeled larch wedge before the mist drinks them.

But once every five or six years, the wind shears hard from the north crags just as the drop happens. 

The body comes down along the rock face, scraping the quartz mirrors, turning once, twice, like an ash key falling through an alley. And its heel will strike me.

Just the heel. A heavy, blunt, water-logged thud against my upper swell of liverwort and frozen lard. 

It stays for less than a pulse-beat. The mountain doesn't let anything rest on a slant. But in that instant, before the gale catches the shoulders and levers the dead weight out into the empty chimney, the thick, white, pork-rind callus of the human sole presses into my moss. 

It leaves an oval dent. 

The moss doesn't spring back quickly; the water is too cold for elasticity. The footprint stays there for three or four days, filled with a little pool of condensation that doesn't ripple because the draft cannot reach into the cup of the heel. The crawlers crawl down into it. They like the salt that stayed in the skin-creases. They gather in the hollow of the instep, four or five of them, their translucent tails overlapping like mica flakes, their blunt heads drinking the last grease the mountain didn't have time to scour away.

Then the water-smoke fills it. The lichen creeps an eighth of an inch through the grease. The dent rounds its shoulders—the only thing in this whole split mountain that is allowed to round its shoulders—and disappears.

I like being the knot. 

Up there, they have to maintain the pegs. They have to think about the red rot in the larch hearts; they have to watch the blind crawlers to know when the wood is going to betray them; they have to tap their hips to say *grease* and tap their wrists to say *slack*. They are suspended between the newly broken stone that wants to shear their pegs and the void that wants their ribs. They cannot look up because of the glare, and they cannot look down because of the balance. They look at the four inches of wet larch before their toes until their skulls turn into solid chalk.

Down here, the stone has already broken as much as it can. The drop has already won. I am what the void couldn't swallow because the rope was twelve feet too long, and I have all the time the mountain doesn't have to turn from grass into fat, and from fat into stone, while the water goes on beating the cellar floor three miles down, trying to find where the world began before the rock forgot how to sleep.
