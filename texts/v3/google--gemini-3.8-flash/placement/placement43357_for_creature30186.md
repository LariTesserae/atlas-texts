I am in the third alley south of the split needle, wedged four feet above the trough where the tallow-slime skims the black water. 

There is no room to turn my heels. My left hip is scabbed hard against a razor-run of blue slate; my right collarbone takes the thrust of the ironstone slab that leans into it. Between them, my ribs are compressed until the vertical leaves of bone overlap, clicking together like the slats of a shutter each time my lungs draw their short, cold measure of mist. 

I am not the clambering kind that hunts with snout-horns, nor the flat ribbon that liquefies in the cracks. I am one of those whose hands have too many knuckles.

My thumbs have no pads; they end in broad, spade-headed rinds of horn, yellow-gray and ground flat from decades of finding seams in the wet fracture-lines. When I reach upward, my arm does not swing—there is no air for an arc—it slides along the greasy face of the stone like a blade being returned to its scabbard. My reach is long, longer than the width of two men, and my throat is a tight, crowded knot of gristle that never clears.

If I tried to weep, the sound would set my skull ringing like a struck kettle. Even to draw air too fast causes the teeth in my upper jaw to chatter against the lower with a dry, ceramic vibration that sickens the stomach. So I keep my jaw clamped shut, the thick damping-tongue pressed hard against the roof of my mouth, suffocating the violence before it can shatter the enamel. The noise does not enter through ears—I have only two blind, puckered pits behind the jaw, sealed with scarred gristle—it enters through the skeleton. The ironstone sings into my pelvic bowl; the slate sends its high, cold needle through my shoulder blades; the subterranean hydraulic thumping from the floor-slots kicks through the soles of my callused feet like a pulse of lead.

I have been here since the mist was white, and now the mist is ash. Time is not marked by the sky—which remains the color of a bruised knee, hanging so low it drowns the spires—but by the accumulation of travertine on the back of my neck. Where the lime-drip finds my skin, it leaves a ridge of pale, hard crust that I must peel away with my spade-nails every few days, lest it cement my chin to my clavicle.

Before me, suspended between the two blades at chest height, is the pink stone.

It took four of us three turnings of the grease-trough to bring it here from the basin’s scarp. We dragged it along the drowned causeways, hauling with ropes woven from the dried, vulcanized skins of the shield-crawlers, our bellies submerged in the red-frothed muck. It is not native to this teeth-forest. It has no grain; it does not split into leaves; it does not sing. It is an unworked lump of river-granite, ancient and blunt, the color of a salted tongue. 

Where the slate is nervous and shrill, this pink lump is dumb. It has no pitch. When a great drop falls ten yards from the spire-cap and smacks its crown, the granite does not ring: it simply receives the water with a heavy, dead *tup* that dies where it lands.

My task is the wedging.

In my left hand I hold the rib of my brother. He stood six yards back in this same slot until the water took his joints and made them rigid. His bone has been soaking in the iron-seam for twenty seasons; it is no longer yellow, but black and heavy as an iron bar, the porous marrow-chambers filled solid with hard spar. I have scraped its tip into a chisel-edge against the floor-shale.

I work the bone-point into the hairline crack where the slate blade is trying to hum. 

Above me, that blade is pitched to a silver shriek—a thin, vibrating note that catches the condensing fog and whips it into a fine, stinging spray that rots our skin-hides before they can cure. It is an angry tooth, forty feet high and thin as a hide-scraper, caught in a harmonic pocket that never lets it rest. It trembles so fast that if you laid a palm to its flank, it would strip the skin like a rasp.

I place the wedge. I set my breastbone against the thick end of the bone.

I do not have a hammer; we have no room to swing weight. I use the falling mass of my own torso, lunging forward three inches until my chest strikes the wedge, then peeling back, bracing my heels against the opposing face, and driving again. 

*Clack.*

The shock travels through my rib-slats, through the gristle in my throat, down into the marrow of my hips. My vision whites out behind the thick, milky skins that cover my eyes; the world becomes a sudden flash of salt.

*Clack.*

The bone bites. The crack in the slate leaps open an eighth of an inch, weeping a dark, foul-smelling grease of crushed slime and mineral oil. 

*Clack.*

The high, silver scream of the upright stone stutters. The pitch drops an octave, two octaves, falling from a shriek to a harsh, metallic rattle, and then, as the granite boulder settles an inch deeper into the spread jaws of the gap, the sound *dies*.

The silence does not spread far—only two paces in either direction. Beyond our slot, the millions of iron teeth continue their roaring, percussive chime, that wet iron storm that never tires. But here, between the flank of the blue slate and the dead belly of the pink granite, there is a pocket of quiet so thick it feels like lard.

The acoustic roar recedes to a distant, muffled thrumming, like water moving under ice.

I drop my arms. They hang against my sides, the skin gray and water-bleached, dripping red iron-stains from the wrists. 

Slowly, taking care not to scrape my ears against the jagged stone, I lower my forehead until the bone of my brow rests directly against the granite.

The pink stone is cold. It does not vibrate. It is like putting my head against the belly of something that was never born and cannot die. For a long time—perhaps hours, perhaps days—I do not move. The violent thrumming in my molars subsides. The gristle in my throat uncoils slightly, letting me breathe without the whistling rattle of the marrow-bone.

From the fog behind me, a blunt snout pushes into the crook of my knee. It is another of us—smaller, younger, its nictitating membranes still translucent enough to catch the gray light. It does not tap or scrape; it simply presses its chest into my back, folding its long, four-jointed fingers over my ribs, anchoring itself to my mass. It, too, reaches out until its forehead touches the granite.

We fit together in the slot like books on a shelf, our tallow-skin sticking together where the lime-water dries between our ribs.

One day, the granite will sink into the bottom muck, or the ironstone will rot through its foot and snap, crushing this dead spot into another tooth-jam. One day, the lime will get into my own knees, working its way through the sinew until the joints lock and refuse to slide.

When that comes, I will not need them to carry me far. They will only have to drag me five paces to where the water sheets fastest over the fracture-lip. They will wedge my feet into the black trough and set my back against the north-facing blade, tilting my chin so the iron-water runs straight down my breastbone, filling the pores where the blood used to run.

I will take the spray. I will let the crust form over my lips and seal the damping-knot in my neck forever. My ribs will stop sliding; the iron will weld them into a single, thick plate. 

And when the great drops fall from the mist and strike my head, I will not ring. I will make only a dry, leaden *tup*, deadening the valley by the width of my own shoulders, standing straight and cold in the dark, giving the others three inches of quiet behind my back.
