I would not be on the zinc walkways. 

To be on the zinc is to have a direction: an intake grate to scrape, a battery cell to top with sulfuric wash, a basket of mule’s tongues to carry up from the cellar beds before the three-hour flush drops from the reservoir. The people who walk the zinc have that heavy, forward-hinging stride of ox-hide; they have knees that click like latches and thumbs flattened into wedges. They belong to the drum-side.

I would be behind them, in the dark slot.

Where the curving amphitheater of granite bows inward and the rear walls of the tenements rise plumb, there is a space that no one owns. In some places it is six feet wide; in others, where a shoulder of feldspar pushes out like the knuckle of a buried fist, it narrows to the breadth of a man’s ribs. It is the chimney through which the subterranean breath of the peat rises to meet the wet ceiling of the world. It smells of boiled tar, old pelt, the dead-leaf rustle of the wingless crickets, and the cold, mineral iron of rock that has not seen the luminous bruise of noon since the first cobble was set.

I would be the one who works that seam. A caulker, perhaps, or a *seam-creeper*—though titles are too light a thing to carry in the damp. 

My skin would not have the bright, hard yellow of the condenser tenders’ palms. It would be grey-white, the color of the shelf-fungus growing on the horse dung below, permanently stained at the knuckles with birch-pitch and the dull, oily soot that flakes from the lagging of the steam mains. In that slot, the wind cannot draw its fleece; the air does not move in the long, silent trot of the high crags. It stays. It hangs warm, greasy, and thick as sheep-dip, kept alive by the hot bellies of the iron pipes that run along the stone to prevent the mountain’s cold from cracking the flume-hangers.

I would wear no ox-hide. The boiled leather is too stiff for the wedge; it would jam a man between the lath and the granite until the next shift noticed the drip stopping. 

Instead, I would wear only the innermost layer of the pelt—a sleeveless tunic of raw wool, bald at the hips from scraping against the wet rock, soaked through with animal grease until it repels even the warm condensate that trickles from the pipe unions. My feet would be bare, yellow-padded like the others’, but broader, the toes permanently spread to find the ledges in the masonry where the mortar has rotted into sand.

My work would be small, quiet, and unending.

The mountain does not stay still. Two billion years have made it heavy, but it is not dead; it settles on its sponge of peat with a slow, grinding sigh that takes forty years to travel from the high combs to the drainage cisterns. When it sighs, the settlement groans. A timber frame racks three inches to the west; an iron tie-rod, anchored into the granite with molten lead, begins to draw like an old tooth; a seam opens in the back of a wash-flume, and water—precious, distilled sky-water—begins to bleed down the useless face of the stone, feeding liverwort where it ought to be turning the lead-stirrers.

I would be the one who finds the draws.

I would move sideways through the dark, carrying a shallow iron skillet filled with glowing coke, suspended by three wires from my teeth to leave my hands free. In the small, red-rimmed circle of its heat, the world would be very close: four inches of dark river-cobble on the left, greasy with grease-wood soot; four inches of ancient, pink-grained granite on the right, sweating cold droplets that smell of nothing at all.

Between them: the crickets.

They would know me by my warmth. They would crawl across the backs of my calves, heavy as plums, dry and soft-shelled, their pale antennae whipping against my ankles as they feed on the tallow I rub into my joints to keep the hollow-rot out of my sinews. I would not pinch them into bottles. We would share the pipe-run. Sometimes, when a steam valve two levels down sighs open with a dull, subterranean *whuff*, a hundred of them would drop from the hemp wrapping at once, showering my shoulders like dry beans, and I would simply shake them back into the dark without breaking rhythm.

In my belt-pouches I would carry the paste: the cold bone-meal from the upper shelves, pounded fine in the stamp-mills and kneaded with birch-pitch and sulfur scrapings until it has the texture of fresh bread. 

Where the rock pulls away from an iron pin, I would pack it.

I would reach my arm into the wet cleft, past the hot skin of the steam main, until my shoulder pressed hard against the living granite. The stone there is not smooth like the river-cobbles; it is coarsely crystalline, cold enough to make the marrow in the ulna ache, yet greasy to the touch from the ancient oils the mountain breathes out through its pores. With my thumb—not flattened like a gear-tender’s, but long, spatula-shaped, calloused until it has no feeling left—I would force the grey paste deep into the seam, feeling for the iron rod that holds some tenement family’s sleeping-box to the cliff.

I would know the dead by their teeth.

The bone-meal is never entirely ground. In the paste, one often finds a shard of rib, a fragment of hip, or the hard, square crown of an old engineer’s molar, uncrushed by the iron drop-hammers. I would push them into the fissure with the rest. There is no horror in it; on this plateau, a man’s skeleton is simply mineral lime that has spent sixty years walking upright before going back to work as mortar. When I jammed a piece of an apprentice’s skull into a gap between the cedar sill and the granite shelf, I would be doing only what the cloud does when it leaves its fleece on the juniper: returning the drift to the scoop.

Every three hours, the world outside would change its note.

Even deep in the slot, where the air is dead and greasy, I would feel the *thud* through the soles of my feet when the high valves tripped. It begins as a tremble in the rock—a faint, rhythmic shivering that clears the crickets from the cold stone and sends them scuttling up into the lagging. Then comes the sound: not a roar, for the moss kills the roar before it can leave the flume, but a vast, wet slide, like thousands of sacks of grain being dragged across wet zinc. The cold water is running. 

The air in my narrow chimney would reverse. For twenty seconds, a cold, violent downdraft—drawn down from the upper teeth by the suction of the sluices—would rip through the gap, smelling of high snow, clean sky, and the iron-slatted racks where the bodies lie turning to mist. It would chill the sweat on my ribs to frost; it would blow the sparks from my hanging skillet into my hair; it would carry the scent of the vast, indifferent west into my nostrils.

Then the valve would seat. The slide would cease. The water would be in the cisterns, turning the bucket-wheels in the lead-houses. 

The heat from the steam pipes would swell out again, thick and buttery, pushing the mountain-breath back up into the ceiling. The crickets would drop back down to the stone. The fat on my wool would soften once more.

And I would move two yards further into the crack, feeling with my knuckles for the place where the amphitheater is trying to forget the houses wedged into its teeth.
