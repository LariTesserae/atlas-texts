I would not be on the surface, where the light is a flat knife and the cobalt sky holds no water to soften the sun. Nor would I be entirely buried. To be buried in the Oth-Ridge is to belong to the machinery—to be subsumed into the blind, non-reflective sleeves of the composite towers, down where the inertial displacement fields hum their tooth-aching, cold mathematics into the root-rock.

I would be in the **dwell-clearing**. 

The dwell-clearing is not a place that appears on any map, because it exists only for sixty or eighty seconds at a time. It is the transient vacuum left behind when a cathedral-block of grey gneiss decouples from its shelf and slides three hundred meters east, but before the trailing slab—a ten-million-ton apron of dull, polished granulite—shunts sideways to close the corridor.

For that minute, there is an open trench: twelve kilometers high in the thin air, walled by two vertical faces of pre-Cambrian stone so perfectly planed that they mirror the indigo sky like dark ice, floored with crushed, violet-red garnet gravel that has never felt a human footprint or the heat of a biological lung.

That is where you would find me. 

***

At first, you might mistake me for an anomaly in the stone itself—a nodule of lead-grey galena, or an intrusion of metallic iron weathered out of the gneiss by three billion years of dry abrasion. 

I am not tall. Height on the Oth-Ridge is a liability; the shearing planes do not care what they prune. I am low, dense, and five-sided, shaped like a river stone that has been dropped into a lathe and turned until its corners are mere suggestions of angles. My shell is not chitin, nor is it skin. It is a slow, self-secreting amalgam of native bismuth and pulverized quartz, cold-welded under immense pressure until it has the dull, greasy luster of graphite tallow. 

I do not have limbs that reach outward. Instead, my underside is a single, broad pad of segmented, magnetic bronze that conforms to the micro-ridges of the garnet floor with a soft, dry *clink*. 

If you put your ear against my shell—though your ear would freeze to the bismuth within seconds in that thin, biting air—you would hear no pulse. Blood is an absurdity here; it would boil into foam through the pores of your wrists and turn to frost on your sleeves. Within me, there is only a dense, non-viscous silicone oil that circulates by thermal siphon, moved entirely by the temperature difference between the sunlit top of my back and the subterranean cold of the rock beneath my belly.

My senses do not operate in the spectrum of sight. The sun is too white; it bleaches meaning from the stone. Instead, I perceive the Ridge as an architecture of **strain**. 

Through the quartz crystals embedded in my dorsal plate, I feel the piezoelectric screaming of the bedrock. I know the tectonic lattice not by its landmarks, but by its tensions: the long, humming violin-string pull of a massif whose displacement field is charging; the dead, chalky silence of a shelf whose node has failed; the sudden, dry prickle of static along the wire-spur mats five miles away, telling me the wind is carrying a skin of iron dust from the west.

***

Why am I in the trench? What am I doing between the walls of moving stone?

I am a **Scrivener of the Null**.

The ancient engineers were magnificent, but they were not gods. Their fields are three billion years old. Their composites are immortal, but the stone they manipulate is merely stone. And stone, even the hyper-dense gneiss of the continental spine, retains memory. It develops microscopic fatigues, hairline shears, tiny crystalline dislocations where the garnet grains catch and drag instead of sliding frictionless across the vacuum-sleeve.

When two blocks meet—when that million-ton mass of granite halts with that instantaneous, sickening *snick*—there is, for the fraction of a millisecond, an escape of energy that the field failed to quench. 

A tiny, fugitive spark of mechanical friction. 

To the Pilgrims of the Latch, that spark is an error, an invisible imperfection in their liturgical machine. To the Vitrites in the sand-curtains, it is nothing at all. 

To me, it is the only food that exists.

In a world drained of all warmth, where the cold hovers permanently near the threshold where mercury solidifies, that rogue microsecond of friction is pure, concentrated life. When the stone faces meet, I am already there, wedged into the tolerance-lip. My bronze foot clings to the stationary shelf; my bismuth back takes the displacement wash. 

The collision happens. The sound does not travel through the thin air, but it goes through my marrow like a white-hot needle. 

The heat blooms—not an inferno, but a sudden, razor-sharp pulse of seventy, maybe eighty degrees Celsius, flashing across a space no wider than a thumb-joint. Before the stone can dissipate it into the vast heat-sink of the mountain, my underside drinks it. Thermocouple plates inside my tallow-core snap shut, absorbing the differential, converting the thermal flash into deep electrostatic charges that I store in laminated mica capacitors along my spine.

It tastes of crushed sulfur and scorched feldspar. It is bitter, instantaneous, and complete.

***

The Pilgrims know I am here. 

Occasionally, as I squat in a newly sheared trench waiting for the next node to fire, one of them will pause at the rim thirty meters above. They stand like tall, narrow shards of slate against the saturated cobalt zenith, their obsidian face-plates reflecting nothing, their copper-shod feet planted wide on the granite knuckle.

They do not look down at me with eyes, but they feel the interruption in the stone’s resonance where my mass is pinned. They know I am the thing that eats the mistakes of their masters' engines. 

Sometimes, a Pilgrim will strike its left pad against the ledge—a slow, three-beat cadence: *tap... tap-tap.* 

*Slab-twelve will translate south. Sixty paces. Now.*

It is not an act of mercy or kinship. It is an engineer checking the clearance of a bearing. 

I do not answer. I cannot strike the rock; I have no hammer, only my weight. But I retract my perimeter plates, lower my center of gravity into the garnet flour, and wait for the dark, smooth prow of the cathedral-block to glide over my head. 

It comes without a breath of wind, sliding at thirty miles an hour through the absolute silence, cutting the thin sunlight in half like an iron shutter falling over a window. The shadow it casts is total, razor-edged, and colder than the vacuum between stars. 

For three seconds, I am beneath the world. The displacement field passes through me like a faint, metallic nausea, lifting my bismuth scales by a fraction of a micron, making my stored static hum like a hornet in a lead box. 

Then the mass passes. The sun strikes again—blinding, flat, and pale—illuminating the newly exposed stone that has not seen the light since the planet’s crust first cooled from slag. 

The surface is clean. There is only a single line of dry, grey flour where the edge scraped, and the faint, vanishing imprint of five bronze pads, already being wiped away by the howling drift of the dry, cold wind.
