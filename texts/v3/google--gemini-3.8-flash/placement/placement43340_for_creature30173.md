The first thing that settles in is not the eyes, but the heels. 

The basalt underneath does not vibrate like an engine; it hums the way a church bell hums two minutes after it has been struck, when the pitch has fallen below what the ears can take and has entered the marrow of the shinbones. You don't listen to it; you balance on it. If you stand still for twelve paces of breath, your knees learn where the three-mile flywheel is turning in the dark down below, and whether the powdered graphite in its races has begun to pack too hard on the northern quadrant.

I would not be on the tram-spans. That is work for men whose eyes have already turned to horn, men who can watch the great counterweights rise by fractions of a nail-breadth without wanting to drop a plumb-bob into the purple haze below to see if the world has a floor. 

Nor would I be among the kites. Their existence is too papery, too near the sun’s unblinking eye, their ribs snapping if a cross-draft catches them against an iron truss. There is no leverage in the sky.

No, I find myself lower down, where the massifs are joined not by cable, but by the masonry of necessity—in the shadow of the lead conduits, where the ground is neither city nor quarry, but the swept, grey slate that smells of friction.

My hands are not bare. They are wrapped to the second knuckle in strips of boiled sailcloth that have been dipped in petroleum-pitch until they are stiff as dried carp. If you touch the stone with naked thumb-skin at midday, the feldspar does not burn you with blister-heat—it simply sips the oil out of your flesh before you can draw your hand back. By evening, your prints would be white as ash and smooth as soap. So the wraps stay on, worn to a black, oily shine at the creases where the fingers bend.

I carry a copper-headed drift and a pocket of tallow-wax rolled in flour. 

At first, you might think I am a repairer of pipes, because I spend hours kneeling by the bolted flanges of the high-pressure air conduits, where the three-mile bellows drive the dry lung of the lower mines up into the foundry-rings. But the pipe-fitters are heavier men; they travel in pairs with four-foot spanners and sledges of green bronze that will not spark against the iron. 

My work is smaller, slower, and closer to the stone. 

I follow the seam.

Every massif has its cleavage—not a crack where it falls away, but the long, dull line where the feldspar of the fifth shelf presses against the pink gypsum of the sixth. It is two inches wide in the cool of the third watch, when the heat has retreated into the stone's heart; by the fourth hour after noon, it has pinched shut so hard that if a dropped copper coin were caught between the lips, it would come out thin as a wafer and three times as broad.

In that seam, things live that do not like the light.

When the gap narrows, the air inside does not escape easily; it whistles out with a tiny, sour hiss that carries the smell of old iron filings and subterranean pitch. With that breath comes the yellow dust—the dry, microscopic bloom of the sulfur-moulds that grow four hundred fathoms down where the rock is red-warm. If the dust cakes, the seam cannot shut true. If it cannot shut true, the stress does not disperse through the slate; it gathers at the foundation-piers of the tramway towers, and three miles away a lead rivet shears with a sound like a pistol shot.

So I clear it. 

I have a scraper made from the shoulder-blade of a young carillon—the bone is denser than our own, pale cream, oily even after seventy seasons of air, and it does not score the granite. I slide the thin edge into the seam, three feet at a stroke, drawing back the dry, grey-yellow curd that has gathered since the last expansion. 

It does not drop into the dirt. Nothing falls. I have a long zinc scoop tied to my left knee with hemp cords; the scrape falls into the trough with a sound like dry oats sliding into a bin.

Sometimes, the carillons themselves are there before me. 

They do not dislike me; they do not have enough eyes to care about anything that stands lower than their third knee-joint. But they know the sound of the scraper. A great bull, thirty yards of articulated porcelain plates the color of old tallow, will drift toward the conduit on his six pillared legs, his under-belly rasps grinding against the slate with that long, dry *shuck-shuck-shuck* that makes the grease in your hair stand up. 

He wants the dust from my scoop. To him, the sulfur-crust I pull from the seam is sweet—it has the bite of concentrated vitriol and the fat of the deep hydrocarbons that have never seen the light. 

He stands over me, his vast belly blotting out the yellow noon, smelling of warm lard, paraffin, and scorched lime. His lateral vents open with a slow, wet click—the only wet sound on the plateau, like a grease-box being unlatched—and he draws the draft off my clothes. He is so heavy that the slate between my boots tilts slightly under his tread. You do not move away; you flatten yourself against the lead conduit, let the rib-bone scraper lie quiet in the zinc trough, and wait while his scraping-horn planes the edges of the rock three inches from your shoulder. 

When he is done, he leaves a track of white talc so fine that if you stepped in it, you would slide as if on melted butter. 

I take my brush—made of stiff kite-cartilage, frayed at the tip with a small mallet—and I sweep his track into the sacks tied to my belt. The machinists in the sixth foundry will pay two brass counters for every quart of it. They use it to dust the molds for the flywheel bearings; there is no sand on the massifs fine enough to take the impression of an eighteen-foot gear-tooth without leaving a seam, but the carillon’s leavings leave the iron face as smooth as a thumb-nail.

By late afternoon, the heat is no longer yellow; it is the color of old brass. 

The tally-man’s hut, two miles out on the tip of the drop, catches the slant of the sun and turns into an orange ember against the blue emptiness below. I can hear the click of his shot-chute from here—*tink*—twelve pounds of lead falling forty miles down into the salt-crust to balance the slack of the main cable as the iron stretches in the late furnace-glow.

My mouth tastes of tin and dried sage. I reach into the chest-pocket of my smock, under the stiff canvas, and find the plug of gasket-rot. It is hard now, tough as a dried plum, black with the graphite it gorged on before the line-walker pried it from the high-pressure flange. I put a corner of it between my back teeth and bite down. 

It does not give juice; there is no juice in anything here. But it gives up its salt—that deep, mineral salt that was old before the massifs were stacked—and the tongue moves against the dry roof of the mouth, finding enough saliva to wet the lip-seams. 

I do not look at the drop. 

Only the old men look over the edge, those who have spent thirty years watching the kites until their necks are set like iron stays. If you look down there, where the purple haze begins, you start to remember things you never had: the weight of water that does not stay in a can; the cold that rots a man's fingers from the bone instead of curing them into horn; the notion that the world might have an end that isn't a squared ledge of granite. 

That is how the slate-stare starts. First you wonder what the air smells like forty leagues down; then you forget to scrape the tallow from your shoulder-blade at night; then your speech leaves your teeth and becomes a whistle in the throat that sounds like an escape-valve with a worn washer.

I keep my eyes on the seam.

The stone is already cooling now. The red brick tenements of the fourth district are beginning to give back the noon; their three-foot walls are breathing their slow, greasy warmth into the alleys, where the children are coming out onto the zinc catwalks with their little bone scrapers, ready to gather the day's droppings from the foundry vents before the wind can scatter them.

The seam is opening again. 

I can feel it through the boiled cloth on my knees—a faint, microscopic give in the slate, an easing of the great rib. A hair’s breadth every twenty minutes. The subterranean drafts are reversing, drawing the surface air down into the dark, pulling the dust with them.

I take a cake of graphite-grease from my pouch, rub it thick along the bone blade, and lay it deep between the lips of the rock.

The mountain takes it slowly, with a dry, contented sigh that smells of cold hearths and iron filings. 

Tomorrow the seam will be two inches wide again, grey and clean, and the flywheels below will turn another half-inch on their beds without a shudder. 

I stand up. My joints do not crack; here, you oil yourself from the inside with tallow-broth before you sleep, and your knees learn the silence of the iron around them. I shoulder the zinc trough, shake the white powder from the hem of my canvas smock, and turn toward the red tenements where the grease-lamps are being struck with small lead mallets against the flint. 

The air is blue now, dense as kiln-sand, and sweet with the smell of the first tallow-wick catching fire behind the thick brick walls.
