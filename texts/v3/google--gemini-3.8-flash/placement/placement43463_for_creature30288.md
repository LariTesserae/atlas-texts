At first, the instinct is to protect a throat that isn't there.

If you arrive with the memory of moisture—the heavy, sloshing ballast of water, the soft pocket of a stomach, the wet blind fold of an eye—you would have about three-tenths of a second to understand your mistake before the nitrogen ripped you open from the inside out. Your lungs would crack like dried parchment; your fluids would flash-boil into cold vapor, freezing as they blew through your ribs.

So that has to go first. The interior must be surrendered. 

I would not be on the canyon floor. Down there in the seventy-foot drifts of powdered silica, where the sifters cruise like blind locomotives, the world is too heavy, too dumb, smothered in its own talcum wake. There is a terrible, blunt peace down there, but it is the peace of an ore hopper. 

Nor would I be among the gardens. The clockwork fronds are too hysterical. To live on those vertical walls is to be trapped in an endless, frantic spasm of reproduction—springing open, shedding mica teeth into the howling downdraft, being flayed to the bare rock by the eleven-second pulse, and clawing back out of the stone before the heat of the friction has even faded. That is not life; that is a chemical detonation that doesn't know how to stop.

And the flyers, the ribbon-things—they are too thin. They are kites made of unpolished quartz, living on borrowed milliseconds, waiting for the compressor’s field to snap them into gravel.

No. To exist here and retain some quiet, crystalline edge of awareness, you would have to find the places where the violence cancels itself out. You would have to look for the nodes.

***

I find myself on the stay-cables.

Not an anchor-man, not one of those blind tripods of carbon pipe welding sapphire into the roots of the world, though I share their tolerance for the cold. They are too loyal to the machine; they have welded their own dead into the scaffolding until they are indistinguishable from the struts.

I would be a *traveler* on the monomolecular lines—what could be thought of as a runner, or a harmonic sleeve.

My body is not a vessel; it is a stack. A tight, laminated cuff of grown diamond foil, leaf-springs of cold-rolled graphene, and thin wafers of piezoelectric crystal, no thicker than a thumb's width, wrapped entirely around a single stay-cable that spans the empty nave from the south limestone wall to the cradle of the center compressor.

I have no hollow spaces. Where an animal would have organs, I have alternating laminae of polar and non-polar silicates, sliding against one another on dry monolayers of molybdenum disulfide. When the violent downdrafts hit, there is no drag—the wind simply shears across my outer foils, pressing the leaves tighter together. 

I do not walk, and I do not have limbs. I move by induction. 

The cable I live on is under millions of pounds of tension. It trembles at a frequency so high it is past sound; it is a translucent blur of pure kinetic agony hanging across a kilometer of drop. By flexing the piezoelectric plates along my inner bore, I bite into the standing waves of the cable. I surf the vibration. When I release the clamp, the wire’s shudder kicks me forward; when I grip, the friction strips electrons from the weave and charges the crystalline lattices between my foils.

I feed on the wire's distress.

***

From up here, five hundred meters above the dust-drifts, the daylight is a physical weight. Without moisture to scatter it, the sun does not warm; it merely etches. 

Half of my body—the dorsal foil facing the sky—is baked into searing electrostatic positive, gleaming like chrome. The ventral foil, turned toward the black, augered abyss below, is dropped into absolute shadow, near cryogenic, shedding heat into the void. It is this sheer thermal cliff across my own four-millimeter thickness that keeps my internal currents running. I am a thermocouple wrapped around a singing razor.

I know the landscape not by shape, but by pitch.

Below me, the sifters blow their dorsal flutes through the dust: long, subterranean fifths that rattle my lower foils like distant cannonades. 
To the left, the copper gardens rustle and shriek, a high-register rattle of millions of clockwork coils snapping in unison. 
And above, floating in its gravity-null cradle, the dark polyhedron of the compressor engine hums with that low, tooth-loosening drone that vibrates the bedrock.

And then, there is the count.

Everything in the caldera lives by the eleven-second rhythm of the compressor, but for me, on the cable, it is an architectural fact. 

*One. Two. Three.*

The air begins to thin out further, pulled toward the intake facets of the housing. The ribbon-predators in the mid-gulf feel it first; they tilt their silicate scales, diving toward the rock faces to wedge themselves into the fissures.

*Four. Five. Six.*

The stay-cable begins to warm beneath my sleeve. The tension climbs. The pitch of its vibration rises from an inaudible hum to a dry, screaming whine that sets my piezoelectric plates ringing. I unclamp my outer laminations. I let myself go limp—as limp as diamond and graphene can be—flattening my leaves like a closed fan against the line. If I am rigid when the shock comes, the shear will snap my plates across their cleavage planes.

*Seven. Eight. Nine.*

The shadow of the polyhedral housing twitches. The light bends around it, a greasy, lens-like warping that makes the opposite wall of the canyon look as though it has been dropped through broken glass. 

*Ten.*

I locate the nodal point—a dead zone eighteen inches wide along the wire where the coming harmonic will have an amplitude of zero. I slide there on a film of magnetic repulsion. I lock my diamond prongs into the weave.

*Eleven.*

The snap.

It is not a sound. Sound requires an atmosphere that can carry it; this is a kinetic displacement so total it evacuates the air from its own path. An invisible hammer, a square kilometer wide, strokes through the center of the nave. 

Across the void, an entire hanging garden of copper and calcified lace is struck from the cliff face. It does not fall; it simply turns to an instant, glittering spray of copper dust that hangs suspended in the razor-light like a frozen cloud of mica. Thousands of quartz-scaled flyers are shattered into fragments the size of sand, their kinetic energy converted instantly into tiny, violet static arcs that dance across the abyss.

The cable whips. 

Even at the nodal point, the transverse force is monstrous. It hits my outer leaves like a blow from an iron bar. My laminations grind against one another, their crystalline lattices compressing, discharging blinding bursts of blue sparks through the seams between my plates. For a tenth of a second, I am white-hot with friction, my internal foils sliding at ballistic speed to dissipate the force before it can shear through my core.

And then it passes.

The cloud of copper powder begins to rain down toward the sifter-floor, a slow, shimmering curtain of dust. 
Out from the cracks in the limestone, the needle-like sprouts of the next garden are already puncturing the stone, driven by the static charge left behind by the pulse.
The pendulums drop out of the upper haze, their shells split open, sapphire pulleys screaming against the thread-lines as they dive to scoop the hot crystal shrapnel from the air.

And I open my foils.

I cool in the dry nitrogen draft within seconds, the heat drawn away into the thin, clean slipstream. My plates reset with a series of microscopic, metallic *clicks*, settling back into their leaf-spring order. 

Ahead of me, two hundred meters along the cable, one of the anchor-folk stands motionless on the cradle rim, its dark toroidal head tilted toward the line. It has sensed the phase-slip caused by the pulse. It leans forward with agonizing slowness, extending its white-hot sapphire needle to touch the cable’s anchor-plate, drawing a tiny bead of glowing slag to true the harmonic.

It does not see me. To the anchor-folk, I am merely a lump of mineral ballast, a point of dampening mass that prevents the stay-cable from tearing its own eyes out of the stone.

I tighten my inner sleeve. The cable shudders beneath me, rich with fresh kinetic tension, waiting for the count to begin again.

*One.*

I release the brake. I let the standing wave catch my forward foil, and I slide out into the cold, empty, sunlit gulf.
