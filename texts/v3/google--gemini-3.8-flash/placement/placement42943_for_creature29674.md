It would not begin with a face or an ancestry. To enter this shelf with anything as brittle as an upright posture or an unbroken collarbone would mean being hammered into a red froth within the first three cycles of the nearest derrick. 

At first, I would only be an adjustment to the floor.

A reluctance to rise. A mass tucked low into the slick, cast-iron peat, where the freezing slush doesn't fall so much as roll in horizontal scuds across the fibrous weave. If you looked from fifty paces, you might take me for a discarded bolster of yew-root, or the knot where three conduit-veins have tangled and scarred over under the weight of an old slip-fault. I would have to be broad-based, long in the flank, and soft where the world is hard, because rigidity is an invitation to be sheared.

My skin would not be hide or skin as men understand it. It would have the dull, water-cured density of a leather strop that has soaked forty years in bog-iron and cold tallow. No hair—hair would cake with grease and freeze into stiff wire that catches on the wind—but a close, slippery felt of microscopic cellular teeth, like the belly of a skate, angled so that the gale slides off my back without purchase.

I wouldn't live in the thrash of the piston-trees, where the Weevils burn their bellies against the screaming lignum-vitae flywheels; that work is too frantic, too close to the flame. Nor would I sit out in the wide zinc-gray flats where the Gallow-Gaits dance their stiff, agonizing jigs on the off-beat, forever listening for the snap of their own shanks. 

I would find myself in the *seam*.

There are places where the geometry rots out—the lateral folds, where forty miles of flat moss tucks into an unmapped crease three fathoms deep, vibrating with the blue hiss of subterranean static. In those trenches, the air is thick with vinegar mist and warm marsh-gas, and the concussive *whump* of the five-mile pumps doesn't hit straight on. It echoes. It splits into harmonic overtones, rattling back and forth between walls of compressed, rubbery iron-peat until its killing edge is blunted into a heavy, continuous throb, like the belly-rumble of a foundering bull.

That is where I would lie. Wedged between the root-shoulders, half-drowned in the scalding brown effluent that bleeds off the galvanic junctions.

My body would be built like a bellows or a hydraulic damper. No ribs: a basket of dense, interlaced cartilage that gives three inches under a shockwave and then sighs back into shape. My limbs would be short, paddle-broad, tipped with shovel-nails of blue-black horn, good only for bracing against the lateral thrust of the turf. 

I would have no use for eyes. The twilight offers nothing to see except gray rain and the blinding, pupil-tearing snap of static arcs that burn an after-image into the brain until the mind rots. Instead, along the underside of my snout and down the soft margin where my belly meets the wet peat, I would possess a fringe of pale, fat-filled ampullae—thick as thumb-joints, blind and greasy. They don't see the light; they read the *torque*.

I would feel the engine-trees three miles off: the slow, swelling pressure in their xylem pipes; the hesitation as a piston-rod binds against a swollen burl; the wet, sick shudder of a joint running dry of tallow; the brief, glorious silence at the top of the stroke when sixty tons of ironwood hangs motionless against the zinc sky, poised to drop. 

I would be a creature of the *misfire*.

The Still-Dogs wait for the Vane-Wings to fall from the sky; the Barrow-Grooms wait for the channels to clog so they may scrape them with their horn thumbs. But I would wait for the failures of cadence.

When a combustion chamber deep in the peat misfires—when the cold peat-gas fails to catch and the great barge-thick taproot comes down out of rhythm, half a beat late—the shelf does not simply absorb the blow. The shockwave runs sideways through the floor like a cracked whip. The Gallow-Gaits hear it too late; their non-Newtonian grease locks in their knees at the wrong instant, and their cart-horse shanks snap with a sound like dry birch timbers giving way in a mine-collapse. 

They fall like felled cranes into the cold mire.

That is when I would move. Not with a stride—a stride has daylight under it, and daylight is where the gale tears things apart—but with a heavy, rhythmic hitching of the belly, hitching forward on my brisket, using the scalding slurry of the drainage flumes to grease my passage. 

I would reach the fallen Gait before the Still-Dogs have dragged their tarpaulin bellies out of the lateral folds. 

I have no jaw for tearing meat, no teeth to blunt against the calcified wool of their hides. Under my snout sits a broad, muscular disk—a grinding ring of black horn, wet and circular, set around a short, churning rasp. I would not want their meat. The meat of this shelf is stringy, bitter with lye, and saturated with sulfur. 

I want the bursae.

I would bore directly into the wrecked knees of the beast, where the joint-sleeve has burst open in the slush. I would suck out the great, pale cakes of synovial grease—that thick, buttery fluid that spent fifty years absorbing the iron strikes of the derrick-trees. It would be warm, salty, thick as tallow, and heavy with the electrical charge the animal’s frame had grounded out of the air. It is pure buffer. It settles into the reservoirs behind my own joints, into the heavy, fat-lined collar around my neck, until my own flesh becomes completely acoustically dead: an animal that makes no sound when struck, that can take the blow of an ironwood spar and ring no louder than a sack of wet sand dropped into lard.

And in the long, gray intervals—when the five engines on the horizon are all striking true, five miles apart, an unvarying four-beat measure that makes the slush leap two inches off the turf with every stroke—I would do nothing at all.

I would sink two feet into the peat, letting the cold, iron-rich water seal over my flanks until only my snout-flues break the scum. I would breathe the smell of scorched cambium, vinegar, and machine-oil, tasting the static drift as it tickles the copper-nerves in my mouth. 

I would listen to the Barrow-Grooms droning in the fog—that flat, sixty-cycle hum from their horn faces, holding the land together, keeping the cracks from widening into chasms that would swallow the pumps. I would feel their heavy, wood-shod feet pass three paces from my buried head, shaking the mud, neither of us acknowledging the other. They are the maintenance of the work; I am its consequence, the dampening grease that collects in the corners where the floor-timbers groan.

Eventually, after a hundred years or two hundred, the calluses on my brisket would harden into true bog-iron. My cartilage would take up so much tannin and free silica from the slurry that it would cease to give under the shocks. The non-Newtonian tallow in my knees would turn to resin, dark and hard as amber.

When that happens, I would not walk to the intake troughs like the Grooms do. I would not offer myself to the piston.

I would simply wait in the lateral fold for the cold to get beneath me. The grease would stiffen; the vibrating peat would slowly pack itself around my ribs, layer by layer, driven into every crease of my skin by the endless concussions from above. Within a season, the living stratum would weld itself to my back. The blue-sparking root-threads would pierce my hide, mistaking my iron-soaked tallow for an old knot of dead wood, and run their high-voltage conduits through my marrow to ground themselves against the deep rock below.

And I would stay there: a calcified stop in the plumbing of the plateau, a small, stubborn lump of dead vibration, while the flywheels scream through the freezing needles overhead and the world goes on hammering itself to dust in the dark.
