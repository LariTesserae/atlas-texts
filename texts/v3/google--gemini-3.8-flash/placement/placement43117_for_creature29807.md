At first, it is not a body at all, but a frequency. 

To be here is to be pressed against, to have the marrow shaken before there is even bone to carry it. The stationary diesels do not merely make a sound; they lay down an iron floor in the air, a flat, hard stratum of vibration that everything else must sit upon. In the beginning, you could be anywhere along that line: you could be the dried white lime in the mortar joints, or the cold fat inside the lubricator cups, or the tension in the forty-foot belt before it hits the drum. 

You look for yourself first in the high places, because the light there is so clean. But no—not the gantry. The gantry belongs to things that can turn on a needle and take the sky at sixty miles an hour without snapping their hollow quills. Up there, the steel rings with three-phase power like a wet glass rubbed by a thumb; it is too brisk, too bright, too nervous. 

Nor can you be in the stone flutes with the toads. That requires a kind of vertical patience, a rib-sprung stillness that holds itself upright for days in the spray of the drill-scars, waiting for midges with golden, unblinking eyes. That is an ancient, stunted thing, and this place, for all its slate, is modern. It has a clock.

So you come down. You follow the weeping seams down the lower three feet of the rock face where the water has no time to grow moss, only speed enough to slide. You cross the raked shale, pass the quiet white insulators of the transformer masts, and drop below the line of the rail heads.

You are down where the ballast has ceased to be loose stone and has packed itself into a cold, mineral paste.

And there you take on weight.

It starts in the shoulders—a broad, low-slung heaviness, built to displace gravel without scattering it. Not an animal of the woods; there is no wood here, only creosoted hemlock and lead conduit. The coat that comes over you is not fur in any ordinary sense, but a dense, water-repellent pelt the texture of a sodden wool blanket, stiffened with graphite dust and stone-flour until it matches the pale, tallowy grey of the limestone slurry. When you shake yourself, you do not throw off drops of rain; you throw off a fine, dry mist of lime and cylinder oil that smells like an unlit blacksmith’s forge.

Your place is beneath the south turning-pad. 

It is an iron disc twelve feet across, set flush with the shale between the two narrow-gauge spurs. On top, the rusted rails cross its face in a neat plus-sign; underneath, where you lie, there is a hollow clearance eight inches deep, roofed in cast iron and floored in damp, tamped ballast. 

It is the darkest spot in the basin, and the most stable. In the center of the ceiling sits the pivot pin: a forged steel knuckle thick as a man’s thigh, bedded in a cup of black, fibrous axle-grease that never hardens. The grease hangs from the iron ribs in cold, thumb-sized stalactites. When the counterweight takes its four-minute kick and drops with that hollow, subterranean *whump*, a single bead of heavy black oil will let go of the casting, fall through the eight inches of air, and land with a dry *snick* upon your flank. 

You do not flinch. You have the basin’s pace in your ribs. You count the drops.

Your paws are wide and flat-soled, the skin on the pads hard as vulcanized rubber, with short, horn-colored claws that don't click when you haul your weight across the flange-ways. You are too heavy to run through the hollow conduits like the weasels; you do not have their nervous, predatory slip. You are an animal of clearances, of under-spaces, living in the margin between the underside of the machinery and the bed of the trench.

When you emerge, it is never by day. Not because the light harms your eyes—the high, shadowless daylight of the cut is too pale to burn anything—but because daylight belongs to the tender’s rubber boots and the swallows’ long, screaming dives. 

You come out when the siphon gurgles in the drainage sluice. 

That hour-long intake is the basin’s only true exhalation. As the siphon draws, the water level in the open concrete culverts drops six inches in two minutes, exposing the wet stone-gutters, the slimy footings of the substations, and the tangled, drowned roots of the horse-tails that have shoved their way through the shale. 

That is your table. You lever yourself out from under the rim of the turning-pad with a slow, hydraulic ease. You do not scuttle. You move with the deliberate, heavy shuffle of a river-badger that has forgotten the river and taken up with the drains. 

You graze where the tender has been with his knife. Where he has neatly cut the bindweed back from the tooth-race, you pull up the bitter, fleshy underground runners with your claws, chewing them down into a cold, milky pulp that tastes of iron and bruised dock. You hunt the heavy green ground-beetles along the damp bevel of the culverts, crunching their lacquered wing-cases with your flat back molars; they taste of wet slate and copper grease. When the dynamos throw their pulverized mist of cylinder oil into the dock-leaves, you strip the broad green blades between your lips, licking the mineral tallow from the ribs of the plant with a tongue as rough as emery cloth.

Sometimes the tender is still out when you cross the ballast between the tracks. 

He will be kneeling fifty yards away, his horn-handled oiler tipped against the bearing of an idler-pulley, his white-soled boots planted wide on the shale. The concussive *thung-clack* of the diesels beats between you, a shared pressure against the eardrums, steady as blood. 

He turns his head. His eyes, yellowed by sodium vapor, catch yours. 

He does not reach for a wrench or a stone. He knows the weight of your belly against the ground; he knows that you eat the roots that would otherwise buckle his concrete drains, and that your broad flanks scrape the rust from the underside of the turntable ribs as you turn in your sleep. You know his three cotton waste-cloths; you know that if you leave a chewed runner of horse-tail across the rail, his boot will kick it aside into the trench before the narrow-gauge wagon comes down the spur.

You hold his look for the space of five diesel strokes. *Thung-clack. Thung-clack. Thung-clack.* 

The air shifts, carrying that dry, pneumatic vapor that evaporates before it can touch the ground. High above, a swallow drops out of the gantry, swoops within two inches of your greased neck to snatch a stone-midge, and snaps away toward the raw slate flutes. 

You lower your snout. You pull the last tender white root from beneath a timber sleeper, back yourself down under the rim of the iron pad until only your grey, leather-tipped nose and your small, lead-colored eyes remain in the trench-light, and you settle your belly against the cold, shaking ballast. 

Above your skull, the great wheel turns four minutes toward its counterweight; below you, the deep slate weeps its cold, clear water; and between the two, you fit precisely, without a fraction of an inch to spare.
