It begins not with a shape, but with a measurement against the ribs.

The heat does not come from above. It does not warm the skin first; it enters through the pelvis, through the flat calluses of the heels, through the heavy slab of bone that sits over the chest like an internal shield. The air is too thick to separate into breaths. It is swallowed in plugs, warm and slippery, tasting of zinc and wet slate, sitting in the belly until the body squeezes the water out of it and lets the remainder seep into the muscle.

At first, there is the temptation to be high—perched on the rim where the granite needles pierce into that dry, killing ether, looking down into the white bowl like an observer. But nothing survives up there with its eyes open. Up there, the pressure gives out, the moisture leaves the blood in an instant, and the cold cracks marrow like glass. To exist here at all is to be sunk deep in the cauldron, below the level of the steam-shelf, where the mountain holds the atmosphere down with both hands.

I am not the water, though I move with its densities. I am not the iron, though the iron is the only thing that tells the truth about distance.

I find myself low, wedged into the crawl-space where an eighteen-foot intake sleeve bypasses an amphitheater of dripping porphyry. 

The clearance here is barely two spans. The underside of the conduit is coated in a greasy, sulfur-colored crust that flakes off under a thumb like dried tallow, still hot enough to soften between the fingers. Behind me, the black rock sweats cold, alkaline slime; before me, the iron wall hums at fifty cycles, so loud to the touch that resting a forehead against it blurs the vision into milky bands of light.

My skin is gray, thick as a boot sole, and folded into deep, mud-trapping crevices at the joints. There is no hair to rot in the perpetual damp, only a slick, protective exudate that the skin secretes when the air acidifies after a purge. My eyes are small, clouded by a permanent pterygium from the limestone drip; they see light only as an even, pearl-colored wash that fills the gorge from wall to wall, showing no sun, no sky, only the black ribs of the conduits cutting through the wet green of the fern-terraces.

Sight is a poor sense here anyway. It stops fifty paces out, eaten by the mist. 

What is real is the conduit’s marrow-hum.

Under my palm, the iron sings its long, three-mile song. I know without looking that the brine piston in the sixth bore has reached the top of its stroke; I feel the hydraulic shudder travel through the granite, through the bed of crushed iron ore I sit in, and up into the base of my skull. *Thung.* Then the long, sucking collapse as fifty thousand gallons of mineral oil and salt drop back into the mountain’s gut. *Shhhhh.*

In the quiet that follows, before the rock chimneys exhale, I work.

My hands are broad, square, the nails worn down to thick spades of black horn that do not chip when wedged into seams. With the side of the thumb, I pry a cake of yellow grease from the flange where two massive collars meet. It is warm, the consistency of beeswax mixed with graphite. It does not smell like rot; it smells like friction, like very old stones ground together under heavy oil. I put a lump into my cheek. It does not dissolve quickly. It coats the teeth, numbs the throat, and stops the belly from cramping in the heavy air. 

There is another one near me. Not close—perhaps half a mile down the line, where the conduit arches over the boiling gravel wash. 

I know she is there because the iron carried her weight when she settled. An hour ago, there was a double tap—stone against sleeve—not an alarm, just the dull *tink-tink* that says: *The flange is wet here; the fat is thick.*

I pick up a river pebble from between my knees. It is basalt, water-turned, its pores plugged with iron crust so it weighs twice what it should. I do not swing from the shoulder—there is no room under the pipe. I flick the wrist, letting the stone strike the casing on the up-beat, just as the brine bore bottoms out and the metal is clearest of vibration.

*Clang.*

The sound does not travel through the wet ferns; the mist swallows it ten feet from my face. But through the steel, it runs. It runs down through the moss-beds, through the scree-chokes, through the places where the pipe dives deep through solid granite ribs to avoid the rockfalls. Half a minute passes—the time it takes for the wave to reach the spray of the lower drop and return through the return-flow casing.

Then, through the meat of my thigh pressed against the metal: a single, faint *thump*. 

Heard. Acknowledged. We are still within the skin of the engine.

Above us, the high chimneys begin their five-minute gathering. The air pressure drops three millibars; the hairless skin across my ribs tightens in anticipation. From the canopy, three hundred feet up in the diffused pearl light, comes the soft, heavy rustling of the bronze climbers shifting their grip among the bromeliads. They are moving now, during the ninety seconds of license, their mossy arms reaching from bough to bough before the valve shuts and locks them into stone again.

A rail-bird steps into the space beside my feet. It does not care about me; I have no grease on my exterior that it can pry loose, and I am too wide to kill. It stands on its rusted wire legs, head tilted, one milky eye fixed on the weeping seam above my shoulder. Its feathers are matted into a single sleek mantle, glistening with mineral sweat. It waits for the leak to cough. 

We wait together.

The purge comes: a sound like the sky being torn open from north to south, a clean, dry roar as the chimneys dump their steam into the high troposphere. The white plume billows kilometers above our ceiling, displacing the cloud-cap, driving the heat down into the trough until the valley floor feels like the inside of a closed fist.

The bird strikes. Its beak punches into the joint, comes back with a pale, writhing fat-leech, and swallows it whole with three jerks of its thin throat.

I do not move. Movement during the purge is wasteful; the air is too thin for ninety seconds, sucked upward by the draft of the vents. I draw my knees to my chin, pressing my forehead into the curve of the pipe. The iron is screaming with steam-cavitation, hot enough now that the tallow crust begins to run in thin, golden tears down the rivet lines.

I catch the tears in the cup of my palms. I rub them into the scarred hide of my shins, into the thick padding over my collarbones, into the skin of my face. The grease keeps the lime from eating into the flesh; it seals the pores against the acid-sweat that drips from the ceiling when the clouds roll back.

In this world, there is nothing to build because everything has already been laid down with a permanence that makes stone look temporary. There is nothing to invent because the rhythm is closed; it has no questions to ask of us. We are not its masters, and we are not its vermin. 

We are simply the parts of it that have grown skin.

The roar dies. The valve at the head of the canyon drops with a sound that knocks a quart of water from every fern in the basin at once—*CLUNG-tonk*. 

The general quiet returns, thick, alkaline, and wet. The air settles back down, heavy as lead wool, pressing us into our niches. 

From deep down in the chasm, the bore begins its slow, massive pull. The brine rises. The turnip-skinned things are lifting in the dark, carried up toward the light they will never see. In forty strokes, someone will die of the lung-stiffening down by the gravel bar, or an old one will simply cease to lean against the pipe and slide into the peat. 

We will carry them to the intake bore. We will wait for the brine to drop. We will give the meat back to the pressure that made it.

Until then, the conduit is warm. The yellow fat is deep in the seams. I close my clouded eyes, let the chin rest on the breastbone pad, and wait for the third stroke, feeling the long iron spine of the valley breathe against my ribs.
