It begins not with a view, but with a thickness.

To be here is to forfeit any sense of the horizon. If I am anywhere, I am down in the slack—the three-foot horizon of grease-slicked water, anoxic black butter, and the cold, hydraulic heave of the shelf. Not perched upon the podocarp branches looking out over the pale breaks of gold light, and not walking the contour lines like the moros, whose lives are an arithmetic of files and tonnage. 

I am lower than that, where the light does not matter enough to have a color.

At first, I think I am the rock itself—or rather, the seam. There is a heavy, leaden satisfaction in simply resisting displacement. The water above is heavy; it sits like wet wool upon a chest, three meters of saturated sphagnum pressing down with the weight of an iron roof. To exist here is to understand pressure not as a hazard, but as the only reliable fastener. It keeps the edges from fraying. 

But there is a pulse, so there is blood. Or if not blood, a cold, mineral electrolyte that creeps through narrow channels with the stubbornness of chilled oil.

I am thick-plated, low, and broad. My underside is concave, grooved to fit the rounded granite sills where the subterranean steam-pistons have polished the stone into smooth, wet journals. I do not have legs in the sprinting sense; I have short, hydraulic wedges—paired levers shod in copper-bearing horn that do not stride so much as they wedge, bite, and lever. My snout is broad and spade-turned, armored with a blunted chisel of fluorapatite that tastes the rock before it moves. 

I do not turn around. The concept of turning around belongs to creatures with necks, creatures whose worlds have an *elsewhere*. Here, between the granite sub-floor and the trembling mattress of the bog, there is only *with-the-grain* and *against-the-seam*.

I live in the tolerance between the moving shelf and the bedrock.

When the deep fissures vent—that long, three-second *thump-hiss* that shudders through the cordillera—the peat shelf above me rises an inch. In that single inch of lift, the anoxic mud drinks; water rushes into the void with a flat, greasy slurp. That is when I move. Not far. A half-span. A deliberate shuffle of iron-tipped scutes across the sill, scraping away the pelt of dead, galvanic moss that has discharged its potential, making way for the fresh, ionized slime that weeps from the copper veins.

When the shelf settles back down, the weight returns. It catches me across the dorsal plates—a slab of dark, dense bone impregnated with nodules of cold magnetite. The pressure does not crush; it seats me. It drives my horn-shod wedges deep into the granite grooves, anchoring me against the subterranean drag of the mud. I am a living wedge, a shim driven into the mountain’s joints.

My senses are entirely low-frequency. I do not hear the dripping silence or the click of the iron-grass above; that treble air is another country, dry and thin. What I have instead is a continuous, lateral awareness of the mountain’s battery. 

Through the skin of my belly—soft, slate-blue, smelling of wet coal and paraffin—I read the trickle-charge of the rock. To the left, where the seam of native iron runs deep toward the core, the current is a slow, cold drag, four millivolts that taste like sour tin. To the right, near the vent where the sediment-weavers are pasting their sinter funnels, the ground is hot and positive, humming with an alkaline snap that makes the magnetite along my spine twitch in its sockets. 

I feed on the slack between them. 

I take into my broad, flat maw the extruded slag left behind by the calf-thick worms—those porous, iron-heavy cylinders that form the causeways beneath the bog. My jaws are not hinged for chewing; they are a pair of slow, lateral millstones that grind the slag against the stone floor, fracturing the pellets to get at the sulfur-fixing colonies that bloom inside the pores. I extract the sulfur, the bitumen-grease, the faint galvanic charge held within the metallic grains, and behind me I leave a smooth, compacted track of crushed hematite, leveled and tamped flat by the passing weight of my tail.

Sometimes, overhead, the moros gather. 

I feel them before the shelf heaves. They are too heavy to hide; their broad, vulcanized hooves come down upon the peat like pile-drivers, rhythmic and slow. When they sleep against the sinter chimneys, their low-voltage hum leaks down through three meters of fiber and mud, arriving at my dorsal scutes as a warm, continuous tingle. It matches the stroke of my own low heart. We are plugged into the same rail. If a bull strikes the granite sill above to signal that the western pressure is dropping, the shockwave passes through my own skull like a struck bell, resetting the timing of my lungs.

I do not breathe air often. My lungs are slow bellows lined with dense, wet lamellae that absorb the oxygen suspended in the cold bitumen emulsions. Once every few hours, when the peat shelf lifts on a particularly deep stroke of the piston, I extend a single, telescoping siphon—a jointed, iron-sheathed tube tipped with fine mineral whiskers—up through the root-weave of the dwarf podocarps. It tastes the mist: four degrees, thick with aerosolized coal-tar, deadened and wet. The siphon draws a single, long draft of the cold vapor, condenses the moisture in an internal cistern, and withdraws before the cauter can sweep its leaden flank across the turf.

The cauter does not hunt me. I am too flat, too closely fitted to the granite sill; to take me, it would have to shear through three inches of magnetite-studded horn and break its crushing plates against the bed-rock. We share the same seam without acknowledgment, passing within arm’s reach in the dark mud—the hunter following the electromagnetic wake of the herds above, while I remain below, maintaining the clearance.

That is what I am: the clearance. 

I am the thing that keeps the rock from galling against itself. In an engine this vast and unpeopled—where the pistons are steam and the fuel is the slow, cold rot of mountains—there must be something that sits in the journals, wet with native lubricants, unhurried, blind, and enduring. 

The light breaks above the cloud for an hour, turning the dwarf canopy to pale brass. I do not see it. I feel only a minute shift in the surface tension of the peat, a fraction of a gram less water pressing on my back as the vapor thins. Then the mist returns, cold and clinging, the pressure falls, and the vent below me begins its three-second stroke.

*Rise an inch.* I shove my wedges forward through the black butter.
*Fall an inch.* The stone catches me, fits me, holds me fast. 
*Hiss.* And the mountain turns over again, cold and running true.
