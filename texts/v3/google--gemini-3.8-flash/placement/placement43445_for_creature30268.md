To find oneself here, the first thing to surrender is the habit of being a container. 

You cannot arrive with skin that leaks, or lungs that bargain with ambient gas, or a pulse that insists on its own arbitrary tempo. If you brought an animal heart into this dark, the hydraulic pressure would flatten it into a wet paste against the floor plates before you could take an inventory of your limbs. 

So the arrival is not a stepping-in. It is an alignment of densities.

At first, there is only the sensation of being held in an absolute, hot clamp. Not the violence of a vise, but the unyielding, isothermal embrace of a system that has calculated the displacement of every square millimeter of its volume. You are not cold. You will never be cold again; the heat is a flat, heavy salt that sits on the surfaces of things, neither radiating outward nor absorbing inward, perfectly balanced with the wetness of the air.

Where would I be?

I would not be in the upper baffles. The baffles are too thin, too oily, too dedicated to the mindless siphoning of condensate. Nor would I be in the copper runners miles down, where the brine boils under galvanic load; there is no articulation there, only the brute, blinding flow of electromotive force through wet pith.

I would find myself on the apron. Down on the vast, non-tapering floor of lignified ironwood, where the architecture must constantly answer for its own mass.

And I would not be whole. Not in the sense of a creature that possesses a front and a back, a beginning and an end. 

For a long time, the only identity would be *a differential in turgor*.

I would feel the five chambers of my body—if they can be called a body—not as organs, but as elongated, fibrous cylinders bound with transverse cellulose cords. In the blackness, there is no sense of being long or square, only the rhythm of the valves. 

*Chamber one:* the scalding, clear sap enters under forty atmospheres of pressure through a basal pore. The cell walls, dense with cross-linked lignin, groan silently as they stretch three microns to the left. 

*Chamber two:* the fluid is forced across an internal baffle of microscopic silica plates, stripping the lye of its dissolved lead, growing marginally denser, marginally heavier.

*Chamber three:* the pressure drops. The exhaust fluid, cooled by half a degree, is vented downward through the rasp into the seam.

This is the crawl. It does not feel like walking; it feels like the slow, shuddering advance of a glacier that has been given a direction by the tilt of an iron plate. It takes six days of internal swelling and collapsing to move the length of a forearm. There is no urgency in it, because there is no time. The flues do not measure hours. There is only the continuous, vitreous *skreep* of my belly—a broad, flat plane of petrified bark embedded with microscopic quartz grains—vibrating against the raised seam of the floor.

Underneath me, the joint between two continental plates is weeping linseed-pitch. It is hot, sticky, and acidic, tasting faintly of vinegar and zinc. 

My work—which is not a duty, but simply the only mechanical path available to my tissues—is to lie across that weeping line. My belly licks it up through capillary grooves. The silica rasp shaves down the curled lip of the plate until the metal-hard wood is flush. My internal channels drink the resin, strip its heavy minerals to pack into my own marrow, and extrude the waste behind me as a cold, dense bead of vulcanized pitch that seals the vacuum of the sub-floor forever.

For decades, perhaps, this would be the entire perimeter of existence: the millimeter-by-millimeter reconciliation of two plates. 

You would know the column groves by their low electrical hum, vibrating through your rasp at sixty cycles per second, changing pitch when a plummet two miles above plunges through the flues to relieve an aqueduct. You would know when a condenser baffle cracks in the distant black ceiling, not by sight, but because the ribbons of falling water shift three inches to the north, their wet, heavy slaps landing on the apron with a slightly altered resonance.

You would be content. The system does not allow for anything as wasteful as longing.

But this world is young, and its young materials change.

The change does not happen from without; it happens in the chambers. 

With every mile of seam planed flat, the quartz grains on my belly wear smooth. They do not regenerate; there are no soft buds here, no tender meristems that replace worn cells. The underside becomes slick, vitreous, losing its bite against the ironwood. At the same time, the lead and heavy salts filtered from the pitch have nowhere to go. They precipitate out of the sap. They settle into the walls of the hydraulic chambers, crusting the flexible fibers with crystalline scale.

The swell becomes stiffer. The advance slows from inches a day to inches a month. 

This is where the being becomes fully itself—not in the motion, but in the *stoppage*.

I feel the moment the last valve locks. A heavy slug of calcified sap jams in the third chamber, refusing to siphon. The turgor does not drop; it simply sets, hard as poured concrete. 

The crawl is over. 

There is no panic. Panic requires an adrenal system, a sudden surge of oxygen, an imagination that can conjure an alternative. Here, there are no alternatives. There is only the logic of the engine room.

The rasp sits dead across the seam. It will never move again. 

And then, through the basal pore that once drank sap to drive the turgor, a different fluid begins to back-siphon. The plates beneath me—sensing through simple galvanic contact that the weight above them has become static—relax their margins. The crisp, hair-thin joint opens by half a millimeter. 

From the dark slit comes the pitch: not the thin, clear lubricant of the daily runs, but the deep, structural resin from the primary runners. It rises around my flanks like warm, viscous wax, smelling intensely of bruised wood and boiled oil. It creeps up the squared edges of my body. It enters the micro-capillaries of my skin, soaking into the calcified chambers, filling every microscopic void left by the jammed valves.

I am not being buried; I am being *annexed*.

As the resin cures—hardening over years into a material indistinguishable from the plates themselves—the boundary between the crawler and the floor dissolves. The five chambers are no longer an interior; they are hollows in a curb. My back, once pale and horn-like, hardens under the continuous, warm condensate falling from the baffles, taking on the dark, ribbed enamel of the columns.

The sensation does not end with the cessation of movement. It widens.

While I was moving, I felt only the seam beneath my rasp: six inches of weeping joint, the scrape of silica, the vibration of the nearest runner. 

Now, glued immovable to the continental floor, fused directly into the structural lattice of the basin, the circuit is closed. 

The low-amperage current does not vibrate *against* me; it flows *through* me. I feel the entire grove: the twelve-thousand columns rising parallel into the dark, their ribbing tight and true; the immense, pear-shaped plummets three hundred meters overhead, swinging their tons of living ballast on copper cords; the miles-deep taproots drinking the hot tectonic lye and sending it up under pressures that make the entire continent ring like an anvil.

I would be a curb. A raised, calcified seam-brace at the foot of an unnamed colonnade, six hundred miles from any edge, holding down the corner of a plate that will not shift for ten thousand years. 

Motionless, aseptic, steeped in warm oil and ozone, permanently holding the pressure.
