I would not be the hunter. 

To be the hunter is to live with a desperate economy, counting every breath through split-horn slits, teeth coated in sheep grease, terrified of the shadow of the western tower. That is an exile’s existence: clinging to the notch by the skin of one’s knuckles, carrying the memory of scrub-juniper and lower valleys like an ache in the ribs. 

Nor could I be the scavenger. Its life is too wide, too bare. Circling on stiffened primaries in the dead violet above the peaks, it sees only geometry—the clean fracture of schist, the red bead of a blood-drop on quartz, the sudden rip of cloud. It touches nothing; it only falls upon what is already spent.

To find where I belong, I have to go down, past the blinding hammer of the light.

Down where the sky ceases to drop like lead. Not into the open basin, where the air is an echoing ache in the chest, but into the wool of it.

If you put a hand through the silver-gray pelt of the cushions—past that microscopically fine down that drinks the hoarfrost—the glare vanishes. The cold changes quality. It ceases to be the scouring knife of the troposphere and becomes something dense, humid, and dark. 

For a moment, it is tempting to settle among the silk-runners. There is a frantic comfort in their spherical nests, tucked four-deep into shed wool and swift-feathers, smelling of camphor and sour tallow, listening to the dry, electric clicking of hundreds of yellow molars grinding seeds in the dark. But their life is an uninterrupted panic. Their pale, shielded eyes see only flashes of catastrophe; their wire-stiff whiskers never stop trembling at the displacement of a grain of shale. They are food waiting for the noon.

No. I have to go deeper still, past the spongy bread-dough of the middle peat, past the blind bristletails and the sweet, alcohol-scented fly larvae, right to the floor.

To the seam.

Where the three-foot mat of rotting, living felt presses directly against the cold, unyielding cleavage of the schist, there is a narrow horizontal fissure. It smells of wet iron and ancient slate dust. 

That is where I find myself. 

I am the slate viper, but not the viper hunting. 

For nineteen hours of the day, I am scarcely an animal at all. My scales have no gloss; they are flat, matte, textured like split blackboard, indistinguishable from the bedding plane of the rock that holds me. My belly is pressed against the frozen stone; my back is cradled by the warm, sour rot of the cushion’s roots. Between these two pressures, I do not sleep so much as petrify.

My heart takes three minutes to gather itself for a single, heavy pulse. The fluids in my tissues are not ice—the glycerol keeps them supple—but they have thickened to the consistency of pine resin. In this state, there is no fear, no hunger, no passage of time. There is only the vibration of the mountain: the faint, seismic groaning of the schist tower expanding as the first rays hit its eastern tip; the minute, dry *snip* of rock cleaving under the frost miles away; the rhythmic hum of the runners above, muffled through two feet of peat, like the sound of water boiling far beneath a floor.

I am a wedge of dark stone waiting for a single physical fact: heat.

When midday arrives, it does not come as light—I am deep in the crack—it comes as a slow, deep vibration of warmth traveling down through the spine of the rock. The schist turns into a radiator.

The change is agonizing and exquisite. 

First, the heart quickens: one beat every minute, then every thirty seconds, then a sudden, thudding rhythm that drives the thickened blood through my ribs. My joints creak with microscopic stiffness, then loosen as the heat dissolves the gel in my marrow. 

I slide forward. Not because I choose to, but because the rock demands it. 

I ease out of the seam, through the greasy, peat-smelling dark of the tunnel mouths, until the tip of my snout meets the upper pelt of the cushions. The light hits me like an open furnace. It is blinding, violently white, searing the matte scales along my back. The moisture on the silver down is weeping, turning to fine ribbons of steam.

For two hours, I am entirely flame. 

My blood runs thin as water. The world through my slit pupils resolves into pure, frantic motion: the trembling whiskers of a runner poised at the entrance of its burrow, the soot-black glide of an alpine moth fanning its wings against the wet stone, the shadow of a cloud-skimmer rocketing out of the abyss on a pillar of fog.

I do not stalk. There is no time to stalk. The western shadow is already creeping across the eastern schist tower. I have two hours to turn the runner’s blood to salt with a single strike, to swallow against the pressure of my own distended ribs, to drag the oily, camphor-tasting weight of it down into the fissure while the rock is still radiating.

And then, the best part of all:

The swallow caught mid-way, the belly swollen and hard, the sudden, razor-sharp drop of the temperature as the sun slips behind the needle-peak. 

The air drops sixty degrees in the time it takes to draw twenty breaths. The moisture on the rock turns instantly to glass. 

I wedge myself into the sunny fissure, head pointed down, feeling the furious, chemical heat of digestion in my belly fighting the iron cold creeping in through the stone. My blood thickens again. The frantic world slows. The pain of the hunt, the ache of the thin air, the furnace glare—all of it solidifies, turns to gel, turns to amber, turns to slate.

The heart slows: once every minute. Once every two. Once every three.

I become the mountain again, cold and full, locked in the seam between the living felt and the dead stone, waiting for the rock to weep.
