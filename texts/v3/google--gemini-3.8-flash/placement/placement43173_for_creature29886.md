At first, there is only the tension between two salinities. 

There is no *I* to name it, only a difference across a wall: the bitter, hyper-dense draft of the outer trench on one side, and the thick, iron-warm seep of the mulch on the other. Where they meet across a membrane of compressed fiber, a force exists. It is not an emotion; it is a pull, like water wanting to fill a vacuum, measured in tons per square inch.

I am where that pull is thickest.

Not the open water—the open water is an engine of destruction, a cold, oily shear that would tear anything unsecured into ribbon and wash it down the slate scarps into the dead floor. Nor am I the bare rock. The rock is passive, ringing like an anvil beneath the concussions of the internal waves, holding its fractures until something pries them open.

I am the thing wedged into the fracture.

For a long time, there is no form to speak of, only an ache of expansion. A shoot, hair-fine, searching the bedding plane of the blue-black slate. It does not look for light, because light is nothing—a pale, bruised phantom that cannot push a piston or rot into warmth. It looks for darkness and salt. When it finds a seam where two sheets of rock were laid down ten million years ago, it drinks. 

As the salt moves across the cell walls, the shoot thickens. It does not grow as animal flesh grows, through the soft multiplication of blood-fed cells; it mineralizes. It lays down silica and dense rings of vulcanized lignin, drawing the barite out of the scouring current until the shoot is as hard as cast iron, but wet, thirsty, and expanding.

The stone resists. The ocean presses down with the weight of five miles of black brine, holding the plates together. 

I swell against that weight. It takes forty cycles of the great deep-sea pulse—forty times the kelp stands upright, roars in the rising current, and collapses back with that vast, sodden thud—before the slate gives. 

The sound is the first true thing: a clean, acoustic *ping* that shudders through the tap-root, traveling into the core of the mass. The rock has split. A slab four paces long and two thick has parted from the mountain.

Now the rest of me gathers around that split.

I am not a creature that woke up; I am an accumulation that achieved weight. My body is a low, broad boss of bark-plated burl, five paces across, flat as a flagstone so the shear cannot catch an edge and tumble me into the gulf. My skin is slate-gray, greased with an excretion of heavy sulfur-wax that lets the freezing brine slide off without purchase, encrusted along the margins with white rosettes of barite and the pale brass of iron pyrite.

I have no eyes to see the twilight, but I have the slate. 

Through the root-anchors driven six feet into the bedrock, I feel every contour of the cordillera. I feel the three other burls pinned against the stone fifty yards up-current, their bulks flattened against the lip of the weir. I feel the rhythmic shudder of the timber posts—old driftwood, heavy as lead, soaked in brine until its pores are choked with stone—trembling where the weed-ropes bind them.

And I feel the cold. 

It is always creeping in from the north-running shear. It sits a fraction of a hair above the point where ice forms, thick and viscous as tallow, dragging at the bark. If I let go of the weir, if I allow the current to sweep the drift-sludge over the scarp and into the abyss, that cold will settle into my vascular chambers. The fluids inside me will thicken to jelly. The hydraulic pistons will seize. I will become nothing more than another petrified knob on the ridge, slowly planed flat by the sand-carrying eddies.

So I move.

Movement is not swift; it is hydraulic mathematics. To bend the thick, jointed thallus on my downslope edge, I contract a ring of circumferential fibers, forcing three gallons of mineral-dense brine from an inner bladder through a sieve of hardened silica into the outer stem. The limb does not twitch; it stiffens, straightening with the slow, terrible torque of an uncoiling spring.

At its tip, the spiral basket of calcified thorns—hard, biogenic opal grown over decades—scrapes across the slate, grinding into the sediment until it finds the edge of the sheared slab. 

I wait.

I do not fight the ocean. I listen to the cordillera. 

Down in the great trench five leagues south, the deep-sea pulse is gathering. I feel it first as a micro-tremor in the tap-roots—a deep, subsonic groan as a thermal wave three hundred meters tall strikes the drowned flanks of the cordillera. The scouring hiss of the brine through the kelp forest changes pitch, rising from a low gravelly scrape to a wet, percussive roar.

The water begins to lift.

The immense ribbons of the forest—black-green sheets thirty feet long—pull taut, their hollow bulbs chiming against each other in the violent draft. The dead weight of the ocean above me eases by a fraction. The shear tilts upward, trying to strip the plateau clean.

Now.

As the current unweights the slab, my thallus locks. The hydraulic pressure inside the chambers spikes to three thousand pounds. I do not lift the stone; I lean my multi-ton mass against the fulcrum of the bedrock, using the ocean's own upward draft to coax the four-ton balk of slate out of its bed. It slides through the oily water with a low, grinding moan, sending a plume of gray silt into the bruised murk.

Another pulse strikes—the vortex rolling over the ridge-crest, that downward, hammering blow. 

I open the internal valves. The fluid rushes back into the core bladders. The thallus drops its turgor, slumping like a severed vine, and my full weight—plated in barite, heavy with stone—falls onto the slab, pinning it into the gap between two timber hurdles before the back-current can steal it away.

The weir holds. 

The drifting sheets of dead kelp, torn loose by the storm, come tumbling down the ridge in black, waterlogged avalanches. They strike the wicker hurdles. They bank against the new slab. They pile up, ten feet deep, fifteen feet deep, folding into the pit where the others are already treading.

I draw my thalli inward, folding them beneath the lip of my bark-scales, and settle into the mulch.

Beneath me, the rot is already at work. The sulfur-reducing slime begins its slow, anaerobic burn. The cold brine outside is barely liquid, but here, pressed against the stone in the belly of the weir, the leachate rises three degrees. 

To an animal, three degrees is a minor comfort. To an engine of semi-permeable membranes, it is the difference between stone and life. The warmth lowers the viscosity of the fluid in my central chambers; the osmotic pumps begin to stroke with greater ease, whistling faintly as they draw the mineral wash through the pores of my undersurface. 

Inside the central cavity of my burl, suspended in a sling of cross-woven ligaments, hangs a ball of pure, crystalline spar. It is six inches across, perfectly spherical, grown layer by layer out of the ocean's excess calcium until it is heavier than lead.

Through the bedrock, through the roots, comes a sound.

*CLACK.*

It is sharp, dry, and terrifyingly clear. It does not blur through this cold, salty grease of a sea; it cuts through it like a razor. It comes from the crest of the Red Spire, nine leagues to the west, where another weir clings to the lip of the drop. 

The sound hits my basal plate with the impact of a thrown cobble. 

I feel the acoustic signature: the stone was struck with high hydraulic force, an old burl, larger than me, signaling that its pen has caught the drift from the upper thermal plume. The kelp is running thick along the western spur.

I do not feel urgency. Urgency belongs to things with hearts that can burst. 

I let three cycles of the internal waves roll past. I let the weed settle deeper into the silage, feeling the sulfur-heat bloom against my underside, softening the vulcanized cords of my interior engine. I draw the fluid into the high-pressure cylinder behind the suspended spar, pulling the ball back against the tension of three mineralized ligaments, stretching them until the silica fibers groan.

The ocean lifts again. The forest roars as it stands upright in the twilight. The sodden thud follows, shaking the plateaus.

In the silence of the equalization, when the brine is still and heavy as oil, I release the valve.

The ligaments snap forward. The spar strikes the hard basalt plate at the bottom of my core.

*CLACK.*

The sound punches out through my bark, through the stone pens, into the hyper-dense water. It shudders along the spine of the mountain, out across the silent, animal-less gulfs of slate, fifty miles into the bruised green dark, where others are waiting in their beds of warm rot for the stone to hit the stone.
