Not in the flumes. The flumes are for the heavy, blind silicate things that have surrendered the sky to become millstones, grinding against the boiling sluice. 

And not out on the naked, lunging cantilevers—not at the tips, where the Turnbuckles stretch their yellow-white bellies until their fibers weep vinegar and tear. To be a Turnbuckle is to be only an argument between the gale and the green wood, an argument that the gale almost always wins.

I would be deeper in. 

I find myself at the knuckle. 

There are places where three monumental arches spring from a single hexagonal pier, ten thousand feet above the sulfur floor. It is a junction the size of a public square, but tilted at forty degrees, wet as a slaughtered flank, and shuddering with such violence that loose wood does not bounce—it atomizes into fuzz. Here, the wood is not green shoot nor brittle dead-heart; it is the compressed, amber-dense lignum of the middle growth, under three million pounds of constant, twisting shear. 

This is where the structure nearly tears its own hip from its socket. And this is where I am wedged.

At first, I look like a slump of dried gum, or a blister in the wood that has cooled and wrinkled. I am long—not twelve feet like the Joiners who run the high trusses, but thicker, compacted by decades of surviving the draft. My skin is not merely glazed with wax; it has taken in the dissolved silicates of the spray until it has the grey-green hardness of horn, slick to the touch, impenetrable to the scald of the localized squalls that smash into the knuckle every eight minutes. 

I have no need for eyes. The white glare of this sky would only burn them to salt, and sight is too slow for what happens here. My skull is long and sloping, a solid wedge of bone and fibrous cartilage, ribbed like a melon. Along the ridges run thousands of stiff, translucent vibrissae—not soft hairs, but bristles like split quill-ends, rooted directly into the cranial plate. 

Through them, I do not hear the wind; I *read the grain*.

To me, the entire three-mile arch to the north does not exist as an image. It exists as an octave. When the wind drives hard against its flank, the tension sings through the wood at a high, clean B-flat. If the B-flat sharpens, climbing toward a scream, the fibers are drawing tight, parting cell by cellular cell. If it drops to a thick, muddy thrum, the truss has flooded with boiling sap, grown too heavy for its cantilever, and is beginning to droop toward the cavitation point.

I am a mass damper. A tuner. A creature that lives in the crotch of the joint to keep the joint from thinking about breaking.

My four limbs are not long and spindly like the runners'. They are short, hydraulic pistons of corded, pale muscle, ending in broad, spade-like pads of keratin that are slotted directly into the deep, natural fissures of the lignum. I don't hold the wood; I *am* a shim. I drive myself between the tensioned hawsers of the arch-root and the flank of the vertical pier. When the arch heaves upward in an updraft, my belly flattens, my internal fluids dumping into broad lateral sacks beneath my ribs, taking the shock so the timber doesn't slam against its own mortise. When the draft drops and thousands of tons of wet green wood fall into the void, I swell, driving my mass into the gap with the wet, piston-crack of compressed gristle, catching the fall before the shear can shear off the heads of the growth-pins.

It is work that never pauses for a heartbeat. If I were to relax, the knuckle would fret. Wood rubbing wood at this scale does not make sawdust; the boiling heat and friction turn the lignum into steam-scorched slurry, the seating fails, and the whole three-thousand-foot span leaps out of its socket to plunge through the sulfur floor.

Around me, the Joiners come and go. 

They do not look at me—they have no eyes either—but they feel me through their feet. They come down from the whistling, frantic growth-fronts like pale spiders, smelling of the caustic, chewed mulch in their secondary gullets. When a seam near my head begins to hiss—that terrible, thin whistle of sap vaporizing into a microscopic split—they don't hesitate. They swarm across my back. Their prehensile, four-clawed feet click across my hardened skin without ceremony; they use my shoulders as an anvil, bracing their heels against my ribs while they strike into the split with their calcified palms, driving wedges of fresh iron-wood until the seam is choked.

I tolerate them the way an ancient root tolerates the ants that clear its bark. Sometimes, when the torque is so monstrous that my own hide begins to heat and smoke against the wood, a runner will lean down, hook its long, baffled snout near the acoustic crest of my skull, and tap.

*Tap-tap-t-t-tap.*

A question in the lignum: *How deep does the rot go? Is the heart-wood weeping?*

I do not have hands to tap back. Instead, I shift the fluid in my left groin-sack. A heavy, rhythmic thump shudders out from my hip, through the horn-pad, straight into the marrow of the pier. 

*Hold. Hold. The north stay is stretching. Bring pulp.*

They feel it through their soles, wheel around with that terrifying, double-jointed agility, and are gone into the steam, their long forms swallowed by the howling blue chasm before the mist can settle.

In the deepest lull of the wind—which is never quiet, only the roar of an iron furnace dialed low—I can feel the other things. I can feel the Scald-Crabs five hundred feet above me, their silicate picks *tink-tink-tinking* against the flume beds as they fight the boiling river, their small, hard lives locked in an endless, mineral crawl against the current. I can feel the Turnbuckles out in the empty air, shuddering like over-strung violins, their torn ends thrashing against the white sky whenever a squall clips them.

And below, through the six-foot-thick sole of compressed lignum beneath my belly, I feel the planet we never see. 

Not stone. Not dirt. Just the monstrous, slow pump of the roots, miles down in the sulfur-boil, shoving the scalding broth up into this white, thin air with the force of an uncoiling spring. It is a blind, furious thing, this architecture. It doesn't know it is making stairs that lead nowhere. It doesn't know it is building courtyards that have no roofs. It only knows how to drink from the abyss and spit green wood into the gale until the wood breaks, and then spit more wood to catch the break.

I will not leave this knuckle. Eventually, the silicates in the weeping water will coat me completely. The wax of my hide will mingle with the caulking gum the runners drop from their mouths. My joints will stiffen; my vibrissae will snap off one by one in the grit of the localized squalls; my spades will fuse with the braided rushes of the pier-deck. 

The timber will grow over my shoulders, translucent and lime-green at first, then darkening to raw amber, burying me inside the hinge. 

And that is well. When I am completely wood, I will be the densest shim in the tower. The arch will rest on my calcified spine, the sap will boil through the pores where my ribs used to be, and when the wind hits the three-mile span with the weight of an unmade sea, I will not sing B-flat. 

I will hold the note until the sky goes dark.
