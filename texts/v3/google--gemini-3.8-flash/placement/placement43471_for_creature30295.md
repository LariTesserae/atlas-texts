It begins with the clearance.

Not the space itself, which is negligible, but the sensation of a dimension that exists only intermittently. When the thirty-ton horizontal trunk—the one ribbed like an undershot culvert—completes its lateral throw to the north, it leaves behind an absence. For seven-tenths of a second, before the counter-tendril heaves and the peat beneath it rebounds like bruised rubber, there is a wedge of cold air four fingers wide and nine paces long.

I am not the shim. The shim is too wide, too hungry, too wet with its own tallow; it belongs to the contact face, licking the friction-scum off the gray boxwood. 

Nor am I the auger sleeping in its tallow-plug within the burl, nor the dead things anchored against the sky. 

If I have an outline, it is defined by the stroke. 

At first, there is only the waiting in the damp felt of the peat wall, drawn down into a seam so narrow that the slate-blue light cannot enter. Down here, the smell is of cold tannin, shaved turnip, and that faint, alkaline bite that rises when wet lignin is subjected to two thousand pounds of shear per square inch. You do not breathe this air so much as let it settle on your membranes like hoarfrost on grease. 

Then the timber begins to draw back.

The signal does not come through the ears—the deadening fleece around the roots kills any wave that might travel through gas—but as a sudden, sharp drainage of weight from the soles. The compressed moss beneath me sighs, expanding three millimeters upward with a soft, spongy suck. 

That is the clearance opening. That is the pocket.

I roll into it.

I do not have limbs that reach or kick; I have ribs—scores of them, fine as whaletooth, curved like the hoops of a dry cask, overlaid with a dry, parchment-like skin that does not sweat. If I were wet, the bark would catch me. If I were soft, the liverwort-grease would adhere and draw me under the trailing lip of the beam as it returns. I must remain as dry and smooth as a beach-pebble that has spent a millennium in a glacier’s belly.

Inside the clearance, the cold is distinct. It is not the mountain’s dry, scouring gale, nor the humid greenhouse-fever of the lower chutes where the sump-feeders wallow in their green mash. It is a dead, preserved cold—the air of an iron safe opened in an icefield. 

In the brief window of the dwell, while the flume has paused at the end of its stroke and the return-valves deep in the root-knots are only just beginning to hiss shut their internal ports, I feed.

I do not eat the meal. I do not tap the bitter sap-pipes. 

Between the moving trunk and the stationary shelf of petrified peat, the machine leaves something else behind: *splinters*. Not the coarse fragments that the fulcrum-beetles break into when they shatter the gears, but threads—hairs of heartwood, six feet long, sheared away by the lateral draw, fine as horsehair and stiffened with silica. They hang in the newly opened draft like the strings of a dismantled harp, vibrating with the residual torque of the wood.

My mouth is not a jaw; it is an open notch, lined with transverse plates of gray slate-colored enamel. I move along the line of the clearance like a shuttle through a loom, collecting the tensioned hairs. When they hit the plates, they snap under their own internal strain with a dry, muted *tink* that only my own skeleton can register. They do not digest into blood; they pack into the long, segmented hopper behind my ribs, dry and aromatic, aligning themselves into a core of dense, fibrous straw that keeps my skin taut against the rising pressure.

I am becoming heavier. I am becoming more like the floor.

Three-tenths of a second remain.

The air in the pocket changes. The cold becomes hard, pressed thin by the wall of wood that has already begun its silent lunge back down the terrace. 

To leave the pocket would be a mistake; the fleece on either side is too thick, too sluggish to accept a body moving at speed. Instead, I let the shape of the stroke do the work. 

My ribs do not collapse horizontally like the shims, which spread like oil on a pond. They fold rearward, shingling over one another like the scales of a spruce cone, until my diameter is no greater than the groove worn into the peat by an ancient, fossilized knot. The wood arrives.

The iron flume sweeps over. 

The weight is absolute. It is not an attack; it is an environment. For two seconds, the entire mass of the ridge—the horizontal forest, the water-trains, the blind gears grinding their green meal—is resting upon the three inches of dry horn that protects my spine. The cold oil on the bottom of the log touches the dorsal ridge; it tastes of winter water and ancient iron-wash. 

Through the plate of my skull, I feel the whole machine speaking in its true dialect: not the manic industry that the sky sees, but the slow, mineral settle of an alpine world that is trying, through millions of frantic, soundless strokes, to grind itself down to an absolute plane. Every gall that heaves, every tendril that winches a burl toward the drop, is only trying to find an angle of repose that the cold will allow. 

The timber passes. The horn un-shingles with a faint, dry rattle, like dead sedge in the wind.

The pocket is gone. I am back in the compression, pressed between the peat and the gray cambium, waiting for the return-cycle to clear the draft once more.

Am I alone here? 

No. Along the seam, eight paces to the south, where the ribbed bark has gouged a slightly deeper trench in the peat, I feel the dry, rhythmic clicking of another set of ribs folding down. Another hopper full of spruce-thread. Another throat of slate plates. 

We do not breed; there is no clearance large enough for two of us to meet without being sheared into paste by the return-stroke. Perhaps we were never born at all, but simply peeled off the underside of the first flume when the basin first froze and began to cycle its levers against the slate-blue sky.

The sap surges in the conduits below. The liverworts weep their cold grease. 

A shadow falls across the dead wood overhead—a beetle has let go of its frost-crusted branch, plummeting downward into the beard-lichen to seek its jam. 

I feel the shock of its landing five hundred yards away: a tiny, hard pinprick against the mountain’s drum. Then a lever snaps. The stroke shortens by an inch. 

The clearance narrows. 

I take the difference into my bones, flatten three ribs to compensate, and wait for the cold to draw back again.
