At first, there is only the difference between what can be crushed and what can only be ground down.

The sound does not come into me through the ears; those were sealed with boiled sumac gum when I was small enough to fit inside a hollow reed-sheaf, until the drumheads withered into dry parchment plates. I feel the city instead in the small of the back, in the hollow behind the collarbones, and through the heel-pads, where the calluses have grown an inch thick, greasy and pale as salted pork. 

The rhythm is never steady. It is a syncopated, stuttering lurch: *thud-drag, thud-drag*, as the ironwood tiles of the lower avenues creep over the subterranean root-knots. Every forty beats of my heart, a jolt passes through my pelvic girdle that means a lintel three levels down has sheared off its anchors, the sap-head behind it spitting six hundred cubits of hot, white froth into an empty vestibule.

I am not standing on a floor. I am wedged between two things that want to become one thing.

Above me is a transverse arch of pale ash, thick as a grain barge, curving down with all the blind, hydraulic malice of forty atmospheres. Below me is the head of an upright pillar—a squarish, fluted thing that grew up out of the pavement four days ago and is already buckling at the waist, weeping clear, mint-smelling rosin from every pore. They are twelve inches apart. By dawn tomorrow, if the grain keeps its course, they will have fused into a solid, seamless elbow of heartwood sixty spans broad, leaving nothing between them but a yellow seam like a surgical scar.

My legs are drawn up to my chin. My back is braced against the descending ash; the soles of my broad, bowed feet are planted against the rising pillar. 

Between my knees I hold the cobble.

It is nine pounds of basalt, dark and wet-looking, though it is dry to the touch. It has no grain. It has no vascular bundles. It does not drink, it does not weep, and when the sap-vents hiss their scalding vapor into this crook, the basalt stays cold for three hundred breaths before it even begins to take the room’s fever. It was tumbled smooth in some water that ran under an open sky ten thousand years before the first banyan-spire broke the crust of the basin. 

The ash groans. A thread of sap—hot as fresh mutton tallow and bitter as gall—drips onto my shoulder. It does not burn me; my skin has been cured in elder-smoke and rubbed with tallow from the dead calkers until it has the dull, leaden slip of an old boot. I let the milk run down my arm. It crusts there into a dry, sugary crumb that I will scrape off with my teeth once the light turns chartreuse.

I am what the runners call a *ballast-man*, though there is no ship to steady. We are the ones who stay in the hinge.

The families have already cleared out of this gallery. Two hours ago, when the sound of the pillar’s core changed from a dry hum to a thin, bird-like whistling, they gathered their bladder-skins and their hemp-slings, thumped their mallets three times against the osier partition to tell me the western conveyor was clearing, and dropped down into the slipstream of the lower avenues. Their children went like grease down an auger-hole, their double-jointed shins folding flat against their thighs, dropping forty spans into the wisteria canopies without a rattle.

They have left me the room. It is three yards wide, smelling of fresh cucumber, bruised mint, and the hot, oily iron of the floorboards. In another hour, it will be the size of a dog-kennel. In three, a seam.

I wait until the ash arch gives a sudden, wet *shudder*—the unmistakable lurch of a cambial layer giving up its water—and then I drive the basalt home.

I do not use a mallet. A mallet bounces, and a bounce in this noise will cost you a thumb. I use the heel of my left foot, driving the stone into the narrow V where the grain of the pillar splits to receive the arch. 

The basalt bites. 

It does not take the sap. It does not yield a hair. The wood hits it with a sound like a copper kettle struck by an iron rod—a high, flat *clink* that rings straight through the bone of my ankle and rattles my teeth in their gums. Around the stone, the grain goes wild. Denied its clean, self-grafting weld, the wood begins to twist in an agony of frustrated speed; it boils outward in grotesque, tumorous knuckles, spitting splinters of yellow flint-wood that hiss through the hot air like hornets. One of them cuts my cheek, open to the bone. I do not wipe the blood. The alkaline sweat pouring from the broadleaves above catches it, curdles it, and washes it down my neck in pink strings.

Now the pocket is jammed. 

The room will not close tonight. The ash will continue to swell, the pillar will bulge until its sides split like an overcooked sausage, but they cannot kiss. In the lee of the stone, there is a hollow six feet long and two feet deep: a dead pocket where the air does not move, where the floor-runners cannot crawl because the pressure has sheared their feeder-tips.

I settle down into the hollow. I pull my knees to my chest. 

From here, through a tear in the wicker ceiling where a Strider has recently fished out a grub, I can look across the great trough of the Seventh Avenue. 

The sun is directly overhead. The sky through the canopy is the color of bile, blazing with an unbearable, clean yellow light that makes the moving roofs look like sheets of oiled horn. Two miles out, an ironwood avenue is grinding into an arcade of ninety-span banyans. Even through my gum-plugs, the sound is like the earth’s guts being dragged across broken glass: a slow, rhythmic, mountain-scale mastication. I can see the white steam rising from the contact-line—tons of cambial water flashing into vapor as the wood combusts from the sheer fury of its own friction. 

A Strider moves along the lip of the buckling arcade. It looks no thicker than an insect’s leg from here, its great, fan-shaped sounding-skull tilted downward, listening to the death of the lower tiers. It takes four long, stilt-steps—*one, two, three, four*—its caustic-secreting spurs leaving black, smoking pockmarks that stay bare for three heartbeats before the green runners lick them smooth again. It does not look at the city. It does not look at the sun. It simply balances on the razor-edge of the catastrophe, its bone-plate vibrating with the news of every split between here and the horizon.

I do not envy it its legs. 

To run like that, never to set the flat of the heel down, never to let the weight settle into a thing that does not give—it must feel like being made of dry straw, constantly waiting for the spark. 

Here, in the hinge, it is different. 

I reach out my hand and lay my bare palm against the basalt. It is already warming, drinking the fever of the wood that writhes against it. The stone does not belong here. It was born in the deep bottom, in the quiet gravels where the roots have not yet reached, where things are finished, cold, and dead. Every stone we hold in the city has been carried up through the siphon-wells by six generations of men whose lungs rotted from the pressure, passed from hand to hand like dry bread, stolen, fought over, buried in the fat of dead calkers to keep the grain from carrying it off.

A man who has no stone must run until his knees turn to water. A man with one stone can sleep for three hours. A man with three stones can have a wife, for three nights, until the wedges are spat out by an explosion of the lower heartwood and the floor-tiles sweep their bed out into the thoroughfare.

I close my eyes. I can feel the pillar under my calves trying to turn my skin into bark. It sends out microscopic, hair-like suckers that tickle the grease on my thighs, searching for a pore, a grain, a drop of salt to seize upon and fold into its imperial column. 

I let them touch. Then I shift my weight—just half an inch—grinding the skin against the rough wood, breaking the tiny green threads before they can drink. 

*Not yet,* I tell the wood, through the heel, through the tailbone, through the flat of the smoked hide. *Not this dawn.* 

The basalt holds the gap open by the width of a hand. Outside, the Seventh Avenue rolls forward six inches toward the collision, carrying a thousand empty rooms into the sun, screaming as it goes; but here, in the little wedge of darkness behind the stone, there is half an afternoon of stillness, bitter sap, and the cold, unyielding weight of something that refuses to grow.
