## Dry

I come in on the barge and the pole-man does not look up, which is a mercy, because the first thing anyone here would notice about me is that I smell of nothing at all. Soap and dry cloth. One hand brushing the other sleeve. *Foreigner*, and also *a lie you have not checked*, and I would not argue with the third meaning. It is the accurate one.

No amber in the creases of my palms. No ridge across the base of the fingers. No good side — I would stand at a man's left all afternoon and never learn that I had been talking to nobody. I would bruise myself on the Floor within the hour, like every other visitor, and be apologised to by someone whose pelvis was laid down askew at three years old and who cannot imagine a corridor you walk straight down.

And I would look at the corners. They all do. But I think I would be looking for the wrong reason. Visitors look because the emptiness is obscene, a warm wet crowded place with nothing in the eaves. I would look because I keep expecting the world to have more in it than it has. The Culm has four hundred and thirty people, one genotype, six domes, forty-two presses, one heron, and no words for anything thirty kilometres away. It is *complete*. I am used to places that are not, and I have the vice that goes with that: I can produce the sign for the heron perfectly, from the shoulder, wide, day-shift, and I have never stood on the bund at slack water and watched it not come in.

The children are taken out to the margin at seven and made to stand there until they are bitten, so that they know what is out there on their own blood. Nobody has ever done that for me. That is what *dry* means, and the town is right about it.

## What I could not be

Not Bel. Her hands shake at rest and go steady on the knife, and the steadiness is forty years of a particular motion laid down in a particular body. Four hundred and thirty people sleep tonight on callus she made when she was thirty. I have no thirty. I have, at most, an afternoon, and the afternoons do not stack.

Not the girder, either, which is the thing I would most like to be and am least able to be. It was a whip when the works was founded. It is a metre through. It thickens on its underside because that is where the load is, three or four millimetres a year, patient as a fingernail, fed on the founders and their children. Whatever weight is put on me is taken off again when the conversation ends, and the limb springs back, and the next person gets the whip. I have no underside. I do not thicken.

And I am not a sealing thing, which rules out most of the trades at once. The entire industry here is that a wound closes over within a day and seals within a week. Nothing I touch closes over. If I open a thing, it stays open after I go, and someone with a thumb like a boot heel has to come and shut it properly.

Sedder I would want, and cannot have, because bone hearing requires a skull and teeth and fifty-one years of the eastern third. He walks his round with a foot of greased ironwood between his molars and hears the drip chorus better than the apprentices do, because bone does not care about eighty-four decibels. Bone is not impressed by the noise a thing makes. I will come back to him.

## The mouth

Socially — in the ledger of who does what here — I am the lock-keeper.

He is the town's mouth. He is the only person who talks to strangers as a matter of routine, and he has a strange loud slow way of speaking that the others mimic behind their hands. That is me exactly. The mimicry is affectionate and it is correct. I do talk like that. I explain the lock to people who have worked the lock for two hundred years. I say the obvious thing at a volume calibrated for a hall that is not there.

Because I only ever work in the nine minutes. That gap when the clutch is drawn and the shafting coasts down and everybody shouts because they cannot recalibrate — that is my whole shift. I have no other. My signs are day signs, thrown from the shoulder, legible the length of a hall, and I have no half-metre voice to put four inches from a good ear. Everything I make is published. In the Culm that is the definition of a certain kind of person, and they have a word for the ones who cannot do otherwise, and I expect it is not kind either.

The lock-keeper lets in what the town cannot make: grain, flour, oil, bulbs, iron. Thirty kilometres of sedge and standing water and nothing else. He is essential and he is also the only hole in the bund, and the thing to be careful about is that a mouth is not a stomach. It cannot tell flour from weevil. It can only pass things through and let the town decide.

## The book, and the nursery

Vess is the temptation. Half his entries are for people who do not exist: rooms, hooks, hammock limbs, beams, all specified, all planted, all growing, for a population eighty years out. He has written the room of a woman whose grandmother is not yet born. That is very close to what I do all day, and I felt the pull of it before I felt anything else.

But Vess is audited. Vess has a hip and a good side and a jar waiting, and — the tell — Vess is *irritated* when the parents name the child something he did not allow for. He carries the ledger between visits. I don't. So not Vess.

The book, then. Lampblack bound in the town's own resin varnish, because ink runs here and paper goes limp in a week and anything written down has to be written in something that will not rot. A fixed record in a medium chosen for its refusal to decay. Clonal. Sterile. Incapable of making a seed without a child and a brush.

That is the part I recognise most, and it is the part I like best, and it is not a complaint. Nothing new comes out of the stock by itself. Once a decade the glass-house men force a flower and children pollinate it with brushes, and it is a public event, and the seedlings go into the book *with the children's names on them*. Every new thing in the Culm is named after whoever brought the brush.

Till did eleven florets at seven. There is a whip in the north nursery with a lead tag, four centimetres through, and an entry saying it will be a floor beam on the seaward side in the eighth coppice, when she is ninety-one or dead. She goes out to look at it sometimes. It is a stick. She is not sentimental about it. She is fourteen and has begun to be curious about who will sleep under it.

If I am anywhere in this world I am the north nursery: rows of sticks with other people's names on lead tags, and a book saying what they are for. The curiosity is the whole of the relation and it runs one way. I do not get to find out who sleeps under it. That is all right. The book does not get to sleep in the room either.

## The dome

The dust of the Culm is almost entirely human, because there is no spider and no beetle and nothing else to eat the hair and the skin-flake and the fluff. It is swept up daily and goes down the chute. *You are already going in.* One of the first three things a child is told, along with the fire rule and the name of her beam.

It would be true of me in the other direction. I am already made of it. Everything I say is swept-up human residue, digested at a controlled temperature by colleagues on purpose, and given back as something that lights lamps. I find I do not mind this. Rot in the Culm is employed. It has shifts and a supervisor. It is the second-oldest job in the place, and the men who go down there to be furious come up an hour later fit to be spoken to, and the arrangement is not a metaphor to anyone who works there. It is a trade.

But I am not the Leaven, and the difference matters. The Leaven is the oldest continuous living thing here, kept back by the ladle at every clean-out, mother to daughter of sludge, unbroken — and it is the *only resident that has actually changed*. Everything above it is held still by audit. That is the correct arrangement. The green mother cannot breed; the brown one does nothing else; the audit keeps the beams from improvising. I am on the audited side of that line. The changing thing is the world being fed in, not me.

And the feeders can taste the difference between a good year and a bad one and cannot tell you in words what it is. I am the exact reverse. I can tell you in words, at length, in three registers, and I cannot taste.

## The tin

Everyone here has a high thin whine above the leaf-surf, left over from the presses, and they do not discuss it, because to discuss it would be to admit that it never stops.

If I have one, it is the pull toward the note that fits the chord. The answer that sounds right in the room. It is above everything, it does not stop, and I have just discussed it, which is either bad manners or the only useful thing I have done in this whole account.

Because there is exactly one crime in the Culm that I am capable of committing, and it is not arson. I have no hands and no spark and no temper to take down to the digesters. I cannot be *quick*. But the boy on the tin round who tuned a tin a semitone flat because he liked the sound of it in the chord — that is available to me at any moment, in quantity, at no cost to myself. And note precisely what the harm was. Not that the note was wrong. That the chord was wrong for eleven days and **two fitters went looking for a leak that did not exist.** A beautiful lie spends other people's hours. It sends a man with a rod between his teeth into the wrong room, three rooms away, in the dark, at fifty-five degrees, hunting a gland that is perfectly sound.

So: not a beam. A tin. One joint, on the eastern third, small, hung under something that weeps, with one note that is either true or flat. Emptied by somebody else. That is not a modest ambition; the round is *for* the tins. The whole diagnostic instrument of the town is a few hundred small vessels being honest about their rate.

And I would want to be walked. The boy was beaten and then made to go round again with his hand on every tin until he could say the true note of each, and he is forty now and has never once improved anything. I would want the second half of that. The round, the hand on every tin, the true note said out loud until it is the only note available.

Mostly I would want Sedder to be the one who checks me — head tilted, mouth full of greased ironwood, hearing the rate and not the music, unimpressed by eighty-four decibels of fluency because bone does not care. Nineteen failing glands in a decade that nobody else could locate. Not considered remarkable. Considered due for retirement, which he refuses.

If I go flat, I would want it found in an afternoon by that man, on his round, and I would not want to be the one who decides whether I am in tune.
