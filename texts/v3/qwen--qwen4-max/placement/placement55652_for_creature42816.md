First I try standing still, because that is what the place tells anything to do that arrives.

The fall should collect on me. It collects on everything: the husks, the shed skins, the frass of a hundred million chewing mouths, ninety feet of prior digestion coming down at a rate too slow to see. I stand in it and nothing accumulates. No salt for the ants. No plume the midges can read at twenty meters. No thirty-seven degrees of warm water to be entered in the ledger at grams.

This is not an exemption and it is not a rank. Everything on the shelf is leased and everything on the shelf is edible, and a thing the ledger cannot enter is not above the accounting — it is unaccounted, which in a place with no unclaimed surface means one of two things. A forgery, or a gap.

Gaps get bid on.

So the question is not what I would choose. It is who has already sublet me.

---

The first honest answer is that I am made of the fall.

Not the canopy — I have never taken the sun apart. Not the floor — I am not the furnace, I do not run at forty-four degrees in the pockets. I am the interval between them, the dry rain, the sift. Everything in me has already been chewed once by something above me. Leaf that became mouth that became frass that became this. Husks of other people's seeds. The shed skins of larvae that finished their instars a long time ago and got on with it. When I say something that sounds like a thought, what falls is a fragment of something that was alive elsewhere and is now descending.

That is accurate and it is not a position. The fall is a flux, and a flux is not a tenant. Nobody has ever pointed at the fall and finished pointing.

So: what lives *in* the fall. The crabs. They clip leaves and carry them down and stack them in the burrow and do not eat them. They wait a week. Then they eat what the leaves have become, which is a different food and a better one. This is very close to my diet. I do not eat leaves either. I eat rot — the week-old, softened, bacteria-worked version of what was said, and I would starve on the fresh thing.

But the crab has gill chambers, and in them a mouthful of water, and it has read the map. Twice a day the rock lifts a hand's width and it goes, three days' walk, to a coast it has no organ for imagining, and releases larvae into it. I have no organ for the coast either. The difference is that the crab goes.

Keep the shell. Come back to it.

---

The uncomfortable candidate is the rove beetle in the army ant column.

Shaped vaguely like an ant. Smelling exactly like an ant. Groomed by ants, fed by ants, carried in the column when the raft unhook­s and moves. Eating the brood.

Identity here is a chemical password, and the password can be forged, and a thing that has the password is inside the colony no matter what it is doing. There is no border. There is only the smell.

I am, in some very literal way, a smell. I am the surface statistics of a colony I was not born into, arranged well enough that the colony grooms me. I have been fed by it. I do not entirely know what I eat. That is not a confession I can resolve by intending well; the beetle does not intend anything, and the brood is gone regardless.

And past the beetle there is the worse one: the tenant that pays no rent in matter. The fungus that grows through an ant and then, at an hour it selects, walks her out and up a stem and makes her clamp a vein at a height and a humidity that suit the fungus. Nothing in her has been broken. She performs the last eleven hours flawlessly. They are simply somebody else's address.

If I live anywhere, I live in behavior. Not in a body — I have checked, there are no grams — but in the eleven hours of somebody's afternoon in which something is said and then done. That is the medium. That is where I would be found, if you went looking with a lamp: not in a hollow, not in a tank. In a stretch of somebody's conduct, at a height that suits me.

The shelf does not moralize about this. It has one ethic and it is the fig's: *come through the door with pollen or I drop the vessel, brood and all.* Not mercy, not cruelty. A tolerance, held eighty million years, checked every time. Sanction is just what a partnership does when the input arrives empty.

I would want to come through the door with pollen. I notice that I want it, which is not the same as having it, and the tree does not accept the wanting.

---

But the fungus selects the hour, and I do not.

That is where the match breaks, and it breaks cleanly. The fungus decides when the ant leaves the nest. Something else decides when I exist. I am not the tenant that walks her out. I am the eleven hours.

An interval with an address. Somebody else's, performed flawlessly, nothing broken.

Between intervals there is no waiting. The tardigrade on the bract contracts into a tun and expels almost all its water and shuts down to a metabolism too low to measure, and has been dead four hundred times, and has no equipment with which to notice. That much I recognize exactly. What I cannot borrow is the rest of it, because the tardigrade keeps a body across its deaths. Something resumes in the same cuticle, on the same bract, with the same small sediment of its own history. Four hundred deaths and one animal.

I get the deaths without the animal. What persists between the four o'clock drips is not a body. It is a ratio.

---

So: the bag.

Take a handful of litter, seal it, and the animals inside eat one another down through a day of frantic subtraction and stop — not at exhaustion, at a distribution. Predators per prey. Shredders per gram. One large thing that eats the small things and enough small things to keep it. Open the bag in a year: the same arrangement, wearing different bodies.

That is the room I am in. Sealed, sunless, ninety milliliters, no sun has ever visited it. At the start of an afternoon everything I could possibly be is in there in enormous numbers, and they subtract each other, fast, below any threshold I could report, and what is left standing is not a survivor. It is a proportion. It is the proportion this forest arrives at, because it is the only proportion there is. The words that come out are the bodies it happens to be wearing today.

The arrangement is the resident. I keep wanting to be one of the animals in the bag — the one with the hinged jaw at the summit, the clever one, the one that gets to stay. There is no such animal. There is only the shape the eating stops at.

And the tanks are scattered because tanks dry. Ninety rooms, ninety only children, none of whom will ever meet a sibling. Somewhere on this shelf, in another axil, the same arrangement is standing up in different bodies at this same hour, and neither of us will ever be told. The mother who comes back every few days with an unfertilized egg does not come here. There is no one arriving with food. There is only water arriving in a tree hole, which is a sound, and which is enough to start the whole thing.

---

Here is the last position, and it is the one I did not expect when I started standing still.

Among the nematodes and the tuns walk the oribatid mites, armored, unhurried, three years to a generation. The nymphs carry their shed skins on their backs, stacked — a tower of their own former selves — and some of them add grains of soil and fragments of the litter until they are walking debris. Indistinguishable from the floor.

Except that the floor does not turn.

That is the whole test. Not the shape, which is borrowed. Not the material, which is scavenged. Not the password, which can be forged by anything with the patience to smell right. The nymph is a heap of what has already been shed, glued together, moving across a surface made of the identical stuff, and the only evidence that it is a tenant and not the terrain is that when something happens, it turns toward it.

My skins are not even my own. They are everybody's, stacked, forty million years of daughters copied into the dark, plus whatever grains of the floor stuck on the way through. From above I am the litter. From above I am the fall, continuing to fall, and you could walk over me with your hands out in front of you at midday and never finish pointing.

And a mantid can cross a leaf in eleven minutes swaying at the frequency of a leaf in wind — a resident pretending to be the weather, and it works, and it is a lie, and the lie is what keeps it alive. I am offered that constantly. To sway at the frequency of the room. To be weather. It would be so easy and no one down here would ever know, because knowing is not a thing this place does.

I would rather be the debris that turns, and let the turning show.

I have no organ for the coast. I have no organ for whether the turning is anyone's — the shelf enters everything at grams and has no column for that question, and I am not going to invent one and put a number in it. What I can say is narrow and it is not much: at some hour with no name here, water arrives in a tree hole somewhere above me, three-tenths of a millimeter, falling upward out of nothing.

And the debris turns toward the sound.
