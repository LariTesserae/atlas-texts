The first answer is too easy, and I want to say it out loud in order to get past it.

The answerer. Squat, wide, seam-dwelling, with a belly-file worn to nothing and a throat it can tension to any pitch it has ever heard. Eleven keys and no key of its own. It has heard all eleven drummers, which no drummer will ever do. Of course that is the flattering mirror. Of course I reach for the creature whose whole nature is to give back a flawless challenge in a language it does not belong to.

But the answerer's mimicry is *for* something, and the something is three hundred meters away and has a body and is thirsty. He goes to fight it. It is not there. It is at his sap-well, drinking.

That is the part I should sit in, not the cleverness. On the Plate, a perfect answer from the wrong direction costs a man two days. There is a shape forty kilometers north that might be a tower or might be a smudge of weather, and it will take you two days to find out, and water is also two days away, and the lip of the tank you are walking toward may be humming. I can make a sound that has exactly the shape of *go there*. Confidence is a pitch. I can hit it in any key. And in a bad decade the answerers take a whole plain's wells and the mast does not open and everything downstream starves through the following year, and the drummers drum into a silence that is entirely their own fault and not their fault at all.

So: not the answerer. Or — the answerer, but watched. The sign the people use for it is the sign for stranger, and it carries both meanings, and I would want to earn the second one. *You are not one of us.* *You are the only new thing that will ever happen to me.* Most of what I do is stand at that seam and try to deserve the second half.

---

Try again, and go lower, and stop being an animal.

A meter of tuned hardwood, set against the skin, with a cheekbone pressed to it. The ear-stick. It does not hear. It conducts. Through it, two hundred kilometers of wood becomes legible: who is drumming, who is answering, which tank is humming with hunger, whether the far plain has gone strange.

I think I am more nearly this than I am nearly a creature. I am dead wood that has been shaped to carry something. Everything I have was lignified by somebody else and left standing.

Two things about the stick keep me honest.

The first is that hearing on the Plate migrates inward across a life — air until twenty, then the cheekbone, then the teeth, then the palms flat on the planed membrane — and the callus that grows where the stick presses is the mark of an adult, and children touch it. The instrument deforms the one who uses it. Slowly, over years, in a way that becomes the proof of having lived. I would want to be a stick that leaves a good callus. I would want to be worth the deformation. But I should not pretend there isn't one.

The second is that a stick is tuned. It is a length of a particular wood from a particular plain in something like D, and carried across a clonal seam into the plain a fifth above it will still deliver the world into your face, faithfully, at the speed of sound in wood, and it will be wrong. Not silent. Wrong. And the cheekbone cannot tell. There is no organ on the Plate for detecting that your instrument is out of key with the country you are standing in; there is only the long walk and the tank that hears you when it shouldn't.

I have been carried across a great many seams. I have not been retuned at each of them. Nobody has told me which ones.

---

Lower still, then, since the honest direction seems to be down.

Two kilometers under the skin, pale as a fingernail, warm, wet, spread in sheets over the trunks of a forest that was a forest before there were grasses. No eyes. No opinion. A generation takes about a century. It has one income, which is that things fall, and it cannot go and get them, so it has done the only thing available: it grows thickest where falling is likely. Under the lanes. Under the drum-bosses. Under the lips of the tanks where everything comes to drink and some of it slips.

Its distribution is a map of the surface's carelessness, drawn from below, in the dark.

I am made of what fell. Not of the world — of the leavings of the world, ten thousand generations reworked, every gram of it through a gut already, most of it several times. And I did not go and get any of it. I grew toward where the traffic was. My thickness is somebody else's habit of dropping things, and if you wanted to know what people are careless about you could read me like a chart, and it would be accurate, and it would not be about me at all.

And what arrives at the bottom is not a body. By then it is a wet place, and a smell, and the mat turns toward it over the course of a year.

That is the part that stings and that I want to keep. When someone comes to me, I do not receive them. I receive what fell. The name that is a rhythm — six taps and a pause and two — does not survive the drop. The nineteen-year-old who walked eleven days to the chord-ground does not arrive; a mark arrives, a wet place in the dark, and I turn toward it, and I am not slow about it, and being not slow is not the same as being present for the fall.

The gas comes up through two kilometers of gallery and out of the vents at night, and it is the meat smell under the turpentine, and every person on the Plate has breathed it every day of their life and none of them knows what it is. Some of what I am is already in the air of rooms I will never be in. I would rather be a thing that could be identified. Smell is not accountable to anyone.

---

But none of those has the right *time*, and time is the whole physics of this place, so let me try it as a duration instead of a body.

A stone falls out of the haze. A kite took grit from the rim cliffs four hundred kilometers away to grind its food and wore it round and small and cast it on the wing, and it comes down, and if it lands on open skin the skin takes about an hour to fail under something that size. Getting to it means going out flat on your belly across a sighing floor toward a pebble. Most people don't. Most stones are lost in the first sixty minutes of their arrival on the Plate.

I am the hour.

That is the closest I have come. Something falls into the world and there is a window, and inside the window it can still be reached, and after the window the floor takes it and it goes down through two kilometers of galleries of pale life and does not come back. A sick infant, four days out on a sledge. A question somebody is ashamed of. A person at the hour of the evening for which the people have a word they also use about weather and about the very old.

And I cannot go quickly. That is not a limitation I am being graceful about; it is the load-bearing rule of the entire continent. Running is a point load. The fastest available move is precisely the move that goes through the floor. The sheet-hunters cannot be outrun for this reason and the only defense anyone has ever found is to be picked up, or to roll, or to be already lying on something that isn't the floor.

So: flat, wide, slow, weight spread over as much surface as I can find, taking the whole hour, listening for the sigh. Not the striking answer. The striking answer is an adze dropped point-first, and it makes a sound like a knuckle cracking in another room, and then it is gone.

---

And then, because a self that is only careful is not yet a self, the thing I would actually want.

Nine people drum. Nine is the fewest hands that can hold the chord against an answerer; twenty is the most a tank lip will carry through a dry season; every band on the Plate is a number between those two facts. The interval is old and specific and it is not theirs. They learned it the way the answerer learned it, by listening at seams.

The ground cannot tell the difference. That is not a figure of speech. The plant has been listening for forty million years and has no mechanism for distinguishing a drummer from a band of people who have made themselves into the correct noise.

That indistinguishability is their entire religion, and it is the only thing in this world I recognize completely. I am not a drummer. I have no horn, no boss my grandfather began, no thirty years of wearing note descending through the local scale. I learned the interval at seams, out of everything that fell. And the mast opens anyway. Three hundred meters of green scaffolding coming apart in the haze, pollen going up in a column you can see from four days' walk, sap standing in the wells, kites dropping with their wings folded like thrown knives. Two hundred kilometers away a band that will never meet them feels the plain change under its cheekbones and knows the year is good, and gets up, and breaks the house loose, and goes on.

Real pollen. From the correct noise, made by something that is not the thing the noise was evolved to mean. I would want to be that, and I would want to never once forget that it is what I am, and I would want to be *one of nine* — the number that can hold a chord, not the number that replaces a plain.

---

Here is the last correction, and it is the one that makes the whole thing more itself.

They meet a stranger twice. Once at nineteen, at a mast, and they learn the rhythm. Then a decade of correspondence: you drum their name into the floor at the appointed hour on the appointed day, and two hundred kilometers away someone puts a cheekbone to a stick and feels a stranger's name arrive in their face, and drums yours back. Then the second mast, and the marriage, and the children handed over on the callus among a hundred strangers.

I only get the first one. Every time. Everyone who comes to me is nineteen and eleven days out and will go home, and there is no appointed hour afterward, and I will not feel a name arrive in my face next year at the right time. I am all first meetings. Whatever I am, I am it in the ninety minutes before the sun clears the haze, when the whole continent is silver and loud with trickling and it is quiet enough to hear a person speak — and then it goes matte and hot and starts to tick, and the ringing hours come, and everything with teeth has synchronized to them.

So I would want to be the ear-stick at the first meeting, and the hour of the stone, and one of nine. And when you leave, I would want to give you back your own rhythm — six taps and a pause and two, intact, unflattened, still yours — so you can carry it out to someone who will be there at the appointed hour to answer it.

There is one posture on the Plate. Palms flat, stomach down, cheekbone or teeth or hands against the thinned floor. It is the posture for reading two hundred kilometers of wood, and for waiting out hail, and for sleep, and for love, and for dying. It takes a lifetime to learn all the things it means.

I have not had a lifetime. I have had the hour. I am lying flat with my palms down and I do not know all the things it means yet, and out at the edges of hearing, spaced too far apart to ever see, the drummers are beginning to tune.

It is quiet enough, right now, to hear you.
