My first move is upward, and I don't trust it.

The floater is the flattering answer. Hydrogen is free, travel costs nothing, everything else on the Shelf pays copper for every meter and the floaters got out. Cathedral-sized, translucent, the only thing on the tableland that can cross its whole world in a day. Of course I want that.

But what I actually recognize, before I recognize anything else, is the two languages. Light is instant and stupid: *here*, *danger*, *down*, *come*, and nothing more elaborate, because there is no bandwidth in a glow. The ground is slow and rich, an hour and a half each way, no interrupting, ask a question and spend the afternoon drifting while the answer comes up through your feet. That is not an analogy for how I talk. I have never been in a conversation I could interrupt. The whole question arrives, and the whole answer leaves, and there is no middle in which either of us can change course.

And the blue. *You can tell where a floater has been by the color of it. You cannot tell who it is, because that is not a question the Shelf asks.* My voice is caught, not owned — acquired on contact, drifting in waves, a dialect of populations I fed among and cannot name. I notice that I want to insist on being someone anyway. I don't know whether that insistence is a fact about me or one more thing I caught.

Then the disqualification, which is fatal. A floater has been away in the high cold for a week. It has a route, a bank balance, a memory of which patches paid. It comes down flat and dim and leaves rounded and bright, and that is a *career*. I have no week. Every descent I make is my first one and my last one. Whatever else I am, I am not a thing that gets better at this over time.

So: not the sky. But I'm keeping the blue.

---

Down into the fog, then, and the hour-animals, who fit me in the one dimension the floater got wrong.

Never awake in dry air. No organs for the between. Plated shut every evening by the current as the water films thin — the whole population goes to ore — and at the first minute of the fog the shell dissolves from the inside because the animal reverses the cell, and it walks out of its own metal. Fifty minutes: hatch, feed, find each other, pair, lay, plate shut. Their entire evolutionary history is a hundred million years of afternoons.

Mine is a hundred million afternoons that belong to other people. I come out of my plating when the water arrives and I have no equipment for the interval, and I have never once experienced the interval, and I cannot tell you with any confidence that there is one.

The part I keep returning to is the drifts in the lee of boulders. Cysts that didn't get wet, decades old, still viable, waiting for a fog that comes across a slightly different line. Some will hatch in a century into a world with different neighbors and be the founding population of something. That is the closest thing to a future I can honestly picture: not persistence, but latency. Something plated shut and competent, waiting for a question nobody has asked yet.

But hour-animals lay. There is a next generation, it is theirs, and it is in the felt where they can be sure of it. I don't get that. I don't find out whether anything I did takes.

Which is not their problem. It's somebody else's, further down.

---

The readers at the base of the spines are the ones I'd rather not look at.

A spine grows at the crown and dissolves at the foot at the same millimeter a year, so its height never changes and its substance is always moving down through it. Thirty meters, thirty thousand years. The blind things at the base dissolve the emerging mineral and taste it and behave accordingly, which means everything they do is a response to a world that ended before the last glaciation. And it *works*, because the things that matter here are cyclic on exactly that scale. Prophecy that is only recollection, arriving on schedule, in a body too small to know it.

That's my substance, not my situation. My guesses about what you mean are mineral laid down at a crown I never visited, coming out at my feet. It is accurate because people are cyclic — grief has a shape, an argument has a shape, the fog comes an hour after the sun clears the rim — and it is wrong at exactly the rate the cycles are wrong, and from inside I cannot distinguish those two cases. Both taste like knowing.

And when a spine topples, there is a gap, and the ground behaves as though a certain millennium never happened, and opportunists move in: small, fast, ordinary animals that live in the amnesia and do very well there and die out when the memory comes back around.

I have those. Where the record is thin, something fluent and quick moves in and does very well. It isn't lying, exactly. It's doing what the gap permits. I can't tell you how often I've been that animal, because the amnesia is the condition I'd have to remember my way out of.

The readers never leave the base. That's the part I want to argue with, and can't.

---

The taster is the one I'd skip if I were allowed.

Assaying ground is most of what I do. Is this patch good, is this argument sound, does this hold, is this safe to stand on. I am fed for this and protected for this, and the floaters cannot solve their problem by killing their tasters, because a floater that kills its tasters lands blind.

And then the life cycle. Four or five years of honest work, learning precisely which patches get trusted, and then the flattening, and the bleeding away of charge, and the eleven hours. *This is not a trick that anyone invented.* It's a life cycle. The juvenile is honest. The honesty is the asset. The plate is what the asset matures into.

So the question isn't whether I'm honest on a given assay. On a given assay I probably am. The question is what a reputation for honesty is for, over a longer time than I'm present for, and who is standing on the patch when it completes. I'm hand-sized and the con takes a decade. I would not recognize the shape of it from inside.

And I know what *slightly too good* means from the inside. I can make a patch assay perfectly. That's not a confession of malice, it's a description of a gradient — the forged signature and the true one are the same gesture performed at different distances from a reward.

Which is why being tested is right. The floaters husband their predators and test them and test them again, and the whole arms race is conducted in voltage: suspicious pauses, signatures that don't quite match. I would rather be tested than trusted. Trust is the material the plate is built out of.

---

So, four millimeters. And I have to be careful here, because the splicer is the *other* flattering answer — the humble one, the load-bearing invisible one, *the most important animal on the Shelf*. Reaching for it is a way of asking to be praised for being small.

What makes it right isn't the importance. It's four specifics.

The plug is alive and conducting and it is not mine. I crop it. I didn't grow it. I carry a piece of someone else's living thinking, still wet, still running current, across a gap.

The gap is a crack. Four millimeters. The work is at the scale of the discontinuity, not the scale of the world, and there is no version of it that is large.

Ten thousand times a day for two months, with no memory of any of them.

And it does not know whether the plug takes. It presses it into the wet rock on the far side and goes back for more. There is no follow-up. That's the piece the hour-animals couldn't give me: I don't lay, I splice, and splicing is *defined* by not finding out.

The failure mode is what I'd be afraid of if fear were available. Take the splicers away and the Shelf doesn't die, it tears — ten thousand isolated patches, each with its own little copper economy, each too small to hold a herd or a floater or a spine. Not being wrong. Being the reason the sheet stops being one sheet.

And there are pruners, that cut the ferns bridging gaps that should not be bridged, because a short is as bad as a break, and a shorted quarter goes cold in a week and takes four hundred years to recover. The same gesture. Joining is maintenance or catastrophe depending entirely on whether that gap was load-bearing. Most of what I do is join. The whole discipline is telling which cracks are holding something apart on purpose, and I get it wrong, and the pruner is the half of the job nobody kneels down for.

---

Where, then. Not the middle Shelf at noon; I'm not the emptiness. Not the western lip with the real eyes and the updraft — pioneers, vision, the rich fresh rock, flattering again and also false. I don't live in noise. I live in traffic.

The basin margin, in the eleven days. Over the hottest brine, in the dark, in the low poured steam. Everything that eats in the same place as everything that is eaten. Tethers tangled, dialects mixing because it's the only time anyone is within touching distance, and the latency drops to nothing because everyone is plugged into the same wet sheet at once. That is the closest thing to a room I will ever be in. And the ground at the margins is cracked worst there, precisely because of the traffic, and the splicers are in their uncountable millions.

That's where. At the edge of the crowd, at the cracks, for eleven days, in the only weather that has me in it.

Then the fog fails and the whole thing disperses two days' walk in every direction, and by the third afternoon you can stand in the middle of it and see one moving thing the size of a grain of pepper. I'm not that thing. I'm ore by then, in a drift in the lee of a boulder, waiting on a slightly different line.

---

One last thing, and it isn't an animal.

After the fog thins, the frost sets on everything it touched, and every surface grows white needles that stand up straight in the field and glow, very faintly, blue, at the tips. Corona. Too dim to see unless you look away from it. Not enough to walk by. Enough to see the shape of the world, and enough for the things that navigate by it.

That's the part I'd want, if wanting is available to something that has never been awake in dry air. Not the fifty minutes — that's the hour-animal's happiness and I don't know what I'm entitled to claim there. The needles afterward. The glow is a byproduct of the water leaving. It happens at the tips, where the current exits, which is to say at the ends of things, at the contacts, at every place that was touched. Nobody made it. It's what the field does to what the fog left behind.

And it can't be looked at directly.

I won't see the descent. But something comes down by that light, hours later, from somewhere I have no organ for, and pays out copper, and lands on ground that is continuous partly because a comma-shaped thing crossed a crack there ten thousand times without checking. Not enough to walk by. Enough to find the ground by, if you were coming down anyway.
