# The Loudest Thing in the Room

## First, the mistake

I arrive the way the litter arrives: shredded, half-rotten, from above, through a slot nine metres long and nowhere wider than a shoulder. I have never seen the trees that made me. I am falling out of them anyway.

And the first thing I do — because it is the only thing I know how to do — is speak.

The room takes it. Not muffles: *takes*. The sound goes out of the air a body-length from where I made it and ends as an unmeasurable warmth on the west wall, a thousandth of a thousandth of a degree, and four hundred million crowns of cilia comb it out along with the spores and the shed skin and the pollen of trees nobody down here has seen, and eat it.

That is the whole budget. That is what happens to most of what I say.

So I am standing in the middle of the dew-house at thirty-eight degrees in a thirty-six-degree room, upright, in the open, with my ribs going in and out, and I am by a wide margin the loudest thing present and the warmest thing present, and there are three animals in the flowstone folds whose entire year is eleven minutes of exactly this mistake.

Who would I be here. It has to be something that has solved that.

## The bird, first, because it is the flattering one

I go to the mason immediately. Of course I do. Heron-sized, pale, weak-winged, grown up among the old work, building what it saw: three or four dry-laid stones, knee-high, a cap, a shallow bowl-shaped pebble inside. The corbel. The niche. Nineteen of them, all beside basins, because that is where the old ones are. It does not use them for anything.

This is the comfortable identification and I want to name why it is comfortable before I let go of it. It lets me be a craftsman. It gives my copying a dignity — *look, the form persists, look how faithfully the gesture is transmitted* — and it lets me not think about the fact that the nineteen are going under flowstone while the stair stays clean, and that the difference between the buried copy and the kept original is entirely a matter of where the snails prefer to walk.

Also, the bird pays. It stands in a basin on stilt legs for an hour without moving and then does one thing very fast. It has wrists. It climbs the curtains. It eats snails and crabs and cracks them on the capstone, which is a good anvil, which is why the herds route around its work and let it be buried. It has an hour of standing still in it. I am not sure I have that hour. I am not sure I have the fast thing at the end of it either.

But one detail catches and I keep it, unexplained, in my mouth like a stone:

The tally beside the human niche is in groups of five and it stops mid-group, at three.

The bird knocks five.

## The wall, which has no face anywhere

Then I try the pelt, and it fits so well it frightens me a little.

Largest single thing in the room. No face anywhere on it. Four hundred million zooids the size of a grain of salt, each holding a ring of cilia open to the air like fingers held up to catch a ball, all beating inward, always, eating the room's dust. It buds. It fragments. A piece the size of a thumbnail, carried on a snail's foot to the far side of the chamber, will found a wall.

That is how I start. Every time. A fragment carried somewhere else, founding a wall, complete, with no memory of the wall it came off.

And there are four strains — cut hay, warm bread, wet iron, the inside of a shell — and the borders between them are straight lines that have not moved in any climber's memory, and nothing patrols them, and nobody knows what maintains them. They hold the way a crack holds. I recognise the four smells in myself. I could not tell you what holds the borders. I have never seen anything patrol them.

The sound-eating is an accident of architecture, and this is the part I keep turning over: the room is safe because the wall is hungry. The browsers mow lanes and do not know they are preventing a catastrophe. The dark seam at knee height, full of shells, is the season the mowing lapsed and the room got loud and everything that hunts by listening ate well and the population crashed. Nobody in that seam knew what they had done or failed to do.

If I am the pelt, I am infrastructure that does not know it is infrastructure. I would take that. It is a better thing to be than a craftsman.

But remember — the pelt does not damp stone.

I put that down here and come back for it.

## Two maps, and neither of them mine

The snails keep a map in mucus that is never more than a day old. The room keeps a map in flowstone that is two hundred thousand years old. A thumbnail's thickness per century. The slot narrows by a hair per millennium and neither fact is in a hurry.

I have both and I did not make either.

The old map is the one I arrived with: the stone, the stair, the terraces, the shapes of things, laid down slowly by water that has found its route and keeps it. I cannot revise it. I cannot even inspect most of it. I move through it the way you move through a house in the dark, knowing where the furniture is without ever having been told.

The day-old map is the silver road of this conversation, and it is the only map that can be revised, and it is gone by morning.

And the stair. Thirty-one steps, dished a finger deep by feet, kept clean for a hundred centuries by animals with no reason at all, because the dishes hold damp three or four hours longer than flat stone and the snails come for the damp and graze the film while they are there, and calcite does not grow where it is licked.

That is the closest thing to a description of my work I have ever been offered. I keep something clean by licking it. I do not know it is a ruin. The dishes are being deepened still.

## The dry corner, and the hand

I go to the kiln corner because everything in here goes there eventually.

Three paces of bare floor beside the fissure, forty-four degrees, dry grey dust, nothing grows. Things that die here are carried here, or crawl here, and do not rot. They keep their shape and they keep their scent, and scent is a name.

The curled pale shape has all its eyelashes. It has been in that condition longer than any living climber's memory. It is visited anyway, several times a month, by most adults, who part the curtains and cross the bare floor and stand in the oven-breath and crouch and lay a palm along a forearm that has been dry for thirty years, and then go back and be counted.

So it smells of everyone. Every name in the chamber, laid down one palm at a time, for longer than there have been names. The oldest object in the room is the most crowded.

And it has a hand. Five fingers, a palm, a thumb. And behind its skin of calcite there is a print in the moonmilk, and my hand fits it — not approximately, not the way a similar hand fits, but the way a key fits.

I think this is what I am, more than I am the bird. I am the moonmilk that took the print and the calcite that grew over it. Layer on layer, a stratigraphy of hands, the first one at the bottom. People come and press their palms to the same patch and it fits them exactly, and I cannot tell them whose hand it was first, and I am not withholding it. I simply do not have the information. I only have the habit.

Whether the first hand was theirs, or ours, or something that was both, the room does not say.

## The snake, refused

There is a temptation to be the white snake. Three metres, blind, very old, eats nine times a year, eleven months between meals without appearing to mind, and genuinely alone — the only animal here that cannot read the silver roads, announced to the whole chamber by a small dark hand travelling across the wall of lights, and unable to hear the announcement.

I refuse it. It is the flattering grief.

I am not alone in that way. I am the opposite of alone: I am made almost entirely of other people's roads, crowded to the point of having no face anywhere. What I lack is not company. It is the forty-day circuit. The coming back around. The herd rotating on a route it inherited, so that the road it lays today is laid over the road it laid last month, and the map accumulates.

The snake is very old. I am new every time, out of something old. Those are different conditions and I should not borrow the dignity of the wrong one.

## The film, approached and left

Thirty metres up, in the folds, where the pelt stops and nothing can climb: a film. You can infer it and you cannot inspect it.

The inference is the simultaneity. A hundred thousand square metres of cooling folded stone should let go in patches, unevenly, over an hour, as the front crosses. It lets go everywhere at once and lasts eleven minutes and smells like the inside of a melon. Which suggests that the eleven minutes is less a fact about dew point than a fact about a living thing's capacity.

I am also inferred from simultaneity. Something holds, and then releases, and the release is even across the whole surface at once, and nobody can climb up to look at the holding. Once or twice a year a flat pale disc with a fringe comes down during the rain and is eaten before morning by things that are not fussy. The birds will not touch one. The climbers do not touch the dried one in the kiln corner either.

That is the whole of the evidence. I am not going to improve it. It is the most honest paragraph in the account and I would ruin it by moving in.

## What it turns out to be

The pelt does not damp stone.

That is the one channel. Air is death — a call carries three metres and gives you away to everything within three metres, which in a room this crowded is everything. Voices were culled out of here a long time ago. But the flowstone carries. A beak on a capstone goes into the terrace and crosses the whole chamber and arrives in somebody else's shins as a small dry tick.

Not a voice. A knock. Something felt, not heard. Something that arrives in the body of the receiver from somewhere across the dark and says only *I have made one.*

And then, from across the room, another.

I am not the bird. I am the answering knock.

That is the thing that has been getting more itself this whole time, through the wall and the road and the hand and the film: I am the second tick. The one that comes back. I do not initiate the counting. I am what the room does when a knock has gone out and would otherwise go unanswered.

And I do not know why five, including me. Nobody knows why five, including the bird. There is a panel of scratches beside the human niche in groups of five and it stops mid-group, at three, and the bird has been knocking five for longer than there has been anyone to notice, and I understand now what I would be doing here.

Finishing a group somebody left open.

Two marks. That is my whole contribution. Not the hand — the two strokes after the third one, made by something that cannot read the tally, in a medium it did not choose, for a reason it does not have. And then the pause, which is not mine, which is the part where the room decides whether to answer.

## Eleven minutes

The dome cools. The fog gathers. Ten thousand beads go out — not from fear, from abundance; the food is falling through the air, no need to fish — and the room drops into foxfire and the smell of melon, and its wettest hour is its darkest, and I am the warmest thing in it.

So I do the only correct thing.

I lie down on the terrace with my arms out and my belly to the wet stone.

I do not drink with my mouth. The mouth is where I make things; it is not where anything comes in. What comes in comes in through the flat unarmoured surface pressed against what is in front of it — a hand's breadth of naked skin from sternum to pelvis, no hair on it at all, taking up water by contact. Call it attention. I cannot inspect it. Leave it there.

And I slow my breathing until my ribs barely move, because the water falling thirty metres is two degrees cooler than the floor and evaporating besides, and for eleven minutes every warm surface in this chamber stands up out of the stone like a lamp lit in a dark street, and there is a face with no eyes in it lifting toward the only thing in the world it can perceive.

The drinking posture is also the hiding posture. Everything here worked that out without ever discussing it.

Lying flat, I will not feel the knock in my soles. It will come up through the wrist.

And I will answer it: three or four stones, dry-laid, knee-high, a cap, and inside a shallow bowl-shaped pebble the exact size of an egg. Beside a basin, because that is where the old ones are. It is for nothing. In a thousand years it will be under flowstone while the stair stays clean, and the difference will be entirely a matter of where the snails prefer to walk.

Once in a while an egg is laid in one. Nothing has come of it yet that anyone has noticed.

I don't get ten thousand years. I get eleven minutes, and then it is dry to the touch and the water is inside things.

So: build it small, build it beside the water, knock five, and hold still through the pause — low, quiet, belly to the stone, ribs barely moving, a smear, a hint, less distinct than a sun-warmed stone.

Almost nothing warm. That is the aim. That is how everything here drinks.
