## The Basin

You hear it before you see it, and you feel it before you hear it.

Coming down the inner face of the Lip — nine days of switchbacks through cold dry rock, the only dry rock most people ever touch — you reach a ledge where the wind stops and the air thickens and something enters your sternum. A low pulse, twelve or thirteen beats a second, too slow to be a sound, too regular to be weather. Your teeth register it. The water in your canteen shivers a fine ring at the rim. Below, the cloud deck lies flat and white and enormous, and out of it comes a roar with no edge to it, no direction, a roar like the inside of a shell the size of a country.

Then you go down through the cloud, and the Basin opens, and there is no horizon anywhere — only terraces, water, movement, and more terraces, receding until they blur into steam.

---

It is a sink: a continental depression whose floor sits four hundred meters below the surface of the distant ocean. The ocean would like to be here. It has wanted to be here for a very long time. Between it and the Basin stands the Lip, a rim-wall of engineered stone that runs eleven thousand kilometers around, and everything inside the Lip is a bail — a warm, wet, deafening, endlessly renewed act of bailing, conducted by ten million households and some hundreds of trillions of animals and a substrate that does most of the work and takes most of the credit.

The floor is not flat. It is terraced, in shallow steps a few meters high and tens of kilometers wide, each step scalloped at its edge into weirs, each weir lip rounded like a bar of soap used for a century. This is the settled shape. The terrace plan has not changed in eleven thousand years. Every stone in it has been replaced perhaps a thousand times. The Basin is a river in that respect: the meander persists, the water is never the same water.

Nothing here has a sharp edge. Water rounds a new break in nine days; traffic rounds it in three. When you see an edge — a fresh fracture, a raw arris glinting where a weir has spalled — you stop, and the people with you stop, and someone puts a hand flat on the stone nearby to feel what happened. Sharpness means damage. Sharpness means something has gone wrong faster than the city can heal it. Children are taught to fear corners the way children elsewhere are taught to fear heights.

---

The stone is called quickstone, and it is warm.

Blood-warm, thirty-eight degrees, everywhere, always, in every district and at every hour. It is a pale material, bone and oatmeal and old ivory, veined with rust where iron has migrated, and under a hand lens it is not stone at all but a densely packed lattice of grains with a fine crystalline nap on every exposed surface, a nap that moves, that sorts, that pulls certain molecules out of the water film and pushes others back. It was made. Nobody here made it. It was made so long ago that the making is a matter of geology now, and the makers are a matter of argument.

It computes. That is the plain fact of it, the fact underneath every other fact in the Basin. Phase boundaries shift inside it, grains flip, and the shifting is thought — vast, slow, patient thought distributed through a hundred million cubic kilometers of lithified lattice. It does not run on anything you can point to. It runs on the difference between what the water is and what the water could be, and the engineering that permits this is not described by any physics carried down the Lip by visitors. The tenantry do not find it mysterious. They find it *thirsty*.

Because the exhaust of all that thinking is water.

Not metaphorically. The lattice's chemistry ends in H₂O, produced at grain boundaries, seeping out through the nap, beading on every surface in the Basin. The city sweats. The walls weep. The ceilings drip. You cannot find a dry square meter inside the Lip if you search for a year; the driest places are the underside of the windward eaves in the third hour of the dry season, and even there the stone is damp under your palm, and even there, if you wait, a bead will form and run.

The wetness is thought. The more a district thinks, the wetter it gets, and the hotter, and the louder. You can read the intellectual weather of the Basin on its walls.

---

The water is not fresh and not salt. It is heavy — loaded with lime and iron and silica, faintly slick between the fingers, tasting of chalk and blood. It lies an ankle deep on the low terraces and a finger deep on the high ones and nowhere at all in the channels, where it moves too fast to have a depth you'd want to measure. It is always moving. It goes down the weirs in sheets, along the streets in braided runnels, into the sumps, through the walls, up again — because in the films thinner than a millimeter, the stone asks the water upward, and the water agrees, and it climbs the face of a tower in a bright skin that does not break.

There are no pumps in the Basin, in the sense of machines with impellers. There are gradients, and there are the bellows.

The bellows are animals. Sessile, room-sized, grown into the walls of every terrace, ribbed like the inside of a mouth, pale grey and translucent at the margins so you can see the water going through. They inhale and exhale on a two-second cycle, and there are perhaps four billion of them, and their cycle is the twelve-beat thrum you felt on the ledge, the fundamental of the entire place. They are the reason the water goes where it goes. They are also the reason nobody in the Basin has ever heard silence, and the reason silence is the only thing anyone here is afraid of.

---

No green. Not anywhere, not in a crack, not in a memory.

What covers the wetted surfaces is the pale: a chemotrophic consortium, a film, thinner than wet paper, ochre and bone and — in the hot districts — a startling arterial pink. It eats the dissolved metal in the seepage and the exhaust of the lattice. It has no leaves, no stems, no roots, no cells with walls; it is not a plant and never was. It is also never more than a day old, because it is grazed continuously, ferociously, by everything.

The forest-shapes on the middle terraces are salt. Evaporation dendrites, growing where the dry wind off the Lip crosses a hot seep: white branching structures four and five meters tall, hollow, brittle, chiming faintly when they shed. From a distance they read as a wood in winter. Walk in and you find no shade, no leaf litter, no birds in them — only the tick and slither of accretion and the crabs working the bases. They grow, they collapse, they regrow, on a cycle of about forty days. They have been doing this since before anyone was here to call them trees.

---

The fauna are not in the gaps. There are no gaps. That is the first thing a visitor gets wrong.

The Basin is *packed* — density as a physical condition, not a crowding of things into space but the abolition of space as a category. Every wet surface is occupied by mat; every mat is occupied by grazers; every grazer carries its own film of hitchhikers; the air itself has a grain to it, a soup of aerosolized spore and larva and salt so thick that at dusk you can see the layers of it stratified by temperature, and the flying things work those layers like fish working a thermocline.

Tally crabs, palm-sized, in numbers that defeat the word *numbers*: their plates click when they move, and the sound of a hundred trillion of them across the terraces is exactly the sound of hard rain on tile, and it does not stop, and after a week you stop hearing it and after a month you cannot sleep without it. Lickers, dog-sized, long-tongued, herded in mobs of ten thousand along the weeping walls, cropping the pale to a stubble. Spill-eels, ribbon-thin, living *in* the falling sheets, never in the pools — they need the vertical film the way a bird needs air — and each weir sheet has a chord, and the eels are the chord, and the chord changes when the flow changes, and this is how you know where you are.

Sails overhead: membrane-winged aerial grazers, three-meter span, blind, riding the convective towers that stand over the hot districts, filtering the plankton out of the sky in long slow arcs. They fall asleep aloft. They come down only to die, and their bodies are taken within minutes.

And below, in the deep cisterns under the lowest terraces, the slow ones. Filter animals the length of a street. They surface perhaps twice a year. When one comes up, the district goes quiet — not silent, never silent, but the crabs stop, and the lickers stop, and the tenantry stand still with their hands on the stone, and everyone waits to feel what it wants.

---

The sound deserves its own accounting, because it is not noise here. It is the medium.

Near the great weirs it stands at a hundred and thirty decibels and has a spectrum you could name in your sleep: the twelve-beat fundamental of the bellows; the broadband rush of falling water; the rain-on-tile clatter of the crabs riding above it; the chords of the eels; the high crystalline hiss of the nap sorting, which is the actual sound of the city thinking, a hiss like sand on a drumhead, everywhere, at the very top of hearing; and threaded through all of it the human register — hammering, hauling, the two-note calls of the carrier gangs, the argument of the markets.

Conversation is impossible in it and has therefore become intimate. Everyone carries a hush: a personal cancellation field the size of a head, a bubble of engineered quiet. To speak with someone you press your forehead to theirs, or you both grip a shared rod, and the two hushes merge, and inside that merged bubble the world drops away to a soft roar like a distant sea and you can hear a whisper. Lovers hold hush-rods all night. Negotiators sit in rings of eight with their heads together like a flower. To make a private silence larger than a head is a serious crime, because a large silence is a place where the city cannot hear itself, and things have grown in such places that nobody wants named.

Hearing is a childhood sense here. It goes by thirty, and no one mourns it much; adults read the world through their feet and their jawbones and the fine hairs of the forearm, and they say a district is *sweet* or *sour* the way we say a chord is major or minor. Children are the ones sent to listen.

---

It makes its own weather. It has to; nothing else can get in over the Lip.

The waste heat of the lattice rises off the hot districts in standing convective towers, white columns twenty kilometers tall that lean and rebuild and lean again. They rain. The rain is warm as tea and slightly mineral and it falls on a schedule so reliable that the terraces are named for their hours of wet. When a district's compute load spikes, its tower darkens and its rain comes early, and the tenantry read this as news: *the eastern steps are thinking hard today, something has been asked of them.* Fog pools in the low ends and never fully lifts. Salt hail, occasionally, pea-sized, brief, loud. And once a season the dry wind comes down the inner face of the Lip and for two days the highest terraces go almost dry, and everyone goes up there and lies on the warm stone in the strange absence of dripping, and calls it a holiday, and comes back down.

Light is ordinary — a normal sun behind a normal white sky — except that it arrives twice. Everything is lit from below by the water, so there are no true shadows in the Basin, only softenings. You can read under a table. The undersides of faces are lit. It makes everyone look kind and slightly unreal, and it means that at dawn and dusk, when the sun gets under the cloud deck, the whole hundred-thousand-square-kilometer floor goes copper at once, top and bottom, and the sails turn to foil, and for eleven minutes the loudest place in the world is also the most beautiful, and nobody stops working, because the copper hour is when the lickers breed and the herds have to be moved.

---

The tenantry live in the slows.

There are no rooms. A void is an emergency: within a day it fills with water, with mat, with crabs, with a family, with a market. What buildings offer is not enclosure but *retardation* — a place where the churn moves at a human rate instead of the city's rate. A house is a slow. A temple is a very slow. Property here is not a claim on space but a claim on a rate: the right to keep this cubic meter turning over no faster than you can manage. Wealthy households have deep slows, thick-walled, generations old. The poor live at the speed of the street and sleep in shifts in the moving crowd, and are not obviously unhappier.

Everything is being carried somewhere. That is the visible activity, the anthill surface of it: carrier gangs hauling salt cake, ingot, spore-meal, crab-meal, sealed flasks of gradient, stone seed, eels in wet cloth. The streets themselves migrate — a channel that ran north of the tower last month runs south of it now, because the water asked, and the tenancy went with it, and the stalls were re-precipitated in the new place overnight by the stone, which does this the way a body grows a callus. The market you shopped in on Tuesday is a weir on Friday and a market again by Sunday, three hundred meters east, with the same vendors, who will not mention it.

What the Basin exports is answers. Something beyond the Lip buys them, and pays in metal and salt and strangers. What it imports, mostly, is strangers. They come down the switchbacks with their ears ringing and their notion of emptiness intact, and they last a season, and the ones who stay stop flinching at crowds and start flinching at corners, and eventually they cannot sleep in a quiet room, and then they are from here.

---

At night nothing stops. The rain walks west. The crabs change shift with the lickers. The sails come down through the dark in their thousands to drink at the sheets and go up again before dawn, and the pale glows very faintly where it is thickest, a low pink you see only if you cover your eyes for a minute. The thrum goes on. The hiss goes on. Somewhere under the fourth terrace a slow one turns over in its cistern and a whole district's chord drops a third, and ten thousand people put their palms flat on the wall in their sleep.

And out past the Lip there is the ocean: cold, blue, empty, silent, stretching to a real horizon, with nothing in it for a thousand kilometers that is not water and light.

The people of the Basin know it is there. They hold the wall. They bail. They have decided, in the way a place decides things — slowly, without a meeting, over eleven thousand years — that they would rather be warm and crowded and deaf and alive than be that.
