# The Sough

You hear it four hours before you see it, and by then you have stopped believing it is a sound. It arrives first in the mule's shins, then in the cart's axle, then in the small bones behind your ear, and only last — grudgingly, as an afterthought — in the hearing itself. By the time the road climbs the scarp and the plateau opens out, the noise has become the medium you are traveling through, like warmth, like altitude. There is no direction to it. There is no getting away from it by turning your head.

Above the scarp: forty miles of bare grey limestone, laid out flat as a floor, jointed into slabs the size of dining tables, runnelled and scalloped and rounded at every edge by two hundred thousand years of rain. The stone is warm. It is always warm. This is a high country, high enough that the clouds come through at eye level, but it sits near enough the equator that the air stays at blood heat and the rock holds noon until midnight. Nothing here shivers.

And nothing here grows, or almost nothing. There is no soil on the Sough. There has never been soil. Rain falls on it and is gone in the time it takes to watch — down the fissures, down the swallow-holes, into the enormous dark plumbing beneath — and so the surface is a wet place with no puddles, a soaked place with no mud. The stone sweats. The air films your skin and your spectacles and the inside of your lungs. Every afternoon around four the sky quietly fills and lets go for eleven minutes, warm rain, ordinary rain, and the whole plateau hisses and drinks and is bare again. There is no dust anywhere on the Sough. There is also no mud. Visitors find this the first genuinely unnerving thing, before the sound, before anything: a landscape with the cleanliness of a scrubbed table.

---

The sound has floors to it, like a building.

At the bottom is the drainage — the entire plateau emptying itself into the rock at once, some millions of tons of warm water falling down shafts nobody has ever bottomed. That is the part you feel in your teeth. It sits below hearing and above nausea and it does not vary. Above it ride the penstocks and the turbines, three hundred of them, sunk in cave galleries under the towns, a mid-range roar with a grain to it, like a river made of gravel. Above that the kiln blowers and the compressed-air exhausts, which cough on a nine-second cycle. And over everything, everywhere, the highest floor: the hiss of the tufa sheets, ten thousand acres of water running two inches deep over stone, a sound exactly like rain that never stops falling and never gets heavier.

It comes to about a hundred and fifteen decibels at the combs and ninety in the streets. It has come to that for six hundred years. This is the thing that people from quiet countries cannot hold in their minds: the noise is not an event. Nothing is happening. The Sough is not loud the way a battle is loud. It is loud the way a mountain is tall.

---

The water does not simply leave. On its way down it is put to work, and the work is mostly *making more stone*.

The rain comes off the pavement loaded with dissolved lime, and where it is led out into thin broad sheets, over baffles and weirs and long shallow terraces, it gives that lime back. Millimeter by year, the terraces lay down a pale porous rock, cream-colored, full of the ghosts of leaves and bubbles — tufa, the stone that grows. The Sough farms it. There are stone fields out there running to the horizon in low contour bands, silver with moving water, steaming faintly at dawn, harvested by gangs who come through with wire saws every eleventh year and cut the crop out in blocks the size of loaves.

This is why the pavement is bare. It is not only geology; it is policy. Moss fouls the sheet, algae stains the block, roots break the crust. The scrapers keep the clints clean the way a dairy keeps its floor clean. The only green on the whole plateau is in the grikes — the deep fissures between slabs, waist-deep, cool, out of reach of the wire brushes — where ferns hang in wet green ribs and a few wind-bent shrubs hold on with their knuckles in the rock. You walk for an hour over grey nothing and then look down into a crack and there is a jungle in it two feet wide and forty feet long, dripping, breathing, full of small pale lives. Then the next crack, a quarter mile on. Then nothing again.

The stone the water takes away and the stone the water puts back are nearly in balance. The plateau lowers about a millimeter a century. The terraces rise about three millimeters a year. The city is slowly building itself out of a hole it is slowly sinking into, and everyone knows the arithmetic, and nobody is troubled by it, because the timescale is not a human one and never was.

---

Seven towns, and they are knots, not spreads. Each sits on a shaft-head where the water goes down, because that is where the power is, and each is dense — six and seven storeys of tufa block, layered and re-layered, arcades over older arcades, stairs that arrive at the third floor of a building whose ground floor was sealed two centuries ago. Kiln stacks. Carbide furnaces, since lime and coke and an arc will give you calcium carbide, and carbide and water will give you acetylene, so the Sough lights itself by burning its own dissolved rock: white flares at every gate, that faint garlic stink under the wet-chalk smell, lamplighters going round at dusk with their poles.

And the towns are nearly empty. A hundred thousand rooms and perhaps nine thousand people. Whole quarters standing maintained, swept, re-pointed, lit, and unoccupied. Machines fill them. The industry runs on falling water and does not require an audience; a crew of four will tend a gallery that occupies a city block. But that is only half the reason for the emptiness.

The other half is that most of the Sough is not habitable, in a sense that has nothing to do with air or water or heat.

---

The roar reflects. Off the pavement, off the walls, out of the shaft-mouths, and it interferes with itself, and so the plateau is threaded with *nodes* — narrow seams where the waves cancel and the sound falls away to almost nothing. They are not rooms. They are lines, wandering, a few feet wide, curving through streets and across courtyards and up the sides of buildings and in at one window and out at another. Step into a seam and the world drops out from under you. You can hear your own breath. You can hear a moth. It is so intimate it is nearly obscene, and people who have lived here all their lives still go slightly dizzy at the edge of one.

Everything human on the Sough happens along these threads. The markets are in them. The schools. The beds, the sickrooms, the places where a marriage is argued out and a death is sat with. The rest of the fabric — the vast majority of it — is for the water and the machines, and stands empty and immaculate and screaming.

And the seams move.

They drift because the caves beneath are enlarging, because the drainage is very slowly changing shape, because the note of the whole plateau is falling — a semitone, roughly, every two generations. The Ledger of the Note has been kept since before the furnaces, and it records the pitch and the positions of the quiets, street by street, decade by decade. A seam creeps perhaps a hand's breadth a year. Over a lifetime it will cross a house.

So there is a trade here called tuning: masons who go through the empty quarters shaving walls, adding mass, adjusting the dimensions of rooms so they do not fall into resonance with the new note and shake themselves to rubble. There is a condemned district in the fourth town that was not condemned for plague or fire but for going out of tune in the 1830s, and it came apart over eleven years, and the ledger has a photograph.

And there are families who have waited thirty years for silence to arrive in the kitchen. Who chart it. Who plaster and re-plaster the wall it will come through. A grandmother will say: *it was here when I was small, at the pantry, and it went out past the stairs in my mother's time, and it will be back at the range by the year I die.* And she means it as an ordinary statement about property.

---

Nobody over twenty hears. This is not a tragedy here; it is the baseline, the way everyone in a fishing country can swim. Children are taught the cant before they are taught letters — a fast, dense hand-language with a regional accent, spoken at the chest, one-handed when the other is carrying. And under the cant there is the older thing, the tap: knuckles and clog-soles on the stone, because the pavement carries vibration beautifully and the air carries nothing at all. Strike a clint with a mallet in the west field and a man two miles off will feel it in his heels and know the number. The whole plateau is a drumskin and has been used as one since before anyone thought to put a turbine in it.

Engineers diagnose by bone. They carry a short steel rod, bite one end, press the other to the machine casing, and listen with their skulls. It is a respected posture. You will see a woman standing motionless in a turbine gallery with a rod between her teeth and her eyes shut for four minutes, and she is doing something equivalent to a doctor with a stethoscope, and she will come out and sign *bearing, third, going* and be right.

The animals have solved it the same way. There are no songbirds — there cannot be; a song that no one can hear is a wasted life, and the Sough has been screaming since before the birds arrived. The only birds are vultures, which have no voice to lose, twenty or thirty of them turning over the kilns all afternoon on the warm updraft, saying nothing, beautifully. In the grikes there is a pale flightless cricket that does not chirp and instead drums its abdomen against the rock, and a gecko that courts by push-ups, and a small white crab that reads the world entirely through its feet. Once every few years someone brings back a blind fish from the resurgence and it is written down.

Below the scarp, where all the water finally comes out of the hillside in one enormous green mouth, there is a gorge with a real forest in it. You can see it from the edge on a clear day: a dark wet ribbon, ferns and moss and trees, deaf with birdsong. People go down there on holiday and cannot sleep.

---

The light, at least, is normal. That is worth saying. The sun is an ordinary sun; the shadows fall the way shadows fall; the four o'clock rain has a rainbow in it about one day in nine, and the carbide flares at night are white and steady, and the mist that comes up out of the shafts after dark is warm and smells of chalk and moves like mist anywhere. Nothing about the sky is strange. The sky is the only part of the Sough that behaves the way the world behaves elsewhere, and people are fond of it out of all proportion.

Once in a generation the water table drops below the intakes, or a gallery chokes, and there is the Hush.

It lasts three or four weeks. The terraces stop growing. The furnaces go cold. And nine thousand deaf people find themselves standing in a silence so total that they can hear their own blood, and some of them weep in the street, and some cannot bear it and go down into the gorge, and the children born in a Hush are marked in the ledger with a small circle beside their names, because they will grow up hearing, and will never entirely belong.

Then the water comes back up through the rock, over about nine days, floor by floor: the hiss, then the gravel-roar, then the thing below hearing that gets into your teeth. And everyone signs the same sign, which is not a word, just a hand laid flat and pressed down, the way you'd settle a lid.

The seam that will reach the kitchen in the fourth town in eleven years is currently crossing a doorway in an empty arcade three streets away, where nothing lives, where the tufa walls are freshly shaved and the lamps are lit and no one has stood in forty years. It moves a hand's breadth a year. It is on schedule. It has always been on schedule.
