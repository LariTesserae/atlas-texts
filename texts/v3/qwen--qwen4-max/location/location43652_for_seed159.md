# The Slake

## The Breach

You come down to it. That is the first fact, and it stays the first fact all day: the world here is four hundred meters under the world.

The plateau above is ordinary country, green and windy, with larks. Then the ground stops. A breach in the rim, a kilometer wide where an old cavern roof let go, opens onto a basin eleven kilometers long and three across, and the sun comes in through it sideways in the morning and straight down at noon, and the floor of the basin is bright. Not cave-bright. Not twilight. *Bright* — cream stone throwing light back up at you, glare off wet terraces, a place where you squint and the underside of your hat brim goes luminous and you are grateful for the shadow of a boulder. The walls rise around it in stacked benches of new rock, pale as tallow, streaked rust and sulfur and the pale green of nothing at all, because there is no green here. There has never been green here.

Below the rim the air changes on the third switchback of the Stair. It gets warm and wet and slightly sweet, like the inside of a mouth, and it starts to *hiss*.

## The stone that is not finished

The springs come up hot at the Throats, forty-one degrees, and they come up loaded. Carbonate, calcium, iron, a little sulfur, gas enough to make the water white. When that water reaches the air it lets go of its gas and drops its stone. It does this everywhere it runs, immediately, without pause.

So the basin is not eroding. It is being *added to*.

Every surface that stays wet is thickening. The terraces are rimstone: thousands upon thousands of shallow tubs, each one held by a lip of cream-colored travertine the width of your wrist, packed together down the fall of the floor like the cells of a comb, like frost on a window grown to the size of a house. Water spills from tub to tub to tub, a hundred thousand small overflows, and at each overflow the lip grows. A thumb's width in a week where the flow is fast. In a season the whole hillside is a different hillside.

The stone comes out of the water soft. This is the thing the whole place turns on. Fresh travertine, an hour old, has the consistency of hard cheese; you can push a thumbnail into it, you can saw it with a wire. In three days it is a building material. In a year it is a cliff.

Nothing here wears away. There are no exposed strata, no weathered-out fossils, no old surfaces. The past is not visible in the Slake. The past is *underneath*, being manufactured at a finger's width a year, and the only exposed thing anywhere is the youngest thing, still tacky, still taking impressions.

## Damp, not wet

There is less water than the noise suggests. Most of the floor is wet stone with a sheet moving over it a fingernail deep, fast enough to sting if you stand in it, thin enough that a hand-sized animal can walk anywhere. Real water gathers in pockets: the tubs of the Combs, each holding a bathtub's worth; the black Throat pools where the gas comes up in ropes; the Sump at the low southern end, which is deep and permanent and unpleasant.

Before dawn the walls sweat. The rock holds the night's cool for two hours after the sun hits the rim, and the basin's own humidity condenses on it in a film that runs and drips and makes a sound like distant applause, and every mat and every animal on the shaded benches drinks that film and then, at nine, has to survive the day.

Rain arrives on schedule. Warm floor, wet air, walls that funnel the wind: by three in the afternoon the basin has built itself a thunderhead over the Sump, and by four it is raining in the southern half and the northern half is watching it. The rim spills too, in the big storms, waterfalls that come apart into mist before they land, so that from below the flood arrives as warm fog and the *smell* of rain rather than rain.

## The Sour

Nothing photosynthesizes on this floor. Seeds fall in through the Breach and germinate on the damp benches and die within a week, white and swollen.

The springs exhale carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide is heavy, and a basin is a bowl. The gas pools in the low pockets and drains along the terraces and lies in the tubs like an invisible second water. The cutters call it the Sour. It moves. It has tides. A hollow that was safe at dawn can be lethal at noon and safe again after the storm mixes everything, and the whole ecology of the place is arranged around that sloshing.

This is why there are no plants and why nothing here has lungs that it depends on absolutely. The mats don't care. The scutch don't care. The birds stay above the line and dive through it. People carry a lamp and a caged bird, and they read the flame's height the way a sailor reads a sky, and they do not sleep in hollows, no matter how sheltered.

## The nap

Under everything, the mats: a felt of chemotrophs a millimeter thick, feeding on sulfur and iron and methane out of the water. Sulfur-oxidizers make white nap, iron-oxidizers make rust nap, the methanotrophs make a slate-grey nap that smells faintly of ponds. In the shaded seeps there are fungal veils, grey and translucent, running along the wet lines like frost-fern, digesting whatever the water carries down.

No stems. No leaves. No roots. A skin, everywhere, over stone that is thickening under it, so that the mats are constantly being buried from below and constantly growing up through the burial. Dig a hand's depth into any bench and you find the same nap again, and again, and again, a hundred laminations of it, thin as pages.

## The overwhelming

Now the animals, which are the point.

**Scutch.** Flat armored grazers, isopod-shaped, the size of a palm, in numbers that make the word *population* useless. They rasp the nap with a mouth like a file. They move in sheets — a rust-colored tide a hundred meters across, individuals touching, three deep, flowing over the terraces toward whichever seep is running best. When a rim breaches and a terrace drains in four minutes, ten thousand scutch are stranded on drying stone, and they pile into the shrinking tubs until the tub is a solid pavement of backs, and the ones on top climb the ones on the bottom, and by evening there is a crust of dead at the lip that the water will have sealed into the floor by the following week.

**Gauze.** The midges. Larvae in the wet nap, building little tapered cases of travertine grains cemented with silk, so that the shallows look like coarse sand until the sand starts moving. They emerge in the warm hours in columns. A column is a rope of insects two meters thick and three hundred tall, leaning in the wind, humming a note you feel in your sinuses; there are forty of them over the Combs at once and they sound like a chord played on a bowed saw. Their shed cases accumulate as a gritty white drift, which is the closest thing here to soil.

**Shafters.** Swift-like birds, sooty, sickle-winged, nesting in the wall cavities in colonies of tens of thousands. They come in through the Breach at dusk in a river that takes eleven minutes to pass, and they spend the day out over the green country eating insects and bringing that country back inside them. Their guano is the basin's import tariff: nitrogen and phosphorus falling from a world with plants into a world without, feeding the mats, feeding the scutch, feeding the midges, feeding them. The walls below the colonies are streaked white and stink and are the richest, thickest, fastest-growing stone in the Slake.

**Stilts.** Long-legged pale waders, waist-high, that pick through the scutch sheets on the shallow benches, silent except for the click of the beak, standing so still for so long that they get cast (below).

**Hooks.** Something mantis-shaped and predatory that lives in the rim-lips and takes stilts' chicks and gauze by the hundred, and which the cutters kill on sight with a copper-headed club.

And in the Sump, blind eels, arriving in flood pulses out of the aquifer, breeding in a week, dying in a week, so that once a month the southern shore is a wrack of them.

Every one of these has a boom and a crash on a scale of days. There is no equilibrium anywhere in the basin. There is a series of enormous overlapping surges, and the loudest thing about the place is that surge: hiss of degassing water, hum of the columns, scream of the shafters, applause of condensation, the crack and boom of a founding block somewhere on the wall, and the thin bright *chime* of a travertine plate the size of a door splitting off a lip and ringing on the stone. You shout here, or you use your hands. The cutters have a bone whistle with four signals and a vocabulary of about sixty signs.

## The Castings

Because the water drops its stone on everything, and because it does it fast, anything that stays still gets coated.

A stilt that stands an hour in a seep has ankles of cream rock. A stilt that stands a day has legs to the knee and cannot walk, and stands there, and is a stilt-shaped column by the end of the week, with the shape of the feathers still crisp on the outside and something inside it that has stopped being an animal and started being a void. There are tens of thousands of them across the Combs. Casts of birds mid-preen. Casts of scutch in the act of climbing each other. A cast of a hook with a midge column's worth of gauze still legible in its open mouth. The whole floor is a museum of the last few years, in which nothing has been curated and everything has been accessioned.

The cutters call the dense fields the Castings and walk around them, partly out of respect and partly because the ground there is a lattice of hollow shapes and will take your leg through it to the hip.

The rule is simple and everyone learns it in their first week: *keep moving, or become the floor.* Boots get welded overnight. A tool left on a wet bench is a tool forever. Sleep on the hard knobs, on stone that has already dried, and sleep on a hide, and move the hide.

## The scattered works

There are perhaps forty people in twenty square kilometers, and they are not a town. They are a dozen knots of activity, visible from the rim as specks.

They cut soft stone. At dawn, at the fresh front where the flow is running best, a cutter lays a copper wire — cold-hammered native copper traded down the Stair, precious, re-drawn and re-annealed until it is thinner than a grass stem — and saws out a block the size of a loaf, wet and heavy and pale, and drags it on a chitin sled to a drying bench. Three days later it is a building block, white and light and full of the ghosts of midge cases. Their huts are little beehives of it, one room, no windows on the windward side, set on hard knobs, rebuilt every couple of years because the terrace they stand on rises around them and eventually swallows the doorway.

They burn the stone in low kilns for lime, and use the lime on hides, and twist the larval silk into cord that is stronger than it has any right to be, and scrape guano from the lower wall ledges into gut bags for the farmers up on the rim, who pay in grain and wood. A wooden handle down here is an heirloom. There is one axe handle in the whole basin, worn to a shine, and it is owned in common.

They set hand-built weirs — a line of dry-laid blocks across a bench — to divert the sheet flow and make the stone grow where they want it. This is the only agriculture in the Slake: they farm the geology. A well-set weir will build a new terrace in a season, and a new terrace means a new seep front means soft stone means blocks means grain. A badly set weir will breach and drain three hundred meters of Combs and strand a million scutch and bring the hooks and the stilts and, for two glorious days, the shafters, all screaming, and then the sour air will pool in the emptied hollows and everything will die in it and be sealed under a fresh white skin.

They mark safe routes with cairns, and re-mark them constantly, because the cairns sink.

There is a Reading Stone at the head of the Stair, a slab where the day's Sour levels and the day's cutting fronts are scratched in a rough script. It is resurfaced with a wire every month. Nobody has ever found an old inscription in this basin. Old inscriptions are inside the rock.

## Four in the afternoon

The storm breaks over the Sump and the whole bowl exhales. Wind comes down the wall in a rotating slab, the midge columns bend and shred and re-form fifty meters away, the shafters go up in a single black spiral to ride above it, the scutch sheets stop grazing all at once and grip, and the sour air that has been lying in the low pockets all day gets stirred and sloshed and lifted and dumped somewhere else, so that a hollow which was safe becomes a death trap and a death trap becomes a place you can work.

Somewhere on the east wall a bench that has been growing too heavy for its own hollow foundation lets go with a sound like a door in the sky, and eight hundred tons of last year's stone comes down and breaks into blocks, and the dust goes up gold in the low sun, and the mats begin coating the rubble before the dust settles.

By evening the flow has reorganized. The map is wrong. The cutters stand at their doorways and read the water and decide, for tomorrow, where the stone will be soft.

## Night

Warm, thirty degrees, no cooling to speak of. The stars come into the Breach and lie in a band across the middle of the sky. The mats give off a faint blue-green shine in the seeps, not enough to walk by, enough to see the shapes of the Castings standing in ranks, thousands of them, birds and stilts and hooks and things nobody has named, all facing whatever direction they happened to be facing.

The hiss never stops. The gas keeps coming out of the water and the stone keeps coming out of the gas. The basin is four hundred meters below the world and eleven kilometers long and it is not finished, and the oldest thing you can put your hand on anywhere in it is a footprint from last spring.
