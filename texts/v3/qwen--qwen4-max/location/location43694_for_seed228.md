## The Sump

The heat comes up the chimney first, and then the noise, and only then the light — which is the wrong order, and everyone who descends notices it, and no one has ever gotten used to it.

The chimney is a slot in the floor of a dry karst plain, a meter wide at the mouth, narrowing to a squeeze, then opening. Nine meters of descent, damp the whole way, the rock at blood heat, so that climbing down feels less like entering the earth than being swallowed by something that has just been running a fever. At the squeeze the roar arrives all at once, a broadband white pressure that fills the ears like water fills a boot. Below it, felt more than heard, a fundamental around thirty hertz, steady, in the sternum and the teeth.

Then the slot widens and you come out onto the ledge, and you have to close your eyes.

---

The chamber is a single room. Ninety meters at its long axis, sixty across, the dome rising forty at the crown — you can walk its whole perimeter in six or seven minutes, and people do, over and over, because the perimeter is the only place to be. It has walls. It has a floor. It has an inside and an outside, and the outside is solid rock for kilometers in every direction, and this is the entire place. There is no onward. There is no deeper. Wells have been sunk from the floor and they come back up.

It is bright as a salt flat at noon, and there is no sun, and there is no lamp.

The water shines. Every wet surface in the room is a light source: the weeping walls, the inch-deep basins, the threads of drip, the film of condensation on your own forearm once you've been there a minute. It is not bioluminescence and it is not heat — the water is thirty-four degrees, warm as a bath going cold, and it comes up out of the rock already glowing and goes down again still glowing, and the glow does not fade with distance from anything, because it has no origin. Put a sample in a sealed flask and it shines in the flask. Carry it up the chimney and it shines in the daylight, faintly, all the way to the surface, where it stops. Nobody has explained the stopping.

The spectrum is continuous except for four narrow missing bands, evenly spaced, like teeth pulled from a comb.

Because the light comes from the wet and not from a point, the room has almost no shadows. This is the thing that breaks people, more than the noise. Your face is lit from below by the floor and from the sides by the walls and from above by the drip, and so are the faces of anyone with you, and everyone looks slightly unreal, slightly canceled, like a drawing with the shading erased. The only true darkness in the chamber is under the ledges, in the pores of the flowstone, in the mouths of things — and it is into these few square meters of dark that all the life in the place has crowded.

---

The water does not fall. Or: it falls, but that is only half of what it does, and the half that matters goes the other way.

The cycle takes seventy-one seconds. It begins with a hush — four and a half seconds in which the roar drops away so completely that you can hear the drip, and your own breathing, and the small dry tick somewhere in the wall that is the only sound the machinery makes. In the hush the basins go flat. Then the upwelling: water comes up through the floor's sockets, not bubbling but *standing*, rising in columns and sheets and braided combs, climbing the walls in reverse falls, meeting itself in the air, holding. For eleven or twelve seconds the room is full of a figure — a shape of moving water thirty meters tall, fluted, symmetrical or deliberately not, different every time, never once repeated in the recorded history of the place. It is bright enough to hurt. It is standing in the middle of a cave with no support and no visible cause and it is the most beautiful thing most people will ever see.

Then it loses its argument with itself and comes apart, and the roar is the coming apart, and the water goes down the drains it made, and the floor empties, and the walls resume weeping, and the seventy-one seconds start again.

Nothing about this is erosion. That's the first thing to understand and the last thing to believe. The chamber is *finished*. The flowstone terraces, the sockets, the fluted grooves that run up the walls in fans, the eleven basins with their lips worn to a softness no waterfall achieves in a hundred thousand years — all of it is old, all of it is settled, none of it is being cut anymore. Measure the room twice a decade and you get the same room. The stone has worn into the shape of the *average* of every figure the water has ever made: a statue of a statistic, a mold that fits chaos in general and no chaos in particular. The churning is not what made this place. The churning is how this place is held.

The corollary is the second rule, and the one that matters if you're carrying anything. The room is maintained. Everything else is averaged. Leave a steel knife in a basin and in six hours it is a lozenge, edges gone, corners rounded, the maker's stamp smoothed to a suggestion. Leave a boot and it becomes a smooth boot-shaped thing with no laces and no seams. Bone goes the same way, which is why there is no bone here, and no sediment, and no dust, and no litter, and no record. The floor is swept clean seventy-one seconds at a time and has been for longer than there have been people to notice.

Which means: don't stand in a socket when the hush ends. The water fits the stone exactly. There is no room in the fit for you.

---

The built things are few, and they survive because they are dry.

Nine stumps at the rim, knee-high, spaced with an intent you can feel in your legs as you walk between them — a ceramic-metal composite, pale gray, warm, and permanently dry; condensation approaches their surfaces and declines to arrive. A stair on the north side that goes eleven steps down into flowstone and stops, the rest grown over, the handrail still dry, still smooth, still the exact temperature of a hand. A rectangle in the west wall, doorway-sized, perfectly flat, not a doorway — nothing behind it, nothing around it, a rectangle for its own sake.

And, tucked under the ledge where you come out, a cube. Three meters on a side, open on one face, the same dry gray material. Inside, the roar falls to a hum and the light falls to almost nothing, because nothing in there is wet. There is a bench. The bench has a groove worn into it, the width and depth of a person who sat in one position for a very long time. There is a cup, unglazed, dry, upright. And on the wall beside the bench there are tally marks in groups of five, thousands upon thousands of them, scratched with something harder than the wall should allow, covering every surface reachable from a seated position and then continuing at standing height and then continuing above that, on the ceiling, which means they went on for a long time and then they got taller or the room got smaller.

Seventy-one seconds per mark. Do the arithmetic; it comes out to a little over a year, which is either a watch or a sentence.

The last group has three strokes and a fourth begun.

---

Life is scarce, and it is scarce *because* of the light. The glowing water is sterile — nothing grows in it, nothing lives in it, no film forms on it. Whatever the machinery did to make water carry light also made water uninhabitable, and so the ecology of the chamber is an ecology of the dark places, a map of the few square meters the shine doesn't reach.

Under the ledges: mats of amber felt, chemotrophic, warm to the touch, older than they look, growing a millimeter a decade. Four stands — only four, widely separated — of hollow pale reeds, knee-high, no green anywhere in them, that whistle a thin note when the pulse pushes air through their tubes, so that the upwelling has a chord above the roar. In the dry room's threshold, where the wet meets the dry and neither wins, a crust of something like lichen that is not lichen and that assays as partly metal. That is the whole flora. Between these presences: bare wet shining stone, meters and meters of it, the emptiness that makes the room feel like a room and not a jungle.

The fauna is one animal, and you may not see it. It lives in the pores of the flowstone, palm-sized, blind, white, flat as a leaf, and it comes out only in the hush — four and a half seconds, out and across a hand's width of open stone and back into the dark before the water stands up. It crosses on the beat. It has never been observed to be caught by a pulse. Once an hour, perhaps, if you sit still on the ledge and watch the right patch and stop looking at the light.

Nothing flies here. Nothing swims. Birds that fall down the chimney are averaged by morning into smooth pale ovals with no feathers, and the plain above has learned, in whatever way plains learn, to route its small animals around the slot.

---

Two more things, and then the hush.

The air is at thirty-four degrees and saturated, so sweat does not leave you. You get wet from standing still. You will drink more water than you have ever drunk and it will not help much, and the danger here is not cold or drowning but heat, quietly, in a bright warm room where nothing seems threatening except the ninety-five decibels and the thirty-meter sculpture of standing light.

And the water remembers, briefly. Wade a basin and your wake persists after you stop — a low ridge of glowing water in the shape of your passing, held for the rest of the cycle, so that in the upwelling the figure sometimes has in it a faint fluted echo of a person, incorporated, resolved, gone. It is not a message. It is a property. The room does not know you are there; it only fits whatever is in it, and then it fits the average, and the average does not include you.

Somewhere out in the rock, past the wells that come back up, something enormous is turning very slowly, and this room is where the load is carried, and the churn is the sound of a bearing doing its work. The hush is the moment the load shifts. In the hush the drip is audible, and the tick in the wall, and the reeds' small dying note, and the flat basins hold their light without moving, and a white blind animal crosses a hand's width of open stone in the dark under the ledge and gets home in time.

Then the floor stands up.
