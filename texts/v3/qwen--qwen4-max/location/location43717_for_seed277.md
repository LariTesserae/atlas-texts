## The Anneal

The stone is warm. That is the first fact and the last one; everything else here is a consequence.

Put a palm flat on the pavement and you will feel a heat like a sleeping body, thirty-eight degrees, blood-warm, steady at midnight and steady at noon — though there is no midnight and no noon, only the long maroon dark and the darker dark. The warmth comes up from below. Four hundred meters down, a sheet of molten rock lies spread between the older strata like a hand slipped under a tablecloth, and it has lain there since before anything on this surface had a spine. It does not erupt. It rarely has. It simply leans upward through the basalt, patient as a landlord, and keeps the whole tableland at the temperature of a fever breaking.

The tableland is the size of a continent. It has an escarpment on its seaward side eleven hundred meters high, and from the lip of that escarpment the ground runs inland, level as poured metal, past the horizon and past the next one. It is paved in polygons — the old columnar joints of the cooling flow, hexagons three and four meters across, their edges rounded off by a hundred million years of weather until the whole surface looks less like masonry than like the skin of something enormous and asleep. Grit collects in the seams. In the seams, thin filaments of metal grow.

There is no sun here. There is a lid: a permanent stratus deck at four kilometers, held in place by the plateau's own convection, and it reflects back down what little light the ground makes. So the sky is a low dull mirror, maroon and slightly grainy, and if you learn to read it you can see the weather coming — not as clouds, but as the *shadow of the ground beneath the weather*, a paleness sliding across the ceiling days before the rain arrives at your feet.

---

The chimneys stand in fields.

They are sinter, grown from the water that goes down cracked and comes up loaded. Rain sinks, meets heat, turns, dissolves iron and silica and sulfur out of the rock, and returns through the vents carrying its cargo; and at the lip of each vent the cargo precipitates, ring on ring, and the ring becomes a pipe, and the pipe becomes a flue, and the flue stands four meters, nine meters, twenty. Some are slender and fluted. Some are bulbous, tiered, crusted with terraces of orange and dull green where the overflow has settled out in basins — settling tanks, exactly settling tanks, in rows, in series, each one a little clearer than the last. Whole valleys of them. A hundred kilometers of pipe-work breathing at once, and the breath is a thin haze that smells of hot iron and struck matches and, after the rain, of ozone.

Nothing here was built. Everything here was manufactured.

That distinction is the whole character of the place, and it takes a while to feel. There are no ruins. There is no worked stone, no slag heap that someone shoveled, no road, nothing anywhere with a hand behind it. But the processes that a hand would have organized have organized themselves: leaching, transport, precipitation, sorting by solubility, waste heat, exhaust. The plateau runs a refinery. It has run it since the Eocene. It has no product. The product is the refinery.

---

Here is the rule that makes it alive.

The ore bodies are sulfides, and sulfide in wet contact with iron-rich rock is a battery. Each lode is a cell. The pavement between them is a circuit with an enormous resistance, and in the dry season that resistance is effectively infinite, and the whole continent lies idle — warm, dark, silent, ticking, waiting.

Then water comes, and lays a film a few molecules thick over everything, and the circuit closes.

Current begins to move through the ground. Not lightning — nothing so quick. A slow amperage, kilometers wide, following the ore veins, leaking up through the damp grit. Where it crosses a contact between two rock types it makes a faint blue crawl, ankle-high, that runs along the seam for a hundred meters and dies. Where a chimney's flue gases are rich enough, the discharge climbs the pipe and stands at its mouth as a bead of cold light the size of a fist.

And the mats wake up.

The vegetation here does not eat light; there is none. It eats the current. Wire-lichen — a low grey-green crust with metallic filaments running through it like the veins of a leaf — grows along the potential gradients, threading itself between anode and cathode, thickening where the flow is thick, thin and starved where the ground is dry. Sparse: a few percent cover, scattered patches on a great deal of bare warm stone. But when the wet comes those patches flush a dull copper color from edge to edge in about four hours, and put up fruiting stalks no taller than a finger, and the air over them fills with a smell like a coin held too long in the mouth.

There are tube-groves at the big vents, thickets of pale hollow cylinders that filter sulfur out of the plume, and they are the only things here that could be called trees, and they are not trees, and they cluster and stop, cluster and stop, and between them there is nothing but warm hexagonal stone going on and on.

---

The rain is not a season. It is a place, and it walks.

The plateau's heat draws moist air off the distant ocean, up over the escarpment, and lifts it, and wrings it out in a single wandering band of weather some two hundred kilometers wide and eleven hundred long. That band drifts. It has drifted, in a slow irregular circuit, for as long as there has been a plateau. It moves at about the pace of a grazing animal. It takes something over a year to come around again to any given vent field, and behind it the ground steams for a week and then goes dry for four hundred days.

So the wet edge is the front of the world. Everything follows it.

You can hear the herd an hour before you see it, and what you hear is not bellowing. It is a soft irregular chiming, like a distant shelf of glassware being moved by someone careful. The platers — squat, six-legged, a meter across, grazing the wire-lichen and the sulfur mats — plate their own shells out of the current. Metal deposition on a living cathode: they are, each of them, a small electrolytic cell with legs. Their carapaces come out in the colors of tempering steel, straw and bronze and blue-black, and when the herd walks, the shells knock. There are millions of them. They are also, at any moment, hundreds of meters apart. Abundance here is a statistic you infer over a hundred kilometers, not something that crowds you.

Above the vents, flue-swifts. Tens of thousands in a single chimney field, nesting in the warm throats, riding the thermal columns in a slow rotating wheel that never quite disperses, and their guano feeds the mats, and the mats feed the platers, and the platers' molted shells lie scattered over the pavement in drifts — the only debris on this continent that looks like industrial scrap, and it is skin.

And the tenders: big, slow, hump-shouldered, with dorsal cisterns of porous bone in which they carry eleven or twelve liters of water across the dry interior. They go to the salt licks at the electrical contacts, because they crave the salt, and they leak there, because they always leak there, and their leakage is what closes the circuit at a hundred thousand junctions across the tableland. They do not know this. Nothing knows this. The maintenance crew of a continent-sized factory is a herd of thirsty animals licking a rock.

---

The hunters are flat-headed and blind and they read the field.

A body moving across damp conductive ground distorts the potential gradient — a small dimple in a very large sheet of voltage. The hunters carry an array of pits along the front of the skull, wide as a shovel, and they lie in the grit and wait for a dimple. The platers' defense is to stop, and to ground: to press the belly flat, spread the legs, and shunt their signal into the stone. A herd under threat becomes a chain, each animal touching the next, and the whole chain's electrical signature smears out into something too broad and vague to be a body. From above — if there were an above — it would look like a hundred animals holding hands in the dark, which is essentially what it is.

This is the loudest thing that happens here, and it makes no sound at all.

---

Mostly it is quiet. The chimneys breathe rather than roar; the flow is laminar, the throats are smooth, grown smooth, tuned by a hundred million years of the same gas at the same pressure. The great hum of the current is at four hertz and you feel it in the sternum and the back teeth and never hear it. Water ticks in the sinter basins. The pavement ticks as it expands and contracts by a tenth of a degree. Swifts chatter at altitude, thinly, like a radio in another room.

Once or twice a season a flue outgrows its own strength and falls, and that sound — a dropped sheet of iron, a hundred tonnes of it — carries sixty kilometers across the flat and arrives as a single flat *whump* with no echo, and everything within earshot goes still for eight seconds, and then resumes. The fallen flue will be dissolved and regrown. The chimneys grow at exactly the rate the wind erodes them; they have for a very long time. The escarpment retreats a millimeter a century. The polygons wear round and are re-cut by cooling. Nothing here is young and nothing is dying. The refinery is in steady state, and has been since before there were mammals, and the animals inside it are not its purpose, they are its bearings.

---

Stand at the escarpment on a dry night. Below and behind you the tableland goes out flat and warm and dark for two thousand kilometers, sparsely lit: a bead of blue at a pipe mouth, a copper flush of lichen where a tender leaked, the slow revolving wheel of swifts over a vent field, a chain of chiming shells holding still because something flat-headed is lying in the grit nearby. Above you the cloud lid shows you a paleness moving — the wet edge, four days out, dragging its closed circuit behind it, bringing the shift change.

The air is warm on your face. The rock is warm through your boots. Somewhere under everything, the sill goes on leaning upward, and the water goes down cracked and comes up loaded, and the continent quietly, without anyone at all, keeps working.
