## The Warm Throat

Above it, the mountain is a bright and unbreathable place: a summit plateau of shattered andesite at the height where snow does not melt but simply thins away into the air, grain by grain, all winter. The wind up there has a edge like a blade held flat. Nothing lives on the plateau except a lichen the color of old rust, which grows a fingernail's width in a century, and one tree — a meter high, four hundred years old, lying along the ground like something that gave up standing and decided to continue anyway.

The tree is the sign. Its roots go down into a cleft between two boulders, and if you follow the roots you find the cleft is not a cleft but a chimney, choked at the top with fallen blocks, and out of the gaps between the blocks comes a breath at blood heat, and a sound.

You hear the sound before you understand it, from two hundred paces away, as a pressure behind the sternum. Closer, it separates into layers. On top, a hiss like rain falling on dry leaves, continuous, without pulse or gap. Underneath that, a low bowed note, so steady it seems less like a noise than a property of the stone, the way wetness is a property of water. Underneath *that*, below hearing, something enormous and slow turning over, once every two seconds, which you feel in the fluid of your eyes.

The chimney goes down sixty meters through three turns. The turns are why it is dark. Light enters at the top in handfuls and is spent on the walls before it reaches anything; the rock is warm to the touch, damp, and greasy-feeling, and it does not reflect. At the bottom the darkness is nearly total, and warm, and loud, and full.

---

The chamber at the bottom is thirty paces across and shaped like the inside of a cupped hand. It has one floor, one wall, one ceiling of jammed boulders, and a low fissure at the base where the mountain's cold air comes in. That fissure is the whole reason the room exists. Heat rises from somewhere far below — not volcanic fire, nothing so dramatic, just the patient warmth of depth leaking up a fault — and it draws cold thin air in through the fissure, and the two meet at about twelve meters above the floor and make a fog.

The fog is permanent. It is a stratum, a floor of cloud you could stand on if you were light enough, and it is where the room's water lives. Above it the walls are dry and warm. Below it they sweat. The fog condenses on the cold ceiling blocks and drips back down through itself, so that inside this room, sixty meters under a summit where precipitation has not fallen as liquid in eleven thousand years, it rains. Slowly. A drip every few seconds somewhere on the floor. The rain never stops and never amounts to anything.

There is one pool, the size of a shield, in a worn basin near the fissure. Tepid, faintly sulfurous, never rising, never emptying. Around it a collar of moss, and along a green-black seam in the wall a hand's-breadth of another moss, and above, hanging down through the chimney's last turn, the tree's roots: pale, fingered, blind, groping in the dark for the fog. They drink there and go back up. That is all the vegetation. Two mosses, one lichen, and a root system belonging to a plant that lives in another world.

---

The floor moves.

They are called grigs, where they are called anything, which is nowhere. Wingless, blind, the length of a thumb, amber going brown going almost black with age. They cover the floor three and four deep, and the wall to a height of nine meters in a living felt, and in places they hang from the ceiling blocks in ropes of themselves, holding on to each other, which is the only structure anything here ever makes. Eleven million, perhaps. The number stops meaning anything at about the second layer.

Each grig carries, in a groove under its thorax, a flake of black glass.

The glass comes from a vein in the eastern wall, an old dome's remnant, obsidian so fine it breaks with a sound like a word being cut off. The grigs get it by prising: a hundred, a thousand of them wedging into a cooling crack and pushing, all together, for a season, until a plate lets go and shatters. No individual makes anything. The crowd quarries the way water quarries. Then each grig tests the fragments — presses one to the wall, draws the file of its forearm across it, listens with its legs — and keeps the one that rings, and drops the rest.

They do not carry the flakes as possessions. They wear them out. A flake dulls in a year or two of scraping and is let go, and another is taken, and the discarded one falls into the floor's litter, which is a scree of used glass forty centimeters deep, layered like leaf-fall, each layer a few generations thick. Under it, older layers. Under those, layers where the glass is smaller and cruder, because the vein was different then.

An archaeologist would spend a career arguing about them. No retouch, no hafting, no symmetry. Only wear: a bright polish along one edge, in exactly the place a forearm would ride. There are no hearths, no nests, no walls, no burials, nothing stacked or joined or kept. The whole industry leaves behind nothing but polish and a hole in a vein. It is a technology with no artifacts and no toolmakers, only users, and it has been running for two hundred thousand years.

---

Here is what the glass is for.

A grig alone is a small insect making a small hiss. Pressed against the wall through a flake of obsidian, its file becomes a bow, and the wall becomes the instrument. Thirty paces of resonant andesite, sixty meters of standing air. Eleven million bows.

The hiss is their bodies. The low note is the room, played by them.

And because it is a room, it has nodes. The low note reflects and folds and cancels itself, and there are places — pockets a meter or two across, scattered through the floor and up the wall — where the note simply is not. Stand in one and the pressure leaves your chest. The hiss remains, and without the note under it the hiss sounds thin, distant, like weather in another valley. The grigs call them nothing, but they know them absolutely. A grig navigates by the low note the way a fish navigates by current. In a hush it is blind in a second sense, and it turns back.

So the hushes are the only territory in the world.

The spiders wait in them: pale, long, web-less, sitting in a silence pocket with their legs out, taking whatever drifts in disoriented. The centipedes hunt the boundaries, working the seam where note meets no-note, where grigs hesitate. The dying drift into hushes, having lost the strength to hold a bearing, and there the springtails and the pale fungus threads take them apart in a week, and the mites — every grig carries mites, and the mites carry their own smaller riders, so that the density here is not eleven million but a number with no bottom — the mites leave the cooling body and walk back into the crowd.

In the pool there is one salamander, white, eyeless, older than it should be. It eats what falls in. It has never needed to be more than one.

And once or twice a year something comes down the chimney from the bright world: a snowflake the size of a coin, a wind-killed bird, a moth that mistook the warmth for a season. The room takes it. A bird is gone in a day, feathers and all, and the only trace is a small clean articulation of bones lying in the glass, which the next thousand feet will scatter.

---

Everything here is slow.

The fog does not lift. The pool does not fill. The crowd's density shifts by a body's width in an hour, in tides nobody could see from inside them. The grigs live four years and their pattern does not change in four hundred.

But the wall is being dressed. Eleven million files, riding glass, riding stone, wear the rock. There are grooves — bands of them, hand-deep, running around the chamber at nine heights, each band a place where the crowd found the wall's best voice and worked it. The lowest band is the living one. The higher bands are abandoned, smooth, dead, and they are a record: as the room wears wider, its note falls. A semitone every four centuries, near enough. The grigs follow the note down the wall, generation by generation, the way a shoreline follows a sea.

The hushes move too. That is the slowest thing of all. As the note falls, the whole map of silences slides across the floor, a hand's breadth in a decade, and the spider lineages follow their pockets — a family holding the same absence of sound for six thousand years, migrating eleven meters, building nothing, owning nothing, inheriting only a place where the mountain does not speak.

Above the highest groove there is a band so old it has been softened by fog into a suggestion. Higher still, near the ceiling, the wall is virgin, and there is a note the room used to make when it was narrower, and no living thing has heard it.

---

Once a month, for eleven minutes, the moon lines up with the chimney's turns and a coin of light gets all the way to the floor. It lands on the glass and the glass throws it back in a thousand small black sparks, and the crowd does not react at all, because none of them have eyes, and the salamander does not react, and the spider in its hush does not react. The light is for nobody. It is the one thing in the room that has no function.

Then it goes, and the warmth stays, and the fog stays, and the rain that amounts to nothing keeps falling, and the mountain goes on bowing itself in the dark at a pitch too low and too patient for anything outside to notice — thin air above, ice that will not melt, a tree lying on its side holding on to the hole with both hands.

The room will widen until it opens, and the cold will come in, and the note will stop. Not for a hundred thousand years. There is no hurry here. There has never been any hurry here.
