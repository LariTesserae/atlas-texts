The roar arrives before the place does. You feel it in the sternum on the descent, a pressure with grain to it, and by the time the fog takes you it has stopped being a sound and become a medium — something you are inside of, like heat. Later, in the city, you will learn to distinguish its parts. At first it is only the noise the world makes here, and it does not vary, and it never has.

The valley is four kilometers long and less than one wide, a shallow trough dropped seventy meters below the rim of a limestone plateau, its floor barely sixty meters above the sea it has never seen. Warm water comes out of the plateau's foot at fifty-seven degrees, some hundreds of separate mouths, and it comes out saturated: heavy with dissolved carbonate, sulfide, silica, iron. Where it spills and cools and gives up its gas, it lays down stone. It has been laying down stone here for eleven thousand years at a rate that has not measurably changed. The whole valley is a stair of pools — three thousand of them, rimstone basins stepping down and down, each lip a rounded dam of cream-white travertine, each basin brimming and spilling evenly over its entire circumference in a sheet a finger deep. Ochre where the iron is. Sulfur-yellow near the vents. Rust-red in the fast chutes. In the cool lower basins, a green so dark it reads as black.

Above it all sits the ceiling: a stratus lid two hundred meters up, made by the valley's own evaporation, held in place by the valley's own heat. It does not move. It leaks at a constant fine drizzle, warm, and the drizzle is the only weather that has ever occurred here. The light comes through it evenly and from everywhere, the color of the inside of a shell, and there are no shadows in this place at all. Nothing has a shadow. Distance is judged by loudness. Night is the ceiling dimming by half, a bruise-colored dusk with no stars in it, and dawn is the same event reversed, and both happen at hours nobody can point to in the sky.

The air is thirty-three degrees and cannot take another drop of water. Sweat does nothing. You are wet within a minute of arriving and you stay wet, and the smell is sulfur at the top of the valley, ammonia in the middle, brine and iodine at the bottom, and under all of it, everywhere, always, the smell of the first minute of rain — geosmin, breathed out by the mats. It is the smell of a rain that is perpetually about to start and never does, because it has already started and will not stop.

---

There are no plants. There is no soil for them, no dust, no sand, no seed bank; the valley is ringed by a hundred kilometers of salt pan and dry nothing, and what few seeds arrive on the feet of birds germinate in the warm shallows and are sheathed in calcite within a fortnight. The city keeps these. Small white buds, hard as sugar, with a green ghost at the center if you crack one. They are the most valuable objects in the valley and they are not money; they are relics.

What lives here instead is slime. Sulfur-eating streamers, white and waving, in the hot chutes. Felted mats, ankle-deep in places, in the tepid basins — cream, rust, black-green, layered like the pages of a wet book. Fungal fur on the guano banks. This is the entire bottom of the food web and it is fantastically productive, and everything above it is crowded.

Snails carpet every wetted surface, ten thousand to the square meter, grazing the felt; their shells fall and cement and the stone is full of them, so that cutting a wall is like cutting a fruitcake. Flies stand in clouds over the hot pools, larvae in the mats, and the clouds make a high needle-tone that sits above the water's roar like a wire stretched over a drum. Small fish, thick and stupid and heat-hardened, pack the tepid channels. Crabs work the outflow. And on the rimstone dams, on every dam, in colonies that run the length of the valley, the waders: long-legged, white-bodied, shrimp-pink at the joint, filtering the mats with their heads upside down. Two hundred thousand of them. Their nesting cones — calcite grit and guano, rebuilt daily — raise the dams faster than the water does, which means the birds are also geology. Their noise is brass, ragged, unceasing, and it is louder than the falls.

In the old caves up in the plateau wall, where the water comes from, swiftlets nest in the tens of thousands and throw gravel-sounds into the dark. At dusk the bats come out of the same mouths and go up into the fog to eat the flies. In the coldest lower pools, the dead shrimp glow faintly for a night, bacteria doing what bacteria do, and this is the only light in the valley that does not come from the ceiling.

---

The city is on the middle terraces, eleven thousand people, and it is not built so much as kept open.

Understand what the water does. Every surface it touches, it thickens. A centimeter a year on the spray faces, five in the fast chutes. Doors narrow. Channels shallow. Streets close. So the city's entire labor is subtraction: ten thousand scrapers working every wetted face every day, cutting back exactly what the water lays down. The buildings are near-touching because they are touching — they are one continuous mass of travertine, growing together, and the masons' craft is the maintenance of separations. The gaps. The negative space. A doorway is not a structure; it is an achievement, renewed each morning. A street is a canyon that would heal in a decade. The poor live in narrowing rooms. A house left alone for three generations is not a ruin. It is a hill.

There is no rubble here, no dust, no worn step, no rounded edge, no patina. Nothing is old. The stone under your hand was dissolved out of a mountain last year and precipitated last month and cut yesterday, and it is sharp, and it is warm, and it is wet, and it is being made faster than anything can wear it away. At the downvalley end the newest terraces are still gel, rims you can push a hand through, pools with no proper lip yet, the land visibly not finished. Upvalley, at the vents, the rock is arriving. In between, the city holds its shape.

This is the thing that takes visitors longest to grasp. Everything here is being replaced and nothing here is changing. The water builds, the scrapers cut, the rates match, and they have matched for four hundred years. The city is the shape of an agreement between a spring and a population. It is a standing wave. It is a flame, which is a shape that stays still while everything in it goes through.

---

They have no fire. There is no wood, no dry dung, nothing anywhere that will catch; the only dry places in the world are up in the caves, and dryness is a thing priests travel to obtain. So they cook in the Kettles, the three vents at ninety-six degrees, baskets of fish and eggs and mat-cake lowered on cords. No kiln, no pottery, no smelting. Their tools are chert and obsidian traded down from the rim, native copper hammered cold, bone, and mostly the local stone against itself. Cordage is the great expense — hair, gut, braided tendon, and imported flax, which functions as currency. They export natron from the outflow lagoon, salt-cured fish, feathers, and casts: the perfect white hollows of things.

Because when a house seals, the mats and the beetles get in first and eat everything soft, and then the stone finishes filling, and what remains inside the hill is a void in the exact shape of a basket. A sleeping mat. A curled body. Masons cutting a new room break into these hollows sometimes and find old air, and the shape of a bowl, and a handprint with the ridges still in it. They plug most of them. Some they keep, and set a lamp of fish oil at the mouth, and these are the shrines.

Their records are grooves. Incisions fill in — a line left unattended for a year is a faint line, for five years is gone — so the archive is a wall of ten thousand grooves maintained by a guild of re-cutters, and importance is depth, and a thing repeated often enough becomes deep enough to be true. To forget is to let a thing fill in. They have no word for a lie that distinguishes it from a neglected fact.

Time is thickness. A reference rod stands in a reference pool at a reference depth, and it gains its centimeter, and the notch is cut, and that is a year. Ages are stated in fingers of stone. There are no seasons, no floods, no droughts, no frost, no wind but the steady indraft the water itself makes at the rim. Their word for change is their word for error. Their word for justice is their word for level.

---

Nobody can hear anybody. The roar sits at ninety decibels in the streets and a hundred and ten in the colonies, and everyone here loses the top of their hearing by twenty and lives the rest of their life inside a pressure. So they speak with their hands, on each other: a language of scrapes, drawn along the forearm, the shoulder blade, the nape. It grew out of grooming, which is the necessary act — you must be scraped or you crust, rime on the eyebrows, plaques on the shoulders, a white lace growing where the spray found you and you were too tired to attend to it. To talk to someone is to clean them. Affection is a slow polish with a soft cloth. Contempt is a coarse grit, fast. Names are patterns tapped on the back of the hand. Fashion is deliberate: standing in a chosen spray to grow a chosen whiteness. Mourning is letting yourself go.

They also all sing, constantly, inaudibly to anyone but themselves, bone conduction carrying it up through the jaw — a city of eleven thousand people each in a private opera, and it is the worst rudeness to watch a moving mouth. Children, whose ears are still intact, are apprenticed as instruments: pressed against walls to listen for hollows, for the change in a channel, for a crack. They retire at ten. And the walls carry taps, because the city is one stone, so a knuckle on a partition travels a hundred meters, and everyone feels everyone, and there is no privacy of any kind, and nobody has ever conceived of it.

The dead go into the pools and become the walls, and the walls are cut back and re-cut, so the ancestors circulate. Unless you are important, in which case you are carried up to the dry caves and kept, whole and brown and light, in the only air in the world that will not add anything to you.

That is the inversion at the center of this place. Nothing here is worn. Their own tools do not dull by use, only by encrustation. So an imported chert blade, rounded, scratched, thin from a century of sharpening — a thing that has been used and has not been renewed — is the holiest object they can imagine. They pass such things hand to hand in the dark and cannot explain why they weep.

Traders come down the stair from the rim, from the dry world, where there is a sun. Those who go up and see it come back sun-blind and strange and unable to render it in touch. They try. They put a warm palm on your face and hold it there, and take it away, and put it back, and this is not it, and they know it is not it.

The city's name is two flat hands sliding over one another, leveling. It means *held*. It means *even*. It means the debt is paid to the water for another day. Below, the nursery pools are still setting. Above, the mouths are still pouring. In between, ten thousand scrapers hiss under the roar, and the doors stay exactly as wide as they were, and the light does not change, and the noise does not change, and the smell of the rain about to fall goes on and on.
