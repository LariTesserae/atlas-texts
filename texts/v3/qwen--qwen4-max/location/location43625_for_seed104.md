Nothing here is lower than anything else.

That is the first fact, and the others come out of it. The rock is a shield of gneiss a billion years older than the idea of hills, and it has been worn to a plane so nearly perfect that a plumb line, hung anywhere on it, agrees with a plumb line hung four hundred leagues away to within the width of a hair. Rivers used to run here. You can find their signatures in the stone if you kneel and put your face close: braided ghost-channels a hand deep, filled with silt, going nowhere in particular. The last river stopped in a season no one would have noticed. It had cut everything it could cut. There was no downhill left to want.

So the water stayed.

It is warm — blood-warm, thirty-four degrees at the silt line and warmer in the plumes — and it lies over the plain in a sheet that is knee-deep in one province and overhead in the next and, averaged across the continent, deep enough to be an ocean by volume while never once being deep enough to be an ocean by feeling. You wade. You swim a few strokes and your hand finds the bottom again, polished, faintly furred. The water has no surface. This is the thing that takes longest to accept. Swim upward and it thins, becomes schlieren and drift, becomes a warm fog with the same green-brown light in it, and the fog thins in turn, and it never stops being water. There is no line to break. Two hundred meters up you are in a ceiling of vapor that goes on becoming less without ever becoming air. It rains from there constantly, all across the world, water falling into water, which makes no sound at all.

The heat is old heat. Every river that ever ran here spent its energy on friction, and the friction went into the stone and the water, and there is no gradient left to carry it away. The plain is warm with the memory of motion.

---

Light comes down through the lid as a general rumor. There is no disc, no direction, no hour you could point to and call noon, though the ceiling does brighten on a cycle of about twenty-six hours, swelling to the color of weak tea and then subsiding toward the color of a bruise on the inside of an eyelid. Shadows are soft and point every way at once. Past twenty paces, red is grey. Past sixty, everything is the same warm nothing, and the nothing has weather in it.

And the plain is not empty. It is crowded, in layers, and the layers hardly touch, and within each layer everything touches everything.

At the bottom: meadows. Ribbon-leaved things rooted in the laterite, blades a meter long and translucent, packed so close that pushing through them is like pushing through the hair of something asleep. They have no cellulose to speak of. They eat solution — dissolved silica, dissolved feldspar, the whole worn-down inheritance of mountains — and they precipitate it inside their tissues, so that each blade is edged along its margin with a row of crystals too small to see and easy to feel, a fine rasp that leaves your palm scored and painless. When a blade dies it does not rot. It dissolves. There is no smell of decay anywhere on this world. It smells of wet flint, warm iron, and cut cucumber, and under that, faintly, of the inside of a mouth.

Above the meadows: the drifters, bladder-bodied, tethered by root-threads that trail down to the silt and let go and re-anchor when the plumes shift. Above those, at forty and sixty and ninety meters, the hanging forests — mats that hold to nothing, that began as spores in the vapor lid and have grown downward for a hundred thousand years, sending curtains of feeder-root down through the column in strands a kilometer long. Look up from the bottom and you see the ceiling as a canopy, dim on dim, and the roots coming down like the beginnings of rain that decided against it. Where a warm plume rises off the rock, the whole hanging forest above it thickens and darkens and hangs lower, a slow green throat swallowing.

Nothing here has needed to become anything new for a very long time. The flora is finished. It arrived at its final arrangement before the last mountain lost its name, and it has been repeating that arrangement, gorgeously, ever since. Everything happens. Nothing arrives.

---

Animals are the scarcity. You can wade a week and see none.

There is a flat pale worm the length of your forearm that swims once a year, in the days after the Clear, and eats the crystal margins off the meadow blades, and is eaten in turn by nothing, and dies, and dissolves. There is a thing like a hand with too many fingers that lives in the flank of one inselberg and has never been observed elsewhere. There is a carrier — no one has seen it, only its work: seeds set down in a straight line between two monadnocks a hundred and forty leagues apart, one seed every nine years, arriving in order.

Most of the animals are not currently present.

The water holds everything that ever dissolved in it. That is the second fact. A billion years of erosion did not destroy the mountains; it took them apart into ions and kept them. The sheet above the plain is a solution of every peak, every ridge, every scree slope and river boulder that ever stood here, and it is very close to saturated, and sometimes it lets go.

They call them returns, or the water does, in the only language it has, which is the sudden appearance of a thing.

A return begins as a warmth in the water and a smell like struck stone. Then the light: crystallization gives up its heat and a little of its light, and a shape begins to glow into being out of the solution, faintly, from the inside — a ridge line, a scarp, a whole massif standing four hundred meters over a plain that has been flat since before there were eyes. It comes back granular and slightly wrong, the way a face comes back in a dream, with the strata in the correct order and the wrong colors. It lasts between an hour and a day. Water runs on it. The water runs uphill, along gradients it remembers, in channels that were cut when the world had a tilt.

And every leaf on the plain turns toward it.

That is what the vegetation grows toward. Not the lid. The lid is too dim to be worth the trouble. Fertility here is migratory, following the returns, so the lushness comes in provinces that move: a hundred leagues of dense, near-touching, layered growth where the ghosts have been coming often, and beside it a hundred leagues of bare polished rock with a few blades on it waiting like a held breath.

The animals come back too. That is the part that is hard to hold in the mind. A grazer precipitates out of the solution beside a returning scarp, complete, mid-stride, hungry, and it grazes for its hour and goes back into the water. The meadows have evolved around this. Certain blades grow with a notch already missing from them — a bite-shaped absence, precisely sized to the jaw of something that has not been continuously alive for nine million years. They budget for it. They grow the wound in advance, because being eaten by an intermittent animal is a reliable feature of the world, and reliability is what a plant knows how to use.

---

Sound has nothing to strike.

There is no hard surface anywhere. The rock is polished but robed in silt; the silt is held by roots; the roots are soft; the water is thick with dissolved everything and takes the edges off a noise the way it takes the edges off a stone. You can shout and the shout goes about four meters and lies down. What you hear instead: the tick of gas beading out of a leaf. The sift of silt resettling behind your ankle. Your pulse, which is loud, and which you eventually stop hearing, and then hear again a day later, louder.

The one exception is a return. Crystal growing against crystal makes a slow chime, a ticking somewhere below the range where a chime should be, and because there is nothing to absorb it in the first hundred meters it goes out across the plain and is heard, faintly, a thousand leagues away, by no one. Everything on the plain hears it. Everything turns.

---

The marks are scattered because the water answers them.

A doorframe: two uprights and a lintel, gneiss, standing in three feet of water with no wall, no house, no road, no rubble. The only right angles on the continent, and going soft. There are no cut marks on it. It was made by rubbing — stone against stone, wet sand and patience, generations of it, the way you would wear a groove in a step. Whoever made it had no tools and had time.

Nine steps descending into solid rock, ending at the ninth, because the rock below is solid and there was never anything down there to reach.

A ring of eleven stones, each from a different inselberg, which means someone walked a hundred leagues between one stone and the next, and then did it ten more times, and then stopped.

A hollow in the flank of a monadnock, thumb-sized, polished.

Nothing built lasts more than a few seasons. The water is hungry and undersaturated at the edges and it takes the doorway back into solution a grain at a time, and the doorway will come back someday as a return, glowing, for an hour, somewhere else. You may already have met it four times. Distance is unreliable here, for the same reason: with no gradient and no landmarks and nothing to keep places apart, the water sometimes forgets to bother. Two monadnocks a hundred leagues distant stand adjacent for an afternoon. The rain falls in curtains you can see the far side of, and the far side is the weather you are standing in.

---

And then, every few decades, the Clear.

The saturation breaks all at once across a province and everything dissolved comes out of the water together — a snowfall of stone going down through the warm dusk for three days, mountains falling as flour, the whole inheritance settling onto the plain. And the water goes transparent. And you can see, suddenly, a hundred miles of flat rock in every direction, and the monadnocks standing in it like teeth in a jaw, and the hanging forests hanging in nothing with all their roots visible, and every plant on the plain starving in the clearest, brightest, most beautiful water there has ever been.

They wait it out. The rain keeps falling into the water without a sound. The rock keeps giving up its last atoms. Somewhere out past the edge of sight the solution thickens again, and a warmth begins, and a smell like struck stone, and the light of a mountain that no longer exists comes leaning across the plain —

and every leaf turns.
