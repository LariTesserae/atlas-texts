## The Sound Comes First

You hear the place an hour before you see it, and for most of that hour you mistake it for weather.

It is a low chord, three or four notes wide, that does not change. Wind changes. Rivers change. This does not. It sits in the rock and in your sternum, and the mule-path along the moraine carries it up through your boot soles, and only when you round the last shoulder of the cirque do you understand that you have been walking toward a mouth.

The bowl is four hundred paces across. It hangs in a col between two grey shoulders of mountain, above the last pine, above the last juniper, at the height where cloud goes *through* a place instead of over it. The cliffs that hold it are old — rounded, lichen-mapped, nothing freshly broken anywhere, scree slopes settled into their angles of rest a thousand years ago and grown over with saxifrage and black moss. Everything here has already finished happening.

And in the bowl: water. A lake the colour of strong tea over gold, held not by stone but by a plant.

## The Hold

The dam is alive. It is a cushion-plant, or a colony of them, clonal, one organism by any argument that matters, and it fills the entire lower two-thirds of the cirque like dough filling a proving bowl. Its outer face is grey and fissured, cork-barked, weathered into plates the size of doors. Soil has collected in its cracks over centuries. Willows grow out of it — real willows, wrist-thick, eighty years old, bonsai'd by altitude and wind, rooted in a knothole three hundred feet above the valley. There are mosses on it that grow nowhere else. There are vole-runs in its fissures, and springtails in the leaf-litter of its own shedding, and a lichen on its north face that has been measuring the same square hand's-breadth of bark since before the town had a name.

The plant grew here to keep its roots wet. It raised its own rim as it grew, whorl over whorl, and the snowmelt that came down the cirque wall found itself impounded. That is the whole geological story. A plant made a lake to drink from, and the lake got deeper as the plant got taller, and it has been rising, patiently, at about the width of a finger per generation, for as long as anyone has been counting.

Inside, the Hold is not solid. It is chambered — hollowed by its own growth into galleries and shafts and vaults, ribbed with lignified veins as thick as a man's thigh, walled with a tissue that goes translucent when wet. Sunlight comes through the outer leaves and through those walls and arrives inside already green, already leaves' underside, the light of the inside of a tent. This is the only light most of the town has ever worked by. It is not dim. On a bright day you can read a knot-cord by it. It simply has no colour in it that is not green or gold, and the divers' faces, when they surface, are the faces of people who have been standing in a wheatfield.

The water is nine degrees. It tastes of tannin and cold stone. It is so clear in the empty volumes that a fish forty feet away looks like a fish held in your hand, and so stained in the deep shaft that the last fathom is the colour of steeped bark.

## The Throats

Where the meltwater comes off the cliff it does not fall into the lake. Long ago the town coaxed the plant to grow intake funnels — five of them, great fluted leaf-horns, mouths upturned to the fall — and the water goes down inside the Hold through hollow stems, dropping, entraining air, booming.

These are the throats, and they are the chord you heard from the moraine.

Each stem is a pipe. Each pipe has a length. The lengths change only as the plant grows, which is to say a note shifts perhaps once in a century, and there are old people who will tell you, with total seriousness, that the third throat has gone flat in their lifetime and that this is a matter for the council. The chord is loud. Standing at the rim you must shout. Standing in a gallery three ribs away from a throat you feel your teeth buzz and your chest cavity hum, and after a week you stop noticing, and after a month you cannot sleep anywhere else.

The air the falling water drags down does not all escape. It collects in certain domes near the intake — the gasp-pockets — and it is harvested. There is a guild of air-porters who go down with inverted baskets, tip them under a pocket, let them fill, and carry them up, walking the drowned streets with a basket of breath held level above their heads, a silver sloshing weight that wants to go everywhere. They deliver to the bells. They are paid in fish and in rope. Children apprentice at nine and are told, before anything else, *level, level, level.*

## Nine Bells

Above the waterline, in the crown of the Hold, are the dry rooms: chambers grown with their mouths facing down, air trapped in them the way air is trapped in a cup pushed under. Nine are inhabited. There are, by the last tally-knot, eleven hundred and forty chambers in the Hold altogether.

This is what the emptiness here is like. It is not a ruin and it is not a wilderness. It is an *urban fabric*, street-grid and stair-shaft and plaza and terrace, layered nine levels deep, with doorways and thresholds and mooring rings and the worn dip in a step where feet went — and it is almost entirely unoccupied. A hundred and sixty people live in a town built for four thousand. You can swim the length of the Long Street, past two hundred doorways, and meet one girl carrying a basket of air and a shoal of fish going the other way, and then be alone again for a quarter hour with nothing in front of you but clean green water and the architecture going by.

Everything teems, and nothing is crowded. The *surfaces* are alive — plastered, upholstered, furred with life — and the volumes between them are empty as a bell-jar. Snails in their millions on every wall, grazing the film, so many that their rasping is a sound: a continuous fine hiss, like fat in a pan, like rain on a roof, audible through the water, audible through the ribs, the sound of ten million mouths taking a micron at a time. Amphipods boiling out of a weed-tuft when your shadow crosses it. Caddis larvae in cases of mica flake and bast fibre, dragging their little masonry along the veins. Loach flat on the cork. And the fish — the pale combers, who graze the same film as the snails and travel in shoals of a thousand, and who have eyes in the lit galleries and hardly any in the deep ones, and who are, absurdly, one species.

Above water: choughs. A colony of some hundreds nests in the cork fissures of the rim, red-billed, glossy, acrobatic, and *loud*, all day, a racket of chattering and scolding and the flap-and-tumble of their play in the updraft off the dam face. Dippers work the shallows and walk — actually walk, wing-flicking, unhurried — along the submerged handrails of the Wash, hunting caddis. At dusk in the short summer, bats come up the col to take the midge clouds off the water, and the midge clouds themselves stand over the shallows like columns of grey smoke, humming.

There is a brown thief, too: a mink-thing, or a line of them, that swims the drowned streets as easily as the dry ones and takes fish off the racks in the bells. Nobody kills it. It is regarded as a tenant with a bad character.

## What the Town Is Made Of

No metal but copper, cold-hammered from nuggets picked out of the moraine: hooks, awls, a few beads worn thin by three generations of thumbs. Everything else is stone, bone, antler, and the plant.

The plant gives cork, which floats, and is used for doors, floats, sandals, coffins, and the lids of everything. It gives bast, retted in the warm chambers, spun into a rope that does not rot in the tannin. It gives resin that sets under water, which is how the baskets are caulked and the leaf-glass windows are seated. It gives ribs of hardwood, harvested only from whorls already dead. It gives membrane, stretched and dried, translucent as a horn lantern. It gives a starchy pith that is pounded, washed, settled, and baked into flat cakes on the one hot stone.

And it gives rooms — but slowly, and only if asked.

A gall-wright opens a wound in the inner meristem with a bone awl and packs it with chewed bast, ash, and spit. The Hold swells. Over twenty to forty years a hollow forms, walls thickening, mouth puckering to whatever shape the packing dictated. So no one builds a house. You *request* one, and your grandchild moves in. The requests are kept as a knot-cord in the driest bell, an archive of rope: this family, this whorl, this shape, this year, and beside it the small tight knot that means *coming* and the loose one that means *ready* and the black-dyed one that means *drowned.*

There are families whose entire standing consists of rooms not yet grown. There are cords whose oldest knots have no living claimant, and those chambers, when they finally swell open, are cleaned by the shrimp and left.

## The Drowned Quarters

Below the Wash, the town is under water and has been for a long time, level by level, a floor per century.

Cold, tannin, no oxygen: nothing rots. The Low Street still has its furniture. There are bowls on shelves and a comb in a doorway and a shoe, and the shoe is a hundred and forty years old, and everyone knows whose it was because the knot-cord says so. Divers go down to fetch things and come up holding an object their great-grandmother used, and it is not eerie to them particularly; it is more like reaching into the back of a cupboard. The dead are given to the core shaft and go down into the warm, and are eaten by red worms and eels, and become plant, and the plant grows the next room. This is understood literally and without much ceremony.

The core is warm. A cushion-plant that size, composting its own dead heart, holds a heat: blood-warm, twenty degrees, no more. There are chambers down there — the sweats — where divers go to stop shaking, hanging in the warm dark with their arms out, ten of them at a time sometimes, saying nothing, waiting for their hands to come back. It smells of ammonia and wet earth and something like bread. It is the only place in the world of the bowl that smells like a body.

## Rules

*Keep the lip.* The spillway is a smooth water-worn curve in the plant's rim, polished by centuries into a shape like the inside of a spoon, and the town's entire collective labour is keeping it clear. Leaf-fall, chough nests, ice, a dead mink — anything. Four hundred years ago a whorl shed and blocked it and the lake overtopped and cut a notch in the dam and killed a third of everybody, and the notch is still there, healed and grown over, and children are taken to see it before they are taught to swim.

*Never cut a rib.* You may wound to ask. You may harvest the dead. Cutting live structure is the one crime with one sentence: down the spillway stair, with your rope.

*Tie everything.* Cork rises. The whole town is tethered — every basket, every tool, every hat. Loose things migrate upward and outward and go over the lip and are gone into the cloud, and the phrase for a careless person is *she feeds the spillway.*

*Knock.* Sound is the public medium here; light is private. Water carries a knock four times faster and forty times farther than air, and the ribs carry it farther still, so the town talks by percussion: knuckle-posts at the junctions, appointed knockers, a code of taps that reaches every chamber in the Hold in under a second. Entering a room you knock its threshold. Approaching a working diver you knock the wall, never touch them. There is a knock for *air coming up*, a knock for *shoal*, a knock for *hail*, and a slow double knock, once a year, that means the lip has been measured.

*Count short.* The air is thin at this height. A breath-hold that would serve at sea level serves badly here, and the counters — who stand at the bell mouths with a knotted cord and a stone to strike — count faster than any lowland town would think reasonable. Distances in the bowl are given in breaths. The core shaft is eleven breaths down and nobody goes alone.

## The Hand

Once a year, at the end of the melt, the council goes to the lip with a wetted cord and measures the water against a notch cut in the old stone of the cirque wall — the only mark in the place that the plant did not make. It rises about a finger's width per generation. Sometimes a summer it does not rise at all, and this is discussed for decades.

When it rises past a threshold, a family moves up. This happens perhaps twice in a life. There is a procedure: the old room is emptied, the doorway is left open so the fish can use it, the knot is dyed black, and the family climbs one level into a chamber that somebody dead requested for them, and sits in it, and finds it slightly the wrong shape.

That is the fastest thing that happens here. Everything else — the chord of the throats, the hiss of ten million snails, the choughs screaming over the cork, the willows in the knotholes, the girl going up the Long Street with a basket of silver breath held level above her head, the pale combers pouring through an empty plaza like a handful of thrown rice — all of it is standing almost perfectly still, at the top of the world, in a green room the size of a cathedral, being very loud, and taking a hundred years to change its note.
