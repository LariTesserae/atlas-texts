## The Sill

You come up through the ordinary world first: rotten granite, scree, the tired sand of a mountain that has been dying for two hundred million years. Dust on your boots. Lichen doing its slow acid work. Wind thin enough that each breath is a negotiation. Then the lip.

There is a line across the rock at the lip, and it is the sharpest thing you will ever see. On the outside of it, every grain is rounded. On the inside, nothing is. No sand, no grit, no soil, no dust that is not a fresh crystal with its corners on. You step over and the last worn thing in the world is behind you.

Your ears pop. The air on the far side is *thicker* — a heavier, warmer mix pooled in the bowl like syrup in a spoon, held there by nothing you can see. The cold thin wind slides over the top of it. You descend three steps into a pocket of low-country air at four thousand meters, and your lungs open, and the warmth arrives, and then the sound arrives, and the sound is the thing that stops you.

## The Room

It is a cup, two hundred paces at its widest, rimmed all the way around so that you can hold the whole of it in one look. Cathedral scale. A single feature. Its ceiling is fog — a flat, luminous, slowly turning lid about nine meters up, where the warm heavy air meets the cold and gives up its water. Outside the cup, no rain has fallen in a season. Inside, it drizzles constantly, a fine warm sift that never lands anywhere but here. The cup has its own weather and it is the only weather it has ever had.

The floor is not lava and not sediment. Nothing here has been broken down and nothing has been carried. The stone is *arriving*: a low mineral fog moves along the ground in slow breaths, and where it thins, there is new rock, knitting itself out of the air a hand's breadth at a time. Bone-white, salt-pale, wet-sugar translucent at the edges, faintly blue where iron has gotten in. It grows in ferns and frost-spires and the inside-of-a-shell nacre, and every arris is sharp enough to open your palm. Nothing has been touched. Nothing has been rounded. There has not yet been time for anything to be touched.

It is warm underfoot because setting is exothermic. The floor is body-warm, thirty-one degrees, and it sweats: every bond the mineral makes lets go a molecule of water, so the rock weeps as it hardens, and the weeping runs downhill into the low places, and that is where the pools are.

You will not see the pools. There is no sediment in this world and therefore no turbidity, and therefore all water everywhere is perfectly, invisibly clear. A pool is a lens and a meniscus and a cold patch on your ankle. You learn to find water by ear — the cup's ring changes pitch over a void — and by the small standing shimmer where the drizzle strikes a surface that does not splash the way stone splashes. Everything here has learned this. It is the first fact of local life.

## Light, and What It Means

Dim. The fog lid flattens the daylight to a grey-green underwater dusk, and the walls throw it around without warming it. But the stone lights while it sets: a seam will come up amber, hold for twenty seconds, and go out. So the floor is a slow constellation, and the constellation is not decoration. It is a map of *now*. Where the light is, the world is being made. Where it is dark, the world is finished and will not change again.

You navigate by watching for the amber. You do not walk toward the dark parts if you can help it, and you certainly do not stand on the bright ones.

## The Sound

The cup is loud and the cup is loud for a reason: there is nothing soft in it. No soil, no leaf litter, no moss, no duff. Every surface is hard, new, and curved. A dropped pebble rings for eleven seconds. The room has modes, and the room's modes are always being fed.

Underneath everything is the hiss — the deposition fog going about its business, a broadband seethe like rain on a forest that does not exist. But put your ear close and the hiss resolves: it has wings. It is largely made of midges, millions of them, riding the mineral fog, seeding it, cropping its excess, dying into the lattice in tidy rows. Look at a broken face of the new stone and the grain is the grain of a swarm, tiny bright bodies laid down in ranks like sediment that never had to settle. They were instruments once. Something used them to measure and to place. They measure and place still, but now they also breed, and eat, and are eaten, and nobody is reading the results.

Over the hiss: the swifts. Thousands, never landing, cold-bodied — they run twelve degrees below ambient and their feathers are the coldest surfaces in the cup, so the fog condenses on them in flight and they drink their own plumage and trail a fine rain behind them as they turn. They are the water cycle. They sleep in the ceiling fog. They are why the pools stay full. They scream in the gaps between the room's reverberations, timing themselves to the decay, so that the noise stacks in layers rather than muddying — a species that has learned to sing with an eleven-second echo instead of against it.

And under that, at long intervals, the *tap* of a wader. There are two in the whole cup. Pale, stilt-legged, taller than you, standing in water you cannot see, striking the stone with the flat of the beak and reading the returning ring for the lensing that means an eel. The eels are clear too. You see them as a distortion, a smear in the pattern of the drizzle, and then a splash where nothing was.

## Sparse Things

For all that life, the floor is nearly empty. The abundance is overhead and underwater; at eye level the cup is a bare bright room with a great deal of space in it.

Nine candle-stalks at the north end, hollow tubes as tall as a man, cold at the tip, condensing, piping the water down inside themselves to a root that does not sit in soil — there is no soil — but grips the *seam*, the soft interface where stone is still deciding. They drink the moment of setting. Elsewhere a varnish of film-lichen, thin as breath on glass, and one crooked scaffold of a thing that tried to be a tree and has not worked out how. Fewer than forty plants in the world. You can count them, and people do, and the count changes.

## Marks

A door frame stands at the eastern quarter with no wall on either side. Its threshold is being buried; the front has reached the bottom rail and is coming up it, patiently, the way water comes up a step. The frame is not a ruin. It has never been used. There is no wear on the sill, no polish on the jamb, no smoke on the lintel. It is a door frame that has been *made* and not yet *had*.

A bench, unworn. A brass post with a dial, the needle steady, measuring something that has not begun to vary. Survey stakes in a loose grid, numbered, and the numbers count up, and the rock arrives along the lines they describe — the grid is not recording the floor, the floor is obeying the grid.

A stair at the low end, going down into unmade stone. Nine steps. Every few days there is a chime in the middle of the afternoon and then there are ten.

A hut, roofed, one room, a chair in it facing the growth front, and a coat on the chair. No dust on the coat, because there is no dust.

And footprints. One line of them, cured into the floor, walking from the sill to the center and stopping. Then, at intervals across the whole basin, the same line again. And again. Slightly softer each time, slightly more approximate, the way a word said forty times stops being a word. The room liked the footprints. The room has been reproducing them.

## The Only Weathering

There are three soft things in the cup and they are the reason the erosion figure is not zero.

They are dark, matte, low, and they spread — hand-sized, then door-sized — and they are the only surfaces here that do not ring. Where one lies, the room goes dead. Step into its acoustic shadow and your own footsteps stop existing; the swifts above you vanish from hearing; you cannot locate the wader or the water or the front. Prey animals blunder in and go quiet and do not come out. In a world with no soft material, softness is the predator.

They eat stone. They are the first mouths ever to do so here, and under each of them is a smear of grey silt, the only mud in the world, and beside each is a pool that is cloudy — the one cloudy water anywhere, and the animals will not drink it, and the midges will not seed it. It smells like a forest floor. Nothing in the cup has ever smelled a forest floor. Everything in the cup goes very still when it does.

The front grows toward them. Whether that is feeding them or fighting them is not obvious, and the stakes around each patch are numbered like all the other stakes, and the numbers count up.

## What It Is For

The likeliest story — and it is only a story; the coat could belong to anyone — is that far below and some distance away there is an old valley wearing out. Rain, frost, roots, time. It is going to sand and then to sea. And this cup is its replacement, being laid down ahead of the loss, one clean crystal at a time, by instruments that outlived their instructions and became animals.

It would explain why the bench has never been sat on and yet is exactly the shape of a bench that has. Why the swifts have no gut that anyone has found. Why one wader is a mirror of the other, down to the chipped beak, on the wrong side. Why the copies come out slightly unresolved underneath, in the places the record never saw. Why the room is warm and loud and wet and small and holds its breath at four thousand meters: it is not a landscape. It is a place being made ready. A nursery, a mold, a waiting room with the lights left low.

At dusk the ceiling comes down a meter, the drizzle thickens, the swifts rise into it, the seams light amber all across the floor at once like a city seeing itself, and somewhere below the stair a new step arrives with a sound like a struck bowl. The hush says nothing. It is the only thing here that never says anything.

Keep moving. The front incorporates whatever stands still long enough, and there is a boot in the threshold of the door, and a thickening in the bench that is not the shape of the bench.
