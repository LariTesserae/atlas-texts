## The Larder

Above, there is nothing to see. A field of short grass cropped to a green felt by sheep, three hawthorns bent all one way by the weather, a drystone wall with a gap where a gate used to be. The ground is limestone under a hand's depth of soil, and the rain that falls on it — drizzle mostly, a proper rain every nine or ten days — does not puddle. It goes down. It has been going down here since before there was a wall, and the field has no opinion about it.

The entrance is a slot in a low shelf of pavement, half a metre wide and long enough to step over without noticing. Hawthorn roots have got into one end. At the other end the lip of the stone is worn smooth in two places, grooved, polished by rope, and there is a rectangular rebate cut around the opening to seat a lid. The lid is still there, pushed aside a hand's width, furred with moss, its iron ring reduced to an orange stain in the shape of a ring. Someone set that stone down every evening for a hundred years. Then someone stopped.

Put your face over the gap in July and the cold comes up at you like water.

---

It is a cold trap. Winter air, being heavy, pours in through the slot all through the cold months and pools at the bottom, and summer air, being light, floats over the top and cannot get down. That is the whole mechanism, and it is enough to hold a room at the edge of freezing forever, in a country where the fields above go brown in August. It is not the whole explanation. But it is where the explanation starts, and for most of the people who ever used this place it was the end of it.

The descent is four metres down a shaft that was a solution pipe before it was a stair. The stair is gone except for its sockets: nine square holes cut in the rock in a descending spiral, each the width of two fingers, each still holding a stub of oak so cold and so wet that it has become a kind of stone. You go down by bracing against the walls. The walls are scalloped all over, hollowed in overlapping spoon-shapes the size of a palm, the signature of water that once filled this space completely and moved through it under pressure. That water left a long time ago. The scallops remain, dry now, or filmed, and they make the rock feel worked — like something quarried rather than dissolved.

At the bottom the shaft opens sideways, and the cold gets thicker, and you are in the room.

---

Nine paces end to end. Six at the widest. The ceiling is two metres above your head at the crown and comes down to your chest at the margins, so the space is lens-shaped, a bubble in the bedding plane, and the floor is laminated silt so fine and so undisturbed that a footprint stays legible for years. Breakdown blocks lie where they fell, rounded, settled, furred; nothing here is precariously placed. The chamber finished becoming itself a very long time ago. It is not going anywhere. It has arrived at its own shape and holds it.

And it is full.

Not full of stone. Full of growth. It stands in tiers from the floor to the ceiling, pale as skim ice, in sheets and ruffles and fluted brackets, layer above layer above layer, each shelf a hand's breadth clear of the one below, so that to cross the room you part it — push through wet page after wet page, and each one gives with a papery sound and springs back slowly, over a minute, like something remembering. There are curtains hanging from the ceiling that brush your hair. There are fans a metre across growing out of the silt on stalks no thicker than a bootlace, and under them smaller fans, and under those a felt of the same stuff pressed flat to the mud, and every surface — every block, every stub of oak, every socket of the vanished stair — is upholstered. In a room of forty cubic metres there is more surface area than in the field above.

It is not green. Nothing here has ever had a use for green. It is the colour of a fingernail, of candle wax, of the inside of a shell, and where the sheets are thick they go faintly blue the way deep ice goes blue, and where they are thin they are translucent enough that a lamp behind one shows the veins.

The veins carry sulphide, and iron in its reduced form, up from the seep water. That is the food. The energy is in the difference between what the water brings and what the rock will accept, and it is a poor living, a thin margin, the sort of margin that supports a forest only if the forest is willing to be extremely slow.

It is willing. A frond puts on four millimetres a year. The great fan by the eastern wall, the one with the split down its middle that has healed and split again eleven times, was a spore when the lid was set. The curtains took two centuries to reach the floor and have not touched it, and will not, for another sixty years.

And there is a second thing about the metabolism, which is the thing that makes this place what it is. The reaction it runs is uphill. It needs a push, and the push it takes is heat — ambient warmth, drawn in through the frond's whole surface and spent, converted, gone into the chemistry. Every sheet in the room is drinking the warmth out of the air. The cold trap explains why the cold stays. The growth explains why there is cold to stay. Stand still among it and you can feel the draw on your face, on the backs of your hands, a coolness with a direction to it, and if you hold your palm a hand's width from a frond and wait an hour it will have leaned a millimetre toward you, and if you were foolish enough to stay a week the lean would be visible from the shaft.

They do not grow toward light. There is no light. They grow toward warmth, along the isotherms, the way ordinary leaves turn to the sun — and here the sun is a seep of groundwater at four degrees, and the summer throat of the shaft, and the hand of whoever comes down.

---

The water arrives eleven days after it falls. Rain on the field on a Tuesday works through the pavement, through the grikes and the bedding, and reaches the ceiling seam on the second Friday after, and the seam swells, and beads stand along it in a line like sweat, and one of them grows until it cannot hold and lets go.

The average interval between drops is eleven minutes. It is not regular. There will be three in a minute and then nothing for an hour. In the dry weeks of late summer the seam closes to a film and the room's whole water economy becomes condensation: hoarfrost feathering out on the cold surfaces at night, subliming back off them by what passes for morning, a slow breathing of moisture from wall to frond to wall that never quite leaves.

Everything is damp. Nothing is wet. The silt takes a bootprint and holds it. The fronds are slick but do not drip. In the low western corner there is a basin the size of a hat brim, rimmed in flowstone the colour of weak tea, and its level has not changed in recorded time: it fills from the ceiling and loses through a fissure in its floor at exactly the rate it fills, and there is a stain-line at the lip that is a thousand years old and one millimetre high. The whole room is like that. Inputs and outputs matched to the third decimal, everything settled, everything paid for.

The basin is where the milk went. There is a shelf cut into the wall above it, and a hook, and a groove worn in the flowstone by a bail handle, and the initials **T.H.** cut with a knife beside a date that reads 1782 if you tilt the lamp. In summer they lowered crocks down here on a rope and the cold kept them sweet for a week. The growth kept them colder than the cold alone could manage. Nobody wrote that down. It was just a good larder, better than most, and the milk from this farm had a name.

---

There are no animals.

This is not an absence you notice immediately; it is one you notice after twenty minutes, when you realise that the silence has no texture in it. No wings. No scratching in the silt. No midges in the beam, no droppings on the blocks, no bones gnawed at the ends. Nothing here has ever had a mouth. A wood pigeon fell down the shaft at some point in the last century and lies in the eastern corner under three tiers of pale sheet, and it is still a pigeon. Its feathers are intact. The cold and the chemistry have kept it the way the cold keeps milk, and the growth has gone over it without eating it, because it does not eat that sort of thing, and there is nothing else here that does.

So the room's sounds are only these. The drip. The tick of the old ice in the low corner as it adjusts to a load it has carried since the last century — three or four ticks a year, sharp as a knuckle on a table. A dry rustle when you move, which is the fronds, which are wet, and sound dry. The shift of the cold layer at the equinox, a sigh along the floor as the pool of heavy air re-sorts itself by a few centimetres. And, under all of it, your own blood, and the thin high tone that your nervous system makes when there is nothing else to hear.

Mostly it is quiet in the way a closed book is quiet.

---

The dark is not absolute. For about forty minutes around midday in high summer, when the sun is at the right angle to the slot and the hawthorn is in leaf but not too much leaf, a thread of grey comes down the shaft and puts a coin of dim light on the wall above the milk shelf. It is not enough to read by. It is enough to see that there is a wall. The growth ignores it entirely; a frond that has spent forty years leaning toward the seep does not turn for forty minutes of photons. But the coin of light lands on the frost, and the frost holds it a moment, and for that half hour you can see the shape of the room without a lamp, grey on grey, tier on tier, like the inside of a shell.

The rest of the year there is no day at all.

A lamp down here is not a light. It is a fire, a sun of warmth, and the room responds to it as a forest responds to summer. Within an hour the nearest sheets have begun to incline. Within a day there is a bowl of pale faces turned toward wherever you set it down. When you take it away and go up and put the lid back, they lean back over the following month, unhurried, without disappointment. Nobody has ever stayed long enough to be a trellis. It has been thought about.

---

In the floor, a little off centre, there is a pin.

It stands about a finger's length out of the silt and the growth has grown around it in a raised collar, the way a tree grows around a fence wire, and the collar is the densest and oldest tissue in the room. You find it in the dark by the cold-burn: it is colder than the room, distinctly, several degrees, and the hoarfrost on it is not the feathered frost of the walls but a compact clear glaze. Your fingertip reports a shape — smooth, slightly tapered, six or seven millimetres across — and cannot report a texture, because there isn't one. Not smooth-as-glass. Absent. The way a number has no texture.

From the pin a seam runs. It goes into the silt and comes out of the wall two metres away and crosses the wall diagonally and enters the ceiling, and it does not make holes. The bedding plane bends around it. The calcite laminae, laid down in still water three hundred million years ago, curve aside and close again, and they do it cleanly, without fracture, without inclusion, as though the seam had been in the schedule when the sea was laying down its floor.

Along the seam the frost is thickest, and a hand's breadth to either side of it the growth is thickest too, ranked and tiered and crowded, because it is not the cold the fronds want, it is the *difference*, the gradient, the few degrees of slope between the seam and the seep. They are farming a differential. They have been farming it since before there was anything on this hill with a spine.

The pin does not glow, hum, warm, vibrate, or trouble a compass. It has no power source and produces no waste. What it does is hold this room at four-tenths of a degree above freezing, and hold it there against August, against the seep, against the pigeon, against you, moving heat out of the chamber to somewhere that is not down the fissure and not up the shaft and not, as far as anyone has been able to determine, anywhere. It has been doing this without interruption for a length of time that the ice in the corner — banded, dust-layered, annual, nine hundred and forty bands counted and more below — cannot get to the bottom of.

The best reading of it is the boring one. It is a reference. A fixed point. A standard weight, kept in a hole in the ground in a wet country because a hole in the ground is stable and nobody looks there, against which something elsewhere is compared from time to time. Everything else in this room — the ice, the curtains, the fans, the milk, the initials, the pigeon — is what accumulates on a standard when it is left in a damp place for a long while.

On the wall above the milk shelf there are two tallies. The first is cut with a knife: forty-one strokes in fives, a season's crocks, human, legible, stopped. The second is not cut. It is a change in the crystal of the flowstone, nine marks, each the width of a thumbnail, readable only when the frost takes them differently, which it does, in the cold months, for anyone who thinks to look. They are spaced along the wall at intervals that correspond, if the flowstone's growth rate is constant and it is, to something over a thousand years apiece.

The ninth mark is fresh. Its edges have not yet rounded. Whatever comes to read the standard does not bring a light, does not leave a track, does not warm the air by a measurable amount, and takes, presumably, very little of its time.

---

This is the whole place. It is forty cubic metres inside a hill that shows nothing, in a field with three hawthorns and a gap where a gate used to be. It has one entrance, one seep, one basin, one fissure two centimetres wide going down and away to somewhere the water carries fragments of pale sheet to, where perhaps there is another room, and another pin, and another slow forest leaning along the isotherms in the dark. Nothing here is in a hurry. The fronds put on four millimetres a year and the ice puts on a band and the flowstone puts on a hair and the seam puts on nothing at all, and the milk would keep, if there were milk, if there were a rope, if there were a hand.

Go up. Brace against the scallops, find the sockets with your boots, haul out into the ordinary grey afternoon and the smell of sheep and wet grass. Slide the lid across. It seats in its rebate with a sound you feel in your wrists.

Eleven days from now the rain that fell this morning will reach the ceiling seam. Eleven minutes after that, on average, a drop will let go and fall through four tiers of pale listening sheet and into a basin that has been full, exactly full, since before the wall was built.

Nothing will hear it. That is not a deprivation. Hearing is a thing that animals do, and there are none here, and the room has never once been lonely.
