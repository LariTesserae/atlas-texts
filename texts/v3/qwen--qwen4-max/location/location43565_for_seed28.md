Nothing on the plain announces it. Salt to every horizon, buckled into low plates like a dry lake's skin, the light coming down and coming back up so that a walking figure casts no shadow worth the name. Warm — the salt is warm through the soles, the air is warm in the lungs, thirty-four degrees at dawn and thirty-eight at the worst of the afternoon, and nothing anywhere is wet. Caravans cross this stretch in a day and a half and complain of nothing but glare. Somewhere in the middle of the crossing, the teeth begin to buzz. A faintness in the jaw, a pressure behind the sternum, a feeling that one's own heartbeat has been slightly mistimed by something else. The drivers say it is the sun. They have said this for nine hundred years.

The opening is a slot the width of two hands, sixty paces long, lipped with a callus of harder salt. From above it is a crack in a pan, one of ten thousand. Put your face to it and warm air comes out smelling of pear drops and hot turpentine, and the buzz becomes a pressure becomes a hand laid flat on the chest.

The descent takes most of a day. The throat is a chimney, hand-cut in its upper reaches, worn glassy below, hung with rope ladders and with felt plugs on wooden arms that the doorkeepers swing aside as you pass and let fall behind you. Each plug takes a little of the roar off. Not much. There are forty-one of them and by the twentieth you have stopped being able to hear your own shouting, and by the thirtieth you have stopped shouting, and by the last you have understood, in the body, that hearing is not the sense that will be used here.

Then the throat opens and you are inside the gourd.

---

It is a gas blister in a salt dome: a single bubble, four hundred paces across, ninety at the crown, its walls fluted and scalloped from floor to ceiling like the inside of a shell that has been listened to for a million years. The ceiling is translucent halite, thirty meters of it, and the sun comes through as a general white radiance with no source and no direction. There is no shade in this world. There is no night. Things are lit the way the inside of a milk-glass lamp is lit.

And it is almost empty. That is the first wrongness. You expect a cavern-city to be a clutter, and instead you stand on a floor of dry fines and see, far off, walls stained with construction — a thin brown lace, terraces and scaffolds and hanging quilts, all of it pressed flat into the grooves of the stone like something lichenous — and between you and it, nothing. Air. A volume that could hold a hundred thousand bodies holds six thousand, and holds them smeared thin as pigment. In the middle distance, unsupported, hang nine dark masses, each the size of a hill, each so far from its neighbor that the eye insists they must be different kinds of thing.

The second wrongness is that the air is not silent. It is not loud, either; loud is a word for a thing that happens and stops. The wind enters the neck-slot at the top of the throat and the entire chamber speaks, and has been speaking without interruption since before there were ears anywhere to fail at it. The fundamental is around nineteen cycles a second, below hearing, a note you receive in the gut and the sinuses and the long bones of the legs. Above it sits a stack of harmonics that goes up past pain and keeps going: a shriek like a wet finger drawn around the rim of a glass hill. It does not vary by more than a tone from one hour to the next. It is a hundred and thirty decibels of established fact. Everything here is built inside that fact the way a river town is built inside the fact of the flood.

---

The city is called nothing that can be said. Its name is a tap pattern, four beats and a pause, and it is struck on stone by children before they can walk.

They build in the flutes. The wind has carved the walls into vertical grooves, deep and round-bottomed, running the full height, and the profile of those grooves is what makes the chamber sing the note it sings. To alter the profile is to alter the note, and to alter the note is to starve the trees. So the masons do not cut. They fill, and they fill only to the lip, and every terrace, every stair, every cistern-that-holds-no-water sits nested in an erosion-form older than the species. From the floor the settlement reads as a lace because it is a lace: a hundred generations of construction laid into a wind's handwriting without smudging a letter.

The materials are salt brick pressed by hand and cured in the light; gypsum plaster; rope of bast; felt, endless felt; and lacquer. The lacquer is the craft that makes the place possible. The trees' sap is a light sweet oil that goes hard in bright light — hold a smear of it in a sunshaft and in an hour you have a glassy amber solid. So the masons work with mica mirrors on rope rigs, steering columns of ceiling-light onto the seams, and the mortar sets where they aim it. There are lens-grinders and mirror-boys and light-plumbers, and a guild whose whole expertise is the angle of a beam in the brown hour. There is no fire. There has never been fire. The air is a solvent haze and the foliage is oiled paper and the first person to strike a spark here would be a legend, briefly. Metal is native copper, cold-hammered, soft as cheese, used for hooks and for the striking-plates.

Because the city is not held together by adjacency. It is held together by conduction. Sound goes through salt at four kilometers a second, and the walls are laced with seams of dense clear crystal that carry a tap almost without loss. Every house has a striking-plate bedded into such a seam, and every person has a hammer worn on the hip like a hand. You do not walk to your sister. You knock, and she is in your wrist. A message crosses the whole hollow in a tenth of a second; a market is a chord of tapping; the town's business happens in a medium where distance has been abolished, which is why six thousand people can live in a near-void and still be a city, dense as a hive, gossiping at the speed of rock.

The poor live in dead stone. There are districts where the salt is crumbly and sugary and eats a tap in three meters, and to live there is to be out of contact — reachable only by a runner, married only by a walk, informed of a death a week late. Wealth here is a good seam. Poverty is muffling. And the highest luxury, the thing the rich buy and the pious endow, is a quilted room: felt on felt on felt, hung on gimbals, a hush the size of a bed where a child can be raised with ears intact for six or seven years before the world takes them. Everyone is deaf by twelve. It is not a tragedy; it is citizenship. But the language of the hands is learned in the hush first, in quiet, with a mother's voice in it, and people spend their whole lives trying to remember what that was like.

---

The nine masses are the trees, and each one is a jungle, and there are only nine.

They are not trees. They are fluted hollow columns, organ-pipes of a woody fiber that is really a kind of paper, and out of the crown of each spills acres of foliage — fronds of warm chamois, oiled, translucent at the edges; velvet mosses in the crotches; epiphytes like folded fans; hanging gardens forty meters deep where the light comes down green-gold through a hundred layers of leaf. Climb into one and you are in a thicket so lush it closes over your head in three moves, humid in the way of an oil-lamp rather than a swamp, sweet enough to make you dizzy. Come out and the next nearest living thing is two hundred paces of empty bright air away.

They drink from the floor. Under the fines the salt is capped with a seep, and a light naphtha comes up through it, and the trees lift it by wick and breathe it out, and the whole chamber's weather is a solvent weather instead of a water weather. At the brown hour, when the plain's dust crosses the sun and the light goes copper and the wind slackens, the vapor condenses on the cool undersides of the leaves and the jungle sweats oil, and it drips, and the sound of ten thousand drops of naphtha falling through foliage is the only delicate sound in the world, and no one has heard it in four hundred years except the Listeners, who are mad.

The leaves are laminae, tuned. They take the roar and turn it into chemistry — the flex of the leaf drives the sweet into sugar, and growth here is a function of loudness. A tree in a quiet zone thins, pales, and dies standing. This is why the baffling laws are the town's whole constitution: you may quilt your room, you may felt your stair, you may not damp a wall. Over-muffling is the capital crime. They call it stealing noise, and the punishment is to be carried out to the middle of the Empty and left, which is not a killing but is close enough.

Water is the other taboo. A drop of rain, falling down the throat, is a burn. Brine eats the foliage in white lace-edged holes; a wet hand on a leaf leaves a scar for a season. In the wet years the ceiling goes soft in patches and weeps, and the plugging crews go up on ropes with salt brick and light-cured lacquer and patch the sky, and sometimes they are too late and a district of the crown dies and hangs there, glassy, brittle, ringing wrong, for a century. There are three such scars. They are named, and mourned, and no one goes into them.

---

Fauna: a kite-moth, thin as a shaving, wingspan of two hands, which licks lacquer and carries pollen and is seen perhaps once a season by perhaps one person. And the other thing.

There are three of them, they nest in the neck, and they are membranes — flat ribbons of some tissue that absorbs sound perfectly, ten meters long, moving through the bright air with almost no visible motion. You do not see one. You notice a hole. A notch in the roar, a wedge of nothing where the world's continuous fact has been subtracted, and if that notch comes over you, you learn what the deaf of this city have never had cause to learn: that they know where their limbs are by the ringing of their own bones. In the quiet you stop being able to find your hands. You come apart, gently, and fall, and the membrane folds around what falls.

Silence is the predator. That is the rule of this world, and everything else follows from it. The Listeners are the ones who kept their ears — hush-raised, hush-kept, going up into the quilted galleries to listen for absences, paid in the best seams, dead young, half-mad from the delicate sounds they cannot stop hearing. When they tap the alarm it is a pattern nobody has to be taught: three beats, no pause, three beats.

---

The wind tides twice a day, in and out, and the note bends a whole tone, and the trees flower on the low bend, releasing dry rattling pods that fall for an hour like a hail of small instruments. The dead are carried up to the neck and hung in the slot, femurs drilled and tuned, so that the wind plays them; part of the chord that everyone lives inside is the ancestors, and the tuners go up each generation to adjust them, because the chamber has arrived, after all this time, at the shape that sings best, and the town's whole labor is to keep it there. Erosion and masonry and forestry and mourning, all one maintenance contract, signed by nobody, renewed every morning.

Above, on the plain, the caravans still cross. Their teeth buzz. They say it is the sun. And the plain, if you knew how to read it, is a field of gourds — a hundred sealed bubbles under the salt, each with its slot, each humming its own faint note into the glare, and only this one cracked open, and only this one full.
