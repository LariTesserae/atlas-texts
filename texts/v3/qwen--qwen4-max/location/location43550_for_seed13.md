## The Sum

The air is thin and cool and does not move. That is the first thing — not the peaks, not the light, but the absence of any push against the face. Smoke from the hearth-mouths goes up in straight grey ropes to the height of the rim's shoulders and there flattens and spreads and stays, a lid over the whole basin, so that the daylight arrives bright but softened, ordinary, the light of a cool morning at ten o'clock, and stays that way until it doesn't. Nothing flaps. Nothing gusts. A dropped thread falls where you dropped it.

The rim is a ring of teeth: pale grey stone, arêtes like the edges of broken crockery, couloirs of angular chips, and along the north faces a fixed band of old snow that neither climbs nor retreats. Every fracture up there is fresh. Every fracture up there has always been fresh. The basin below is a day out of the sea and has been a day out of the sea for as long as the knots go back — you can see the sea in the rock, coiled shells and the fans of brachiopods, and the living snails in the fissures wear the same shells, and the people say the sea is coming back a film at a time, and that the counting is what keeps it from hurrying.

## Clints and grikes

The floor of the basin is a pavement: broad flat slabs of the pale stone, called clints, split by a lattice of straight fissures, called grikes, knee-wide and sometimes two men deep. From a distance the floor reads as bare — grey, dry, ringing underfoot, extending away in every direction past the reach of the eye, a day's walk across and nothing in it taller than a person. From close up it is veined with green so dense it looks wet even when it isn't. The lushness is all in the seams. Everything soft has crowded into the cracks, where the moisture hides, and left the slabs to the sky.

There is no sand anywhere. There is no mud. A dropped stone hits rock and rings; a tarn has no soft bottom, only clean grike-fissured pavement going down into blue. The chips in the riverbed are as sharp as the day they were split and cut the feet of anyone who wades without leather. Water here carries almost nothing solid — it carries the stone dissolved, invisibly, and puts it down again somewhere else, exactly, on schedule.

The clints are veined with white seams, some hairline, some as wide as a hand, and the seams are scars: places where blocks were taken out and the rock was persuaded to knit. There are thousands. The basin looks like something dropped and mended, mended and dropped again, and the mending is always just finished.

## Two waters

The springs come out at the base of the rim at rates that do not vary — the Long Drip, the Nail, the Kettle. Their water is hard and clear and slightly blue in depth, and it is warm at the Kettle, warm enough to sit in, the one warm thing in a cool country, and its warmth goes off into the cool air and the cool air takes it and neither ever wins.

This white water builds. Left in a channel it lays down a skin of lime; left in a channel with a woven form in it, it fills the form. It plating-over is the reason the peaks stay sharp and the chips stay angular and the animal road stays polished instead of worn: every night the dew comes, and the dew is hard, and by morning everything outdoors wears a new white film a fraction of a hair thick.

The brown water unbuilds. It seeps out of the moss banks on the high shelves, tea-coloured, sour, and it eats lime as fast as the white water lays it. So the city has two plumbing systems, two sets of stone conduits running side by side along every causeway, and the whole craft of the place is the balancing of them. White water on the quarry faces to make them heal. Brown water on the door-jambs so the doors do not seal, on the stair treads so the stairs do not grow round and useless, run in a thread down every grike so the green seams stay exactly the width they are. Everyone carries a small stoppered flask of brown at the hip, and every morning everyone washes in it, and the washing is not about cleanliness.

Rain is a fixture rather than an event. It falls at the ninth stair and at the eastern casting beds and along a diagonal band of the southern pavement, at the same hour, in the same amount, and you can stand with one foot in it. The river runs down the middle of the basin at a constant hand's-depth and drops into the Throat at the low end, and the water goes down and does not go anywhere else: in the afternoon the fog comes back up the gorge in a slow white tongue, at the same speed, to the same height, and condenses on the rim, and the rim drips into the springs. It has never been more or less. There is no flood mark on any wall. There is no word for flood.

## The green

In the grikes: moss banked a metre deep, ferns with fronds like combs, hart's-tongue and maidenhair and a small tough spleenwort, cushions of pink-flowered saxifrage tight as fists, and in the deeper wetter clefts a low thicket of willow no higher than a shoulder, cropped flat. On the clints: lichen in maps, grey-green and rust-orange, and the tussock grass in scattered islands, each island a hand's-breadth of soil in a pit the width of a coin. In one hollow on the south side, out of the cold, there is a tree — one, rowan-ish, twisted, with a crown you could sit under if you sat cross-legged. It is counted. It has the same number of berries.

The moss banks are warm from inside. Their undersides rot and their tops grow and the rotting makes heat, and people sleep in cut beds in them, and the peat that forms is dug and burned in small hearths, and the ash goes back into the grikes, and the beetle larvae eat what's left, and the dust of them goes back to the moss. Nothing heaps up. There is no midden anywhere in the city, no rubbish hill, no silt fan, no soil horizon, no drift. That absence is the strangest thing about the place, stranger than the standing fog. It is a settled country with no leftovers.

## The city, spread thin

It is a city — organized, layered, infrastructural, a place with addresses — but it is a city with the population shaken out over a whole basin like salt on a table. Households sit a hundred paces apart, three hundred, half a league; between them are bare clints and green seams and nothing. What makes it urban is what connects: causeways of fitted stone running arrow-straight for miles, stair-flights of two hundred steps cut where the pavement steps down, the paired aqueducts, the retaining walls, the casting beds, the census hall, the long low reservoirs with their lids of grown slab. The construction is layered — wall inside wall inside wall, seven shells of it in the older quarters — but the layers are all in use at once, all maintained, all swept. There are no ruins. There has never been a ruin here. When a wall is superseded it is not abandoned; its outer shell is drawn off and its stones go back into the water.

They have no metal to speak of. Their tools are stone, bone, antler, withy, fired clay, cordage of nettle and of spider silk. They split rock by driving dry wooden wedges into the joints and soaking them, and the wood swells and the stone opens with a sound like a knuckle cracking — one crack, then silence for a long while. This is the loudest regular noise in the city and it happens perhaps forty times a day across twenty miles.

And they grow their building stone. In the casting beds below the springs, forms of woven willow are laid in shallow races of white water, and the water fills them, season after identical season, and when a block is ripe it is levered out and the form is reset. The blocks come out with the weave printed on their faces. Every wall in the city is a fossil of basketry.

## The living, everywhere, quietly

Life is abundant and mostly small and mostly hidden in the seams: snails in the grikes in numbers that make the moss look like it's moving, and if you put your ear down you can hear them rasping, thousands of tiny files; springtails, mites, beetles, worms; a small brown frog in every wet cleft.

Because there is no wind, the insects do not drift. Their swarms stand — columns and knots of midges hanging at fixed points over the seeps, motionless in the still air, permanent enough to be named and mapped and walked around. The birds work these columns along routes they do not vary. There are swifts in the rim cliffs in colonies of tens of thousands, and they are almost silent: song is for holding ground and for saying something new, and here the ground holds itself and nothing is new, so each kind has one note, given once per circuit at its station. That is how the day is told. The hour-bird at the Throat. The two-note bird over the eastern beds.

Spiders build between clint and clint knowing nothing will ever come to tear it down, and their webs are felted, layered, architectural, thumb-thick in places, curtains a yard long. Between the webs and the moss and the fog, the basin is upholstered. Sound goes into it and does not come back. Twenty miles of city and you can hear your own blood, and the drip of the Long Drip from a quarter-mile off, and the breathing of animals.

The animals are the herds: pale, long-haired, goat-footed, in their thousands on the far terraces, moving on a circuit they have always moved on. Their hooves polish the clint rather than wear it, because the night's dew re-plates the road, and their track is a mirror-gloss ribbon across the basin called the Shine, visible from the rim as a line of light. They spend much of the day de-stoning one another, licking the rime off each other's faces and legs, and their teeth and hooves grow continuously, as everything here grows continuously at the surface, being continuously taken back.

## The number

In the census hall there is a wall of knotted cords, silk and hair, and the cords are the number: of people, of names, of hearths, of blocks in the beds, of berries on the tree, of sitters. It is checked every day. It does not differ. They have no chronicles — nothing has happened to record — only inventories, and their whole literate apparatus, their notched sticks and their knot-work, is arithmetic. Discrepancy is the only sin. A household that cannot make its tally come out will sit in the doorway all night going over it, and the neighbours will bring them moss-tea and not speak.

There are exactly as many names as people. A name is a place in the number, and it is passed along.

Out on the pavement, scattered, are the sitters: figures seated cross-legged on the bare clint, glazed white, part of the floor now, their features still readable, some of them very old and softened, some so recent you can see the eyelashes. They are counted. They are boundary markers and they are also, in some way the people don't discuss with strangers, the reason the count holds. Nobody is put there. It is chosen, and it is honoured, and when a child comes there is generally an elder who has been getting ready for a while.

The rule for the living is simple and everyone learns it before they learn to walk: keep moving, or be kept. Sit too long on the clint in the dew and the film builds under you and bonds you, a night's worth at the ankles, a week's worth at the hips. Children are washed in brown water and chided. Tools left out are stone tools by the next month, and are dug out of the pavement and put back in the beds.

## Ordinary light

The sun is a plain disc through the lid. There are no unusual colours here, no green flash, no auroras on the teeth, nothing in the sky that means anything. It is simply a bright cool day, the same bright cool day, over a basin of mended grey stone veined with wet green, with white fog coming up the gorge at its accustomed speed and a herd shining on the far terrace and a single crack from somewhere east as a wedge does its work.

Nothing here is getting older. Everything here is being replaced. The peaks stay sharp because they are always freshly broken; the seams stay open because they are always being dissolved; the moss stays a metre deep because it rots as fast as it rises; the river runs clear because it carries the mountain invisibly and puts it back tonight.

You are the only thing in the basin that will be different tomorrow. They will notice. They will count you, and be gentle with you, and offer you the flask, and hope you don't sit down.
