There is no stone in the Low Cote. Dig anywhere — under the floor, behind the wall, into the mass that holds the room's shape — and you strike brick, then puddled clay, then brick again, then the packed rubble of an older cote that failed and was flattened and built upon. Seven of them, stacked like plates in a rack. The world outside the shell is twenty feet of that made ground, and then the ordinary surface with its grass and its weather, which nobody down here thinks about much. There is no cave. There was never a cave. Somebody dug a hole and lined it and roofed it and the hole has been a place ever since.

It is a bell of brick, forty-one paces across the floor, nine fathoms to the eye at the crown. You can walk its circumference in four minutes and most people do, several times a day, because the walkway is the only way to reach the upper galleries. One room, one throat, one stair, a sump, three galleries, a landing where the buckets come up. That is the whole of it. It has boundaries the way a jar has boundaries.

And every hand's-breadth of it is subdivided.

**The pots.** Eleven thousand and forty nesting-throats, thrown on a wheel, fired hard, set mouth-outward into the masonry in courses that begin at the floor and go up until they blur. Each is a clay gullet the length of a forearm, tilted a few degrees down so it drains, the lip worn round. They are set so close that the wall between two mouths is often just the thickness of a thumb. Where the dome curves in, the potters made shorter pots; where the stair turns, the risers themselves are niches; the ladders have perches lashed to their stiles, and the perches have been used, and are streaked. From the middle of the floor the walls do not read as walls. They read as texture — a honeycomb, a corncob, the inside of a hive — and they move.

Nothing here is new, and nothing here is original. Every brick has been lifted out and replaced at least twice; the lime is renewed course by course on a rotation older than the family that keeps it. Yet the hollow in the threshold is four fingers deep, and the hollow is older than any brick in it, because each new brick is laid to the old worn line. The wear outlives the material. The pots have been reglazed by nothing but tongues and feet and four hundred years of birds turning around inside them. That is the only erosion this place has ever known: the patient sanding of made things by the lives they were made for. It has arrived somewhere and stopped. The dome slumped a hand's width in its second century and has not slumped since. The ledger balances. The place is finished, in the way a river is finished.

**Light.** It is simply bright. That is the strange part, and the keepers do not think it strange at all, because they work at it every day. The throat above the oculus is a whitewashed flue, kept chalk-white, angled to the sun's road, and at its head stand four slabs of polished selenite that a boy trims twice a day with a wooden handle, walking a catwalk on the surface with his eyes shut against the glare. The light comes down and hits the pale floor and bounces and fills the bell evenly, like an overcast noon that has been carried underground and kept in a jar. You could read by it. You could thread a needle. There is no gloom, no drama of shafts and shadows — only a soft, standing, workaday brightness, thick with feather dust, smelling of lime and ammonia and hot clay.

At night the rush-lamps take over from the galleries, a hundred small flames in fat, and the light does not change much. There is no darkness in the Low Cote. There has not been darkness in it for four centuries. Dawn here is a wooden shutter on a rope, and a woman pulls it, and it is dawn.

**Water.** Not a lake, not a stream. The place is damp the way a mouth is damp.

At false dawn, when the shutter opens and the cold night air falls down the throat into all that warmth, the walls sweat. There is a band of brick two courses high — the sweat course, cooler than the rest because the fill behind it is thicker there — that beads all over in the space of a hundred heartbeats, and then the whole room drinks. Birds hang sideways on it with their tongues out. Geckos lick the mortar. The moss collars, which grow in a broken green necklace along that same band, swell and go from grey to a shocking wet green in the time it takes to eat a meal. Then the walls dry, and the moss crisps, and everyone waits for the next morning. That is the water supply. It has always been enough.

The overflow of the world's small accidents is caught in pots. Along the base of each gallery runs a rank of them, half-buried, each catching a particular drip from a particular joint in the vault, and each one is tuned — sized, shaped, water-level marked — so that it speaks a certain note when struck by its drop. Five notes, repeating, out of phase, forever. The keepers hear the humidity in it. As a pot fills, its pitch climbs; when the phrase goes sharp, a child goes along the rank with a ladle and empties them in order, and the descending figure that results is the sound the birds associate with *nothing is wrong*. They have been taught this by four hundred years of it. Play it wrong and eleven thousand heads come out of eleven thousand holes at once.

Twice a year the surface catchment sends a real rain down a second throat — the swallow, grated with withies to keep the rats honest — and for an hour the Cote is a drum. That water goes to the sump and stands a hand deep for three days and then is gone into the chaff, and everything in the place times its life to it.

**Warmth.** Blood heat, and wet with it. The floor is not a floor but a working compost: droppings, feather-meal, crushed potsherd, straw brought down in bundles, turned with wooden rakes on a nine-day cycle. In the middle it is too hot to hold your hand in. The heat comes up through the soles of your feet and off the walls and out of the birds themselves, twenty thousand small furnaces, and it does not go anywhere, because the fill holds it. The room breathes once a day: cool air pours down at dawn and pools, warm air climbs out at dusk and the whole shaft smells of it above ground, a column of steam in winter that the surface people call the cote's ghost.

**Tenants.** The greys own the day: pale-throated, soot-backed, swallow-shaped, half the size of your fist, and they see well enough but they *navigate by clicking*, so the air is full of a dry ticking hiss, like sand thrown on a hot pan, twenty thousand times a second. They go out through the fly-holes at dawn in a rope of bodies that takes eleven minutes to pass, and they come back at evening with the same rope reversed, and in between the room is only half full and the keepers do the harvest — the saliva nests, glued into the pot-backs, cracked out with a bone spatula, graded, counted, carried up.

The leathers own the night. Small insect bats, in the cool upper courses where the pots are shallowest, pouring out of the oculus at dusk exactly as the greys settle, so that the room is never at less than two-thirds occupancy. The two shifts do not fight. They pass each other in the throat every morning and evening in a knot of confusion that lasts four seconds and has never once, in living memory, produced a collision.

Under them: crickets in the chaff in numbers that make the floor itself sound like a fire; guano-flies in a haze at the turning; carpet beetles working the feather-meal; woodlice; the pale compost worms, descended from a basket of worms somebody carried down before the current language existed. On the walls: tok-geckos, loud as knuckles on a door, and orb-weavers strung across the niche-mouths in a lattice so dense that in the far galleries you must sweep your face with a reed to walk. In the sump, finger-length fish, inbred to the colour of skimmed milk, eating larvae. Two rat-snakes work the galleries and are not killed; they are, in fact, the same two snakes that have worked the galleries since the founding, in the way that the threshold is the same threshold. Each is called by the same name its predecessor had. The current one is called Aunt and is regarded as a colleague. There are cats, small and dust-coloured, half deaf from a lifetime of the choir, who hunt by whisker and by the tremor of a ladder and who are counted in the ledger like everything else.

Green things are few and are all in the light's road: the moss necklace, a slick of algae staining the whitewash where the noon falls, four maidenhair ferns in the lip of the throat, and a vine that came down uninvited eighty years ago and is cut back every spring to exactly two arms, because the keepers like it and will not have it.

**Loudness.** It is not noise here; it is architecture. You speak into an ear or you use your hands. The keepers' counting-chant sits in a low band the birds do not use, a flat muttering drone that runs all day under the hiss, and children learn the numbers before they learn words. Over it: the wing-thrum, which is a pressure on the chest more than a sound; the geckos; the drip-scale; the creak of the wooden windlass bringing the buckets up; the dry rasp of Aunt's belly on brick; and, several times an hour, the *boom* — when the whole flock turns at once inside a closed bell of masonry and the air itself is struck like a drumhead. You feel it in the teeth. Visitors weep, sometimes, and can't say why.

The only thing that frightens anyone is a stop. If the choir drops for more than a breath, every head in the place comes up, because it means a fall of brick, or a snake in the wrong gallery, or the pressure changing, or the shutter jammed. Silence is the alarm. Once a year, at midday, they close the shutter on purpose and hold the room quiet for a minute — the grief minute, for everyone who died in the seventh cote when it came down — and in that minute you can hear the dome settling, one grain at a time, and the water finding its notes, and your own blood going round.

Then the rope is pulled, the false dawn arrives, the walls begin to sweat, and eleven thousand throats start up again, and the made world under the grass goes on being a living thing, which it was never designed to be, and has been for four hundred years.
