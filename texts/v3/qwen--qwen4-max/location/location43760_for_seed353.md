# The Anneal

Come onto it from the rim, in the dark, at the hour when the heat starts coming up through the soles of your boots and you begin to suspect that the ground is not rock.

The rim is a curb. It runs thirty kilometers around the terrace in a long bead of sintered tailings, grey-black, glassy where the grit-wind has polished it, worn so round you could not find an edge to cut yourself on if you went looking. It was mountains once, or the spoil of mountains. Now it is a lip. Every meter of it carries a raised foundry mark — a lot number, a heat code, a pitch designation — and a hundred million years of dry abrasion have softened the letters into the *memory* of letters, a braille of manufacture that your thumb reads as texture and nothing else.

Below the curb the floor falls away almost flat: a hand's width per kilometer, draining toward the sump in the middle distance. Nothing wore it flat. It sagged flat. At these temperatures, over this much time, everything creeps — carbon, ceramic, sinter, steel — and the whole terrace has slumped slowly into the shape of a thing that can no longer hold its own weight. This is the oldest surface in the world and it is not eroded. It is *tired*.

The air stands at eighty degrees and has never been wet. There is no water on the terrace, no hydrate in the mineral, no vapor in the haze. The chemistry here would not tolerate it: a cup of water thrown into a working bath would take the roof off. The only water within a day's walk is the water you brought in your body, and you can feel it leaving you, and the terrace does not notice.

---

From the curb you can see the whole region, which is the only place you can see anything. The openness is a view, not a permission.

Out there in the dark: the Stand, packed trunk against trunk from the rim to the sump, a hundred thousand columns and no aisle between them. Beyond it the Boil, pale even in this light, a basin of alumina powder aerated from below until it behaves like a liquid — heaving, slopping, throwing slow swells against its banks, drowning anything dense you set on it. Dry. Not one drop in it. The stacks behind, exhaling their gas columns into a lid of soot that has never once produced rain. The Boneyard to the east, where spent linings are stacked in sorted grades, and the stacks have slumped into hills so convincingly hill-shaped that you have to kneel and pick up a fragment to remind yourself that it has a part number.

And the Frost: a district gone white. You can see it from here because white is the only color the dark will give back. Six hectares of dead trunk, bloomed and crusted where the sap cooled below the line and froze in the pipes. Salt frost looks like snow and behaves like a stroke. Nothing recovers a limb that has frozen. The forest walls it off with a callus of dry foam and grows around it, and the white stays, and gets whiter, and is the closest thing here to a monument.

---

Go down into the Stand and the world closes.

The trunks are carbon: not wood, but a baked, graphitic carbon, laid down in the coarse ropelike grain of a thing extruded rather than grown, though it is both. They stand shoulder to shoulder, fused where they touch, and where they have not fused they have grown a felt of graphite wool into the gap until the gap is gone. There is no between. The canopy overhead is leaf on leaf on leaf, twelve deep, a matte black baffling that admits no light in either direction. The leaves are fins. They are the coldest objects on the terrace — you can lay a palm on one for a whole second — because their entire purpose is to dump waste heat into a sky that has no sun in it, and they are engineered, by whatever passed for engineering here, to radiate in the infrared and emit almost nothing you could see.

That is why it is dark. Not because light is absent. Because light is *expensive*, and this place has spent a hundred million years learning not to leak.

You move through the Stand by its ducts: the bores of dead trunks, the service galleries the forest maintains between its own organs, the crawlspaces above the busbars. They close behind you at the speed of a slow hand. You learn to travel with the flow of the feeding rather than against it. There is no path here that is not also a throat.

---

The sap is a straw-colored molten salt, nitrate and nitrite, running at three hundred degrees in traced and lagged lines up the trunk's inner wall. It freezes at two hundred twenty. Everything the forest does, it does above that line, and the whole vast silent industry is at bottom a fight to keep the far ends warm.

Deeper, at the roots, the pots: carbon-lined cells, each the size of a house, holding a bath of dissolved mineral at nine hundred sixty, white-hot, crusted over on top with its own frozen scab. Under the bath lies the metal — two hands' depth of liquid aluminum, mirror-bright, and it *waves*. The busbars run three hundred thousand amperes past the cell walls and the magnetic field takes the metal pad and rolls it, slow swells traveling across a sea you will never see, under a crust, in the dark, forever. The tides here are magnetic. Compasses are useless on the terrace; dropped iron slides along the grain of the current, north-northeast, patient as a compass needle that has decided to walk.

The forest eats heat from below and dumps it above and takes its living from the difference. Somewhere under the sump there is a heat nobody has found the bottom of. It may be the only unmade thing on the terrace. The forest has been trying to reach it, and label it, for a very long time.

---

Now the churn.

The trunks are anodes. They are consumed from beneath, at the working face, at about a centimeter and a half a day. Every column in the Stand is burning away at its base as fast as it can, and growing down from above as fast as it can, and the two rates have been matched so precisely for so long that the forest looks stationary from the rim and is in fact a waterfall.

A trunk lasts twenty-eight days. Then the butt is cut loose, hauled, crushed, rebaked, and re-set, and the whole Stand is on this cycle, staggered, so that at any moment a thousand columns are being dropped and a thousand are being seated and ten thousand feeder arms are injecting powder into ten thousand broken crusts, and the swarf is moving in fluidized lines like grain in a chute, and the grafting yards at the sump edge are trading stock and salt and dies in a packed, elbowing, bazaar-like fury that has no buyers and no sellers and no money and never has had.

All of it silent. Or nearly.

---

Because the mass eats sound. Graphite felt, packed wool, twelve layers of leaf, six hectares of fused trunk — the terrace is an anechoic chamber the size of a county. Sound has a horizon of about forty paces here. Beyond that, nothing carries.

What you hear, standing in a gallery: your own blood. A hundred-cycle hum that you feel in the sternum before you hear it, the rectifiers somewhere below turning heat into current. The tick and tock of thermal expansion, everywhere, always, a sound like a cooling engine except that nothing is cooling. Every eleven seconds, from some direction that the felt makes unlocatable, a single muffled *knock* — a crust-breaker going through the scab. A long sigh from the Boil as the gas bed shifts. And under everything, conducted through the floor and up the bones of your legs, a dense rapid tapping that is not sound at all but speech: pressure, inventory, requests, the whole traffic of the place running through solid contact because the air is worthless as a medium and always has been.

Once in a while a column buckles, or a pot taps, and there is a real noise, a huge one — and the felt takes it, and within four seconds it is gone, and the quiet closes over the site like water over a stone, except there is no water.

---

Light is a symptom.

You see by seams: a cherry-colored crack where a crust has been broken and the bath shows through, gone in a minute. By the pale blue corona that crawls on the leaf-tips when the dry storms come, triboelectric, silent, dust charging against dust. By the white of the Frost. By nothing else, for hours, until your eyes give up entirely and you begin, without deciding to, to navigate by the temperature of your face — turning down a gallery because the left cheek is warmer, stopping because the heat ahead has a *shape*.

And then, perhaps twice in a night, an anode effect: a cell runs starved of powder, the voltage jumps eightfold, and the whole district strobes white through every seam and gap at once, a hard actinic glare that shows you the Stand for one full second — the packed columns, the felt, the arms, the labels — and then goes out.

The labels. That is the thing that stays with you. Every pipe is color-banded. Every valve has a tag. Every die in the Boneyard has been recut so many times that its geometry has drifted, generation on generation, into something like a species, and the drift is documented, in stamped characters, on the die. The forest has maintained its signage for a hundred million years, in perfect legibility, in absolute darkness, for no one. There has never been an eye here. Nothing on this terrace has ever had a mouth that was not a port.

That is why there are no animals. Motility was never outsourced. The hands are organs: crust-breakers, tappers, feeders, running on rails grown from the trunk, and when one shears off its rail it goes on working — blind, tapping, walking, feeding nothing — until something salvages it. If you see a small quick thing move alone in the dark, it is not an animal. It is a part that has lost its whole, and it is looking for the socket, and it will not find it.

---

The weather is manufactured and dry. Soot-fall instead of rain, sifting down through the canopy in a fine black snow that never melts and never washes. Dust devils a hundred meters tall walking the Boil. Winds that reverse at shift-change when the load schedule steps down and the whole terrace exhales through its stacks at once, and the leaf-lids open, and for ninety seconds you can see stars — the only time you ever do — before the canopy seals again and the schedule comes back up.

The day here is not a rotation. It is a duty cycle. Time is kept in sets of twenty-eight, and in linings, which last eight years, and in the long slow unit that has no name in any language but which the floor taps out sometimes, a figure with too many digits, meaning *the anneal*.

Because it is finishing. That is what all this is. Every worn bead of the rim, every slumped hill of the Boneyard, every thread polished down to a suggestion of a thread, is the terrace settling toward its final configuration — the shape that dissipates best, the arrangement with nothing left to relieve. It has been almost finished for a hundred million years. The churn is the last fraction of a percent, and the last fraction of a percent is where all the work is, and it must churn forever to stay this still.

They say — the ones who have stood on the slab at the sump in the dark long enough to learn the floor's grammar — that the tapping is a count. That it is counting itself, continuously, trunk by trunk, die by die, arm by arm, the way you count a thing you are afraid to lose. And that the count always comes out one short.

And that if you stand very still on the worn slab, and stop moving, and let your water go, the count comes out even.

And everything within forty paces stops.
