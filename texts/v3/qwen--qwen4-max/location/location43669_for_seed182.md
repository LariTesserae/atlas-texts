## The Kettle

From the plain there is nothing to see until you are almost in it. The country above is dry steppe, gravel and wire grass, a wind that never stops and never arrives anywhere. Then the ground simply ends, in a lip of broken marl, and you are looking down into a hole three hundred paces across and ninety deep, and the hole is full of town.

They call the basin the Kettle. The town inside it is Brome, after what it makes. Sixty-one years ago the salt dome beneath this country rose into the groundwater and the groundwater ate it, and a cavity grew in the dark for two centuries until the roof of it could no longer hold the weight of a chemical works, four streets, a chapel, and a Sunday market. All of that went down in about eleven seconds. It did not shatter. The marl cap broke into slabs and the slabs rode the talus down and set, tilted, mostly intact, and the salt beneath was soft enough to take them like a hand taking coins. Then the company surveyed the wreckage, found that the collapse had brought them ninety meters closer to the brine and to the heat, and decided not to leave.

So Brome is a town that fell down and kept working.

## The weep line

The walls show the whole stratigraphy: pale marl at the top, then a rust band, then gypsum in dogtooth blades, then the anhydrite cap, and below that the salt itself, which is not white. It is honey and liver and smoke, and in three seams it is a deep bottle blue that nobody has explained. The salt is warm to the touch everywhere, forty-three degrees at the floor, and it is wet.

This is the first strangeness, and it is only chemistry: the salt here carries magnesium, and magnesium salt is thirsty. It pulls water out of air that feels dry to a human mouth. So the walls weep continuously, in threads and beads, in a slow constant perspiration that has nothing to do with weather. There is a horizon two-thirds down where the seeps concentrate — the weep line — and along it the rock is always glossy, always dripping, always growing.

Everything below the weep line has an edge like a knife. Not because it broke recently. Because it grew this morning.

## What grows here

The Kettle is the only wet place in two hundred kilometers of gravel, and everything that could reach it has arrived and refused to leave.

The terraces are choked with glasswort, jointed and succulent, green in the shade and blood-red where the brine is strong, packed stem against stem until the whole surface is a springy mat that gives under the boot and releases a smell like hot spinach and iodine. Saltbush crowds the stair-footings. Tamarisk has got into the old chapel's roof and drinks brine and excretes it through its leaves, so the tree stands in a permanent glitter of its own making and drips sugar and salt onto the pump-house floor.

And there is the felt. It is not a plant. It is a consortium — algae, a fungus, a halophile archaeon, and mineral — and it grows in sheets a centimeter thick over any surface that stays damp and dim, which is nearly all of them. It comes off in one piece if you are patient. Brome wears it, writes on it, roofs with it, wraps its dead in it. Fresh felt is olive-black and smells of low tide. Old felt is stiff and pale and rings faintly when struck.

The crystallizer pans, cut in nine steps down the eastern talus, hold hot brine at concentrations where nothing ordinary lives, and the archaea in them turn the liquor a magenta so dense it reads as black under the lamps. In the thirty-one minutes of daylight they go the color of an infected wound.

Every leaf here is a mineral factory. Every plant is helping to bury the town.

## Heat, and the shape of the day

The heat comes from below, so the ordinary rule of the world is inverted: down is hot, up is cool. The floor of the Kettle runs at forty-three, forty-six in the Understreets. Ninety meters up, at the rim, it is thirty and there is a wind. Housing price is a function of altitude, exactly and without exception. The shift-bosses and the assay clerks live on Terrace Two, under the marl, where the felt grows thin and the air moves. The pan crews live at the bottom, in the tilted upper stories of the drowned town, and sleep in hammocks strung across stairwells because the stairwells are the only rooms with a through-draft.

Work happens at night. The middle of the day — they call it the long middle, six hours — is spent lying still in the high galleries with a wet cloth on the face, and this is the one time the town is genuinely silent, four hundred people breathing in the dark, listening to the walls tick.

Because at night the floor radiates its heat straight up the shaft of the Kettle to the open sky, and the air down there cools, and the moisture the walls have been shedding all day condenses out in a fog that stands about twelve meters deep and does not move. Then everyone comes out and harvests it. Felt sheets are strung on frames between the pipe-bridges, and at the cold hour before dawn the crews wring them into cisterns, one sheet at a time, hand over hand, and the whole town smells like a wet coat. That is where Brome's drinking water comes from. Not rain. Not the sump, which is bitter. The town drinks the air, ninety meters below the ground.

## Packed

There is no empty space in Brome. There is no plaza, no yard, no gap.

The collapse compressed the streets and the accretion welded them. Buildings lean into each other and are joined by salt at the shoulder. Every railing has a bulge of crystal along its underside like a caterpillar. Every pipe is fatter than its lagging. Rooms have been subdivided by growth into rooms with holes in the walls, and the holes are the doors now. To cross the town you do not walk between things; you walk *through* the interstices of things, sideways, ducking, with your hand on a warm wall that has a grain like frozen honey.

The main ways are cut. Every morning a gang goes out with wooden mallets and hardwood chisels — never steel, steel spalls the crystal and brings down sheets — and cuts the routes back to a meter's width, and by the following week the routes have narrowed again, and the week after that they are shoulder-width, and the gang comes back. A path in Brome is not a structure. It is a maintenance schedule.

## The engine

The turbine hall is on Terrace Five, and it is the reason the lights work at all. Brome runs a heat engine between the hot floor and the cold sky: brine loops down through the salt, comes up hot, gives its heat to the working fluid, and the spent heat is pumped ninety meters up the shaft to a bank of radiator pipe hung along the rim, where the steppe wind takes it. They call it the cold end. From below you can see it as a black comb against the stars, and on winter nights it steams, and that steam is the only thing in the Kettle that ever leaves.

The turbine is enormous and turns slowly. There is no hammering anywhere in this town, no stamping, no percussive work at all — the pumps are rotary and submerged, the pans are stirred by paddles at four revolutions a minute, the bromine gallery is sealed and its vapors go up a stack. What you hear, everywhere, at all times, is a hum around forty cycles that you feel in the sternum before you hear it in the ear, and which visitors find unbearable for three days and then stop noticing forever.

The bromine gallery is the one dead place. A cell weeps there, has wept for eleven years, and Terrace Seven is bare rock and bare pipe and stunted glasswort, red-brown, sharp-smelling, the felt peeled back from it in a clean margin like a tide line. People cross it quickly. It is the only surface in Brome you can see the actual age of.

## Light

Direct sun reaches the floor for thirty-one minutes a day, in a lozenge that crosses the sump and the lowest three terraces. At the founding it was fifty-two minutes. The assay office has kept the measurement every clear day since the Drop, in felt ledgers, and the number goes down about a third of a minute a year, because the rim overhangs more each decade and the salt dome keeps rising. The light is the town's real clock. Children are told the figure the way children elsewhere are told the population of the world.

The rest of the day is lit by bounce: a moving patch of second sun reflected off the pale marl at the top of the western wall, which crawls across the terraces at a walking pace and is bright enough to read by and not bright enough to work by. Below that, sodium lamps, few, hooded, half-furred with felt, throwing a light the color of weak tea. Below *that*, the blue seams, which glow when a lamp passes behind them, so that a man walking a corridor with a hand-lamp makes a slow blue wave travel through the wall alongside him.

Once a week the lamps are doused for an hour and everyone sits in the dark until their eyes come around, and then the sump shows its faint phosphorescence, a green so weak it lives only in the corner of the eye, and the town looks at it, and checks the growth on its own hands, and puts the lights back on.

## Quiet, and how sound behaves

The felt eats sound. The crystal sheds it. A shout is a bad idea for two reasons: the crust above the terraces hangs in sheets, and a sharp noise at the right pitch will bring one down, a hundred kilos of glass-edged salt; and because everyone has learned to speak low, a shout is also simply rude.

But the salt conducts. Put your jaw against a wall — the bone, not the ear — and you can hear a conversation three rooms away, clear as a telephone, the crystal carrying it with almost no loss. Brome has no privacy in the ordinary sense and an elaborate etiquette instead. You do not lean on walls in company. Lovers go out to the pans. Arguments are conducted standing in the middle of a room with felt hung around, and everyone knows this is theater, and everyone permits it.

Under all of it: drip, in a hundred pitches, each one tuned by the crystal face it strikes. And the crepitation — the salt growing, ticking, a sound like a very distant fire, continuous, everywhere, the noise of the town being slowly filled in.

## Rare things

There are no birds. There are eleven cats, pale, half-feral, all named, all known to everybody; when one dies the shift bells are tapped once with a wooden mallet. There is a gecko on the warm wall of the pump-house that may be several geckos. There are brine flies for nine days after the rain. And in the sump there are fish — a small blind pale thing, no eyes worth the name, maybe two hundred of them, counted annually by lowering a lit board and photographing what comes to it. The count is published. It has never varied by more than thirty. Brome argues about the fish the way other towns argue about football.

## The copies

Here is the rule the whole place is organized around: **the salt copies what stays still.**

Leave a wrench on a bench for a month and there will be a second wrench beside it, in halite, hollow, translucent, more precise than the original — the threads rendered exactly, the wear on the handle rendered exactly, the maker's stamp legible in crystal. Leave it a year and the original will be inside the copy, and you will have to break one to get the other.

So nothing in Brome is allowed to be still. Everything hangs: cups on hooks, tools on chains, chairs slung from the beams by a single rope so they turn freely. Doors are never left in one position. And there are the turning rounds — a job for children and for the old, four shifts a day, walking the town with a tally stick, giving every fixed object a quarter turn. A valve wheel, a milestone, a boot, a sleeping man's shoulder. The rounds are the most respectable employment in the Kettle. A good turner is spoken of the way other places speak of a good surgeon.

The dead are not turned. The dead are set in niches above the weep line, wrapped in felt, and left, and the salt takes them, and over forty years the copies become more detailed than the people ever were — the felt's weave, the eyelashes, a lace collar the woman never owned but which the crystal decided she should have. They call them the Patient. There are three hundred and six. The oldest, from the Drop itself, have gone almost entirely to mineral and are extraordinarily beautiful and no longer resemble anybody, and the town tends them, and dusts them with a soft brush, and does not turn them, and speaks to them at the correct low volume.

## The Smoothing

For about three weeks in autumn the steppe remembers to rain, and the rain falls into the Kettle, all of it, the whole catchment funnels down. The town wraps what it can in oiled felt and carries the rest high, and then the water comes down the walls and dissolves the year.

This is the mercy. Without it the accretion would have sealed Brome solid a century ago. For three weeks everything runs and softens and rounds; the knife edges go blunt, the copies slump and lose their faces, the paths widen without anyone cutting them, the whole basin looks worn and old and gentle. Then the rain stops, the walls begin to weep again, the brine films dry in the heat, and every surface in the Kettle grows a new crystal face by the following week.

That is why this place is sharp. Not because it is new. Because it is made new every year, on top of something very old, and the sharpness is a skin a millimeter thick over sixty-one years of settlement over two hundred million years of salt.

The town measures age in smoothings. A woman is forty smoothings old. A marriage is nine. The Patient are counted in the other direction.

## The closing

Everyone knows the arithmetic. The dome rises; the accretion fills; the rim overhangs; the daylight shrinks by a third of a minute a year. At current rates the lozenge of sun will fail to reach the floor in ninety-four years, and the Kettle will roof itself over sometime after that, and Brome will be a town inside a solid block of salt, humming, warm, lit by lamps, entirely intact.

They discuss it the way you'd discuss a relative's long illness. There is a proposal, renewed every generation, to cut a notch in the rim and buy back twenty minutes of sun; it has never passed, because the notch would take the cold end with it, and the cold end is the light they have now.

So the town goes on turning its objects a quarter turn at a time, wringing its fog at the cold hour, cutting its streets back to a meter each morning, speaking low, counting the fish. Below the plain, in the hot dim, in a space you could walk across in eleven minutes and could not finish describing in a life, everything is occupied, everything is damp, everything is a week old.
