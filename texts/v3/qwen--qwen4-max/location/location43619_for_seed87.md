## The Held Room

Nothing on the seafloor announces it. Cold plain, grey gravel, a scatter of brittle stars walking on their tiptoes, the occasional boulder that is not a boulder but a lump of carbonate the seep made last century. Twice a day the gravel in one particular patch lifts a little and settles, like the flank of something asleep. That is the whole sign. Water going down. Water coming back. The sea breathing through a plug of broken rock, and under the plug, a room.

Follow the water. It takes you through a hand's-depth of angular grit — nothing here is rounded, nothing has been rolled or worked or worn; every grain is a fresh fracture, sharp as crushed sugar, because the stone that made it is barely stone. It is chalk that has only recently agreed to be chalk. It crumbles under a thumb. It is still wet in the way that a thing is wet before it has ever been dry. Geology here is not ancient and patient; it is *wet paint*. The seep precipitates it out of the water, layer on layer, and the brine dissolves it back, and between those two verbs the room exists.

Then the grit ends and there is no floor beneath you and no wall for four body-lengths, and the cold arrives all at once, one degree above nothing, water so cold it reads as pressure rather than temperature, a hand laid flat on the face.

You are inside a bubble in the crust. One bubble. You could cross it in forty slow strokes. Everything that follows happens in this single room and nowhere else.

---

The room is shaped like the inside of an ear, or a bell, or a cupped palm — narrow at the top where the throat comes down through the rubble, swelling below, curving in at the base to a floor that is not a floor but a surface: a lake, inside the sea, inside the rock. Brine. Four times saltier than the water above it, anoxic, faintly sulfurous, heavy as syrup. It has a shoreline. It has waves. When the tide pushes down through the plug, the brine slops against its own beaches of white chalk and long slow internal waves roll along the interface, and the city on the shore rises and falls a finger's width, twice daily, forever.

The walls are milk-white and unfinished. Not weathered — *un*-weathered, the opposite: raw, sugary, sharp-edged, still being cut. Where the brine's upper vapor eats at the ceiling, the chalk retreats a fingernail's thickness each year and lets fall a silent snow of chalk flour that drifts down through the water for hours before it lands. Nothing in this room has been smoothed. There is no sand. There is no patina. The place has the specific unsettlement of a sculpture in the stage where the block has been opened but the figure has not yet been found.

And into that opening, the figure is being built.

---

It is a city. There is no better word and the animals do not have one.

Towers of cemented grit and secreted carbonate, six and eight and eleven body-lengths tall, standing so close that their upper galleries touch and fuse, so that the skyline is a single perforated mass rather than separate buildings. Streets between them barely wider than a body. Bridges. Stair-flights spiralling up the outside of towers to doors that open on nothing. Terraces stepping down to the brine shore. A wall around the whole of it, waist-high, with four gates, and outside the wall a district of half-built rooms — foundations, stub walls, arches with no walls beneath them — glowing.

That is how you read the growth: fresh mortar luminesces. The bacteria in it burn a weak blue for nine or ten days after it is laid, and then go dark. So the old town is black and the suburbs are lit, and the whole plan of the city is written in fading light, brightest at the edges where the work is, dimmest at the center where the work was. Enough light to see by, if you are patient. Enough to know a shape from a shadow. The color of the inside of a closed eyelid held near a candle.

The builders are pale, blind, many-legged, the length of a fingernail, and there are perhaps two hundred thousand of them in this room. They have no tools. They have never had a tool. Everything they make comes out of their mouths or off their bellies: grit gathered and glued, carbonate secreted, mucus laid down as a line and hardened into a curb. The plan is not drawn. The plan is a *smell* — a gradient of scent laid into the mortar by the previous generation, read by the antennae of the next, which builds where the scent says build and adds its own scent for the generation after. A city inherited as a fragrance.

It copies imperfectly. Whole districts are misspellings: a spiral stair built to a body-shape the species stopped producing eleven thousand generations ago, a chamber four millimeters too small for anything that has ever lived, an aqueduct that carries water from one part of the room to the same part of the room. Nobody inside knows. Nobody inside has ever seen the city. They know it as a sequence of edges and odors, a thing that exists in the fingertips, and the shape it makes — the towers, the wall, the four gates, the terraces descending to the shining lake — is visible only to something that does not live here.

Vegetation, such as it is: four trees. Clumps of tubeworm, red plumes withdrawn to a fringe of dark hair, standing in the plaza at the city's center, older than every building, the reason there is a plaza. Sparse bacterial mat on the open ground in patches like lichen on a step, grazed to bare chalk in some places, thick and velvety in others. Three strands of sulfide filament hanging from the ceiling, motionless in the slack water between tides. That is all the green thing this place will ever have, and it is not green.

The rest is animals. The water itself is crowded — larvae, swimmers, drifting comma-bodies, a permanent suspended throng so thick that to move is to touch. Brittle stars in the wall-cavities with their arms out like hands feeling for a dropped coin. Anemones in the door-niches, farmed for their faint flicker, seeded with food by masons who do not know they are farmers. Sea spiders stepping over the rooftops on legs like scaffolding. Scale-worms patrolling the curbs. Every surface furred, every gap occupied, three and four storeys of life stacked in the vertical, because the water is layered too: cold clear at the bottom, a sulfide-tinted middle where the light goes greenish, a fog of larvae against the ceiling.

---

The lake keeps the dead.

They are given to it — carried to the shore and released, and they sink through the interface into water that has no oxygen and therefore no rot. They do not decay. They accumulate. The brine is a library of every citizen the city has ever had, in order, undisturbed, pickled and perfect, down to the first few hundred at the very bottom who are a slightly different shape. When the tide drives an internal wave across the lake, the whole archive lifts and turns very slowly and settles again.

Gas comes up through the lake floor dissolved and stays dissolved; the brine will not let it bubble. This is important. Once every few years a pocket of it collects under a shelf of chalk and lets go all at once, and a single bubble the size of a plum rises and breaks at the interface, and the room *rings*.

They call it nothing, because they have no names, but the whole city goes flat to the ground for a day and a half afterward.

---

Here is the rule the place is built around.

The room is an ear turned inside out. Its curve gathers what happens inside and throws it up the throat and out through the rubble into the open sea, focused, amplified, audible for a kilometer. And inward it does the reverse: it deadens, absorbs, folds sound into its own soft chalk. Inside, you hear almost nothing. Outside, your smallest click is a shout.

So everything here is silent, and has been selected for silence over a very long time, and the silence is not peace. It is discipline. The masons lay mortar by pressing, never by tapping. The gates have no hinges; they are curtains of mucus that part. The young are brooded in wadded galleries that swallow their movement. Predation inside the city is entirely a matter of touch and chemistry — the nemertean that finds a doorway by taste, the scale-worm that reads a curb — and predation *from outside* is a matter of listening.

The long guest comes in through the throat perhaps four times a season: a pale cold-water octopus, small enough to squeeze the slot, which pours itself into the room and reads the city with eight arms. When it enters, two hundred thousand animals stop. Not hide — *stop*, mid-gesture, mortar still wet on the mouthparts, one leg raised. The city becomes an architecture with nobody in it. The guest moves through the streets taking whom it takes, six or eleven or forty, and the towers do not react, and after some hours it withdraws up the throat and the water carries the news chemically for a day, and then the raised leg comes down and finishes its step.

That is the loudest event in the life of this place, and it makes no sound at all.

What sound there is: the tick of chalk flour landing. The pressure-pop of the brine interface adjusting. A mandible closing on a grain, too small to travel. Your own body, if you had one here, would be an obscenity — the heartbeat alone would empty the streets.

---

It runs on two clocks. The tide, twice daily, in and out through the gravel, driving the currents that ventilate the room and rock the lake and bring the larvae up against the ceiling. And the year: for two months, when the sea above thaws and blooms, marine snow comes down through the plug in quantity, a slow blizzard of dead surface-life, and the plaza districts feast and grow fat and lay their broods, while the deep districts by the brine shore go on eating the seep's steady cold chemistry as they always have, and resent nothing, having no capacity for it. In winter the ice above lets a grey rumor of noon down through the rubble — a coin of dimness that crosses the floor and is gone. It is the only light in the city that does not come from something alive.

And under both clocks, the third process, the slow one: the ceiling is dissolving. The room is getting bigger. Every year the brine's vapor takes a fingernail's thickness off the roof and the city follows it up, plastering the new cavity, lining the widening hole, building in the wake of the erosion like a scab that keeps extending over a wound that will not close.

The masons are not aware that they are patching a membrane. But they patch it. They thicken the roof where it thins, they buttress the places where the chalk goes soft, and they do it with the same inherited odor that tells them where to put a stair. Because when the roof finally breaks through — and it will, in a few thousand years, which is soon — the room will stop being a room. It will be a hole in the seafloor. The bell will lose its throat and its ear-shape and its hush. The light of the open sea will come in, and the noise, and everything that listens.

The city is a held breath, and it is building against its own exhale.

What survives is not the towers. Every few years, a batch of larvae goes up through the gravel with the outbound tide, carrying in their bodies the smell of the plan — the whole misspelled inherited blueprint, the four gates, the useless stair — and drifts, and almost all of them die, and one in a very great number finds another soft place in the young chalk where water is going down through a plug of angular grit, and settles, and begins to press the first grain into place.

There are perhaps a hundred of these rooms in this shelf of rock. None of them knows about the others. None of them has ever made a sound that reached the next one. Each is a small white unfinished bubble, cold, crowded, glowing at the edges, full of blind citizens building a city they will never see around four red trees, above a lake that keeps every body it is given, waiting out the long patient dissolution of the roof.
