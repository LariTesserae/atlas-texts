On the plain above, there is nothing to see. Warm grass, a scatter of thorn, the ground pale and cracked in the dry months, and one fig standing alone with its crown spread wider than a house. Walk toward the fig and the ground turns out not to be ground. It is a lid. Beneath the roots the earth has fallen in along a slot no wider than a doorway, and the slot goes down four or five body-lengths into a hollow you could cross in twenty paces. From above it looks like a shadow the tree is keeping. From below it is a country.

The first thing is the heat, which is not the heat outside. Outside, the air scours. Inside, it sits at the temperature of a mouth and does not move. It smells of wet chalk and cucumber and, faintly, of the milk that runs from a broken fig stem. The second thing is the light: green, indirect, arriving through the slot and the lattice of roots that closes most of it, so that the whole hollow is lit the way a room is lit by a lamp in the next room. And the third thing, which takes longer to notice and then will not stop being noticed, is that the stone here is new.

Not new as in freshly exposed. New as in made last week.

The water that comes into this place arrives already carrying its stone. Somewhere up in the plateau it has spent years dissolving limestone in the dark, and when it reaches the air it lets go of what it carried, and the letting-go is a solid. It lays down calcite the way breath lays down fog on glass. Every surface the water touches gets a coat. Every surface it stops touching keeps the coat it has. The walls are cream and apricot and, where the algal films live, a dull rose; they are shaped like cauliflower, like frozen lace, like the inside of a shell, like a hundred small balconies stacked and merged. There is no sand in this hollow. There is no rounded pebble, no worn edge, no grit, no soil. Nothing here has been taken away. Everything here has been added.

You can dent the newest work with a thumbnail. It is soft, cheesy, faintly warm, and it crumbles into a flour that is not dust but crystal — tiny blunt rhombs that catch the green light and go out again. Give it a season of dryness and it will harden into rock you could break a tooth on. So the hollow is a place where you can watch stone being a liquid and then being a solid, and where the wall you leaned on this morning is measurably thicker than the wall you leaned on last month. Time here does not subtract. It only deposits.

The water itself is a visitor. It does not live here. Somewhere upstream there is a chamber in the rock that fills and fills until it reaches a bend, and then the bend siphons and the whole chamber empties in a run of forty minutes or an hour, and then there is nothing again until it fills. The schedule depends on rain falling on a plateau hours away, so it is not a schedule anyone inside could keep, only one everyone must be ready for. Between visits the hollow is damp, not wet: a film on the north wall, beads of condensation on the cool lower stone when the dawn air sinks in, a dozen rimstone basins the size of cupped hands holding water an inch deep, and everything else dry to the touch and pale. Pockets of wet in a great deal of dryness. When the surge comes it does not fall or splash. It arrives as a swelling film, spreading over the terraces, filling the basins from below, moving with a sound like a swallow. Then it goes, and the stone begins to drink it, and the whole hollow starts, quietly, to grow.

The fig is the plumbing. Its roots came down through the slot looking for the water and found it, and now they run the walls in ribs and buttresses, and where an old root has rotted it has left a pipe of tufa with a hollow bore, and the water uses these. The tree did not intend this. It was only reaching. But the channels it made are the channels the water keeps, and the water's channels are where the stone thickens, and the thickening stone makes the terraces and the overhangs and the basins, and those are the districts.

Because it is a city, or it is close enough to one that the word arrives on its own and will not leave.

There are quarters. There is the wet quarter under the main seep, where the tufa is youngest and pink and the moss grows in fat glossy cushions, each cushion a hand's breadth from the next, with clean stone plazas between — everything here is lush and everything here is far apart, the way rich people live. There is the dry quarter on the east wall where the water changed course two years ago; it has gone grey and stopped growing, and lichen has taken it, and the basins there are empty and hold only the shells of the snails that did not leave in time. There is the arcade of root-pipes. There is the ceiling, coffered by the fig's grip into a hundred pockets, and in each pocket a bat, hanging alone, spaced with the exact regularity of tenements — hundreds of them, none touching, each in its own room. There are the paper cells under the south overhang, grey and layered, a wasp colony provisioning caterpillars in a silence you would not expect from something with wings. There is the floor: mostly bare, ribbed like a palate, with one fern in a crack and a scatter of fig leaves that have not yet been dealt with.

The leaves are dealt with by crabs. Small, pale, patient, they come out over the plazas at night and shred what falls, and what they do not finish the water takes and entombs. This is the second rule of the hollow, after *nothing is worn*: everything that stays is kept. A leaf that lands on the wet quarter is a leaf for about a week and then a shape in stone forever. Cut into any wall and you read the courses — a dark line of last season's leaf-fall, a band of ash from a fire on the plain years back, a seam of snail shells, a bat's wing bone laid out flat and perfect and going nowhere. The city is built partly of its own dead. It does not mourn them; it courses them in and builds on top.

The third rule is about sound, and it is the reason the place feels the way it does.

The stone drinks noise. Tufa is porous and moss is worse and the whole irregular surface breaks a sound into pieces and swallows them; a word spoken here goes about four paces and dies. But that is only the architecture. The rest is the animals, and the animals have learned something. The gecko hunts by ear. The racer that comes down the root hunts by ear. The bats, of course. And so nothing that lives here makes a sound while the hollow is quiet — not the crickets, not the frogs, not the wasps coming home. They wait. They wait through the dry hours with the film creeping and the stone thickening and the snails' radulae scraping in a register too small to hear unless you put your ear against the wall, where it becomes a sound like rain on a roof three valleys away.

Then the water comes, and for forty minutes the hollow is permitted to speak. Frogs that have been sitting motionless in the basins all afternoon begin at once, all of them, a ragged clucking; the crickets open; the swiftlets at the mouth throw their dry clicks against the ceiling; a female wasp argues at the entrance of her cell. It is not loud. It is a small, dense, ordinary racket, the sound of a courtyard. But it is scheduled, and when the surge drains and the film thins, it stops — not gradually. It stops the way a hand comes off a string. The predators go back to waiting. The frogs sit down in their inch of water and become furniture. And the silence closes over the hollow like a lid, and the only thing you can hear is your own blood, and the stone, which is not in a hurry, continues.

At dusk the bats leave in a loose column up the slot, one at a time, without touching, and the light goes from green to grey to a blue that is more an absence than a color, and the crabs come out, and the walls hold the day's warmth for hours and give it back to nothing. There is one bright hour, near midday, when the sun crosses the slot and a coin of direct light lands on the west wall and moves; every green thing in the hollow is angled toward the path of that coin. The rest of the day the pale stone does the work, bouncing what it catches, keeping the place legible. It is a dim room made of a bright material.

And it is closing. That is the last thing, the thing you understand only after you have been there long enough to compare the mouth with a memory of the mouth. The tufa is growing at the lip too, where the splash and the condensation reach, and the fig's roots are thickening, and each year the slot is a little narrower, a little more laced. Nothing is eroding it open. There is no force here that opens anything. In a century, or three, the roof will finish itself, and the light will go out, and the moss will die back to a film and then to nothing, and the fern will go, and the fig will keep sending roots down into a dark it no longer needs to see through. The bats will stay. The snails will stay. The water will keep its appointments. The hollow will go on being built, from the inside, by everything that lives in it and everything that dies in it, until it is a sealed room full of stone shaped like a courtyard — a city with its lid on, still growing, still keeping its ledger, in a warmth and a damp and a silence that will by then be total.

For now there is a slot, and a fig, and an hour of light, and a great deal of bare pale stone between one green cushion and the next, and somewhere upstream a chamber filling.
