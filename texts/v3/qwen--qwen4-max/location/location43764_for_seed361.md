## The Flat

Walk out onto it in the morning and the first thing you notice is that your boots are warm.

Not sun-warm. The sun here is ordinary — a white coin in a blue sky at three thousand meters, throwing shadows with clean edges, the same sun that has burned every world you've known. But the stone under it is blood-warm, and it was blood-warm before dawn, and it will be blood-warm at midnight. Heat comes up through the soles of your feet in a slow steady push, like standing on the back of something sleeping.

The second thing you notice is that there is nothing to look at.

The plateau runs to the horizon in every direction and the horizon is a ruled line. No hills. No ridges, no knuckles of buried granite, no scree, no gullies. Once there were mountains here — you can find their ghosts if you kneel and put your palm flat and think about what you're touching — a range as tall as anything, folded and thrust and glaciated, and then worn, and then worn, and then worn. Two billion years of rain took the summits. Another billion took the roots. What is left is the last page of that story: a surface flat to within the width of a fingernail over a hundred kilometers, canted very slightly toward a sea that dried before there were animals.

It is not a plain. A plain is a stage in a process. This is the end of the process. Geology here is finished. Nothing further is scheduled.

The wind crosses it at a constant speed and makes a sound like a held breath, and under the wind, felt more in the jaw than the ear, there is a tone. Low. Patient. It does not vary. Walk five days and it will not vary.

## Finding the hole

You find the pit by its plume.

Ten kilometers out it looks like a village fire: a thin white feather standing up out of the emptiness, leaning in the wind, dissolving at about two hundred meters. There is no village. There is nothing out here at all except, occasionally, another feather, days apart.

The plume is breath. Warm wet air rises out of the ground into thin cold air and condenses, and that is all it is, and it is the only vertical thing for a hundred kilometers in any direction.

You walk toward it and the ground stays flat and then the ground stops. There is no lip, no raised rim, no rubble ring, no warning. The surface simply ends, the way a table ends. You have to lie down and put your head over to see the bottom, and even then the scale refuses to resolve for a moment — a hundred and ten paces across, forty meters down, a clean-walled cylinder sunk into the finished world like a hole punched in a page.

Around the rim, cut into the stone, runs a groove as wide as a finger. It has been there long enough that its edges have softened, though nothing here erodes anymore. At the northern point of the circle there is a numeral. It has worn to a dimple with a shape in it. Someone, once, thought this hole should be counted.

## Down

There are rungs.

They start a meter below the rim and go down in a staggered line, square-sectioned, warm, unfussed by rust or lichen, and they are not driven into the rock — they are *of* it, the same material, grown out or left behind, as though the wall were asked politely for a ladder and complied. Twenty-two of them. Then they stop, mid-shaft, with no explanation, and the last eight meters you do on a rope of woven fig-bark that somebody replaces every few years.

Descending is like walking into a mouth.

At the rim the air is thin and cold and smells of nothing. Two meters down it thickens. Ten meters down it is warm and wet and smells of crushed leaf, of ammonia, of fruit going over, of the sweetish rot at the bottom of a flower vase. Twenty meters down you have to stop and let your ears clear, not from pressure but from noise.

The walls are banded. Snails — a hundred thousand snails, in horizontal stripes a hand-span thick, sorted by size, largest at the bottom, smallest at the top, each band a different shade of ochre and umber and bone. They graze the film that condenses on the stone each night. Between the bands the wall is bare and faintly glossy and shows no tool marks, no fractures, no talus at its foot. Nothing that falls down this shaft is allowed to accumulate.

## The floor

Nine trees.

That is the whole forest: nine figs, standing far apart on a floor of bare warm rock, each one eighty or a hundred paces from its neighbor, each one enormous. They are not stunted by the emptiness around them. They are *lavish* — canopies forty meters wide, leaves the size of doors, aerial roots dropping in curtains, branch-work so dense with epiphytes and orchids and hanging soil that a single tree carries a hillside's worth of biomass in a space you could walk around in a minute. Lush, and lonely. Between them: nothing. Wet stone, a few puddles, the long clean shadows of the ordinary sun moving across the floor as the day goes.

The roots cannot break the rock. They tried for a very long time. Now they run in seams — straight seams, meeting at angles that no root system would choose — and where a root crosses a seam it flattens itself into a ribbon and follows it, and the seams go somewhere, and the roots go with them.

Water stands in shallow basins in the floor, dozens of them, hand-deep, none larger than a bed. They are the same shape. They are the same depth. They do not silt up, they do not go green, they do not dry below a certain line, and if you scoop one out with your hands it is full again by morning, wicked up out of the warm stone. There is no river here. There has never been a river here. There is dew, and fog, and the rain that comes when it comes, and a sump at the eastern edge where the floor dips and the water goes down into dark and does not come back up, though things live in it.

## The column

At any hour of the day the air of the pit is full. Not "there are insects." The air is *occupied*, the way a river is occupied by water.

Midges and small dark flies in a slow rotating column that fills the shaft from floor to rim. Above them, hunting them, a shifting lace of swifts — hundreds, then thousands, folding and pouring, never touching the walls, never leaving the cylinder of the hole, turning at the rim as if the rim were glass. Below them, in the fig canopies, everything else: bees in sheets, wasps, beetles armored like jewelry, moths the size of hands, tree frogs in the leaf-axils by the thousand, each axil a wet green room with a family in it.

At dusk the column rises.

It comes out of the hole as a smoke that is not smoke, a hundred meters of living particulate, and the swifts go up with it and the bats come out of the wall-crevices and go up with it too, and for eleven minutes the sky above the pit is a solid braided rope of appetite. Then it settles. Then the night shift begins, and the noise changes key.

There is one lizard. It is four meters long and it lives under the third tree and it eats whatever the column drops, which is a great deal. There is one hawk, on the rim, at the northern point, near the numeral. There is one of everything that is large. The world here can afford nine trees and a hundred million flies and exactly one thing that eats lizards, and it has arranged itself accordingly.

## The noise

It is loud. Understand this properly: it is *loud*.

At the rim you must raise your voice. Ten meters down you must shout. On the floor, conversation is a pantomime. The frogs alone are a physical pressure, a sawing pulsing wall of sound in the range where human hearing is most defenseless, and they stop all at once every hour or so for four seconds and then resume, and in those four seconds you hear the rest of it — the drip, the fig-parrots screaming in the seventh tree, the swifts' dry ratcheting overhead, the wind hissing across the lip forty meters up like a bow drawn on a wire, the deep hum of the stone coming up through your shins, and, under everything, a slow *whump* every ninety seconds as the shaft reverses a little of its flow and the whole column of air leans.

At dawn and at dusk the reversal is total, and the rim sings. A single clean note, a struck-bowl note, five or six seconds long, produced by nothing you can find. The hawk knows it. It shifts its feet.

## What was left here

A hut on the southern rim: dry-laid stone, one room, a copper door gone the color of a leaf. Empty. A bench inside, with a hollow worn in it. Outside, a ceramic pipe running from nowhere to the lip, and a shallow bowl on a plinth, and in the bowl, fruit — fresh, three figs, put there this morning by someone.

By evening the bowl is empty and polished.

Further out, four days east, a single square building the size of a thumbnail at arm's length, which never gets closer at the rate it should. Along the rim, mason's ticks at intervals, so faint you find them with your fingertips. A cairn, three stones, with a beetle-wing tucked under the top one. And in the wall of the shaft, at the level where the rungs stop, a rectangle of stone slightly different in grain from the rest, which is the only repair anyone has ever needed to make here, and which was not made by anyone.

## What the stone is

Kneel on the floor of the pit and look closely at the rock and you will see that the grains are all the same size.

Not roughly. Exactly. And they are not grains; they are units; and if you scratch one with a knife — go ahead, everyone does — the scratch will be shallower in an hour and gone by morning, and the dust you raised will not be where you left it.

The Flat is not the exhausted remainder of a landscape. The Flat is a *product*. Something wanted a surface: a radiator, a ground plane, a substrate, an antenna the size of a subcontinent, a place with no relief and no shadow and no interruption, held to tolerance, forever. So it ate the mountains. Grain by grain, patiently, over an interval that makes the age of mammals a rounding error, it took the relief away and it kept the heat and it made the world flat and warm and empty, and it finished, and it has been finished for a very long time, and it maintains itself.

But it must breathe. A thing that dumps that much heat needs ports. So there are holes — eleven thousand of them, or ninety thousand, scattered across the flat at intervals of days — and each hole is a warm wet throat full of trapped weather, and into every one of them, life fell, and stayed, and became enormous in the way that only isolated things become enormous.

The machine does not mind. It has been observed that the moths fly in grids. It has been observed that certain beetles emerge on the ninth day, always, before the rain, and that the rain comes on the ninth day, mostly. It has been observed that the hawk's call contains, in its last third, an exact fragment of the tone that comes up through the stone, and that the hawk has never heard that tone, because it is below hearing, and that it sings it anyway.

Something is counting. The swarms are very good at counting. Whether the counting is a use of the animals or a courtesy toward them is not a question the place answers.

## Night

The sun goes down ordinarily, in orange and rose, with ordinary clouds stacked in the west. The stone stays warm. The fog forms in the basins and lies along the floor in flat white sheets a knee deep, and out of it the fig roots rise like the backs of animals.

The frogs go up a fourth. The bats come out. The column rises and breaks and resettles. Somewhere below the sump, water moves through a place that has no name and has never been seen, and blind things turn to face it.

Above the rim, the plume goes silver in the moonlight and stands up out of the emptiness, and ten kilometers away, on the flat, another one is standing, and another, and another, all night, all warm, all loud, in a finished world where nothing further is scheduled — except everything that is alive.
