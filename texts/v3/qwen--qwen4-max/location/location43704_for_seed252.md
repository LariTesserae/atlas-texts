## The Room Under the Pan

From above there is nothing. A salt plain the color of a struck match-head at noon, flat to the horizon, ticking as it expands. Heat comes off it in sheets. Somewhere in the middle distance there is a slot in the crust about as wide as a boot, with a lip of freshly broken selenite around it, and if you were walking you would step over it and keep going, and that would be the whole of your encounter with the place.

The slot narrows immediately into a chimney, then a tube, then a wet fracture that you go through sideways with your arms up. The rock in there is 61°C. It weeps. The water on it is fresh, which makes no sense in a salt bed a hundred meters thick, and is the first clue.

Then the fracture opens and you are standing in the room.

---

It is nine meters by six, with a floor, four walls, and a ceiling, and it is lit like an overcast noon — bright, shadowless, everywhere at once. Your first thought is that a shaft must come down from the surface somewhere behind you. There is no shaft. The light comes through the ceiling itself: a single cleaved slab of selenite four meters thick, one continuous crystal, lying flat above the void like a lid.

You cannot find its lower face. Look up and your eye goes into the gypsum and keeps going and finds no surface to stop on — no reflection, no glaze, no Fresnel sheen at all. The slab is graded at the sub-wavelength scale from its skin inward, and it does not reflect light, ever, from any angle. What you see instead is the sun above the pan, smeared by four meters of crystal into a white field with no disc in it. It is the brightest ceiling in the world and it looks like nothing whatsoever.

The floor is bare halite, milky, translucent to about a hand's depth, so that standing on it you feel you are standing on lit fog. There are three drip points. There is one pool, four meters across and never more than thirty centimeters deep, stratified into a warm brine below and a lens of fresh water above so stable that you can see the boundary as a wobbling line, like the seam in badly poured glass. There are two crystal columns, floor to ceiling, each about the thickness of a thigh. There are four stains on the walls, none bigger than a spread hand. Everything else is empty white stone and hot empty air.

The air is 58°C and saturated. This is the thing to understand before anything else: you cannot cool in here. Sweat does not leave you. A body in this room only heats, and it heats fast, and you have perhaps a quarter of an hour before your judgment goes, and less than that if you came down the fracture quickly. The room is not hostile. It simply has no opinion about you, and its wet-bulb temperature is above the temperature at which people stop being alive.

---

Every edge in here looks broken this morning.

That is not a figure of speech. The walls retreat by dissolution — hot water comes up the feeder fracture at 91°C, undersaturated, hungry — and they retreat along planes, not in curves. There is no rounding anywhere. No sand, no rubble, no weathered rind, no dust of age. The columns have arrises you could cut bread on. In the corner there is a block the size of a chest that fell from the ceiling within the last few years, and its fracture surfaces are still glassy, still bright, still throwing little rainbows when you move your light.

And yet the room keeps its shape. Scratch the wall with a knife and come back at dawn: the scratch is gone, filled with new halite in optical continuity with the old, no seam, no scar. The dissolution front eats and the growth front replaces, and they are not fighting each other. They are the same operation seen from two sides. The room is being continuously re-cut to a specification, and that is why it is young. It will always be young. It has been young for eleven thousand years.

---

The salt here is not ordinary salt.

It has a defect lattice — vacancies and substitutions in an ordered pattern that repeats over centimeters, which is a thing crystals do not do at 60°C in a wet room, where thermal noise should smear any such order into nothing within microseconds. It holds anyway. The pattern is coherent across the whole chamber, floor to ceiling, and out into the bed for a hundred meters in every direction, getting slower and coarser as it goes, so that there is no place you can point to and say: here the device stops and the geology begins.

It runs between the sun and the sky. Daylight pumps it; the cold of the night sky, radiated to through the transparent lid, is its sink. Between those two it does work, and the work is mostly this:

It sorts. The feeder water arrives as brine and the ceiling releases it as fresh distillate, at ambient temperature, without boiling anything, without a membrane you could find or a pressure you could measure. It moves ions up their gradients and pays for it out of the sun.

It stores light. The walls hold color centers that charge all day and let go slowly, so that when the sun leaves the pan the room does not. At midnight in there it is still as bright as a cloudy afternoon. It dims toward morning and never goes dark, and the four stains on the walls — films of purple sulfur bacteria, the only color in the world here, rust and olive and weak tea — photosynthesize straight through the night on light that was yesterday's.

It anticipates. The three drip points do not fall randomly. They fall on a schedule, eleven seconds and eleven seconds and thirty-four, and the schedule changes when the pool's chemistry drifts, and it changes *before* the drift becomes a problem. Faint bands move in the wall crystal, moiré-like, too low-contrast to look at directly. You can watch them reorganize an hour before the feeder's flow rises. The room is solving for itself, continuously, and the answer comes out as water arriving at the right moment in the right place.

None of this is built. There is not one artifact in here, not one joint, not one tool mark, not one part. There is nothing to take apart and nothing to steal. It grew, and it is growing, and it is better at every one of these operations than anything we have made, and it has no maker in it anywhere, and after a while in the room you stop finding this remarkable and start finding it obvious, the way you find a river obvious.

---

The emptiness is not leftover. The volume of air is part of the apparatus — the working fluid, the resonator, the space in which the standing patterns in the walls have room to be themselves. Clutter it and it stops. That is why there are three drip points and not thirty, four films and not a hundred, two columns and not a forest. Everything present is a node. Everything absent is capacity.

Life is thin here and it is all simpler than fungus. The four bacterial films, and a scattering of archaea in the feeder's throat where the sulfur is, and that is the entire primary production. No plant has ever been in this room. No spore of anything green. The light is enormous and useless to a leaf; there is no soil, no carbon budget, no reason.

The animals can be counted on fingers. A pale hexapod, two millimeters, grazing the films — perhaps sixty individuals across all four patches, and they move between patches only during the rain. A translucent copepod in the fresh lens of the pool, which stays in the fresh lens and dies if the layers mix. And one predator, blind, slow, pincer-bearing, that lives at the second drip point and does not wander, because it does not need to. It has learned the schedule. It waits under the drip with its arms open and takes what the water shakes loose.

They are not pets and they are not accidents. The grazers keep the films from thickening past a few cells and fouling the optical faces; the room's brightness depends on those faces staying clean; the room schedules water so that grazers can exist. Nine or ten animals in a nine-by-six-meter chamber, and every one of them is doing maintenance, and none of them knows it.

---

The day has weather.

Around three in the afternoon the ceiling's underside crosses its dew point across its whole face at once, and for ninety seconds it rains indoors — a single sheet of drops, all of them the same size, falling four meters into a hot white room. The sound of it is the loudest thing that ever happens here. The films drink. The hexapods come out and breed. The predator hunts. Then it stops, absolutely, and the walls take the water back and the schedule resumes.

Otherwise: quiet. Drip. The tick of a cleavage plane advancing somewhere in the bed, a small dry crack like a knuckle. Occasionally a gas bubble letting go from the pool floor with a sound like a swallowed word. Far above, at noon, the pan expanding — a slow crackle conducted down through a hundred meters of crystal, arriving softened, arriving late. And under all of it a tone at the very top of hearing that you feel in your back teeth and cannot decide whether you are hearing at all.

---

The room is enlarging by three millimeters a year. The lid is thinning by rather less. Somewhere in the next few centuries the arithmetic ends: the slab loses its span, the pan comes down, the sky gets in, and the whole apparatus is buried in rubble and brine and becomes ordinary geology in an afternoon.

The lattice has already accounted for this. Two meters off the northeast corner, behind a wall that looks like every other wall, there is a void the size of a cupped pair of hands. It is dry. It is dark. Its edges are so fresh they squeak under a fingernail, and in its wall, faint as breath on glass, the bands have already begun to move.
