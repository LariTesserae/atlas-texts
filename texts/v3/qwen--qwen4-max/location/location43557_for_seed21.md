You climb until the air thins and cools, and then the ground gets hot.

The last hour of the ascent is ordinary mountain: grey schist, wind-scoured, lichens like dried paint, the usual thin cold that makes your fingers stupid. Then you cross a lip of stone and the heat comes up through your boots, and the rock ahead is wet, and the wet rock is steaming, and none of the steam rises the way steam should.

The basin is about three hundred paces across. You can see all of it from where you stand, and from every other point in it you can see all of it too — that is one of the first things you learn about the place. It is a bowl with a floor of black stone, walls of black stone, and a rim that carries a rind of frost all day while the floor sits at the temperature of blood. Fresh stone. Fracture faces still sharp enough to open a palm. No rounded edges anywhere, no soil in the seams, no stain of age. It looks a week old. It is forty-one years old, or two hundred; the marks disagree, and the marks were made by different kinds of mind.

Nothing here glows. That should be said early, because it disappoints people. The sun is the ordinary sun, the shadows are ordinary shadows, sharp as cut paper in the thin air, and the sky above the bowl is a plain and unremarkable blue with a hole in the cloud that never quite closes. Whatever is happening here is happening in matte black wet rock at the bottom of a hole in a mountain, and it is happening quietly, and it does not care that you have come.

---

The floor is covered in water and the water is not moving.

That is wrong, and it takes a while to see why. There is water everywhere — sheets a millimetre thick lying on the sloped stone, beads standing on the flat, seeps weeping out of the walls in long vertical strings, pools in cut sockets, a low fog that stays knee-high and does not swirl. Water is arriving constantly. Water is leaving constantly. And none of it splashes, none of it eddies, none of it ripples outward from where a bead lands. Each droplet touches down and is *absorbed into a lane*, and the lane carries it, and the lane is not visible until you crouch and put your eye level with the film and see that the sheet of water on the rock is braided, dozens of parallel threads sliding past each other without mixing, each one going somewhere in particular.

Drop a pebble in a pool. It goes in without a sound and without a crown of spray. The surface opens for it like a mouth and closes and there is no ring.

Spit into a socket pool. The spit stays a thread. It does not cloud, does not bloom, does not spread. An hour later it is still a thread, coiled now, following a route, being carried.

Diffusion is off here. Not slowed — off, in the water, inside the bowl. The random jitter that makes a drop of ink become a cloud of ink has been withdrawn from the working fluid. Scent does not travel through the wet. Heat does not conduct sideways through it: you can put a finger in a pool at 60° and, an inch away, in water cold enough to ache, and the boundary between them will hold as long as you hold your hand still. This is why a place made of running water is nearly silent. Turbulence is waste. The basin has been built to have no waste in it, and so it has almost no voice.

What you hear instead: your own heart, loud, unmasks itself. Your knees. The wet click of your tongue. A bead landing on stone somewhere to the left — *tick* — and then a long nothing, and then another, forty seconds later, from a different quarter. Perhaps six sounds a minute that are not you. At dusk the frost on the rim cracks and it is the loudest thing that happens all day.

Speak and your voice comes out flat and close, like talking in a wardrobe. The bowl is cathedral-sized and has no echo at all. Sound is water's business here, and water has been told to be tidy.

---

The green is in patches, and the patches are far apart, and each patch is impossibly lush.

Bare wet black stone for thirty paces; then a seam where a mat of something grows waist-deep, glossy, turgid, hot to the touch, leaves like the tongues of large animals. Then bare stone again. Then another mat, identical. Then another. Twelve or fifteen of them, spaced around the bowl at intervals you could measure with a knotted cord, and if you dig — you shouldn't — you find they are one plant, a single clone, running along the fracture lines under the floor, sending up its thickets exactly where the lanes converge.

The leaves shed water in beads of a size you never see elsewhere: uniform, fat, perfect, rolling off along channels in the cuticle that are not the channels a leaf normally has. Hold a leaf up to the sun and the venation resolves into a figure — a small closed glyph, three strokes and a hook. Every leaf. Every patch. The same figure is stamped in the metal of the footing rings by the north wall, and it is also, faintly, the cross-section of the thing growing at the centre of the basin.

The plants are components. They pump; they lift water from the seams to the height where the intake wants it; they perform, with root pressure and transpiration, a sorting job that would otherwise need machinery. They are not owned in any way you could litigate. They have simply been growing into a job for a long time, and they are very good at it, and they are the reason the basin smells — when it smells at all, in the dry air above the fog — like cut cucumber and hot iron.

---

The day has a shape.

**Dawn** is intake. Fog organises into columns. Not wisps: columns, eight or nine of them, wrist-thick to waist-thick, standing perfectly vertical from the seeps to a height of about four metres, where they stop as if cut. They cast ordinary shadows. They do not waver in wind, because there is no wind inside the bowl; the rim sheds it. Put your hand through a column and it parts and reseals and your hand comes out dry.

**Midday** is the hot flat time. The floor steams in sheets, the columns thin, the plant mats click as they shed, and the whole place has the feel of an oven left open. This is when you can walk anywhere safely.

**Late afternoon** is sorting. The lanes speed up. Beads on the flat stone begin to travel — uphill, some of them, in single file, at the pace of a slow ant. The pools develop a grain, like muscle. If you sit still you can feel the air's moisture being *claimed*: your sweat stops falling and stands on your forearms in ranked beads, and then walks, and then leaves you, and you are suddenly, badly thirsty.

**Dusk** is deposit. The columns lean inward — the only time they lean — and something comes down through them onto the centre plinth, too fine to see, arriving with a sound like a held breath. It lasts perhaps ninety seconds. Then the rim frost forms with its crack, and the basin goes cold at the edges and stays hot at the heart, and the gradient the whole engine runs on is re-established for the night.

And sometimes, at any hour, real weather arrives. A cloud comes over the rim and drops unscheduled rain.

Then the basin *rejects*. Everything reverses for about a minute. The films retract uphill into the seeps. The beads climb the plant stems. The pools shrink from the edges inward. The columns collapse into the walls. The rain that fell is gathered, sorted for what can be used, and the rest is walked out across the sill in a sheet and dumped over the lip, and the mountain outside — which is ordinary, which is dull, which has no opinion — gets wet. Then the lanes resume. If you are standing in a lane when this happens you will feel your trouser legs try to go somewhere.

---

Animals: three or four kinds, and you will be lucky to see any.

A pale amphibian in the socket pools, blind, with a fringe of feathery gills that stir constantly, and whose stirring is the only randomness permitted in that water. A moth with a body like a scrap of ash that flies a jittering, drunken, deliberately erratic route between the plant mats, all day, never landing to feed. Spiders on the rim, dry-side, ordinary, taking what the wind brings. One swift that nests in an intake port and is tolerated.

The moth is not drunk. The moth is doing the jitter. Whatever stochasticity the process still requires — the shaking, the mixing, the sampling of possibilities — has been outsourced to bodies, because bodies are cheap and self-repairing and can be recruited by leaving a suitable niche open. The amphibian's gills mix. The moth's flight samples. They are not pets and not pests. They are the last employees, and there are so few of them because so few are needed.

The dead do not rot here; rot needs diffusion. A moth that dies stays intact on the stone for weeks, perfect, dry, filed. Then one dusk it comes apart bead by bead and goes up a column, and the next morning there is nothing where it was, and the plinth at the centre is very slightly heavier.

---

The built things are few and none of them are a building.

Six footing rings in the north floor, machined, flush, filled with water; whatever stood on them was taken away. A stair of eleven steps cut into the west wall, going up and ending at blank rock. A ceramic cup, unbroken, in a seam. A bench.

The bench is stone, waist-high, oriented to the centre of the basin, and its seat is worn into two shallow dishes by a body that sat there for a very long time. Beside it, a pair of boots, laces still tied, upright, as if stepped out of. Nothing else. No bones — bones would have gone up a column.

There is a tally stone next to the bench: rows of small hand-cut marks in groups of five, thousands of them, patient, and the last group has three marks in it. Beside the tally, machined into the floor, an indicator of some kind, still functioning, showing a quantity in units you will not recognise. It changes about once a season.

Whoever kept the tally stopped. The indicator did not.

---

At the centre, on a plinth of the same fresh black stone, is the thing being made.

It is the size of a curled person. Matte, dark, wet-looking though it is dry to the eye. Its geometry is difficult: faces that appear to be the same face from two directions, an edge you can follow with your finger that returns to its start having turned you slightly. Look at it too long and your eyes water, and the water is taken.

It is growing at roughly a hair's breadth a season. It cannot be made any other way. Its tolerances are below the reach of hands, below the reach of any tool that touches, and so it is being *precipitated* — one sorted layer at a time, out of fog, by a hole in a mountain that has been arranged to be a very slow printer with the sky for a print head. The heat is the waste. The wet is the medium. The mountain's cold thin air is the condenser. The plants are the pumps. The moth is the shaker. The rain is an interruption. The quiet is a specification.

It is perhaps a twelfth finished. Nobody is coming to check.

---

If you go: walk in the dry ring at the sill, where a pebble dropped will bounce and roll and make a noise. Drink only from the three unmarked sockets by the south wall — that water is unassigned, a ration left running by a protocol nobody has cancelled, and it is cold and tastes of nothing at all. Do not stand still in a column at dawn. Do not stay past a season.

The danger is not heat, or drowning, or anything with teeth. The danger is regularity. Stay long enough and the field begins to use you as it uses the plant mats — your capillaries, your breath, your beat — and it will make you *exact*. Your heart will settle into a rhythm with no variance in it whatsoever, and you will feel wonderful, tidy, alphabetised, and then you will die, because a living thing is a jitter that persists, and there is no jitter left in the water here to borrow.

At dusk, from the bench, you can watch the columns lean. The deposit comes down with its held-breath sound. The frost cracks on the rim, once, loud as a shot in a room with no echo. Far below, on the ordinary mountain, the unscheduled rain is running off downhill in the usual careless way, spreading, mixing, forgetting, going nowhere in particular.
