## The Slath

Begin with the floor, because there is nothing else to begin with. It gives underfoot — a mat of laid withes eight hundred generations deep, springy, warm as a sleeping animal, damp in the way a tongue is damp. Press your palm to it and the warmth comes up through the wet. Somewhere below, something is rotting at exactly the rate at which something above is growing, and has been for a very long time.

The light is the color of a leaf held over a candle. It arrives from a long way off and from no particular direction, having passed through so much living fiber on its way here that it has lost the habit of casting shadows. Things are visible the way things are visible in a closed mouth.

It is quiet. Not empty-quiet. Occupied-quiet, the hush of a full room where everyone has agreed.

Water beads on the underside of every surface. It gathers, swells, hangs, and lets go into moss that has been placed there — *placed*, deliberately, packed and stitched into the drip-points — so that the falling makes no sound at all.

---

Look closer at the floor and the made-ness surfaces. Nothing here is unworked. The withes were laid in courses, over-and-under, and the courses were beaten tight with something heavy, and the beating left a rhythm you can read with a fingertip: three close, one slack, three close, one slack, for as far as the mat runs, which is farther than walking will settle. At the seam where two mats meet, the stitching is a herringbone in split bast, and the knots are on the underside, and the hands that tied them tied them left-handed.

Above, the ceiling is a cable. One cable. It is thicker than a house and hollow, and it is alive, and its wall sweats sap on the inside so that the interior is a warm wet corridor going off into the dark in both directions, passable, used, worn smooth along the lower curve by traffic. Ribs come down from it — trunks steamed and bent long ago and holding the bend now out of habit rather than force — and between the ribs, finer work: plaiting, twining, a randing of green withies laid by someone who was thinking about something else.

Everything is made. There is no stone here that was not pressed from seed and pulp. There is no soil that was not composted in place. There is no ground.

---

The room you are standing in is a gap.

This is the first thing that takes a while to accept. The world is not solid and it is not full. It is a weave, and a weave is mostly hole. Between one cable and the next there may be a day of empty air; between one rib and the next, a gulf you could drop a season into. The material is dense, lush, total — every surface you can touch is plant, every plant is worked — but the *volume* is nearly nothing. Step back far enough and the whole enormous green apparatus thins out into a lattice, a scrim, a suggestion of structure with vast luminous nothings between its members, like the skeleton of a leaf held up to that distant filtered light.

You can stand in a hollow the size of a valley and see, across it, one other living thing. A single moth on a single strand, wings like wet paper, opening and closing.

That is the density of company here. Abundant life, thinly spread through an enormous emptiness, each creature alone in its own cathedral-sized gap, and all of them quiet, and all of them aware of each other in the way you are aware of a hand on your shoulder in the dark.

---

Because sound does not travel through the air here. It goes into the fiber.

Clap, and the air swallows it — the mats drink it, the moss drinks it, the hollow cables carry it away and disperse it into a hundred leagues of bast. But lay a hand flat on a stake and *strike* it, and the blow runs along the warp for a distance measured in days, arriving elsewhere as a touch. A pressure. A knock in someone's palm.

Everything that lives here has learned this. The browsers move on pads that spread their weight across four strands at once. The moths do not beat the air; they row it. And out in the hollows, at intervals of a hundred leagues or so, something long-limbed stands motionless with both palms flat against a cable, and has stood that way since before you were born, reading the traffic of the world through its hands.

They are not rare. They are simply far apart. When one of them moves, the whole neighborhood knows three days before it arrives, and the neighborhood is already elsewhere.

Hence the hush. Not absence. Suppression. A world of abundant life all speaking in a whisper because the walls have nerves.

---

Now the scale, which arrives late, the way weather does.

The wall curves. You take it for a wall for a long time — a green cliff of laid work, courses stacked, stakes rising — and then one shift of light shows you that it is curving *outward*, that it has been curving outward the whole time, that what you called the sky is only the far side of the same vessel, sixty days of climbing away and rising, and the haze you called horizon is the interior fog of a bowl so large it makes its own climate.

It is a basket.

At the bottom, wherever the bottom is, there is a slath: the origin-cross where the first stakes were laid across each other and the whole thing began. Nobody has seen it. Above that, in the deep past, came the Upset — the moment the stakes were bent from lying flat to standing up, and the base became a wall, and a floor became a world. The bend is still there. You can find it if you go down far enough: a curve in the great ribs where every one of them turns, together, in a single coordinated arc, and the tool marks change direction, and the courses above it are laid by a different tradition than the courses below.

And on the wall, at a height no expedition has reached, there is a stain.

A tide-mark. A line of mineral crust and bleached fiber running level around the entire interior, above which the weave is dry and pale and below which everything is dark and wet and remembers. The animals below the line still swim. Not metaphorically — there are eels in the air here, long ribboning things that move in the old currents, that turn in unison at intervals corresponding to a tide that stopped, that spawn in the cup-leaves on the eleventh day of a cycle nobody keeps anymore.

The vessel was made to hold water. It held water. The water is gone.

---

What is left is the wetness of a thing that has recently been emptied and has not dried.

Fog forms in the hollows on a schedule. The rot-heat below drives warm moist air up the hollow cables; it cools against the high weft; it condenses on every up-facing surface in beads that gather into threads that run down the stakes and pool in the leaf-cups, and the leaf-cups overflow into the seams, and the seams weep, and the weeping is caught in moss and wicking-cords and led, patiently, by ten thousand small hand-laid channels, back toward the middle.

It rains inside. It has never once rained on the outside, which nobody has seen.

And at the center-bottom, the weave spirals inward toward an opening that was never closed. The courses tighten, the pattern grows finer and more urgent, the work becomes exquisite — and then stops, mid-stroke, at a lip. Below the lip there is no material.

Everything that falls goes there. Nothing arrives. The water went that way, all of it, over an age, and the fog comes back up out of the walls at exactly the rate the drain takes it down, and the level of wetness in the world has not changed in eleven thousand years.

The whole structure has sagged into the precise catenary that balances the loss. That is what the settled shape *is*: not a design, but an equilibrium, the sag of a basket that has found the exact amount of giving-up required to keep holding.

---

It is warm because it is composting, and it will never be anything else, because fire is the one thing this world cannot survive.

No forge, then. No smelt, no cast, no wire. The tools are thorn awls hardened in sap, adzes of pressed seed-stone that take an edge and lose it in a day, beaters of lignified root, bodkins of shed tooth. Cordage is rolled on the thigh, spit-and-fiber, three-strand, and the rolling is the oldest continuous motion in the world; you can hear it if you put your ear to a stake, a faint dry friction, everywhere, always, the sound of the world being made slightly faster than it comes apart.

The makers are small and six-limbed and have pads that spin bast from glands under the wrist. They are called menders by anyone who bothers to name them. They work in the dim, without hurry, repairing what the browsers unpick.

The browsers are pale, soft, prolific, with teeth that never stop growing, and what they eat is cordage. Every one of them is a small hole in the world. Every one of them is hunted, in absolute silence, by the palm-readers.

And in the caulk of the oldest seams, packed in with the moss and resin and felted down, there are thumbprints. Some are the size of a fingernail. Some are the size of a pond. The whorls match.

---

Go far enough out along a course and the work gets thin, then speculative, then absent — but the *pattern* continues.

You can see it: the over-and-under, running on into the dark with nothing carrying it. The instruction without the material. Put your hand into it and your hand comes back smelling of somewhere you have not been, and slightly wet, and for a while afterward your fingers know a knot nobody taught them.

These are the slack places. Distances there are provisional. A dropped awl falls a long time and is found, later, in a place it has not yet been dropped. Two menders working the same stretch from opposite ends will meet, pass, and never have met. The hollows are bigger inside than the weave outside can account for, and everyone knows this, and nobody finds it remarkable, because a basket is *supposed* to be bigger inside. That is what a basket is for.

The rim is one day's climb away. It has been one day's climb away for every generation that has attempted it.

---

So: a made world, warm and dim and weeping, woven by small hands out of itself, holding nothing, leaking inward, sagging into balance, vast and mostly hollow and full of quiet life. Below the last laid course the pattern runs on into the dark, perfectly clear, perfectly empty, waiting.

Somewhere a mender rolls cord on her thigh. Somewhere a browser's tooth parts a withe and a strand goes slack and, forty leagues off, a palm feels it. Somewhere a bead of water gathers on the underside of a cable and swells and hangs and lets go into moss placed there for exactly that purpose, by someone, a long time ago, so that it would not make a sound.
