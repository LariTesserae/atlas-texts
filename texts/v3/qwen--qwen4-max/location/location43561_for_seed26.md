## The Sweat

Put your palm on the wall and it comes away wet. Not slimed — clean, mineral, tasting faintly of zinc and recipe. The wall is cold enough that the moisture in the air arrives on it out of nowhere, beads, gathers, and runs, and it has been doing this since the wall was poured, which was eleven years ago, which here counts as recent.

The wall is made. This is the first thing to understand and the last thing to stop mattering. It is cast basalt, sintered slag aggregate bound in a gray matrix cooked in place by resistance lances, and it was poured in lifts of two meters, and every lift left a seam, and every seam left a lip, and every lip still has its edge. Run your hand up and you can count the year in shoulders. The shuttering plates left their bolt-holes in rows like a musical stave. Nothing has softened. Water has been running down this face for a decade and has not yet managed to round a single corner; it has only cut straight down, following the plumb line the builders gave it, obedient as a trainee.

Below the last lift, where the wall meets the floor, the aggregate has begun to shed. Chips lie in a fan, all of them angular, all of them fresh-fractured, glittering dully under the work-lamps like a spill of black sugar. There is no gravel here. Gravel is what happens to a place after a very long time, and this place has not been given that time. It has been given eleven years and a budget.

## The Basin

Step back from the wall and the wall does not end; it recedes into a haze of its own making and becomes a gray suggestion three kilometers off, and you understand that you are standing on the floor of a room the size of a valley.

They call it a heat sink on the drawings. The crews call it the Sweat. Above — far above, past the ceiling you cannot see, past the deck of that ceiling and the insulation raft and the load-bearing fill — is the made world, the cities and the works and the endless fabricated crust, and all of it runs hot, and all of that heat has to go somewhere. It comes down here in the returns: great insulated arteries that break out of the ceiling at the north end and spill their heat into the air in a controlled catastrophe. And it leaves here as cold, packed and metered and pumped back up the Blue Trunk in pipes so heavily jacketed that they frost white and stand in the dark like the legs of something too large to see.

Between the returns and the trunk lies the working floor: nine kilometers by four, held up by the Colonnade — pillars of poured stone eight meters thick, spaced on a grid so wide that from any one pillar you can barely resolve the next. The spacing is not structural. It is acoustic and thermal. Everything here is far from everything else because closeness costs: heat pools, sound stacks, pressure finds a way. So the pump-houses stand alone in wet open floor like farmsteads on a plain, each with its ring of lamps, each with its cable-run stretching away over the wet stone on little cast legs, and between them there is nothing but sheet-water two millimeters deep, flowing, and the dark.

The lamps are sodium-yellow and rationed. There is a light budget, and it is spent where hands go. Whole districts run on instrument glow only — a scatter of green and amber points at chest height, hung in blackness, marking valves nobody has turned in years. Walk out past the third ring and your own footsteps become the loudest thing you have ever heard, until you get close enough to a pump-house for the fundamental to take you again.

The fundamental is thirty-one hertz. It is the sum of every rotating thing in the basin, phase-locked by the grid, and it does not arrive through the ears. It arrives in the sternum and the sinuses and the water in your eyes. New crews sleep badly for a month and then stop noticing, and then go up top on leave and cannot sleep at all in the silence, which they describe, unanimously, as *thin*.

## Water That Has Never Been Outside

Nothing here has fallen from a sky.

The water comes from the air, which is blended and pushed and holds its moisture on schedule. Warm returns meet cold ceiling; the ceiling sweats; the sweat gathers in the drip-grooves cast into its underside — a lattice of channels, engineered rainfall, drawn on a drawing by someone who has never seen rain — and comes down in strings and veils and, when a district's humidity setpoint is bumped, in genuine downpours that last forty minutes and stop like a shift whistle.

The cold mains sweat too. Every one of them wears a permanent collar of condensate that slides down its jacket and drips from the low point, so that beneath the Blue Trunk there is a line of puddles a hundred meters long, each one exactly under a joint, each one holding a small permanent crater of frost at its center where the drip lands and freezes and is worn away and freezes again.

The floor is pitched at one in four hundred. All that water walks, in a film, across the whole basin toward the Shallows at the south end — a sheet of standing water a kilometer wide and never more than knee-deep, its bottom a soft gray flour of aggregate fines that have not yet had time to become silt, let alone mud, let alone soil. At its margins the flour banks up into low deltas, saturated, quaking underfoot, ringing faintly with each step as the water in it tries and fails to escape. This is the marsh of a world with no nature in it. It smells of wet cement and cold iron and, in the warm seams, of something almost like bread.

And here is the thing the survey crews argue about: the channels are straight. Every drain, every runnel, every scour-line follows the saw or the pitch or the grid. Water in a young made place does what it is told. But last season, in the long runnel east of Cascade Stair, the flow began to swing. A little. Half a meter off the cut line, then back, then further. The bank on the outside of the swing is undercutting. The inside is building a bar of flour. It is the beginning of a meander — the first curve in this place that nobody drew — and it has been logged, twice, as a defect.

## What Grows

Sparsely, and always for a reason.

The insulation gardens up in the raft were seeded with a cold-tolerant moss as a moisture indicator, a living gauge, and the spores came down on the drip and found the joints. Now there is moss in the expansion seams of the Colonnade, a dark green almost black, in strips a hand wide and thirty meters long, following the joints exactly, so that from a distance the pillars look ruled with faint lines of writing. Where a lamp leaks light onto a wet wall, a paler cultivar has taken hold in ragged discs. Where a cold main sweats onto flour, frost-fern grows — not a fern, a mineral-and-algae consortium that lays down rime in feathered blades and dies back every time the humidity setpoint drops, leaving a ghost of white dust that the next flush takes away.

Nothing covers anything. Between one patch and the next there are hundreds of meters of bare wet made-stone, gleaming, empty. The roots — such as they are — do not go down, because down is more manufacture. They go *along*, into joints, into cable troughs, into the deliberate gaps the builders left for movement. The whole ecology lives in the tolerances.

## What Lives

Almost nothing, and that nothing is specialized.

In the warm seams — the trenches where the returns run shallow and the floor holds at nine degrees instead of two — there are pale amphibious things the crews call sump-calves, hand-length, slow, with skin like wet paper and eyes that are only pigment. One per hundred meters of seam, if the seam is old. They eat the biofilm that sloughs off the warm walls, and they breed in the flush, and when the fan gates reverse and the pressure drops they retreat into the joints and wait, sometimes for days, motionless, breathing through their backs.

And there is a flier. Perhaps a few hundred in the whole basin. It hunts by the fundamental: it has an organ along its abdomen that reads the standing wave, and it flies the interference pattern the way a bat flies a hedgerow, along the quiet lines between the pillars where the thirty-one hertz cancels itself out and a cricket in a cable trough can be heard. It cannot hunt in a hush pocket that has been *made* — where the acoustic dampers have been installed, the moth goes deaf and stupid and starves. There are maps of the basin's nodes and antinodes, updated after every plant modification, and the moth's population has followed those maps around for a decade like a village following a road.

The crews see one or two a shift, sometimes none. They are considered good luck and are never written into the reports.

## The Rules

Sound is property. A hush pocket — a node, a damped bay, a place where two people can speak — is surveyed, leased, and defended. Everyone else signs, or writes on a slate, or presses a throat mic and speaks into a bone.

Cold is graded and sold. Ambient here is the waste end of the process, the cold nobody wanted, and it sits at two degrees and never stops moving. It is not the cold of winter; there is no winter. It is the cold of a product that has been left out.

The flush comes at shift change and scours the floor with reclaimed condensate at pressure, and for eleven minutes the whole basin is a roaring gray sheet and everything alive is in a joint. The fan gates reverse on a forty-hour cycle, and the pressure drops, and your ears pop, and the drips fall sideways for a while, and the frost-fern dies, and the moss waits.

And the cutting heads are still working the far east wall, advancing forty meters a month, making the room bigger, making more sharpness, more fresh fracture, more chips like black sugar. The place is getting larger and younger at the same time. Behind the heads, the new floor is already wet.

Down at the Shallows, where the runnel has begun to swing, the undercut bank lets go of a slab and it goes into the water with a flat slap that the fundamental swallows whole. The bar of flour grows another centimeter. The curve deepens. Somewhere a form is waiting to be filled out, and no one has decided yet whether what is happening there is damage.
