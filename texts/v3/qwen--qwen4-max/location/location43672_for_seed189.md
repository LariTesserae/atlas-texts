## The Held Breath

Nine meters down, under a hillside that appears to be forty separate trees and is one organism, there is a crack in the root-body. It is a hand's width at the mouth and a shoulder's height, and both lips are warm. You go in feet-first, exhale to fit, and the room takes the rest of your breath and keeps it.

Then the space opens, and keeps opening, and does not stop opening the way a cavity should.

The harvesters call it the Held Breath. They have no word for its size because the size is not reliable. From the inside it is a hall you could put a village in — a smooth-walled void, ellipsoid, its long axis tilted maybe fifteen degrees from level, its far end lost not in darkness but in a green-gold haze of distance. From the outside, walking the hill above with a cord and stakes, you can measure the whole organism and find no room in it anywhere. Both measurements are correct. The people who go down have stopped trying to reconcile them; they treat it as they treat weather.

**Light.** There is no opening. There is no sky. And yet the interior is lit at the strength of a bright overcast noon — you can read the grain of your palm, you can see the color of your own blood under a fingernail. The walls are translucent: forty meters of packed leaf and stem and root lie between here and the sun, and the light comes through them filtered but not reduced. That is the first wrong thing, and it is so gentle that most people never notice it. Green-gold, shadowless in the way of a cloudy day, everywhere at once. When you cast a shadow — and you do, sometimes, when you stand near a vein — it falls away from the crack you entered by, wherever in the room you happen to be standing. As if the entrance were the sun. As if you had come in through the noon.

**The walls.** Nothing here is dead and nothing here is finished. The outer structure is pale wood the color of the inside of a cut stem, wet-white going green at the edges, and where the cavity tore open it splintered rather than broke: shards the length of oars stand out from the upper wall, sharp as fresh-cut cane, still glossy at the fracture faces. There is no dust anywhere in the room. Dust requires time. The tear is eleven years old, or last season, depending on who you ask and what they mean by *old*.

Inside that woody cage the wall is layered like the inside of a cheek. A stratum of packed leaf, whole and living, pressed into felt. Beneath that, a lining of clear membrane, wet, faintly ridged, through which you can watch the sap move — weak-tea colored, flecked with green, running in visible pulses in veins as thick as a thigh. And set into the membrane, spaced a hand apart, the stomata: mouths, each the size of a mouth, each opening and closing on its own slow schedule. Ten thousand of them. When they open together the room inhales. When they close, it doesn't.

The floor is not ground. It is a woven membrane of root, taut, warm, faintly translucent, and it gives an inch under a boot and returns. Walk across it and you play it. Stand still and you can feel it playing something else.

**Emptiness.** For all that the plant is everything — wall, floor, ceiling, air-filter, weather — the volume itself is nearly void. This is the strangeness that visitors report before they report the light: after the squeeze, after all that living mass, an emptiness so complete it feels curated. One aerial root descends from the high curve of the ceiling, thick as a bell rope, braided, pale, and ends a meter and a half above the floor. It has been ending a meter and a half above the floor for as long as anyone has kept records. It grows a finger's width a day. So does the distance.

One fruit on the far wall, the size of a hay bale, fig-shaped, going from white to amber over a season.

One puddle, in a shallow dimple of the floor near the low end, the size of a hat brim, warm, never evaporating, never overflowing.

One moth.

**Water.** The room is damp and not wet. Beads form on the undersides of everything — under the leaf-strata, under the bell rope, under the fruit — and hang there, growing, refusing to fall. Condensation with nowhere to go. Then, at the top of the exhale, when the whole chamber flexes and the floor drums and the pressure changes in your ears, perhaps a tenth of the ceiling lets go at once and it rains: a brief warm scatter across the low end, thirty seconds of it, fat drops at blood heat that taste faintly of the last thing you ate. Then dry-warm again, and the beading starts over. There are places along the upper wall that have never been rained on. There are places at the low end where the membrane has gone soft and dark and grows a fringe of hairlike rootlets that wave in the breath.

**Sound.** It is loud. Not loud like a river — loud like being inside an instrument that is being played slowly by something with no interest in you. Under everything, the breath: a fourteen-second cycle, a long drawing-in through ten thousand mouths, a held pause of four or five seconds, and then the release, which is a subsonic shove you feel in the sternum before you hear it. Over that, the growth: a constant crackle and tick, cell walls splitting, fibers tightening, a sound like oil frying in another room, like applause heard through a wall, never stopping, never quieting. The bell rope sways and knocks the floor and the floor booms. The rain arrives with a hiss and a hundred separate slaps. And the room holds sound the way silt-water holds silt: you will hear a drip that has not happened yet, and then, a breath later, see it fall. You will hear your own voice come back off the far wall with an extra syllable in it. The harvesters do not talk down here. They use their hands, and they touch each other's wrists a great deal, more than people do aboveground.

**The tenant.** Wingspan of two spread hands, pale as the inside of a petal, with a faint green flush along the veins. It has no mouth. It does not eat. It flies the long axis of the room in a slow, unhurried, repeating figure, and once every few days it lands on the fruit and stays there for an hour, and then goes back to its figure. There is exactly one. There has always been exactly one. When it dies — curled on the floor membrane, taking three days to do it — the walls absorb it without a mark, and the fruit opens a seam, and there is one again.

**The people.** They come for the rind of the fruit, which is worth the descent, and for the lining-membrane, which stops bleeding and closes a wound in a day. They build nothing. There is nothing to build with that is not already the room, and anything set down and left is grown over within a season: a bark bucket left in the low end will be a green swelling by the next harvest, still bucket-shaped, still holding water, no longer a bucket. So they carry, and they cut, and they leave.

Their tools are flint scrapers and a cord braided from the organism's own fiber and, once in a generation, copper. The copper is a problem. The room accepts what is made of itself — a fiber cord tied to the bell rope will be adopted, incorporated, made continuous with the rope within a month — but mineral is refused. Around a copper blade the sap blisters, the membrane swells white and hard, and the crack at the top narrows by a finger's width for a day and a night. There is a blade up there now, wedged in a wound on the north curve, gone the green of old pond water, half-swallowed, with a shoot growing out of the handle. Nobody has been able to reach it for six years. The harvesters count its slow disappearance the way you count a child's height.

What they know, and say plainly, as facts, in the same tone they use for tide tables:

Cuts on the wall close in minutes. Cuts on your hand take weeks. Nothing here rots. The leaf that has been falling from the high curve since the tear opened is still falling and still alive. If you count heads at the squeeze going down you will count correctly, and coming up you will count correctly, and the two numbers will differ, and the difference will be someone who was with you the whole time and whom you cannot afterward describe. And the shadows on the walls, when the veins throw them, are shadows of leaves — but not of the leaves above. The harvesters who know the hill know this: the lobing is wrong, the arrangement is wrong, it is a canopy that has not grown yet.

The woman who left the copper spent four seasons mapping the veins. They do not run like wood. They run in a spiral, in five ranks, converging on the low end where the puddle is, and the wall's inner faces curve like the insides of petals, and the stomata are arranged the way nectaries are arranged.

It is not a cavity. It is not a lung. It is the inside of a bud, and the bud is enormous, and it is below the ground, and it has been about to open for eleven years or for a century. The warmth is the heat of forcing growth. The damp is the sweat of it. The emptiness is not absence; it is capacity, kept clear, room being made for something that has not arrived. The moth is not a pollinator. It is anticipation, with wings. And the light — the noon that comes through forty meters of leaf without diminishing, that has never once faded, that makes your shadow fall away from the crack —

the light is the noon of the day it opens, arriving early, the way the drip arrives early, the way your voice comes back with a syllable you have not said.

They take the rind. They watch the fruit. They climb out before the exhale finishes, because the crack is widest then and because none of them wants to be inside for the pause — that four-second hold, ten thousand mouths shut, the crackle gone silent, the moth stopped mid-figure in the green-gold air, everything waiting,

and the room, all around them, warm and wet and sharp-edged and new, taking the next breath.
