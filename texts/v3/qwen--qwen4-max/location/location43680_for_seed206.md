## The Vessel

From outside there is nothing to find. A coastal flat, thirty centimetres above the mean tide, warm rain most afternoons, and on it a stand of trees that are not trees — one organism in many columns, grey-green, fluted, rising forty metres into a canopy that closes like a hand. Ten thousand hectares of the same individual, or of copies so nearly identical that the difference has never mattered. Nothing marks the place. No path, no stone, no blaze on a trunk. The ground is not soil; it is a mat of living root, spongy, warm underfoot, and it hums at a frequency you feel in the shins before you hear it.

The entrance is a stoma on the underside of a root arch, at the mud-line: a lipped slit a metre and a half long, held permanently open by two swollen guard cells gone rigid with age. It should have closed. It stopped closing so long ago that the closing muscles have been reabsorbed and the aperture relined with smooth tissue, worn round at the edges like a doorstep. You duck. The lip is dry — the only dry surface for a hundred metres in any direction — and it is warm, and it gives slightly, like the pad of a thumb.

Then you are in the room.

---

It is roughly nine metres by five, four high at the crown, an ovoid flattened at the base. The first impression is of a peeled thing: the walls are pale, wet, faintly translucent, the colour of the inside of a melon rind, or a celery heart, or the flesh of an apple held up to a window. Light comes *through* them. Thirty centimetres of leaf and water and pigment stand between this room and the afternoon, and what arrives is even, shadowless, greenish-white, about the brightness of an overcast noon. There is no source. You cannot point at where the light is coming from. Your own shadow is a soft grey contact-patch under your feet and nothing else.

Five ribs cross the ceiling and run down the walls, spaced widely — a metre and a half between each, with long empty spans of smooth tissue in between. They are dark, lignified, polished, and along their inner faces they are plated with a thin skin of iron: black-grey, faintly iridescent, harder than anything else here, cool to the eye and not to the hand. Nothing in this room is cool to the hand. Everything is 33.4 degrees, which is close enough to the surface of your skin that you cannot feel the difference. You put your palm on the wall and cannot tell whether you are touching it. You know you are wet only by the drag when you slide.

The floor is a tray of water twenty-one millimetres deep, lying on living wood. Not mud. Not stone. Wood, pale as the walls, worn into shallow scallops and basins and long fluted runnels — the unmistakable geometry of a surface that has had water go over it in the same direction for a very long time. It is spotless. There is no litter. In a room this alive there should be ankle-deep wrack, husks, wings, corpses, fallen tissue, and there is none, not a fragment, not a film of dust on the water. Everything that falls is eaten within eleven minutes.

---

The noise arrives after the light, because the ear takes longer to give up.

It is loud. Ninety-one decibels, broadband, with a fundamental at forty-seven hertz that you feel in the sternum and the back teeth, and a thin hard whistle riding on top around two kilohertz. At the far end of the room a jet of water comes out of a convergent nozzle the size of a fist — four litres a second, at pressure — and strikes a fan of stiff vanes grown on a shaft of iron-plated fibre. The fan turns at two thousand nine hundred revolutions a minute. It has been turning at two thousand nine hundred revolutions a minute for longer than there have been people to be surprised by it.

The shaft drives a press. The press is a pair of ridged plates that come together and part, come together and part, squeezing a clear caustic sap out of a mass of macerated green, and the sap runs down a chute into a bath, and out of the bath, through a black plate perforated with four thousand holes too small to see, comes a rope.

It is translucent when it emerges, the thickness of a wrist, and it hardens as it rises. It goes up through a gland in the ceiling — a fairlead, a lipped throat, wet and puckered — and out of the room, and up, and up, into the canopy, where it is used to build more of the organism that built it. Cellulose tow. Wet-spun. Some tonnes of it a day, from this room, and from forty thousand rooms exactly like this one across the flat, all of them roaring, all of them warm, all of them twenty-one millimetres deep.

This is not a small place. It is a small room in a large works. The works has no exterior you can stand in, because you are always inside one of its rooms, and the rooms are the same room.

The air tastes of sulphur and sweet solvent and hot metal and green sap. It makes the lips tingle. There is a current in the water film — the organism runs a potential across the chamber, sulphide-rich groundwater on one side, oxygenated air on the other, and it plates the iron out of the water onto its own bearings, a few microns a year, forever. If you stand with one foot in the runnel and one hand on a rib you taste pennies and your fillings ache. It is not enough to hurt. It is enough to know.

---

The room is full of animals and reads as empty.

The air column holds perhaps eleven thousand midges, drifting, each one separate, spaced like dust in a sunbeam — you can see the gaps between them, which is how you know how many there are. In the water film, amphipods in their thousands, silver, flicking. On the walls: four frogs, translucent-throated, one at each of four positions, a metre and a half apart, never closer. On the ribs: three spiders, hand-sized, at three other positions, their webs sized precisely to the local airflow and rebuilt, when rebuilt, identically. In the drain throat at the low corner, one blind fish, always one, holding station in the outflow with its head into the current.

Four frogs. Three spiders. One fish. They are not individuals; they are offices. When a frog dies a vacancy exists for about six hours and then a frog is in it, at the same spot, facing the same way. The positions are properties of the standing wave. The room's roar reflects off its own curved walls and sets up a field of nodes and antinodes, and life here has arranged itself along that field the way iron filings arrange along a magnet. The spacing is not loneliness. It is the shape of the sound.

And the sound has a hole in it.

Between roughly sixteen hundred and twenty-four hundred hertz, the machine's noise falls away — an accident of the nozzle's profile and the chamber's resonance, a notch, a clean rectangular gap in an otherwise solid wall of roar. Everything that lives here speaks inside that gap. The frogs' sacs pulse at 1.9 kilohertz. The spiders stridulate at 2.1. The midges' wings beat in it. There is a whole conversation going on, continuous, dense, urgent, occupying a bandwidth seven hundred hertz wide, and it is completely inaudible to you, because your ear is busy being crushed by the other eighty-nine decibels.

You can hear it if you crawl to the low corner near the drain and put your head into a space about the size of a bucket, where two reflections cancel. The roar drops to a hush. And then the room is full of voices, thin and silver and various, calling and answering, and you lift your head six centimetres and they are gone.

Nothing here can hear you. Their hearing is tuned to the notch and has been for a very long time; outside it they are deaf. You can shout until your throat tears and no frog turns, no spider lifts a leg. You are, acoustically, not an event.

---

Here is what the room holds constant:

Temperature, 33.4 degrees, plus or minus five hundredths. Water depth, twenty-one millimetres; the overflow lip takes what the jet brings, exactly. Flow, 4.1 litres a second. Shaft speed, 2,900. Illuminance, held to within three per cent by a layer of pigment cells in the outer wall that open and close as the sun moves — so there is no morning in here, and no evening, and no way to guess the hour. Humidity, ninety-eight per cent; the ceiling weeps at a rate that matches the evaporation from the press, drop for drop. Iron deposition, matched to abrasion. Wall relining, matched to wear. The scallops in the floor are forty thousand years old and also brand new; the shape was solved long ago and has been maintained at the solution ever since, material passing through it like water through a standing wave.

Births equal deaths. Immigration equals emigration. Nothing accumulates: no sediment, no wrack, no fat, no history. The room has no seasons because the flat has no seasons worth the name, and no year-to-year variation because the organism buffers what little there is, and no memory because memory is a kind of accumulation and this place does not do that.

The ribs are spaced at exact intervals. The nozzle's convergent profile is a shape you have seen in a textbook. The plate has four thousand holes, not three thousand nine hundred and something. The notch is too clean. It would be easy to decide that someone made this, once, and that the making has been entirely eaten — every bolt, every plate, every mark digested and relined and grown over until nothing is left but the specification, running. It would be equally easy to decide that this is simply what optimization looks like when it has had forty thousand years and no interruptions. The room offers no evidence either way. It offers no evidence of anything. It is not that kind of place.

---

You are the only variable that has entered it in a long time.

You bring in seventy watts of heat, a litre of water an hour, a plume of carbon dioxide, a smell, a shadow, a disturbance in the air column that displaces perhaps four hundred midges. The governor takes the heat and adjusts the weep. The pigment layer takes the shadow and opens a fraction. The film takes your footprints and closes them in ninety seconds. The fish holds station. The frogs go on calling in their narrow silver band, at 1.9 kilohertz, about things that matter enormously and are none of them you.

When you duck back out through the lip into the ordinary rain, the flat is grey and enormous and smells only of water. The stand looks like every other stand. Somewhere under your boots the whole plain is turning at two thousand nine hundred revolutions a minute, and the numbers in the room behind you have not changed, and will not, and the ledger has already closed.
