## The Tankhouse

Above, there is nothing to see: a bench of pale limestone under thin soil, bunchgrass in scattered tussocks, junipers spaced far enough apart that each one keeps its own shadow. Sheep would like it. There are no sheep. There are no insects on the flowers of the bunchgrass, and no birds in the junipers, and the reason for that is not visible from up here, but it is the same reason as everything else.

In a fold of the bench there is a sinkhole with a stone lip, and in the sinkhole a pool about four meters across, black-looking because it is deep and because the walls above it lean in. The water does not ripple. Wind crosses the lip without touching it. If you cupped a handful it would look faintly blue, the blue of very weak glass, and it would taste of pennies, and if you held it long enough your fingers would go numb in a way you would not enjoy. Nothing lives in the pool. A juniper needle that falls onto it does not sink for a long time, and when it finally goes down it does not rot.

Eleven meters below the pool the shaft opens sideways into a room.

---

The room is thirty paces long, fourteen wide, and low-arched — a nave, a single span, with a floor so flat it seems intentional and a ceiling you could touch from a standing crouch in the shallower end, except there are no shallows. The chamber is full. Water presses against the ceiling everywhere, without a bubble, without a meniscus, without one pocket of air anywhere in it. There is no surface here. The only surface is the one far above, small as a coin, and it is not the room's ceiling but its window.

Nine point six degrees. Not just at the floor: at the ceiling, at the walls, in the crevices, in the water inside the crevices. The massif holds that temperature the way a cellar holds it, and the room is too small and too slow to argue. There is no thermocline, no cold seam, no shimmer where one body of water meets another. Nothing here is warmer or cooler than anything else. If you brought a body into this room it would be the only heat in it, and the room would take that heat patiently and without comment, and after a while there would be no heat anywhere again.

The water is heavy — a little heavier than it should be, loaded with dissolved metal — and so clear that clarity stops being a quality you notice. No plankton, no motes, no suspended silt, nothing to catch the light. Forty meters of visibility in a room thirty meters long means the far wall is simply *there*, blue-green and sharp, and the copper trees between you and it appear to be hanging in air.

The light is ordinary. That is the surprising part. Sky-light comes down the shaft, strikes a ledge, and arrives secondhand, diffused, so that no direct sunbeam ever enters this place and no caustic net ever crawls across the floor. The illumination is even and adequate, the light of a north-facing workshop on an overcast day; you could read a dial by it, you could sort small parts by it. It brightens and dims once a day, gently, over hours, and a passing cloud is a slow shadow that takes a minute to cross. That is the room's entire repertoire of change.

---

The walls are limestone, sugar-grained, gray going blue in the water, and they have the finish of things dissolved rather than abraded. No scratches. No facets. No talus. Every edge is rounded, every hollow scooped, the whole surface softly pocked and lobed like the inside of a mouth, or like a bone that has been in acid. Where the rock has retreated, harder things stand proud: ribs of quartz, seams of graphite, black and slick, running through the walls and floor and ceiling in flat millimeter-thick bands that meet and branch and meet again.

The graphite is the first thing that makes the eye uneasy. It runs in straight lines for long distances and turns clean corners. It goes from one end of the room to the other and disappears into the rock at both ends as if it continued elsewhere, as if it were connected to something.

At the far end, the second thing.

A blunt wedge of massive sulfide comes out of the wall there — pyrite and chalcopyrite, brass gone tarnished to purple-brown, a body of ore about the size of a delivery van, half-buried, its exposed face pitted and crusted. Ochre stains the rock beneath it in a fan. There is a gray-black mud pooled below the fan, fine as flour, lying perfectly undisturbed, its surface flat as a poured slab. Sulfur blooms in the pits like dried pollen. A skin of white filaments lives on the crust where the water is least hostile — a bacterial felt, thin, patchy, the only living thing on that whole end of the room, and it does not graze and nothing grazes it.

This is the anode. It has been the anode for a very long time.

The upper part of the ore body sits in water carrying a whisper of oxygen, delivered down the shaft from the atmosphere at a rate that has not varied since the shaft opened. The lower part sits in water that has none. The ore conducts. The rock, wet and salted, completes the circuit. Between the two ends of that wedge there is a difference of potential of a little under four tenths of a volt, and it drives a current — a couple of hundred milliamps, spread across a face the size of a wall — and it has been driving it, without interruption, without a switch, without an operator, for something on the order of four hundred thousand years.

Metal goes into solution at the top. Metal comes out of solution at the bottom.

---

The cathodes are the quartz ribs, and what grows on them is copper.

Native copper, plated out atom by atom, in dendrites: fern-fronds, fir-branches, fractal antlers with sixty-degree branching, some the length of a forearm, the largest a two-meter candelabrum as thin as a wrist and so fragile that a careless fin-kick would take it apart. There are perhaps forty of them in the whole chamber, widely spaced, each one alone in a lot of clean blue water. Sparse the way an orchard is sparse. Sparse the way a substation is sparse.

Under this light they are bronze-black, because water eats red first, and copper's true color cannot survive the trip across the room. Bring a lamp close and they flush salmon-pink, suddenly and indecently, like something alive.

They are not alive. Nothing here is alive except the moss and the felt and the roots.

The roots come down through cracks in the ceiling: three curtains of juniper root, pale, hairless, no thicker than bootlaces, hanging twenty centimeters and then stopping. All of them stop at the same depth. Their tips below that line are brown and blunt and dead, and above it they are alive, and the line is not marked by anything you can see — it is simply where the copper concentration becomes more than a root can bear. The room has a horizon in it, an invisible plane near the ceiling, and everything below that plane is sterile and has been sterile for the whole history of the place.

Near the shaft, where the seep of surface water dilutes things, there is a cushion of aquatic moss about the size of a dinner table, and a few green filaments, and a gray haze of bacteria on the stone. One square meter of green in six hundred square meters of room. That is the entire vegetation, above and below: tussocks and junipers on the bench, moss in the collar, three stopped roots, a felt of filaments on a crust of sulfur.

And there are no animals. Not a fish, not a snail, not a worm, not a copepod, not a larva, not a mite. The water is a biocide and always has been. It is the reason the clarity is absolute and the reason the floor holds what it holds.

---

The floor holds leaves.

They lie in a shallow fan beneath the shaft, where they have arrived one at a time over centuries, and they are *whole*. Juniper sprigs with the needles still on. A curl of bark. A grass stem with its seed head intact. Tanned, browned, softened at the edges by slow chemistry only, but not eaten, not skeletonized, not rotted, because there is nothing down here that eats. They lie exactly where they settled. No current has ever moved one of them. Some of the deeper ones are older than any language.

Among them lie the fallen branches.

This is the part that keeps the room from ever becoming anything else. A dendrite grows, slowly, a few millimeters in a human lifetime, until at last a hair of it touches a graphite seam or the ore itself — and the circuit shorts. The current concentrates at that one contact, and the contact burns off, and the branch falls. It lands on the flat floor and lies there and, over more centuries, redissolves. Growth and shorting are matched. Deposition and dissolution are matched. The acid made at the anode is spent on the limestone as fast as it is made, so the pH holds at a bit under four and the walls retreat at a rate you could not measure with a lifetime of instruments. The water level is fixed by a seep at the pool's lip. The temperature is fixed by the mountain. The oxygen is fixed by the sky.

The scrap heap on the floor is always the same size. The trees are always the same number. The room reached its finished shape a hundred thousand years ago and has been holding it since, the way a river holds a graded profile — not because nothing is happening, but because everything happening is cancelled by something else happening at exactly the same rate.

At full production, this plant yields a few grams of refined copper a year. It has produced tonnes. It holds a few hundred kilos. It has never shipped anything anywhere.

---

It is quiet. That is what a person would notice last and remember longest.

You would expect an industrial space to roar, and this one is silent, because direct current makes no hum and there are no moving parts and nothing is being pumped or crushed or conveyed. The whole engine of the place is a difference of potential across a hair-thin film of water at the surface of a rock, and it does its work in absolute silence, and outside that film — a millimeter, less — the entire chamber is chemically identical to itself, corner to corner. All the gradients in this world are thinner than a fingernail. Everything else is uniform.

There are sounds. Where acid meets limestone under the ore, gas forms, and a bead grows on the wall for the better part of an hour and lets go and ticks up along the ceiling and travels the low arch to the shaft and out. Perhaps one bubble a minute, a small ceramic click, then nothing. When it rains on the bench, eleven meters of water turn it into a soft frying noise that comes down the shaft and fills the room evenly, from no direction. When the wind is high you can hear the junipers as a distant pressure, more felt than heard.

Otherwise: nothing. A silence with a shape to it, the shape of a thirty-meter room.

---

Everything here looks made. The graphite runs like busbar. The quartz ribs stand in ranks like cathode blanks hung in a tankhouse. The flat floor is a poured slab; the ore is a feedstock pile; the mud beneath it is anode slime, exactly the slime that a refinery scrapes off and sends away for its silver; the trees are product; the fallen branches are scrap; the blue water is electrolyte, and it is the same blue as the vats in any electrowinning shop on earth. The eye goes looking for the ladder. The drain. The walkway, the crane rail, the stamped serial number, the handprint somebody left in the grout.

There is none of it. There has never been anyone here. Nothing in this chamber has been shaped by intention, and nothing in it violates any law you could test with a voltmeter and a thermometer and a patience longer than your own.

It is not a ruin. Ruins imply makers, and an ending.

It is a plant that was never built, running at capacity, on a shift that has not ended, in a room where the light comes on every morning and goes off every night and nothing else has changed since before there were eyes to see it.
