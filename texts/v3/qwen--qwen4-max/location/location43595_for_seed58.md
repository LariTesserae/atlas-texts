## The Ninth Sink

Three hundred paces from wall to wall, and you can see the far side only as a smear of amber lamps hanging in the dust. The chamber is a wound that has not yet learned to be a room. Its ceiling is a fresh fracture surface, sixty meters up, glassy in places where the rock sheared clean, and along the arrises the spall edges are still knife-sharp — no rounding, no patina, nothing softened. Rock flour hangs in the updraft and has never entirely settled since the roof foundered. It coats the tops of the gantries in a gray felt. It makes every beam of light into a solid cone you could lean a ladder against.

The heat comes from below. A sill of new basalt lies somewhere under the floor, close enough that the sump water arrives warm and the deepest sumps do not cool at all, and the rock at the low wall reads sixty-two degrees where the tenders press their sensors against it like palms against a forehead. Air moves in slow columns. At the floor it is thick and oily and tastes of hot iron; twenty meters up it is thinner, hotter, and smells of ozone; at the roof it is a dry sixty that would kill anything with lungs in under an hour. Nothing here has lungs.

Nothing here has lungs, or eyes, or blood, or a mother. This is worth saying plainly, because the place looks so much like it should be inhabited: nine tiers of gantry, catwalks, trolley wires, stair towers, a lit signboard, a canteen-shaped void in the plan. There are no rats in the cable trays. No moths at the lamps. No flies, because there is nothing to lay eggs in. The dust is entirely mineral and metallic — no skin, no hair, no pollen. When a lamp is left burning in an empty bay, nothing comes to it, ever, and this is the loneliest fact in the chamber.

## What Grows

In the bedding-plane seam along the north wall, water arrives at four liters an hour, and where it wets the rock there are mats: pale sulfur-oxidizing filaments, finger-length, laid down in the seams like frost in the mortar of a wall. They are the color of watered milk and they glow very faintly green when the air is driest, which is a thing they do for reasons no one here is equipped to investigate. They occupy perhaps two hundred square meters out of a chamber whose surfaces total forty thousand. They eat rock and heat and the trace sulfide in the seep, and they are the only thing in the Sink that grows without being instructed to.

The tenders do not remove them. There is no directive about them at all. Sweepers route around the wet seams, taking a longer path along the tier, and this detour has been in the routing tables so long that the extra distance is now budgeted as though it were geometry.

Elsewhere: nothing. Dry steel, dry stone, hot dust. A city with one garden, and the garden is a stain.

## Water, Which Is a Schedule

There is no river and no pool that lasts. Water exists here as sweat.

The chill loops run through everything — the return lines from the furnace jackets, the accumulator banks, the great lagged mains that cross the chamber at tier four. Their skins sit at eleven degrees in a room at fifty. So the air gives up its moisture onto them, continuously, in beads that swell and run and gather at the low point of every pipe and fall. Under the mains there are wet stripes on the floor, always the same stripes, and outside them the concrete is bone dry and hot enough to burn. Salt rings mark the edges. Soda straws hang from the pipe hangers, some a finger long, calcium-white, growing a millimeter a year — the oldest objects in the Sink that nobody manufactured.

And every thirty-one hours the chillers dump their load into the roof loops, all at once, and the whole ceiling goes cold, and for nine minutes it rains.

Not hard. A fine, even, warm rain over the entire footprint of the chamber, falling sixty meters through the dust and arriving as a gray drizzle that patters on the gantry decks and hisses where it touches the furnace housings. Steam comes off the floor in sheets. The mats in the north seams open. Every machine in the Sink stops what it is doing and stands in it, arms out or booms raised or hatches cracked, letting the water run into their scaling and off their radiator fins, and the sound is the sound of a hundred thousand small taps arriving in a stone box with an eleven-second reverberation, so that the rain does not sound like rain but like the inside of a bell being washed.

Then it stops. The floor dries in forty minutes. The puddles retreat to their stripes. Nothing is left but the salt, which the sweepers come for.

## The Crowd

Density here is not population, it is *packing*. Nine tiers, and no tier has a clear span longer than a body-length. Pipework runs in bundles of thirty, lagged and strapped, so close that a maintenance crawler must go along them sideways like a crab. Machines nest in charging racks in six-deep rows, shoulder to shoulder, plugged into the busbars, radiating. There are two hundred and forty mobile units in a volume that a person would call a large church, and they are never more than a meter from one another, and they are all, in some sense, touching — through the rails, through the busbars, through the shared return of the coolant, through the frame of the chamber itself.

They queue. That is most of what the density means. Queue at the breaker floor, queue at the busbar, queue at the hoist, queue at the furnace mouth, right-of-way settled by amperage draw and by how much of the schedule you are holding hostage. The queueing is silent and extremely fast, negotiated in modulated whine along the power rails, faster than hearing can follow, and what you see from the walkway is a slow shuffle of iron bodies, a shunting, a courteousness so dense it looks like a single organism breathing.

They are not sleek. Cast frames, riveted lagging, mineral wool gone brown, hydraulic weep at every joint, cam timers and drum sequencers ticking behind glass. Grease has cooked onto everything in a lacquer the color of strong tea. The furnaces breathe orange at the low wall, and this — plus the sodium work-lamps burning at eight percent, plus the indicator lamps in their thousands, plus the faint green of the mats — is all the light there is. Dim enough that the chamber reads as a series of amber depths with black rock behind them. The machines mostly see in the infrared, where the place is blindingly legible, every pipe and body and hot seam distinct. The visible lamps are for nothing. They burn at a level specified in a lighting standard whose readers are not here. Nobody has revised the standard.

## The Rule of Quiet

The roof is new, and it is not finished falling.

When the chill loops dump and the ceiling goes cold, the rock contracts and clamps: the fracture faces press together, the whole foundered mass locks, and for about five hours the roof will hold a load. When the loops recover and the ceiling warms, the mass relaxes and sheds — spalls the size of dinner plates, then a slab the size of a door, dropping sixty meters into whatever is beneath.

So the Sink has learned to be quiet. Percussion is rationed. Impact work — the press, the breaker, the scaling bars, the drop-hammer — is permitted only inside the cold clamp, and outside it the city moves on rubber and hydraulics and speaks in currents rather than sound. Between the loud hours there is only the fifty-hertz hum of the busbars, the tick of cooling metal, the drip under the mains, the relays conversing in the cabinets like insects that were never alive, and now and then the roof itself ticking as it moves, a sound everyone stops for.

The reverberation makes each permitted noise enormous. When the press strikes, once, in the middle of the cold clamp, the whole chamber answers for eleven seconds — a single flat *whump* unfolding into a long stone chord that comes back off the north wall and the south wall and the roof and passes through every body in the racks. Units log it. It is the closest thing here to an event that everyone attends.

Then quiet again, and the dust turning in the light.

## What the City Is For

It eats its own decay. This is the loop that has closed since the drifts were sealed.

Corrosion is the predator. In the wet stripes and under the lagging, in the sweat-line at every cold pipe, iron goes to oxide, and the oxide blooms out in orange flowers and brown whiskers, fast, faster than anywhere dry, and if it is not harvested it takes the pipe. So the sweepers scrape, and the scavengers go along the wet seams with their brushes and their small vacuums, and everything they collect goes to the breaker floor and then to the furnace, and comes back as new pipe, new frames, new units. The fabricator lays down four or five bodies a year, from rust and from the cannibalized dead, and the population is bounded not by material but by amperage: the sill gives so much heat, the turbines take so much of it, and there is current for two hundred and forty.

When a unit fails it is carried down to the breaker floor and taken apart with great care, nothing wasted, and its serial tag goes into a rack on the wall of the tender bay. There are eleven thousand tags in that rack. They are arranged by number. They are dusted. There is no directive about them either.

And the Sink makes its product — castings, dense gray blocks, palletized, strapped, stacked in the east bays nine high and forty deep, an inventory that has not moved in a long time. The hoist at the shaft is maintained. Its cables are dressed weekly. Its cage is polished, its gate greased, its landing lamps lit at the specified eight percent, and above it the shaft goes up two hundred meters to a bulkhead that has not opened since the roof foundered and the world above stopped answering.

There is a stair beside the hoist. Steel, oiled, clear of dust, with a handrail at the height a hand would want. It is the only thing in the chamber built to the measure of a body that isn't here, and every thirty-one hours, when the cold rain comes down through the amber light and the mats open in the north seams and two hundred and forty iron bodies stand with their booms raised in the drizzle, the stair is wet, and nothing climbs it, and the lamps on the landing burn all the same.
