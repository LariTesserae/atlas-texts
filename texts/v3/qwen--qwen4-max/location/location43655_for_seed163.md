## Arrival

From the valley it looks like nothing much: a pale shelf above the treeline, a rim of blue rock, a line of lattice towers walking up out of sight. The town below has a name for it that translates roughly as *the hush*, and the name is honest, because from the town you cannot hear a thing. The high-frequency roar the plateau makes dies out within a kilometer of open air; it does not bend over the rim; it goes up, and away, and is lost. Children in the valley grow up believing the shelf is a quiet place. There is a photograph of it in the school, taken at noon, no shadow anywhere, looking like a folded bedsheet.

The incline takes forty minutes to climb the headwall. Halfway up, the car begins to shiver. At the top, the doors open, and the noise arrives like weather.

## Ground

It is not a moor, though it has been called one. It is a single skin.

Nine hundred years ago the ice let go of this shelf and left it scoured: granodiorite still bearing the polish, striations fine as comb marks, chatter crescents where the glacier chattered, joints tight and unweathered, cobbles lying in unsorted heaps exactly where they were dropped. No soil. No gullies. No talus. Geology in the condition of a thing just made and not yet used.

Then the plants came, and instead of colonizing the ground they *became* it. Cushion rosettes the size of dinner plates, pale sage, waxed over, leaves fringed with white hair; moss packed between them like grout; a wiry sedge threading everything on a diagonal; liverworts in the low places; a lichen crust wherever the mat has been broken long enough to scab. The cushions grew outward until they touched, and then they fused, and now the whole shelf — thirty-one kilometers at its long axis, four hundred square kilometers of it — is one continuous springy pavement, thirty to fifty centimeters thick, with the bottom two-thirds of it dead and perfectly preserved, a brown felt of stems holding the living surface up off the rock like a floor on joists.

You walk on the canopy. That is the fact that takes a day to accept. There is no ground here in the ordinary sense; there is a roof, and you are on it, and beneath it is a lit, flooded, breathing space you can put your arm into up to the shoulder. Push a stick through and it goes down easy for twenty centimeters and then hits gravel with a click you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

And yet the shelf is nearly empty. This is the thing photographs never manage. The mat is total — there is no bare square meter anywhere, no dust, no scree, no litter that is not plant — but nothing *stands up*. The vertical world is scattered so thinly it feels curated: an erratic boulder every hundred and fifty meters, blue-grey, glacier-placed, wearing a green collar where the mat has climbed its flank; a steel marker post; a pylon; a lone wind-flagged spruce in the lee of the head-house, four meters tall, the only tree on the plateau, planted by somebody in 1911 and kept alive by sentiment. Between these, distances that make you check your map. A valley's worth of room with a village's worth of objects in it. The emptiness is not barrenness. It is a very full floor with almost no furniture.

## What the water is doing

There is no lake on the shelf. There is no river either, and this is not a dry place; it is one of the wettest surfaces in the range. It receives two and a half meters of precipitation a year and holds, at any moment, about two centimeters of moving water spread evenly across all four hundred square kilometers, held in the mat's dead felt by capillarity, sliding downhill at a few centimeters a minute, seeping through the roof at ten million apertures a hectare and falling into the void beneath, and running out over the gravel to the rim.

The mat will not let a channel form. Its roots bind the till; its felt spreads any concentrated flow back into sheet; where a thread of water starts to gather strength, the cushions above it swell and pinch it. So the drainage network has never been born. After nine centuries the shelf still has no streams, no beds, no banks, no incision — the geology remains embryonic, held at the moment just after the ice, because its own skin has forbidden it to grow up.

The consequence is the sound.

Water forced through billions of small openings makes a high, wide hiss; water dropping twenty centimeters into a stone chamber makes a click, a plink, a knock; multiply both by a landscape and you get a roar with no low end at all, a broadband ceiling-to-floor white noise, twelve to fifteen thousand separate events per second within earshot, summed into something that is not experienced as many sounds but as one substance. Ninety-four decibels at chest height on a calm day. A hundred and six near the rim weeps, where the sheet finally leaves the mat and falls over blue rock in ten thousand threads, each one bright, none of them thicker than a bootlace.

It does not vary. That is the part that unsettles people. There is no gust in it, no surge, no rise and fall. The mat is a flywheel: it takes the snowmelt of June and the storms of October and pays them out at almost exactly the same rate all year, so the plateau's voice has been the same note, at the same volume, since before the valley had a church. You stop hearing it after twenty minutes and then hear it again, worse, when you swallow. It sits in the sternum. It makes distance feel wrong — a pylon four hundred meters off seems nearby because it is silent, and silence here reads as proximity.

Above all that, thinner: the wires. Six circuits of them, strung tower to tower across the shelf, humming their odd harmonics in the wind, and in the saturated bright air, crackling at the insulators with a fine corona hiss and a smell of ozone that arrives before you can locate its source.

And below all of it, felt rather than heard, the fifty-hertz throb of nine turbine houses, spaced three and four kilometers apart, running at fixed load, as they have run since the year the incline was built.

## Why the machines are here

Nowhere else in the range can you buy water this reliable. Every other catchment floods and starves. This one has been regulated by a living thing that has no interest in the contract, and the scheme was designed around that fact: no dam, no reservoir, no flood gate, no spillway. Just nine low concrete houses, each sitting over a shallow sump cut down through the mat into the gravel; a ring of intake mouths around each sump, waist-high concrete lips with steel trash racks, spaced along the contours like watering troughs; buried penstocks whose routes are legible only as pale lines of trimmed mat and a run of numbered posts; a transformer shed the size of a chapel; the incline; and the towers, going away over the shelf in a straight line so straight it looks like an error in the landscape.

That is the whole of the built world up here, and it is not much: a handful of boxes in an area the size of a small country's province. But the boxes are loud out of all proportion to their number, and the towers are visible from the sea.

The intakes are the interesting parts, because they are losing. The mat grows two to four millimeters a year, vertically, and about a centimeter a year sideways, and it grows over concrete as happily as over stone. Every intake mouth on the plateau wears a green lip that thickens annually and, if left, will close into a green lid. So there is a crew, and the crew's entire occupation is cutting: hook knives, a long-handled shear, turves lifted and stacked and re-laid elsewhere so that no ground is ever裸露 exposed. Twenty-two years is the interval at which a mouth must be re-cut. The concrete lips are dated in paint, and the dates go back through six or seven hands, and the oldest ones have been chiseled deeper by someone who wanted them to last.

The trench scars are the only places on the shelf where erosion is actually happening. Wherever the mat has been broken and not re-laid — the 1954 cable trench, the slip below Intake Seven, the spoil heap at the head-house — water has found a concentration, and the concentration has found the till, and a gully is being invented, headward, at about eleven meters a century. You can stand at the head of the newest one and see the whole future of the plateau in miniature: a raw nick in blue rock, a fan of clean gravel, the mat's cut edge hanging over it like a lip of turf, dripping. It is the only bare mineral ground for thirty kilometers, and it is nine meters long, and it is the youngest landscape in the district.

Older marks, fainter: dry-stone sheep folds from four centuries ago, now soft green humps you notice only when the light is low; peat cuttings, rectangular, healed; a boundary stone with a date and a letter; a survey tripod's concrete feet with the wood long gone. A bothy, pre-scheme, iron grate, initials scratched in the doorframe, used now for boots and thermoses. Nothing else. No village, no road, no fence. The plateau has been used continuously for four hundred years by people who never stayed.

## Life, and why there is so little of it

The ecosystem here is almost entirely plant, and entirely honest about it. Fungi and bacteria work the dead felt; the peat flour beneath is a few centimeters of their leavings. Everything else is a tenant.

There are no fish, because there is no river — two centimeters of sheet water divided among ten million apertures is not a habitat, it is a humidity. No mosquitoes, because nothing stands still long enough to breed. No frogs. Invertebrates live in the lit flooded space beneath the canopy, springtails in their millions on the water film, a flightless black beetle that carries a bubble, a flatworm the color of weak tea. They do not use the air to signal. They tap the stems, and the mat carries it: a substrate vibration, private, in a world where the air belongs to the water.

The plants themselves signal hydraulically, through the continuous water in the felt. Step down hard and the pressure pulse travels; thirty meters away the cushion pores begin to close, some minutes later, in a slow ripple you would never notice unless you had been told. The mat knows you are walking on it before you get there. It just isn't in a hurry to react.

Above the canopy: birds, and barely. A pale harrier quartering the shelf, hunting by eye. A small brown thing the crew calls the mat-lark, which has no song worth the name and instead courts with a shivering posture and a flash of white underwing, semaphore in a place where nothing can be heard. That is the rule the noise makes: any creature that needs to be heard cannot live here, so the ones that remain have gone visual, or gone silent, or gone under. Three or four birds an hour, on a good day, in all that space. Their absence is what makes the shelf feel like a room rather than a country.

## Light and cold

At three thousand one hundred meters the sky is a violet-blue you cannot get lower down, and the sun arrives unfiltered, and then it arrives a second time from below — off the water film, off the wax on a trillion leaves, off the quartz in the till where the mat is thin. The ground carries more highlights than the sky. There is no true shade anywhere on the shelf except under the erratics and inside the buildings; the undersides of things are lit, including faces, which is why everyone up here looks slightly theatrical in photographs. Walk east and the mat is sage. Turn, and the wax bloom takes the sun at a different angle and the whole shelf goes pewter, then nearly white, all the way to the rim.

And it is cold. Seven degrees on average, eleven on a bright afternoon, two at dawn, frost in the hollows, no permafrost, never a hard freeze deep enough to break the felt. Wet cold, the kind that gets into the wrists. Sunburnt noses and numb fingers on the same person at the same moment. Steam standing off the turbine houses. The only warm place on the plateau is inside the mat, a degree or two above air where the dead felt is slowly composting, and it is full of water, and no one wants to be in it.

Below the rim, most mornings, a cloud deck: a white floor with the shelf standing out of it, and the valley nowhere, and the towers going down into the white and stopping. Above, that hard blue. The brightness has no warmth in it whatsoever. It is the light of a place being lit rather than heated.

## The clock

Everything here is slow except the noise, and the noise is slow too, if you have the ears for it.

The mat lays down three millimeters a year. The ice remnant in the northern cirque loses a meter of length a year and has been losing it, patiently, since the survey. The gates flush on a forty-one-minute cycle, and the flush is the only event on the shelf that has a shape: the roar shifts, thickens, drops a little in pitch, and comes back. The oldest trimmer, who has been up here thirty-eight summers, says the plateau's note has fallen measurably in his working life, and the instruments agree with him — as the trench gullies open, as voids enlarge, the apertures widen and the voice deepens. A semitone, more or less, per century.

That is the direction of travel. Give it four thousand years and the trench scars will have become a network; the network will cut; the mat will lose its grip on the till and break into islands between real channels; there will be rivers, and pools, and fish, and insects, and birds that sing because singing works again. The roar will settle into the ordinary voice of a mountain river, which is to say it will become a sound instead of a substance. The pylons will be orange stains in a peat bank. The intake lips will be concrete rings in a streambed, and someone will find one and write a paper about it.

For now the skin holds, and the shelf stays in the condition of just-after-the-ice: raw rock, no drainage, no soil, everything covered, nothing formed. It is a landscape being kept embryonic by its own vegetation and made deafening by the same act — four hundred square kilometers of water falling twenty centimeters, forever, through a floor that will not let it gather. The machines are here to sell that constancy. The crew is here to keep the mouths open. And the whole thing is roaring at the top of its lungs about the fact that it has not yet begun.
