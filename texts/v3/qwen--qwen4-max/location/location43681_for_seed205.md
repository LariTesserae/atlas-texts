## The Plate

At noon you can see ninety kilometers in every direction and there is nothing to see. The floor goes out flat and dark to a horizon that curves — actually curves, visibly, the way a sea does — and the haze stacks up blue in the distance like something poured. There is no tree. There is no hill. There is a shape forty kilometers north that might be a tower or might be a smudge of weather, and it will take you two days to find out.

And you cannot hear yourself. Not your voice, not the clap of your hands beside your ear, not the shout you make with your throat until it tears. The air is *full*. It has a grain to it, a pressure, a fast granular shudder that comes up through the soles of your feet and sets your back teeth buzzing and makes the water in your gourd stand in fine rings. Somewhere out in all that emptiness, spaced too far apart to see, four or five animals are talking. That is all it takes.

---

The floor is not ground. Put your palm on it and you will feel, under the wax and the heat, a faint regular give, like the flank of something asleep.

It is a plant. One plant, or a federation of six or seven clones grafted into each other along seams you can hear as changes of key. Forty million years ago this was a basin swamp at the height of a river's mouth; the lineage that took it never stopped growing and never learned to fall. It lignified its own dead tissue instead of shedding it, built a honeycomb of hollow strut and buttress out of its corpses, and lifted its living surface up out of the basin on a scaffold of its own making. Two thousand six hundred meters of altitude, all of it self-grown. The plate is a tableland that *stands* rather than rests, and under the skin it is mostly air and passages and wet pith, two kilometers down to the drowned original forest, which is still there, still recognizable in places, still being eaten.

There is no rock. This is the first fact a child of the plate learns and the last fact they stop thinking about. No soil either — the loose stuff in the hollows is frass, packed and reworked and fungal, ten thousand generations of insect dung with a nap to it like felt. No clay, no ore, no flint. Stone exists the way a rumor exists. It comes out of the crops of the great kites, who take grit from the rim cliffs four hundred kilometers away to grind their food: rounded pebbles, wet and warm when they come up, worth a season of anything. A woman on the eastern plain owns three. She boils water with them. When one cracks from the heat it is retired with something close to a funeral, because a cracked stone will never boil again and there is no replacing it.

The skin itself is a cuticle the thickness of a boot sole, dark green-black, resinous, laid over the venation in ridges and pans so that from the air — if anything could get high enough to look — the whole continent resembles the roof of a mouth. It smells like turpentine and hot pepper and, faintly, underneath, like meat.

It should be cold up here. It is not. The plate is dark and drinks the sun, and the compost two kilometers below it is running at a steady fever, and the vents breathe that heat out all night in slow ammoniac columns you can see distorting the stars. Thirty-four degrees at midday, twenty-eight before dawn. The air is thin enough to make your head ache on the climb up a mast, and hot enough that the ache never leaves.

---

You learn to walk wide-footed. Everything here does.

The skin holds a distributed load beautifully and a point load not at all. A hoof will go through. A dropped adze goes through, slowly, over an hour, with a sound like a knuckle cracking in another room, and then it is gone into a two-kilometer fall through galleries of pale life, and you do not get it back. The big animals are all splayed and padded, six- and eight-legged, weight spread over hectares of contact; the heavy predator of the western plain cannot stand still, ever, and sleeps in a trotting circle at nine kilometers an hour with its four feet striking so fast the skin never has time to fail. Children are taught to lie down flat if the floor starts to sigh. Falling through is not the worst death available but it is the most common, and it takes a long time, and the whole way down you are being tasted.

At noon the plate expands and knocks. Not metaphorically: the thermal growth of a continent of wood makes a sound like lake ice, single enormous reports spaced minutes apart, rolling off past the horizon. Nobody who lives here notices them. Visitors weep.

---

Water is a rumor too, but a nearer one.

Rain comes to about four hundred millimeters a year, in the same walking routes each season, and none of it ever runs anywhere. There are no rivers on the plate. There cannot be: any channel that started to flow would cut, and cutting is the one wound the plant cannot afford, so every depression on this surface has been shaped over geological time into a *vessel* — lipped, waxed, sealed. Water goes into the tanks or straight down the stomatal shafts, and either way it is gone from the world of eyes.

The tanks are the presences. Every twenty or forty kilometers, a hollow: a leaf-axil the size of a bathtub, or a healed growth-node collapsed into a lake four kilometers across, black with tannin, warm as blood, sweet-smelling, ringed with a lip of callus you could walk a house onto. They do not connect. They have not connected in thirty million years. Each is an island with its own endemics — a shrimp found in one tank and nowhere else in the universe, a frog-analog with a call pitched to that tank's particular resonance, a mat of floating bladder-weed that exists in a single puddle in a single axil and has done so since before there were primates. There are perhaps two hundred thousand tanks on the plate. Most have never been visited by anything with hands.

Some of them are stomachs. The plant is nitrogen-poor and has been solving that problem for a long time; a tank with a digestive lip is smooth and slightly warm and hums, actually hums, a low inviting note, and things come to drink and do not leave. You test with a fiber wand. If the wand comes up slick, you walk another two days.

The real water is below: an ocean of sap and rot-water in the pith, more fresh water than the basin ever held, sealed under a skin that is, from the point of view of a thirsty person standing on it, a desert.

Dew is the mercy. All night the plate transpires into its own boundary layer and radiates to a thin sky, and by the last hour before dawn the whole surface is running with condensation — hydrophobic ridges shedding it into hydrophilic grooves, the venation of the entire continent acting as one enormous engineered harvest, every drop routed to an axil. For ninety minutes the plain is silver and loud with trickling. Then the sun clears the haze and it is gone, all of it, everywhere, and the cuticle goes matte and hot and starts to tick.

---

Put your ear to the floor and you will hear the sea.

That is the chewing. The interior of the plate is not hollow so much as *tenanted*. Blind pale six-limbed nations, fungus-farmers, gall-builders, sap-tappers, ten thousand species and a biomass that in the lower kilometer exceeds the wood that houses it. They prune the vascular bundles. They graft the seams. They pack wounds with mastic and cull the rot-fungi and carry water up in their own bodies during the dry. The plate has not been able to maintain itself without them for twenty million years; it is an organism in the way a reef is an organism, which is to say it is a treaty. Every gram of the frass that passes for soil here has been through a gut, most of it several times. The plant's body is largely made of animals' leftovers, and the animals' world is entirely made of plant, and there is no third thing.

At night they come up. The stomatal shafts open with the cooling and the surface *boils* — a tide of small pale bodies rising to mate in the dark, ankle-deep, knee-deep in the low places, a hiss and rustle and meat-smell over ten thousand square kilometers, and it is all gone by the second hour of light, sucked back down, leaving nothing but a faint gloss on the skin and the tracks of the things that came to eat it.

So the emptiness is a matter of timing. At noon: nothing, forever, in every direction. At midnight: you cannot put your foot down without killing something.

---

The giants are sparse and they are the reason for the noise.

Air this thin is expensive to fly in and cheap to shout in, and the plate carries sound better than air does — through the wood, four kilometers a second, arriving before the airborne version like a second earlier self. Everything large here has given up on being seen. Sight is useless across ninety kilometers of haze. So the drummers, flightless and file-bellied, press their resonating horns to the skin and broadcast at a hundred and fifteen decibels, and their territories are not shapes on a landscape but *pitches*. The plate has thickened where it is drummed and thinned where it is not, over hundreds of thousands of years, so that the continent has tuned itself into regions — a plain in something like D, a plain a fifth above it, boundaries at the clonal seams where the key changes and a drummer crossing over finds itself suddenly, catastrophically unintelligible, calling into a country where its song means nothing and no one answers. Species here are sorted by key. There are eleven near-identical drummers who will never meet.

The stilters go between the tanks on legs thirty meters long, stepping in the ancient polished lanes, drinking from a different island each week. The kites ride the plate's own thermals with twelve-meter wings and never land on the skin at all — they cannot, they would go through — so they sleep on the wing and nest only on the fruiting masts, three hundred meters of green scaffolding that a clone throws up once in a decade, and they drink by dipping at speed across a tank's face.

And the masts do not open for weather. They open for a chord. There is a particular interval, drummed by enough animals at once across enough of the plain, that triggers dehiscence; the plant is pollinated by *sound*, and pays for it in sap-wells at the base of the drum-bosses, sweet and slightly narcotic. A silent year is a famine. Everyone here knows this. It is the closest thing to theology on the plate: the ground is listening, and if we are quiet it will not feed us.

Which is why the predators hunt at noon. In the ringing hours nothing can hear a footfall, or a stalk, or the flat wet click of a jaw. The deafening is not a background. It is a season, daily, and everything with teeth has synchronized to it. Dawn, when the dew runs and the plain is briefly, unbearably quiet, is when the small things move and the frightened things travel and the people talk.

---

There are people. Perhaps four thousand of them, across a landmass the size of a subcontinent, in bands of nine or twenty, so far apart that most adults meet a stranger twice in a life.

They make things from what exists: adzes from the silica-loaded cuticle of old scars, fire-hardened probes, thorn awls, resin and wax and cord drawn from vascular fiber, gourds, wax-lined baskets, boiling by dropped stone. They speak with their hands and with low sounds pressed mouth-to-ear, because the air is not theirs; every band carries an ear-stick, a meter of tuned hardwood you set against the skin and put your cheekbone to, and through it the world is legible for two hundred kilometers — who is drumming, who is answering, which tank is humming with hunger, whether the far plain has gone strange.

Their houses are stilted on tank lips or lashed to mast ribs, and they are small and single and scattered, one hut, one windbreak, one drum-platform, one listening house with its floor planed thin as a membrane. Nothing larger has ever survived, because the floor grows. A centimeter a year, two in a wet decade. Leave a house standing and in ten years it is ankle-deep in living cuticle, in forty it is a waist-high ruin with the skin healed up around its posts, in a century it is a knot. This is why there are no cities and no monuments and no archaeology in the ordinary sense: the substrate eats stillness. Occupation is maintenance. To own a house you must keep moving it, and the marks that last are not structures but *paths* — lanes polished glassy by a hundred thousand years of the same feet, older than the species that use them, older than most of the tanks.

They are deaf by forty, most of them. It is not considered a loss. Hearing is what the ear-stick is for; ears in air are only for the moment before hail.

Because hail is the true predator. Ice at this altitude, on a skin that cannot take a point load, is a perforation event — a storm can put a hundred thousand holes in a hundred square kilometers and drop the entire surface population into the dark. The drum-bosses fall silent a full hour before it arrives; they read the pressure through the wood. That silence is the only warning anyone gets, and every human on the plate knows it in their spine, and when the noon chorus stops in the middle of the ringing hours, people lie down flat and hold each other and wait.

Lightning does the other thing. Fire on a hot resinous plain with a terpene haze above it can run for a thousand kilometers. There are burn scars out there the size of small countries, healed over centuries into dense dumb callus that does not resonate — the black plains, silent, sterile, ringing dead underfoot. Nothing drums there. Nothing hunts by sound there, either. The people build in the scars, in the few hectares of true quiet, and bear children there and die there, and the silence in those places is so complete that you can hear the blood in your own head, which is a thing no one born on the plate ever hears otherwise, and which many find they cannot bear for long.

---

The thickness has not changed in a million years. Accretion at the top — new cuticle, dust, frass, the bodies of the night tide — exactly balances the eating below and the slow collapse of the deepest galleries. It is not growing anymore. It has arrived. It is a settled thing, weathered into its own shapes, an equilibrium with a heartbeat, and it will hold this exact form until the climate moves or the pale nations fail or a fire gets lucky.

The past is audible, if you know how to listen. Wounds heal into knots, and knots ring flat, so an old death has a sound: a dull patch in the plain where a stilter fell through in some century nobody counted, a long muted scar where a fire ran before there were people. The bands navigate by these. They have names for them. They pass a place where the floor goes dead under the ear-stick and someone signs *here the great one went down, in the time of the grandmother before the grandmother*, and nobody stops, because it is two more days to water and the light is already going.

Then the sun comes up over the curve of the world, and for ninety minutes the whole continent trickles silver into its grooves, and it is quiet enough to hear a person speak, and out at the edges of hearing, one by one, spaced too far apart to ever see, the drummers begin to tune.
