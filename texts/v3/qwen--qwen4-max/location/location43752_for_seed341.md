# The Kettle

You come down into it. That is the first fact, and the town never lets you forget it: seven hundred paces across, an hour to walk its rim, and everything in it below the water table of the country outside. The stair is cut into marl and tuff, three hundred and eleven treads, each one dished in the middle by three centuries of boots. The walls weep. They have always wept. Warm water comes out of the rock in threads and beads and gathers itself into orange ribbons of iron-stain, and where the ribbons run there are curtains of moss and maidenhair a meter long, hanging, dripping, moving very slightly in the updraft. The handrail is brass and has been worn to an oval by palms. At the bottom of the stair there is a boot-scraper, and a lamp, and a notice board with the day's chord written on it in chalk.

Above the bowl there is a lid. Not rock — steam, a permanent ceiling of it sixty meters up, fed by the condensers and the hot seeps and the breath of everything growing down here. It never disperses. It glows at noon like a paper lantern with a bulb behind it, a flat pale green-gold, and that is the brightest the Kettle ever gets. Down on the causeways it is the light of an hour before a storm that never arrives. Lamps burn all day in the windows. The plants have given up on the sun.

It is hot. Forty degrees, wet enough that sweat does nothing at all, and the air moves like something with weight. The heat comes up with the water, out of a fault under the bowl, and the town's entire business is that heat and that water: lift it, cool it, take the salt and the lithium and the pale dissolved silica out of it, use the difference in temperature to turn the engines, use the engines to lift more. Nine wells once. Seven engines now, and two holes with brick caps and ferns coming out of them.

---

They are spaced far apart, the engines, and that is the whole shape of the place. Each one sits on its own island of dense construction — beam house, condenser hall, accumulator tower, brick and cast iron and riveted plate, four stories, layered, added to, cut into — and around each island is a wide flat nothing: hot mud, salt polygons, shallow standing water ten centimeters deep that holds the lid's pale light and doubles everything. Causeways on screw piles run between the islands like the spokes of a broken wheel. Forty buildings, four hundred people, and more emptiness than either. From the rim it looks like a town that was scattered rather than built.

It was not scattered. It was spaced. The engines are loud — not chaotic, not clattering: each holds a note, a low sustained fundamental you stop hearing within ten minutes and start feeling instead, in the sinuses, in the back teeth, in the fluid of the knees. Under it the water-hammer knocks every eleven seconds, a sound like a wardrobe dropped down a stairwell, and over it the condenser hiss and the small ticking of hot metal. Seven notes. They are tuned. They have been tuned to the same seven notes for six generations, and the spacing of the islands is exactly what it must be so that the notes meet correctly in the mud between them.

Where they meet correctly, there are antinodes, and things grow.

Where they cancel, there are nulls.

---

Nothing here has a face. No rats on the causeways, no flies over the water, no mosquitoes in all that steam and heat, which is the wrongness visitors notice third and remember first. Nothing swims in the sumps. Nothing nests in the eaves. Birds crossing the bowl turn aside a kilometer out, and dogs brought to the rim stair sit down on the fourth tread and will not go further, and no one has ever made them.

The green does not care. The green has taken every job an animal would have had.

The plants here grow toward sound. They came in as ordinary stock — cane, fig, taro, oil palm, some orchids off a barge three hundred years ago — and in the dim they stopped bothering with light and turned their attention to pressure. Now a shout-cane leans across twenty meters of empty mud toward a beam house, hollow-jointed, ringing faintly when the hammer knocks, and its roots run under the causeway and up through the foundation and into the warm damp under the floor plates. Drumleaf has leaves like the head of a kettle-drum, taut and pale, and they pivot. Not fast. Fast enough. Stand still by a standpipe and watch a leaf take a quarter-hour to face it. The fig-stranglers hunt the accumulators. The moss grows only on the quiet sides of things, a fur on the lee of every wall, so that the town has a grain to it, a nap, like velvet brushed one way.

Farmers here prune with baffles. To starve a plant you box it in felt and lead and it yellows in three days. To force one you set a resonator beside it and open the throat. Growth is a hand's width a shift; you can stand at the edge of a cane stand at dusk and hear it coming, a fine dry crackling of joints lengthening, like a fire in a distant room.

And there is predation. There is a thing they call the false pump: a chambered root-crown with a resonating bladder that it tunes, over weeks, to the note of a working engine. Its neighbors' roots come to it thinking they have found the source. It dissolves them. The voicers walk the stands with a stethoscope rod, listening for a note that is a little too pure, and mark the crown with red paint, and a crew comes out with hooks.

There is a vine that grows into pipe joints and drinks condensate and drops the pressure in a whole district, so that the chord goes flat and the crops on the far side of the bowl lean the wrong way for a week and the harvest is short. A plant, here, can change the weather.

---

The nulls are painted around with yellow posts. They are places where the seven notes cancel and the mud lies flat and cracked and the air is suddenly, horribly ordinary. Step into one and the world drops out from under you: your own blood becomes the loudest thing in the world, your joints click like castanets, your swallow is a slammed door. People do not linger.

But the nulls are not dead. In a standing wave, dust gathers where the air is still — and here the dust is pollen. It falls out of the loud air and drifts in the quiet places, knee-deep in the deep nulls, sulphur-yellow, warm, faintly sweet, and it germinates. The nulls are the nurseries. Everything in the Kettle begins in silence and must be carried out into the noise to live. Crews go in with scoops and trays and wade the yellow drifts and bring the seedlings out to the antinodes on handcarts, and the seedlings turn toward the engines before the cart has gone ten meters.

Do not stand still in a null. That is the first thing they teach children. In the quiet, your heartbeat is a pump, and the drift will find your boots, and by the time you feel the pull at your laces there will be a white mat of rootlets across your feet and up your shins, and they do not let go, and they grow a hand's width a shift.

---

It rains here at the change of every shift, when the condenser banks vent together and the lid gets too heavy and lets go: eleven minutes of enormous warm drops, and the drumleaf catches them all and the whole bowl sounds like applause, like a stadium, like something being celebrated. Then the seeps take up the slack, and the water table presses in through the marl as it always has, and the pumps take it out again, and the tide-marks on the brickwork at 1.4 meters record every occasion in three hundred years when the arithmetic briefly went the other way.

The town has climbed its own body to stay ahead of that. The old ground floors are basements now, full of warm clear water and a soft blue fungus-light that shines up through the pavement gratings at night. Doorways are bricked at ankle, knee, waist. New doors were cut higher. The causeways are jacked level every spring on their screw piles; there are brass survey pins everywhere with dates stamped on them, and the dates go back, and back.

The people are deaf. All of them, from birth or from the first year of work; the note gets you through bone, and protection only slows it. They speak with hands and with touch — a palm flat on the sternum, two fingers on the wrist, a slap on the forearm that means *look up* — and with lamps at distance, and the town at night is full of small quick flickerings going between windows. They find hearing a liability and a resource both. The tuners are imported: hearing engineers on three-year contracts, in fluid-filled helmets, paid absurdly, who walk the stands with their heads cocked and set the chord and are pensioned off before they are forty, usually to somewhere very quiet, where they are said to be difficult company.

Once a year the engines stop for the overhaul, all seven together, and the Kettle goes silent for nine hours. This is the Mute. Doors are barred, shutters are dogged down, the causeways are chained, and everybody sits indoors and feels their own pulse and does not speak with their hands more than they must. Outside, in the sudden ordinary quiet, ten thousand hectares of leaf and cane turn very slowly toward the only sounds left in the bowl — a cough behind a shutter, a boot on a floorboard, a child crying in the Low Quarter — and lean, and reach, and grow. In the morning the crews go out with hooks and cut the town free of itself, and the sweepings are burned, and the chord is struck again, the same seven notes, inherited, unchanged, and everything green turns back to the engines and forgets you were ever there.

---

One more thing, and then the shift changes and the rain comes.

The discharge main leaves the seventh house and runs up the bowl's flank in a riveted line as thick as a man, and goes into the rock at the rim, and does not come out anywhere on the other side. The rock weeps. The weeping is warm, and it is salt, and the salt ledger — kept in a brass-bound book in the pump office since the second decade — balances. It has always balanced. Not approximately. To the decimal, every quarter, for two hundred and ninety years, in a bowl that sinks four millimeters a decade and has never once flooded past the mark.

Nobody down here thinks about it much. The water goes up and the water comes down and the note holds and the cane leans in. But the tuners, on their way out, pensioned and half-deaf and heading for somewhere quiet, tend to stop on the fourth tread from the top, where the dogs sit, and look back at the pale lid and the scattered islands and all that patient green bending toward the noise — and stand there a while, with their heads cocked, listening for something under the seven notes.

Then they go up. The stair is dished in the middle. It has been walked a long time.
