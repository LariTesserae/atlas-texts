The heat does not come from the sky. It comes up through the soles of the world, out of stone that has only just finished being stone, and it meets the air a millimeter above the ground and stops there. That millimeter is the only weather this place has. Below it, rock at sixty degrees. Above it, air at forty-four, saturated, motionless, and going nowhere. Everything else — the whole thirty kilometers of basin, from the black fields at the center to the rim cliffs that stand two hundred meters up and never come down — is the same temperature as everything else. There is no cool side of a trunk. There is no shade. Shade exists as a dimming but not as a relief; you can stand under nine layers of leaf and be exactly as hot as you were in the open, and just as wet, because the air already holds all the water it can hold and your sweat, if you had any, would simply lie on you and wait.

All the condensation in the world happens in that millimeter, and it happens continuously. The rock sweats. Water arrives at the surface as vapor through pores too fine to see, meets the fractionally cooler air, and beads. Every stone wears a skin of it. Every leaf is strung with it. The whole basin glistens like something pulled out of a mouth, and it drips, everywhere, always, at a rate that has not varied since before there was anything here to notice.

It is loud. Not loud the way a storm is loud — loud the way a river is a material. The floor of the sound is a drone somewhere near sixty cycles, produced by the wingbeats of the ceiling, and you feel it in the sternum before you understand it as noise. Over that lies a hiss, broadband and dry-sounding despite everything being soaked: ten trillion flat-backed scrapers rasping microbial film off wet rock with mouthparts like files. Over *that*, the drip, the tick, the small wet slap of bodies against leaves, and under all of it a slow periodic bellow from the pans, so regular you could set a heart to it. The chord has been held so long it has stopped being an event. The animals here do not listen to it. They listen for holes in it.

The light is brown and grainy and comes from no direction. There is a sun somewhere above the haze but it is only a brightening, a smear you cannot face because you cannot find it. Most of the dimness is made of animals: the ceiling is a permanent stratum of flying things forty meters up, three and four deep, and beneath them the mid-air mist-feeders hang in a slower suspension, and beneath *those* the canopy, and the light that reaches the ground has been passed through all of it, chewed, flickered at wingbeat frequency until the flicker averages into a steady granular gloom. Nothing casts an edged shadow. Distance is legible only by how thick the grain gets. Along the seams in the floor there are hairline threads of dull orange, magma-light, too faint to read by, never widening, never cooling, never going out.

Now the ground itself, which is the strangest thing here and the hardest to see.

It is new. All of it. Basalt with the glass still on it, edges that will open a hand, conchoidal fractures sharp enough to shave with, arrises like the corner of a bottle. Break any stone and the inside is identical to the outside — no rind, no discoloration, no softening, no patina of any kind. The cliffs have no talus. That is the detail that would stop a thinking person cold: two hundred meters of vertical fresh rock and not one fallen block at its foot, no scree, no fan, no creep. There is no dust in this basin. There is no sand, no silt, no clay, no mud. There is no soil.

Because nothing wears. The water is hot and clear and faintly syrupy with dissolved salt, and it is exactly full — it has taken all it can take and it will take no more, and it will not put down what it carries either. It runs in sheets and threads and films and it does not cut. Every channel here was born a channel. The gullies are construction seams, joints in the extruded floor, not wounds; the terraces were laid down as terraces. Water goes down them and arrives at the pans and goes back up through the millimeter and comes down again as drip, and in the whole circuit it does not move one grain of the world from one place to another. Nothing is carried. Nothing settles. Nothing accumulates.

So where does the forest stand?

On the animals. Between the outcrops, in every crack and hollow and low place, there is a mattress a hand-span deep of living and just-dead bodies — the crawl — moving in slow fixed circuits, molted shells turning over and over through it, being eaten and reissued. It is warm and it is wet and it is entirely protein, and it is the soil. Roots do not go into earth here; there is no earth. They go into the crawl, and they are not only roots. They are nets, and they close, and they digest. The great waxy-leaved things on the shelves, the funnel-leaved catchments, the black-green lattice of vine on vine on vine, nine stories of it stacked and near-touching — all of it is fed from below by the traffic of small bodies, and the flowers are hot to the touch, thermogenic, stinking of yeast and iron, because there is no light worth courting and no reason to be colorful. Everything here is the color of wet liver and old bottle glass. Nothing needs to be seen. Everything is already touching.

And the scrapers, which feed the roots, feed on the film. And the film — the pale slick on every wet stone, the true base of this entire world — eats the rock. It eats fresh basalt atom by atom, with the hot saturated water and the gases coming up through the pores, and it has been doing so since the first hour. The sun is decorative. This basin runs on geology.

Which should mean the stone is being consumed, and it is, and it does not thin, because the floor is being made at exactly the rate it is eaten. Not in eruptions. In arrival. The black fields well up along the seams a fraction of a millimeter a year, glassy and sharp and sterile, and within hours the aerial plankton has found them and the film has taken hold and the scrapers have arrived and the whole seven-story business is standing on ground that did not exist last season. And at the far end of the basin, in a district of soft dark stone that folds like cooling fat, the floor goes down. It goes down carrying its film and its crawl and its forest with it, and everything that lived there is replaced, upstream, by things identical to it.

The conveyor moves. The pattern does not. The shelf jungle stands in the same place forever and is made of entirely different trees every few centuries; the seep terraces hold their shape while the stone flows through them like water through a standing wave. Every arrow here has an equal arrow. The heat arriving from below equals the heat leaving above. The water going up equals the water coming down. The bodies born equal the bodies taken. It is not stillness. It is stillness made entirely out of motion, and it has no seasons, no storms, no wind, no night, no drought, no year.

There is always exactly one silence in the basin, and it walks.

Something long-legged and soft-footed moves through the crawl on cuticle like felt, a surface that eats sound rather than making it, and where it passes the scrapers stop rasping — not fleeing, there is nowhere to flee and no need, the arithmetic is settled — simply stopping, in a wave, a hush twenty meters across that travels at a slow walk and never dissolves and never doubles. From the rim you could track it as a moving hole in the roar, the way you'd track a cloud's shadow in a country that had clouds. Everything here is born listening for that hole. A predator is not a shape. It is a place where the noise has gone out.

Nothing dead lies around. A body on the rock is disassembled in eleven minutes, shells included, calcium being too expensive to leave lying about. So there are no bones, no middens, no drifts, no leaf litter, no peat, no fossils, no strata, no rings in any trunk. Nothing here records anything. There is no way to read an age off this place — no sediment to count, no weathering rind to measure, no growth band, no buried thing. Time exists here only as frequency: wingbeats, scraper generations, the interval between bellows in the pans. It does not exist as accumulation. The basin might be a year old. It might be older than the moon. It looks an hour old, and it has looked an hour old for as long as it has looked like anything, and it will go on looking an hour old until the seams close.

Stand at the rim, if you could stand it — the air is thick enough that flight is nearly free, which is why the sky is full, and heavy enough to carry the drone for thirty kilometers without thinning. Below, the black fields shine wet and new. The ember threads run orange through them. The shelf jungles stack their nine dark stories along the seep lines, beaded, dripping, steaming faintly in the millimeter. The pans hold their hot clear water and the wallowers lie in it, hairless, patient, their backs carrying gardens, bellowing on the beat. The crawl turns over. The ceiling roars. The hush goes walking somewhere out in the middle distance, and the grain of the light thickens with every kilometer until the far rim is only a texture, and there is no dry thing, no cool thing, no quiet thing, no empty thing anywhere in it.

Nothing here has ever been alone. The place cannot produce solitude; there is no volume left over to be alone in. Every cubic centimeter is occupied, and has always been occupied, by something warm and wet and busy that is about to be eaten and already replaced.
