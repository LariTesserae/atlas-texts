You hear the ground before you touch it.

The first fact is a pressure in the sternum, a low sustained tone somewhere under forty cycles, too deep for the ear to bother with, so the body takes it instead: the breastbone, the fillings of the teeth, the water in the eye. It comes up through the soles. It has been coming up through the soles for longer than anything here has had a name. Later you will learn to call it the mains hum, and to hear it as a note rather than a condition, and to know from its pitch which country you are standing in. For now it is simply the feeling of *here*, and the absence of it, which you will feel once, later, will be the worst moment of your life.

Then the rest arrives, all at once, the way a crowd arrives.

---

The plateau is warm and wet and enormous and black. Not night-black — there is no night here, because there is no day to be the other half of it. There is no moon, no star, no glow at the edge of anything. The darkness is total and has been total for so long that the word for *light* in the local speech is also the word for *rumour*. You will not see your hand. You will learn your hand instead: its heat, its smell, the small sound it makes moving through air this thick.

The air is thick. Warm as breath, wetter than breath, carrying spore and mist and the fine aerosol of a rain that never entirely stops. It moves against the face like something considerate. It smells of chalk and yeast, of hot iron, of the sweetish rot of milkwood, and under all of that, at the low places, the struck-match thread of sulfur coming up out of the rock.

Underfoot: not soil. A mat. A woven crust of root and hyphae and dead cane, sprung like a wet mattress, warm, giving perhaps a hand's width under weight and then firming. It is a floor only in the way a frozen pond is a floor. Beneath it the plateau is hollow — riddled, chambered, throated, a limestone sponge three kilometres deep, and every void down there is either full of moving water or full of moving air, and both of them are loud.

The rock itself is old and settled and has made its peace. This is a karst country: towers, fins, blind valleys, sinkholes, stone that has been eaten into the shapes that eating produces and then stopped changing because those shapes are correct. The towers stand in their thousands and their hundred-thousands, fluted vertically by ten thousand years of dissolution, round-shouldered, hollow in places, drilled through with windows. They are two hundred metres tall and they are all, in a sense, the same object repeated at different stages of being consumed.

They whistle. Of course they do. Air moves through them, and they have been tuned by the only tuner that never gets bored.

---

There is a breath.

Eleven hours, roughly, though it drifts, and the drift is itself a season. The heat body under the plateau warms the air in the deep galleries; the lid above cools what rises; the whole system inhales and exhales through the sinkholes, which the tenders call mouths, and which are not metaphors — they have lips of tufa, and a throat, and a wet sound at the back.

On the **Draw**, cool air pours down the mouths. Every whistle in the country drops in pitch, together, a slow glissando over about forty minutes, and the fog rivers reverse and run inward, and the bellow-toads begin, pit by pit, valley by valley, a staggered ignition of low pulsing that travels across the plateau at the speed of the pressure front. On the **Let**, everything rises again, and the mouths steam, and the spore-fog lifts off the mats in columns you can feel as a warm hand pushing up under your chin.

Children are told the plateau is asleep and this is the snoring. Adults are told nothing, because adults have stood at a mouth on the Let and felt the size of what is breathing.

Rain here does not fall so much as *arrive*. Warm, faintly oily, tasting of iron and cracked pepper. It is black. It leaves a grey varnish on skin that takes four days to wear off and a permanent lacquer on every up-facing surface in the world. Under-surfaces, meanwhile, grow stone: the dripping water is supersaturated, and it lays down tufa, millimetre by millimetre, on every eave and cable and outstretched branch.

So everything here is black on top and white underneath, slick above and gritty below, and a tender can tell the orientation of any object in the dark by running a thumb across it. This is the first thing they teach. It is also how they find the dead.

---

Growth is everywhere and it is not green and it does not want the sun.

The energy comes up, not down. Sulfide, methane, hydrogen, hot water, the reducing breath of the deep rock — the whole biota is built on oxidation of what the mouths exhale, and it is *lush*, in the way a wound is lush. Milkwood stands in pale colonial thickets over the vents, hollow tubes bundled like organ pipes, warm to the touch, bleeding a sweet latex when broken that ferments within minutes and draws a haze of small flying things. Gut-fern sheets the shaded flanks. Gill-towers rise eight metres, fungal, ribbed, shedding spore in slow warm pulses you can taste as pepper at the back of the tongue. Root-curtains hang down into the mouths themselves, sixty metres of woven fibre farming the updraft, combing sulfur out of the wind, humming.

And the flowers are loud.

Colour is useless here; it was abandoned so long ago that nothing retains even the machinery for it. What replaced it is resonance. Bell-cane grows hollow stalks with pods at the nodes, and each species has a bore and a wall-thickness and therefore a voice, and the flowering heads are resonators tuned to the frequency band of the moth that pollinates them. A meadow of bell-cane in bloom is a chord held for nine days. Some are consonant. Some are deliberately not: the sour ones advertise nectar to a particular gauge-moth and are unlistenable to everything else, which is the point. There is a deceptive orchid, stemless, that sits in the litter and produces a perfect imitation of a female moth's wing-thrum, and the males come, and the orchid does not feed them, and the males keep coming, because the orchid has been better at being a moth than moths have, for a very long time.

Against all this: hush-moss. Grey, damp, felted, acoustic deadener, growing in patches on the lee of towers. Step into a hush-moss patch and the world drops away as if a door shut. Tenders roof their sleeping sheds with it. They also line their prisons with it, and the punishment is called *going quiet*, and men have come out of nine days of it unable to speak in anything but a shout.

Because a hole in the sound is not safety. A hole in the sound is a shape.

---

The animals are blind, abundant, and stacked in layers like freight.

Clappers fill the air column: palm-sized fliers with a hard palatal click, in swarms dense enough that their echolocation merges into a continuous sleet-on-tin hiss, and the hiss has grain and current in it, and you can hear a predator move through it the way you can hear a hand move through rice. Below them, gauge-moths, fat and furred, navigating by frequency, pollinating by accident and by deceit. Below them, the litter is a boiling thing — drum-shrimp, springtails the size of thumbs, the pale armoured grazers that rasp the mats and are themselves rasped.

In the water — and water is everywhere, ankle-deep in the doline floors, kilometre-deep in the galleries, running under the mat with a sound like a crowd in another room — sump-eels hunt by the footfall of things walking above them, and strike up through the crust, and take a leg, and are gone. Falling is the principal cause of death here. There is an entire guild evolved to exploit it: fall-catchers, colonial net-organisms strung across the throats of sinkholes, sixty metres across, sticky, patient, faintly luminous in a way that means nothing to anyone. Tenders farm them. Tenders also feed them, on occasion, in the old way, which is not spoken about directly but which has a verb of its own.

And there are the hushers.

Nobody has ever seen one, which is not a statement about rarity. They are known by their fur, which is a metamaterial — a graded fleece that eats sound the way soot eats light — and by their behaviour, which is to walk slowly toward you inside a moving bubble of silence. What you notice is that the clapper-sleet has developed a notch. A quiet moving through the loud. It has a diameter of about four metres and it is coming closer at walking pace, and the correct response is to make an enormous noise immediately, and the incorrect response is to hold still and hope, which is what the body wants to do, which is why the body is not always consulted.

They cultivate hush-moss. They plant it on their approach routes. There are hillsides in the eastern keys where the moss grows in lines.

Once or twice a season, over the top of everything, come the far drums: a slow quadruple impact, felt more than heard, spaced nine seconds apart, receding over about an hour. Something walks the plateau on stilt legs, striding from tower to tower, tall enough to step over the treeline, and it has never been located, and the tenders keep their calendar by it.

---

Now the machines, which are not an intrusion here, and are not new.

The industry is dispersed rather than assembled. There are no factories in the sense of buildings; there is apparatus, laid into the country like a nervous system, and most of it is below. In the mouths sit the scrubber-throats, finned columns of alloy forty metres across, going down out of reach. Along the valleys run the combs — heat-exchanger plates, hectares of them, warm as a flank, colonised so thoroughly by milkwood and tube-worm that the exchange surfaces are now partly biological and the engineers who would object are ten thousand years dead. Cables span between towers, sagging, sheathed in tufa until they are thick as a thigh and look like everything else. Every few hundred kilometres, a pump-house: one room, one door, a hum.

They are worn to karst. That is the thing that takes longest to understand. The apparatus has been here long enough to have acquired the same silhouette as the geology — rounded, fluted, dripping, dissolved on the windward face and accreted on the lee. A stack is a stack. Some are limestone and some are chimney and the way you tell is to strike it and listen to how long it rings. There are towers with a machine still running inside them, entirely entombed, doing its work through a metre of stone, and the stone conducts the vibration, and the tower sings the machine's note, and has for centuries, and no one knows what the machine is for.

The work is this: the deep breath comes up hot and loaded, and the apparatus scrubs something out of it, and sends the something *down* — down the Downpipe, which is a shaft of unremarkable diameter somewhere in the central keys, and which accepts. The tailings are the black. The opacity that roofs this continent is exhaust. The lid — a permanent inversion ceiling nine kilometres up, dense, warm-bottomed, cold-topped, holding a weather system that has never once dispersed — is industrial smoke that stopped being smoke and became climate. The plateau made its own night. It has been running the process so long that the night now has species endemic to it, and turning the machines off would be an extinction event of a size that has no word.

Nobody knows who buys the product. The Downpipe accepts. Perhaps twice in a generation it returns something: a bolt of a metal that does not corrode, a folded sheet of something like paper, once a tooth. These are kept in the shrines, which are small, and scattered, and consist usually of a niche cut in a tower and a stone lip black on top and white beneath.

The maintenance is hereditary and mostly liturgical. Tenders chip the creeping stone off the moving parts, which is Sisyphus with a mallet, and re-tune the cairns, and grease what can be reached. And they diagnose by bird. There is a small brown animal, not a bird, that nests only in bearing housings and sings a faithful imitation of the bearing tone, because that tone is the safest sound in the world to hide inside. When a bearing wears, its tone drifts, and within a season the local population's song has drifted with it. A tender walking a hundred kilometres of cable can hear a failing race three valleys away, in a bird, and go to it, and open a housing that has not been opened since her grandmother's grandmother, and find the fault, and fix it, and never once look at anything, because there is nothing to look at.

---

The people are few: one hut per fifty kilometres, a day and a night apart, except there is no day and no night, so: one hut per two Draws. They speak a tonal language pitched into the notches between the machine bands, and their names are chords, and you cannot call someone from far away in a country whose tonic has changed.

Because the plateau is keyed. The mains hum is not one note across its whole width; it varies by region, by the depth of the heat body underneath, by the age of the local apparatus. Sixty kilometres is roughly the acoustic horizon — the range at which your home tonic falls under the floor of hearing — and beyond it the world is in another key. Travellers carry a set of tuned clinker stones and re-learn their songs at each border. Animals do not. A clapper swarm from the western keys will not recognise your click-stick, and will treat you as a hole in the world, and holes in the world get investigated.

The deaf tenders are the best of them. They read the ground with palms and knees and the long bones of the forearm, and they are not deafened by the loud, and they can put a hand on a comb and tell you which plate will fail in a year. They have a whole tactile dialect, hand-to-stone, and it is older than the spoken one, and it is the only language that works inside a hush.

Their dead are buried under hush-moss, deliberately, so that nothing finds them by listening.

---

Small things, scattered, in all that volume: a valve wheel two metres across worn to a mirror by generations of palms. A ladder bolted to a tower that dissolved out from under it, ending in air, still bolted. A rail line swallowed by tufa for four hundred kilometres, a low white ridge you can walk along, which is the only straight thing in the country. A sign, unreadable, in a script that runs left to right, which nobody does anymore. A shed. A shed. A shed.

And the far edge, where the plateau breaks off in an escarpment eight kilometres high and the ink-rain pours over into a lowland nobody has ever entered. There is an updraft there that comes up over the lip carrying a smell no one can name — thin, sharp, green in a way that has no referent, and salt. Sometimes, at the edge, there is a sound like a sky clearing its throat, a dry crack with no water in it, and the hair rises on the arms, and the taste of a struck nail comes into the mouth. They call it the sky-cough. It happens above the lid. It is the only evidence anyone here has that there is an above.

---

Stand still long enough and the country assembles itself around you as sound: the mains hum under everything, the toad-pulse, the sleet of clappers, the eleven-hour glissando of a thousand fluted towers, water in six registers, the bell-cane holding its nine-day chord, rain arriving. It is not chaos. It is partitioned. Every living thing has a band, and the machines own their bands too, and the whole spectrum is occupied the way a reef is occupied — no gap unclaimed, no silence unowned, ten thousand years of negotiation compressed into a wall of noise that never stops and never repeats.

Which is how you know, before you know anything else, that you have already been located.

Somewhere to your left, at about four metres' diameter, the sleet has begun to thin.
