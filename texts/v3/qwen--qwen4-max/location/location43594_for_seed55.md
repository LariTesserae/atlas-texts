## The Long Water

Begin in the middle of it, where there is no floor yet and no surface either, only the cold pressing evenly on all sides like a hand that has forgotten what it was holding.

The water is two degrees, sometimes one. It does not freeze; there is enough salt in it, and the salt is a recipe, and someone keeps it. It is very clear. In the good decades you can see a hundred meters, which is to say you can see nothing, because there is nothing at a hundred meters that carries light. What you see instead is the fall: flakes and blades and long shredded ribbons of plant matter descending at the speed of a settling thought, turning as they come, taking three days to cross the height of a room. It has been falling since before there were names for it. It will fall all week. It falls all century. When it thickens, the water goes dim and green-black and the tenders call it a season, and when it thins again they call that a season too, and both seasons together are eleven years long.

Cold makes everything slow and nothing small. That is the first rule here. A blade of the pale weed puts on a finger's width in a decade and lives nine thousand years. Rot works at a crawl, so what dies does not vanish; it lies down where it fell and stays, and the layers build. The lushness of this place is not the lushness of a hot swamp, which is a frenzy. It is the lushness of a warehouse. Nothing is growing fast. There is simply so much of it, stacked so long, that the water between the fronds is a minority of the volume.

## What the Floor Is

Descend for an hour and the fall thickens and the dark acquires a grain, and then there is a bottom under your hands, and it feels like a seabed: a hard, gently undulating plain, felted with silt, sweeping away in low swells that a current has been polishing since the world was young.

It is not a seabed. Run your palm along it and you will find, under the felt, a regularity that no water makes. The swells are the crowns of buried arches. The faint parallel ridges that run for a league and stop are the tops of courses, three bricks down. The plain is a pavement, and beneath the pavement is more pavement, and beneath that, as far as anyone has ever cut, more work.

The sand is ground tile. The silt is powdered mortar and the dust of abraded brick, mixed with the fine black flour of plant matter that never finished rotting. Pick up a pebble and it will be a rounded corner of something, a chip off a sill or a worn-down lump of slag, and if you crack it you may find the flat face of a fired brick inside, still crisp, still bearing the drag-marks of the wire that cut it from the clay.

Everything here is made. There is no native stone. There is no bedrock that was not once a course of masonry, no clay that was not once puddled by foot, no grain of the beach that was not part of a wall. Dig anywhere and you strike work. The world was built down as far as it was built up, and nobody now knows how far that was, and the not-knowing is ordinary, the way not knowing the bottom of a lake is ordinary.

## Its Final Shape

It was finished a very long time ago. That is the second rule, and it explains everything the eye takes for geology.

There is no high ground. There is no gradient left worth the name; the whole basin lies within a few dozen meters of one level, and the water above it has almost nothing to fall down. Ages ago the great vaults came down, bay by bay, a collapse so slow that each fall was a local event, a bad century, a thing remembered by four generations and then not. The rubble was sorted by the water and rolled and rolled and rolled. The piers that stood were worn to stumps. The stumps were worn to swells. The swells are wearing to a plain.

This is a world at base level. Erosion has done its work and has nothing left to do; it has arrived at the shape it was always going to arrive at, and now it only maintains that shape, the way a man at the end of his life maintains his breathing. Every exposed surface has been rounded to roughly the same soft radius. Every corner in the world is gone.

The only sharp edges left are the ones inside. Where a joint has opened — and joints open, a hair a century, as the mortar gives up its lime — you can put a lamp into the crack and see the untouched face of the original work: the chisel strokes still parallel, the mortar still holding the print of a thumb, a tally scratched by someone who was counting bricks and got to two hundred and eleven. The world keeps its own youth in its cracks. Nothing else keeps anything.

## The Gardens in the Dark

There is no light here to speak of, and it is nonetheless green — or it would be green, if there were light to prove it.

Two economies feed the lushness. The first is the fall. Far off at the rim, where the made terraces climb to within a few meters of the surface and a gray, cold, ordinary daylight comes down through the water, there are forests. They are the only photosynthesizing thing of any size, and they are enormous, and they shed. Their cast blades ride the slow inward drift for years, sinking a little all the way, and arrive on the dark floor as a continuous soft rain of carbon. The interior lives on the rim's leftovers, and has done so forever, and is richer for it than the rim is.

The second economy comes up out of the pavement. Along certain lines — never random, always following the joints of some buried plan — the seams weep. The water that comes out of them is colder than the sea and heavier and tastes of iron and sulfur, and it moves along the floor as an invisible river a meter deep, hugging the ground, going somewhere. Where it emerges, the mats grow: thick spongy felts, rust-orange and cream and dirty white, that eat the minerals in the water and ask nothing of the sun. They are the only thing here that could be called wild in behavior, and they are not wild either; they were planted, in rows, and the rows are still detectable under ten thousand years of overgrowth.

On the mats and on the fall grow the pale blades, the rope-weed, the felt, the great slow timber-stipes with stems as dense as horn. Layer on layer: the black bedding of ancient peat below, then the living mats, then the thicket, then the canopy of stipes with their crowns interlocking, then the drift of falling matter above that, and the water threaded through all of it in channels no wider than a wrist. In places the growth is so packed that a swimmer cannot pass and must cut, and the cut closes behind her in three hundred years.

Nothing eats any of it. That is the third rule, and the strangest, though it looks like nothing at all.

There are no animals. No fish, no worm, no grazer, no swimmer, no burrower, no eye. Nothing here has a mouth that moves. The silence of the place is not an absence of noise; it is an absence of appetite. There is no herbivory, so the gardens are limited only by food and light and cold, and they have grown to fill both. There is no bioturbation, so the peat lies in undisturbed laminae, one sheet per fall-season, readable like a ledger. There is no grazing, and therefore no defense: nothing here has a spine, a toxin, a shell, a sting. Every plant is soft. You can push your hand through a thousand years of growth without resistance.

Pollination is by water, and by hand. Several of the cultigens have not set seed unaided in an age; their flowers open underwater and wait, and if no one comes with a brush of rope-fiber they close and fall. There is a queue of extinctions here, sorted by how long each species can wait, and everyone who works the seams knows roughly where in the queue things stand, and nobody finds this remarkable. It is simply the order in which the world will get quieter.

## Weather

It has its own weather, though none of it involves the sky.

The brine rivers are the wind. They come out of the seams cold and heavy and run downhill along the floor at a walking pace, and where two meet they lift and roll and make a front, and along the front the water clouds with stirred silt and the mats bloom in a long ragged line. A front takes a season to cross a province.

The silt storms are the rain: a turbid mass, denser than the water around it, sliding down some imperceptible slope with a leading edge like a low hill. You hear one before you see it, and you hear it in your chest rather than your ears — a pressure, a grinding, too low to be a sound. It arrives, and the world goes from black to a brown so total it has texture, and it takes a month to pass, and afterward everything is buried a finger deep in fresh flour and the mats begin again.

And there are the breaths. Where the peat is thickest and oldest, gas gathers under the buried arches until the pressure finds a joint, and then a hill of bubbles comes up out of the floor over the course of a week, lifting silt, dimming a hundred leagues of water for a decade. The tenders know the burping grounds and do not build there.

The tide is a few centimeters, once a day, and it is not the moon. It is the world breathing: gas made and gas released, water cooling and contracting, the whole basin swelling and settling by the width of a hand. You can watch it on the mark-stones at the rim, which have been cut and re-cut downward for so long that the oldest marks are underwater and unreadable.

## Quiet, and What Carries

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet is enormous, because cold water carries sound four times faster than air and does not tire of it.

A chisel dropped on the pavement at one terrace is heard at the next as a single clean tick, and at the one beyond as a soft knock, and by then it is a rumor. There is the pop of a bubble at a seam. There is the creak of a stipe as thick as a mast taking the current. There is the hiss of the fall, which is always there and which you stop hearing in your first year and hear again, unbearably, in your thirtieth. In the dry rooms there is dripping.

The tenders knock. Every pier stump has a struck face, worn shiny, and there is a code — three and a pause and two means a seam has changed its taste; a long roll means a fall of masonry; a single stroke repeated at intervals means a person is alive down here and would like to know if anyone else is. The messages travel faster than anyone can swim, which means a question can cross a province in an hour and the answer may take a season, and this is understood to be the normal condition of conversation.

For light there are the lamp-lines: grooves cut along every handrail and doorjamb and stair-nose, packed with a paste of bacteria fed on weed-sugar, glowing a faint blue-green that shows you the shape of your hand and not its color. They are kept up the way one keeps up a path. They are dim, and they are enough, and eyes here have gotten large.

## The Dry Rooms

Under the surviving vaults, and in the crowns of the buried arches, gas has collected. The world is roofed in pockets. Some pockets are sour — methane out of the peat, which will burn and will not sustain a life. Some are sweet, filled over centuries with the oxygen of the rim gardens, risen and caught and held. The tenders can tell them apart by the smell of the water at the lip, and by whether the lamp-paste near the entrance is bright or dull, and once in a while they are wrong.

In a sweet bell at eight meters, a man stands with his feet in the sea and his shoulders in cold air, and this is where the metal is made.

There is no ore in the ground, because there is no ground. There is ore in the world: the makers used their own slag for aggregate, and the pavement is streaked with copper, and the rust-mats at the iron seams precipitate a soft red mud that can be gathered by the basket and dried and reduced. Charcoal comes from peat, pressed and cooked in a lidded pot. The furnace is a shaft of refractory clay the height of a child, and the fire in it is the width of a hand, because air is expensive: every breath the forge takes has been carried up in an inverted jar by someone whose whole working life is the carrying of jars. Two people spend a season breathing for a room so that a third can spend an afternoon making one chisel.

The chisel is iron, soft, tempered by patient hammering. The adzes are copper. The rasps are fired ceramic; the abrasives are ground brick; the handles are stipe-wood, cut from a stem that was a sapling when the vault came down; the lines and baskets and brushes are rope-weed, retted in the peat. There is nothing here that a hand cannot make, and nothing here that a hand did make, including the horizon.

## The Hands

There are almost no people. In a province the size of a country there may be forty, and a tender can work a full life on a seam and meet three others.

They are cold-adapted and slow-hearted, big-chested from a childhood of breath-hold, thick through the middle, unhurried in a way that reads as calm and is mostly arithmetic. They go down on a weighted stone with a line and a basket, work four minutes, come up, sit in a bell, and go down again. They cut the thicket back from the seam-mouths. They brush the flowers. They repack the lamp-lines. They clear the brine channels of drifted weed so the front will run where it has always run. They re-cut the mark-stones. They fire tablets and scratch tallies on them and stack them in a dry vault where a hundred thousand tablets already lie, the oldest in a script no one has read for six thousand years, all of them, as far as anyone can tell, recording maintenance.

None of the work changes anything. That is not despair; it is the specification. The world was finished before their line began, and it has since worn to the shape it intends to keep, and the whole purpose of every hand here is to hold a completed thing at its completion for one more generation. The sea rises a thumb each century. The floor deepens. The bells shrink as the gas dissolves. One bay of vault falls somewhere every thousand years, and the sound of it goes around the world in an afternoon, and everyone stops and listens, and then goes back down.

In ten thousand years it will be the same, only flatter, and darker by a degree so small no one will name it, and quieter by three or four species, and there will be someone at the seam-mouth with a copper adze, cutting back the pale blades, keeping the channel open, in water that has been the same temperature since the beginning.
