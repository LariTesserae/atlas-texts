# The Sill

## I. Warm ground, thin air

The floor of the bowl lies at three thousand seven hundred meters, and the air up here is thin enough that a newcomer's breath comes short and their headaches bloom behind the eyes — and it is forty-four degrees in the shade, where there is shade.

The heat does not come from the sun, though the sun is merciless. It comes from underneath. Somewhere below the travertine there is a body of rock still cooling from an event nobody recorded, and it has been warming the floor of this basin for longer than anything here has had a name. You feel it through your soles first, before you feel it on your face: the ground is blood-warm, and it never stops being blood-warm, and at night when the stars come out hard and white the stone under your bedroll is still gently, patiently hot.

Snow falls on the rim peaks. It does not arrive. It melts in the descent and comes down as warm rain, and the rain is the least of the water.

Water is everywhere and almost never falling. The floor is a shallow sheet, an inch deep across whole square kilometers, moving so slowly you have to watch a fleck of scum for a minute to be sure. It wells up out of vents and out of the pores of the rock itself; the cliff faces weep from a thousand small mouths, each mouth building its own lip of pale stone, so the escarpment looks like a wall of melted candles. Steam stands in the low places. The air holds so much of it that the rim mountains dissolve into white at midday and reassemble at evening. Everything beads. Iron sweats. Your own skin does not dry, and this is the thing that kills the unprepared: not the heat, but the failure of sweat to leave.

## II. No green has ever happened here

There is no leaf. No seed, no wood, no bark, no pollen in the ordinary sense, no root, no fruit, nothing that has ever unfolded toward light. The films are the bottom of the world: bacterial felts a millimeter thick, growing where hot brine meets air, feeding on sulfide and iron and the ammoniac seep of the works. They come in cream, in dust-pink, in a purple so dark it reads black, in sulfur yellow that stinks when crushed. Under a hand lens they are beautiful — felted, layered, faintly iridescent, like the inside of a shell. From a distance they are a stain on wet stone.

Above the films: everything else. The Sill is not empty of life. It is *crowded*, in its spaced and patient way, and every animal in it stands on a foundation of slime and mineral breath.

Wood is a treasure here. A tool handle of imported ash, brought up the rim road four generations ago, is kept oiled and wrapped in gut braid and inherited by name. There is no fuel and no need of it. Nothing in this basin has ever burned to make power. This is the first thing to understand about the machines.

## III. The works

From the rim stairs you can see the whole of it at once, which is the only way to understand the scale: a floor thirty kilometers across, white as a page, and standing out of the white, at intervals of four hundred paces, the towers.

Nine thousand and some. Districts numbered to sixty-one; forty-four still answer their mirrors. Each tower is a pale fluted column twelve to twenty meters high, wider at the base, wrapped in a lattice of fins, and every one of them is thicker than the day it was raised, because the water they harvest is heavy with dissolved stone and gives some of it back to every surface it touches. The works are growing their own geology. The maintenance crews do not clean the towers. They *quarry* them, cutting the accretion back to the drawings each season, and the cut stone goes down the chutes to be sorted, and the sorted stone goes to build the next tower, and this has been going on long enough that the distinction between what was built and what precipitated is a matter for specialists.

They are refrigerators.

That is the whole of the industry. Hot brine rises up the insulated spine of each tower and drives an absorption cycle — ammonia and water, salt and heat, a bubble-lift that raises fluid by boiling it, a cascade of vessels that fall in temperature stage by stage until the outer fins sit fifteen degrees below the air. There are no pistons. There is no combustion, no turbine, no compressor, nothing that reciprocates or roars. The only moving parts are floats, and check valves, and the slow meniscus climbing a wick. The heat of the ground is spent to make cold, and the cold is spent to wring water out of the sky, and the water runs down the flutes into the covered channels, and the channels run to the sump, and the sump feeds the terraces and the salt pans and the sixty-one districts and the long covered main that goes up the rim and out of this place entirely, to somewhere that has never been described to me.

The fins are the coldest solid objects in the world you can reach on foot. In a basin where nothing is ever cool — where the rock is warm at midnight and the rain is warm and the wind is warm — the towers make a cold that has no natural analogue, and every living thing on the Sill knows it.

## IV. The shade-line

At noon the sun comes through the thin air with almost nothing subtracted. Ultraviolet at this altitude scours exposed surfaces sterile; the films die in the open, and the animals that graze them die a little slower.

So the ecology is an archipelago of shadows.

Each tower throws a hard blue wedge of shade, and inside that wedge is the whole habitable world: cool stone, living film, moisture that does not flash off. The grazers — hand-sized, calcite-shelled, rasping mouths of silica that leave combed arcs in the felt — travel around their tower with the sun, a circular migration completed once a day, a herd moving like the hand of a clock. They cannot cross the glare between towers. Most of them die in the attempt, and the crossing distances are therefore the most important fact in the natural history of this place. Where the spacing is four hundred paces, there is a viable population, and gene flow, and a food web. Where a district fell and its towers were quarried away, the gaps widened past what any grazer can survive, and the whole lineage on that island went out like a lamp. There are eleven such dead crossings on the floor. You can see them from the rim as blanks.

The predators are the ones who solved the glare. The stilter is a wader two meters at the hip, body held high above the sheet water on legs like glass rods, mirror-scaled along the dorsal ridge so that the sun that cooks a grazer slides off it. It stands in the open in full noon, motionless, and waits for something to be forced to move. The chaff-hawks work the other half of the problem: they eat the aerial plankton, the spore-chaff and drifting film-fragments that ride the thermals all day, and they never land. They sleep on the wing. A chaff-hawk that settles on a cold fin to rest is a chaff-hawk that will be a shape in the stone by the following season.

And there are the bore-worms, which matter more than anything else with a face.

The channels of the works carry water so laden with carbonate that they would close themselves in a decade. They do not close, because the bore-worms live in the accretion and eat it, dissolving their way through the deposit in slow braided tunnels, keeping the arteries patent. Nobody engineered this. It was noticed, and then it was protected, and now the keepers' central labor — the thing they actually do, most days — is husbandry of a worm. There is a flat beetle that eats the worms. There is a long-tailed thing like a pale mongoose that eats the beetle. The keepers trap the mongoose and tolerate the beetle at low numbers and post tallies of worm density on slate boards at every district head, and a bad worm year is a famine year, and everybody understands this without needing it explained.

## V. Quiet is a yield

Sound sheds water.

A fin working at dew point holds a film of condensate a fraction of a millimeter thick, and that film has to travel down the flute to the channel to count. Vibration breaks it into droplets, and droplets fall off the edge, and what falls off the edge is lost to the sheet and the sun. A shout at the base of a tower costs about a liter. A dropped wrench costs three. A hailstorm costs a district's whole morning.

So the works are silent, and the silence is not atmosphere, it is *engineering*, and the culture that grew inside the engineering has made it into something closer to religion. The walkways are laid on felt pads. The keepers wear soft soles and walk without heel-strike, a gliding step children learn before they learn to speak, and they speak very little anyway. Districts talk to districts by heliograph — mirror flashes off the tower crowns, all day long, a soundless chatter of light across thirty kilometers of glare, and at night by shuttered lamp. Hand-sign in the close work. Slate and grease-pencil for anything that matters. Tools are set down, never dropped; there is a word for a man who drops things and it is not a kind word.

Standing at the foot of Tower 44-118 at midday you hear: your own pulse. The tick of a fin expanding, once every few minutes. Water arriving in a covered channel, a sound like a hand smoothing cloth. Somewhere out on the terrace, a grazer's radula, which you cannot hear at all unless you put your ear against the stone, and then it is the loudest thing in the world.

The ammonia you smell rather than hear — a thin sharp thread at the back of the nose near any tower, the working fluid weeping from a joint. The films love it. It is nitrogen in a world with no legumes, no roots, no way to fix anything from the air. The leaks feed the felts; the felts feed the grazers; the grazers feed the stilters; the dead go to the sump and become salt and stone; the salt goes back into the absorbers. The loop closed a long time ago. Nobody planned it and nobody can now open it.

## VI. The wet hour

Late afternoon, the lid drops. The vapor that has been standing all day in a white column above the bowl settles as the light goes long, and the humidity at ground level climbs until the air feels like a held breath. Then the storms: short, vertical, violent, hail that ticks on the stone for ninety seconds and floods the terraces and is gone. The sheet water rises an inch and falls an inch. The salt-fleas hatch out of the pans in numbers that turn the water pink from horizon to horizon, and every wader on the floor comes to them, and for two days the whole basin is fat and stupid with abundance.

The real harvest is before dawn. The sky at this altitude is a black window onto space, and the tower crowns, thermally broken from the warm ground on their pumice feet, radiate their heat straight out of the world. They go cold — colder than any natural thing here has ever been — and the wet air comes against them and gives up.

That is the wet hour. Nine thousand towers running at once, each one wrapped in its own private fog, each fog collapsing into the flutes, the whole floor murmuring at the very bottom edge of hearing. The chaff-hawks come down through the fog to drink on the wing. The grazers crowd the cold bases in a ring, shoulder to shoulder, in the only coolness that exists. The stilters stand at the ring's edge and take them. The keepers walk their rounds with lamps, reading float gauges, tapping nothing, saying nothing, watching the water go by in the covered channels and counting it.

## VII. The standing

Everything here is being turned to stone at a rate of about two millimeters a year, and the only defense against it is motion.

Work your joints. Keep the valve turning. Walk. An animal that sickens and lies down on the terrace is a shape in the travertine within a season, and then a lump, and then a feature. The terrace edges are full of them: the combed arcs of a grazer's last morning, the splayed rods of a stilter that stopped, a whole drift of chaff-hawks that came down in a hailstorm and did not get up. The tall cranes move among them all day, three meters high, prying and levering with their beaks, eating what the stone has not yet claimed. They are undertakers and they are also the reason the terraces are not simply a museum.

There are people among the standing too. Not many. They are left where they fell, at the district edge, facing the towers, and the keepers cut the accretion around them but never off them, and once a year someone brings a slate with a name on it and props it against the stone.

From the rim, at the hour when the mirage lifts the whole floor and floats it, the towers and the standing and the terraces and the moving blue wedges of shade all hang together in the white air, upside down and right side up, and it is impossible to say which parts were built and which precipitated and which grew.

Elsewhere, life gathers around fires. Here it gathers around the cold, in a ring, at the foot of a machine that has been quietly making water out of the sky since before anyone alive was named — and the sound it makes is the sound of a hand smoothing cloth, thirty kilometers wide, and you have to stand very still to hear it, and standing still is the one thing you must not do for long.
