## The Sill

At three thousand two hundred meters there is a lip of pale stone where the weather stops being above you and starts being underneath. The storm that owns this plateau is two hundred meters thick and never lifts; from the rim you look *down* into its gray back, and the water pouring off the edge in sheets a kilometer wide goes into that cloud and is never seen to land. It doesn't land. It unravels on the way, becomes mist, becomes weather, comes back up the flank as rain, and is delivered again to the basin it just left. The whole arrangement is a loop with a very long throat.

Behind the rim, the hills are rounded to the shape of dropped loaves. They are not hills. They are spoil — the tailings of the work, stacked and worn and stacked again, and they are made of the same milk-glass as everything else. There is no bedrock here. There was, once, an ordinary mountain of ordinary rock, and it was eaten and replaced so many times that its existence is now a rumor supported only by the chemistry of the springs.

Ahead, the basin: ninety kilometers across, flooded, open to the horizon in every direction, and entirely constructed.

## What the stone is

Pick up any piece of it. It is silica, laid down in laminae thinner than a fingernail, cloudy white at the surface and faintly blue in bulk, the color of skimmed milk held to a window. At a broken edge it is translucent. It is cold in a way that goes past the skin and into the small joints of the hand.

Turn it over and you will find the mark: a shallow radial groove, ridged, about the size of a thumbprint, the impression of a feeding crown. Every stone in the basin has one. Every stone in the hills behind you has one. The boulders in the outflow channels, the gravel, the flour suspended in the water and dimming the light — all of it has been through a mouth. There is no natural substrate anywhere within a day's travel. There is nothing here that was not, at some point, an animal's decision, if a thing with no head can be said to decide.

And yet there are no tools. Nothing here has ever held anything. No lever, no cord, no fire, no plan, no drawing, no memory of a drawing. The construction is entirely secretory and entirely local: a hundred billion small bodies each doing one simple thing in one small cup, and the result is a hundred kilometers of architecture.

## The final form

Here is the strangeness, and it takes a while to see, because it looks at first like nothing at all.

There are no sharp edges. Not on the old work, not on the new. A wall that slumped last week and was relaid yesterday comes into the world *already rounded*, already worn to the exact profile that a billion years of water would have given it. The masons do not build shapes and then let the water finish them. They build the finished shape directly. They lay down the end-state.

So the terraces are low and soft-shouldered, the domes are worn molars, the channels are fluted and undercut in the specific way that flowing water likes, and the great radial gutters converge on the Nine Throats with the patience of something that has already solved the problem. From the rim, the whole basin reads as erosion — as a landscape ground down to its last stable configuration, near-flattened, argued out with time. It is not erosion. It is construction that has converged on erosion's answer and now simply repeats it, forever, at speed.

The shape has not changed in eleven million years. The average atom of the wall in front of you has been wall for about six weeks.

## Emptiness, and how much lives in it

Nine-tenths of the work is hole.

Hold a fragment up: it is a foam, a lung, a sponge of galleries and baffles and blind cups, more void than substance, and this is why the water can move through the entire plateau as easily as it moves over it. It is also why the place is quiet. The geometry that suits the animals happens to be an almost perfect sound trap. Noise enters the Combs and goes into a hundred thousand rounded corridors and does not come out.

The stone is riddled with life and the water above it looks deserted. A cup every arm's length along a good terrace, every ten paces along a poor one; between them, long clean stretches of nothing. You can hang in the middle of a square kilometer of this and see four animals. There are more mouths in the basin than there are grains of sand in a desert, and the impression you will carry away is of a vacant, swept, echoing place with a few things in it, widely spaced, each alone.

Both are true. Abundance here is measured in tonnage, not in crowding.

The **masons** are sessile: a folded gray purse the size of a palm, ringed with a crown of tubes, no eyes, no head, no direction. Each sits in one rounded cup for forty years and never leaves it. Its ancestors are the walls around it — literally; the dead are eaten and dissolved and laid back down as corridor. You are surrounded by the ancestors in the form of architecture.

The **carriers** are free-swimming commas of glass with a dark gut and a sac of supersaturated silica. They come in loose scattered streams, thousands per kilometer of channel and never a crowd, and when they void their load onto a laying front they spark — a single cold blue point, gone. A working front at dusk looks like a constellation being slowly written and erased.

The **borers** are ribbons of muscle with an acid gland, and they take the old work apart at two millimeters a year. They are the reason nothing is permanent and everything is finished. The **grazers** are limpet-shaped and lick the film in the seams, leaving tracks like a thumb drawn through dust; the tracks fade in a week and are the only writing in the world.

Once or twice an hour a **kite** goes over: umbrella-sized, translucent, passive, riding the surge with its fringe trailing, the largest thing you will see whole. And somewhere below, one per hectare, a three-meter hunter moves along the galleries at the speed of a minute hand, keeping the cups sparse.

## The film

There are no plants. There is no green anywhere, no leaf, no seed, no spore that wants light. Nothing here has ever turned toward the sun; the sun is a rumor two hundred meters up, a brighter smear in an overcast that has no direction and casts no shadows, only thickenings.

The only living skin is a chemolithotrophic nap in the seep-lines, gray-yellow, sulfurous, thin as breath on a knife. It is the single warm color for a hundred kilometers and it smells, faintly, of struck matches. Everything eats it, or eats what eats it, or eats the stone, which is the same thing three steps removed. The food chain does not descend from the sky. It comes up from below, dissolved, in the artesian water that keeps this basin full at an altitude where no basin has any business being full.

## The weather inside the water

The stone is finished. The water is not.

All the chaos in this place is liquid. The storm above hammers the surface into a gray ceiling you can see from below and cannot hear at all. The basin sloshes — a standing wave with a forty-minute period and a four-meter amplitude, the whole ninety kilometers of it tilting slowly one way and then the other, so that the deep terraces surge and slacken and surge, and the shallow Milk Flats go from waist-deep to dry and back within an hour. When a dome emerges, a rind of ice forms on it in the thin screaming air, and when it goes under, the rind dissolves with a sound like salt poured into a glass.

The water is one point four degrees and salty enough to refuse freezing. Inside it there is weather: brine rivers with visible refractive edges, rolling density fronts, silt storms that reduce the world to arm's length for six hours and then clear as if a decision were made. Warm pockets well up from the throats and everything they touch goes soft and slumps; cold surges come down off the rim and everything they touch precipitates. Stillness dissolves. Turbulence builds. The storm is not the enemy of the work; the storm is the contractor.

So a terrace can lose four meters of itself in a night and be back to the identical profile in nine days, laid by different animals out of different atoms. You cannot damage the shape. You can only delay it. Break a wall and it does not get repaired — it gets *re-derived*, arriving at the same curve from a different direction, the way a river arrives again at its meander.

## What you hear

Mostly, a house at night: the tick and settle of glass contracting, everywhere, small, unlocatable.

Under that, a granular hiss where a dissolution front is working, like snow settling on a roof.

Pressure more than sound: the surge arrives in the chest before the ear, a slow squeeze and release, forty minutes on, forty minutes off.

And once every few hours, one enormous crack — a cavitation snap somewhere out in the Combs — that goes forty kilometers through the galleries and comes out of the stone under your hand, and everything with a crown stops feeding for about two seconds, and then resumes.

## The rules, as far as they can be stated

Nothing here is unmade. Nothing here grows; things only divide, or lay down. Everything is slightly soluble, so permanence is a rate and not a condition. Cold builds and warmth unbuilds. Sound is expensive and therefore rare. Light has no direction and casts no shadows, only degrees of gray. The form is ancient and the matter is nine days old, and nothing in this world has ever, at any moment in eleven million years, looked new.

At the rim, in the air, with the cloud below and the water going off the edge into it, you can get warm enough to stop shivering by walking. Behind you the basin lies flat and pale and dim and full, breathing its four meters in and out, being taken apart and put back exactly as it was by animals the size of your hand that have never seen it, never wanted anything, and will not stop.
