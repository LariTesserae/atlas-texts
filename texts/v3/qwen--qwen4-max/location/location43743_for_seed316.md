## The Finished Ground

Nothing here is going anywhere. That is the first fact, and the others come out of it.

The land has been wearing down for so long that it has arrived. Not a plain in the ordinary sense — a plain still has a slope, still has an argument with the sea. This is the argument's conclusion. A continent ground to a single elevation, vary­ing by less than the height of a knee across three thousand miles, iron-cemented, pale as a fingernail, warm underfoot at every hour. Rivers crossed it once. Their courses are still legible as chains of shallow pans strung northwest to southeast, ghost drainage, water lying in the memory of water. The rivers themselves left before there was anything alive to notice.

You can see the curve. Stand anywhere and the ground bends away at the edges like the inside of a bowl turned upside down, and the horizon is not a line but a soft brown seam where the wet air stops being able to carry an image.

## Wet

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. It lies in films, lenses, sheets a finger's depth, in pans that touch their neighbors along their whole perimeter so that there is no dry margin between one body of water and the next — only a meniscus, a trembling seam of surface tension holding two worlds apart. Ten thousand square miles of ponds with no gaps. Walk it and you are never out of water and never in it past the ankle. The iron crust rings under the heel; sometimes the crust gives, and you go in to the shin in warm brine and gypsum mush that smells like a struck match.

The air is at its dew point always. Not fog exactly — the atmosphere is simply too full to hold itself as gas, and it condenses on any surface that offers, including you. Within a minute of standing still you are beaded all over. Within ten, you are dripping. The rain does not fall from clouds; there are no clouds, only a low luminous ceiling of vapor two hundred feet up, and the water comes out of it the way sweat comes out of skin, gathering, sagging, letting go. It falls in fat slow drops the size of grapes. It has been falling for a very long time.

It has nowhere to go. There is no downhill. Water leaves this place only by evaporating, and it returns only by condensing, and the loop has been closed since before the mammals. Every drop that ever fell here is still here, going around.

## The Roar

And so is every sound.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand, and it cannot be understood from inside a single minute, because inside a single minute it is simply noise: a deafening, seamless, warm roar, rain on water on stone, no gaps in it, no rhythm, a pressure against the chest and the teeth. It is loud the way deep water is heavy. It is loud in the way that a smell can be a memory. It does not fall off with distance the way sound should.

Sound needs a gradient to dissipate into. This ground has none. Noise here does not fade; it *pools*. It runs off the low rises and collects in the pans and lies there, layered, oldest at the bottom. The roar you are standing in is the sum of all the rain that has fallen on this continent, still arriving, still not spent. Under the top hiss of the current downpour there is a mid-register thunder of storms from centuries ago, and beneath that a sound like a crowd breathing, and beneath that something so old and so compressed that it registers only as a pressure in the sinuses and a conviction, unshakeable and unattributable, that you have been here before.

Distance is not measured in miles. It is measured in loudness. A thing is near if it is quiet — if you are hearing only its own present noise. A thing is far if it comes to you buried under everything that has happened between.

## Light

Dim, and warm, and coming from the wrong direction. The vapor ceiling glows a dull amber, lit from within by nothing anyone has found, and the water throws that glow back up, so the underside of every leaf and the underside of your own chin are lit while the tops are dark. There are no shadows here, only places where the light is thicker. Colors run to rust, ochre, bone, the pink of diluted blood, and a green so desaturated it reads as gray until you are close.

## What Lives

Almost nothing, and the reason is the age of the place. Every soluble thing was leached out and carried to the sea a hundred million years ago. What remains is iron and silica and salt, sterile as fired clay, and water that has been distilled and redistilled by the closed loop until it is nearly pure and nearly dead.

So: a liverwort-colored film over the crust, in patches, thin as a stain. Tussocks of a wiry salt-sedge, one every forty paces, each standing in its own private disc of slightly less poisoned mud that it has spent centuries manufacturing. In the deeper pans, hair-algae, warm to the touch, faintly contractile. Nothing tall. Nothing that could cast a shadow even if there were shadows to cast.

Animals are rarer than the plants and stranger for it. There is a flat pale swimmer, wide as a dinner plate and thin as a leaf, that lives in an inch of water and has no eyes and almost no body — mostly a listening surface, a membrane stretched on a hoop of cartilage, drifting. It eats the film. It navigates by the age of the sound beneath it. And it is desperately, compulsively solitary, because of what happens when the ponds touch.

## What the Touching Makes

The wind — and there is always wind, warm, gusting, unaccountable, the plain breeding its own weather out of its own evaporation — pushes the water films around. Pans swell and shrink. Seams break. Two bodies of water that have been separate for a thousand years slide into contact along a hundred-yard front, and their stored sounds mix.

And things precipitate.

It is not metaphor and it is not hallucination; the plain is simply too saturated with occurrence to keep it all dissolved. When the layers mingle, the noise flocculates, and out of the contact zone come bodies — warm, wet, mineral-fleshed, glossy, assembled in seconds out of compressed old rain and the memory of thunder. They stand up in the shallows. They have the wrong number of everything. Some are shaped like the animals that used to exist here, drawn from the deep strata of sound; some are shaped like nothing, ribbed and fluted and translucent, walking on structures that were never legs. They last as long as the seam holds. When the wind shifts and the waters draw apart, they lose their tension and come down, and go back into solution, and the roar gets very slightly louder.

This happens everywhere, constantly, across the whole vast wet floor. The plain is packed — not with matter, there is barely any matter — but with events. There is no empty moment anywhere on it. Stand still for an hour and you will be shoulder to shoulder with a dozen things that did not exist when you arrived and will not exist when you leave, and none of them will look at you, because looking is not something the roar knows how to do.

The flat swimmer spends its whole life fleeing contact. It reads the seams by their loudness and slides away from them, forever, across a continent that is trying to touch itself.

## The Marks

Scattered — one every few days' walking — there are places where the ground is a different color in a rectilinear way.

A threshold. A sill of dressed stone, worn absolutely flush, its edges only detectable by the way water beads differently on it. A rectangle nine paces by four, faintly paler, with a discoloration at one corner where something upright once stood. A course of brick laid flat in the crust like a spine in a slab, every brick reduced to a ghost of itself, the mortar lines still crisp.

Nothing built them that anyone can find. Nothing here has hands. But the marks are the only things on the plain that have not been flattened, or rather: they were flattened and *kept their shape anyway*, which is not a thing this ground permits. A footprint fills in a minute. A channel scoured by a storm heals overnight. These do not heal, because they were never wounds.

And they are quiet.

Step into the rectangle and the roar drops away — not muffled, *subtracted*, a hole in the loudest place in the world, a silence so abrupt your ears ring in protest. The made-things will not cross the line; they come to the edge and stand and lose interest and dissolve. Whatever built here, built exactly one thing, over and over, across a continent: a small subtracted room where sound does not accumulate. Nothing else survived. Not the walls, not the builders, not the purpose. Only the quiet, held in the stone like a charge.

In one of them, far out along a ghost river, a long-legged wader stands in the shallows. It is the only one for two hundred miles. It has been standing there, by the look of the mineral crust on its legs, for a very long time. It is not waiting for anything. It has simply found the one place where it can hear itself, and it is not leaving.

## Farther Out

The weather here is resident, not passing. Storms live on the plain the way animals live on a grassland — grazing the vapor, breeding, drifting, dying, and their carcasses of sound sink into the pans and become next season's bodies. There is a standing storm three hundred miles across near the eastern seam that has not moved in recorded nothing, because nothing here records.

And past it, if you could get there, the sound is arriving out of order. Louder first, then the rain that makes it. The plain is hearing its own future and it is deafening, and the made-things in that region come up already old, already grieving, and go down before the water that made them has finished touching.

Something is coming uphill. There is no uphill. It is coming anyway.
