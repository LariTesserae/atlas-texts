## The Hold

First there is the pressure, and only afterward the room.

It comes up through the soles of the feet and the long bones of the legs, a nineteen-beat-per-second shove that the body mistakes for its own pulse until it doesn't. Above that fundamental sits the audible part: a hard bright sizzle of harmonics, layered, unmusical, around a hundred and thirty decibels in the fertile districts. It does not sound like a machine. It sounds like the inside of a bell that has been struck continuously for nine hundred years. Nobody who lives here hears it. They hear the *notches* in it — the narrow frequency windows where the standing wave cancels itself — and they hear the frogs and the flutes and the small conversational animals that have evolved to live inside those gaps like fish in a reef's crevices.

Then the room resolves, and the room is enormous, and it is empty.

**

The Hold is a lattice of forty-odd chambers sunk into a basin whose floor lies sixty meters below the sea outside. Each bay is nine to fourteen kilometers across and seven hundred to eleven hundred meters tall. They are not caves. They were not dug. They were *raised*, extruded up out of the basin's fresh limestone by the same apparatus that keeps them standing, and the stone is still warm from making. Nothing here is rounded. There are no pebbles, no cobbles, no clay, no river sand; the gravel is angular glass-sharp calcite that cuts boots and hands, and the walls are fluted in tight parallel ridges that are not water-work but sound-work, erosion by pressure node, sculpture by standing wave. The flutes migrate over decades. You can date a district by the spacing of its ripples the way elsewhere you would date it by its potsherds.

The spans should not stand. Eleven kilometers of unsupported ceiling, four hundred meters of it, held in a acoustic lattice — a pressure field so precisely maintained that the stone is barely in compression, that a plumb line hung from the ceiling drifts *sideways* toward the nearest wall. The engineers who built it are not remembered and possibly were not people. The current physics of the Hold is descriptive rather than explanatory: the tuners know what the field does, in exhaustive and beautiful detail, and have stopped pretending to know why.

What the field does, chiefly, is comb the air.

Anything with mass in the volume of a bay — dust, pollen, spores, ash, a dropped spoon, a moth, a seed, a dead bird — is driven outward along deterministic trajectories to the surfaces. Not gently. Reliably. The air of the Hold has been swept for nine centuries and it is clearer than vacuum, clearer than any mountain sky; there is no haze, no fog, no drifting mote, nothing between you and the far wall eleven kilometers off. And it is *dim*. The ceiling is a lid of engineered cloud and cold rib, the sun never enters, and the only illumination is a faint sourceless violet glow along the antinodal seams and the farmed bioluminescence of the upside-down forest overhead — a low green heaven of fungal stars, seven hundred meters up, with weather in it.

So: perfect visibility, almost no light. You can see the lamps of a town on the opposite wall as a hard little constellation, unblurred, unblinking, and you cannot see your own hands.

**

Everything alive is on the surfaces. This is the first law.

The rind is two to four meters thick and it covers everything — floor, walls, ceiling, the flanks of ribs, the undersides of spans. It is a jungle pressed flat. Because the field delivers to the walls every particle of dust and pollen and corpse and shed skin from a volume of eighty cubic kilometers, the rind is fertilized by an entire sky's worth of nothing, continuously, forever. It is obscenely lush and it has no depth. Trees grow out and *along*, espaliered against stone, fans and brackets and lace, wall-figs whose single root system runs three kilometers along a gutter seam and fruits at nine hundred separate trunks. Ferns grow downward from the ceiling in sheets like green wallpaper. Mosses are the dominant biomass and some of them are eleven thousand years old in the sense that matters, which is continuous.

Walk into the rind and you are inside a warm wet green corridor two meters wide and effectively infinite in the other two dimensions. Damp, not wet. Warm — twenty-six, twenty-eight, unwavering, the stone's own heat. The smell is fig milk, wet calcite, ammonia from the crab flats, and under everything the ozone tang of the glow-seams.

Water arrives on schedule and never in quantity. Certain ribs are kept cold, and the humidity of eighty cubic kilometers condenses on them and runs down in threads that are gathered in gutters older than the language spoken beside them. Every eleven hours or so the beat swells and a bay *sheets*: five minutes of warm rain across the whole chamber at once, then nothing for half a day. There is one true river, a small brown muscular thing that runs along a floor seam in Bay Six and disappears into a throat, and there are pockets — cisterns, drowned stairwells, a flooded district in Eleven where the lamps still burn underwater and something large and patient has learned to open doors. Where the outer sea presses on the sill there is a brine seep, and around it a salt marsh squeezed into a two-meter band, complete with wading birds that cannot fly.

Nothing flies. Not in the volume. A bird that leaves the rind is swept to the nearest wall at forty meters per second and arrives as a stain.

**

The city is on the walls too, and it is a city — stairs, terraces, aqueduct gutters, cable trams, markets, layered construction ten generations deep — but it is a city of a hundred and eighty thousand people smeared across a surface the size of a small country. Encounters are rare. A market hall the size of a county holds thirty stalls and you wave to the far ones. Whole afternoons pass with no one in view but a distant figure on a ladder, a kilometer up the wall, tending a fig. The emptiness is not desolation; it is *spacing*. Everyone here has the loneliness of a lighthouse keeper and the social density of a village, and they negotiate this by being extremely deliberate about visits.

The buildings burrow. The floor of each bay is still being extruded, a millimeter or two a year, and so the ground floors of old districts become basements, and the basements become cisterns, and the cisterns become myth. There are neighborhoods in Four where you descend through six swallowed street levels to reach a shop whose front door is now a manhole. The Hold does not weather. It is reissued.

Real estate is priced in silence. The antinodes — where the field is loudest — are also where the sweep delivers its cargo, so they are fertile, well-fed, and deafening. The poor live there and eat magnificently and go deaf by thirty and speak with their hands and with bone-sticks pressed to the jaw. The rich buy *nulls*: alcoves, pockets, tuned chambers where the harmonics cancel, sometimes no larger than a wardrobe, and they are thin and pale and import their food and can hear a whisper across a table, which is the single most expensive experience available in the world. Conversation rooms are rented by the minute. There is a black market in silence. There are marriages contracted for access to a null.

The tuners, who read the field, are neither priests nor engineers and are treated as both. They can tell you where the pressure will be in nine hundred beats. They are the only people who are listened to, in a place where listening is a technical skill.

**

The fauna is abundant and it is flat.

Shale crabs in sheets across the floor rind, grazing the sweep-fall, molting in synchrony with the beat. Wall-geckos with adhesive bellies and no ear at all, hunting by vibration through stone. Drays — deaf, ox-sized, bone-plated over the tympanum — browsing the wall-forests on ledges, sure-footed, unhurried, the closest thing here to livestock. Notch-frogs in the cisterns, each species tuned to a different cancellation window, twelve species in one pool singing simultaneously in twelve spectral crevices and none of them masking another; a listener with the right instrument hears a chord that no frog has ever heard.

And in the seams, the ribbon-eels.

The nulls are not only pockets. Some of them are *lines* — thin ribbons of cancellation running through the open volume, a hand's breadth wide, kilometers long, joining wall to wall to ceiling in a graph. Inside a seam a body is not swept. Ribbon-eels live there and nowhere else: three meters long, flat as a strap, translucent, swimming through clear dim air along paths they cannot leave. Their world is one-dimensional, a subway map, and their entire behavioral repertoire is junctions. They lay eggs at junctions. Knot-hunters sit at junctions with their bodies spanning the seam and wait, sometimes for weeks, and the eels come, because there is no other way to go. Cable trams run along the same seams, and the tram drivers know the eel timetables better than their own.

**

But the deepest strangeness is the sowing.

Plants here do not disperse at random. They release seed into the void and the field carries it, deterministically, to a specific antinode of a specific harmonic. So a species' distribution across the whole Hold is a *figure* — a Chladni pattern, a rose of dots and arcs eleven kilometers wide, painted on the walls and ceiling in one kind of tree. The seventh-harmonic fig grows only where the seventh harmonic presses; you can stand in a grove and see, in the arrangement of trunks on the far wall, the shape of a sound. Twelve species, twelve figures, overlapping in places, mutually exclusive in others, and the pollinators are wingless wasps that walk the surface film for kilometers because the alternative is being fired into stone.

And twice a season the Hold retunes.

The tuners announce it nine days out. The mode shifts; the figures move; every antinode in eighty cubic kilometers relocates. In the days before, the trees spawn *predictively* — releasing seed toward places where nothing currently grows, where the pressure will be. The drays walk. The crabs go up the walls in columns. The eels' entire map is redrawn and most of them die, and their eggs, laid at the junctions that are about to exist, hatch into a geography their parents never saw. The bay sheets rain for eleven hours instead of five minutes. The flutes on the walls begin, very slowly, to migrate.

For about an hour during the retune the fundamental drops out and the Hold is silent — genuinely silent, a silence with no floor to it — and every unattached thing in the volume, all the dust of eleven days, falls straight down under nothing but gravity, and the rain falls with it, and the whole chamber is briefly full of falling.

That is the only weather here that anyone photographs. That is the hour the rich come out of their nulls to stand in the open, unprotected, deaf for a moment like everyone else, looking up at eleven kilometers of dim green stars coming loose.
