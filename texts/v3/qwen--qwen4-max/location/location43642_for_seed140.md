# The Sump

## The plain above

Nothing in this country has an edge.

The plateau lies four thousand meters up and flat as a held breath, a peneplain, the last worn stump of a range that stopped being mountains before there was anything on the world to notice. It goes out to a horizon you cannot see and would not believe if you could: no peaks, no scree, no fresh fracture anywhere. Every stone is a lozenge. Every hollow is scalloped. The gravel is not gravel but a drift of identical grains, four-tenths of a millimeter, each a truncated octahedron with its corners rounded off — identical, all of them, every grain in every direction for a day's walk, because they were not worn to that shape. They were made that way, and then worn a little afterward, out of politeness to time.

The plain is warm. Blood-warm, thirty-one degrees, and it has been thirty-one degrees for longer than there have been species to disagree about it. The heat comes up from underneath, through the milled stone, and it does not vary between noon and midnight, because there is no noon.

It is wet, too, but not with rain. The whole surface sweats. Water stands in a sheet three centimeters deep across the entire plateau, unmoving, and where it thins to a film it climbs — beads running uphill a hand's width before they give up and lie down again. Walk out there and you wade in warm water over perfect sand, in a darkness so complete it has texture, and the only sound is your own feet, going out from you and never coming back.

That is the important thing about the plain. It is not dark. It is *silent*. Sound leaves and does not return. Nothing that lives here can live out there.

## The lid

There is one hole in the plain.

It is three hundred meters across and it does not look like a hole from above; it looks like a place where the water is going somewhere. It is spanned. A membrane crosses the mouth, a woven lattice of something that was manufactured and has since become geology, sagging in the middle by perhaps eleven meters, and it holds the plateau's sheet of water up like a hand under a cloth. The water does not pool on it. It passes through, pore by pore, in filaments — threads of water, not drops, hanging down into the shaft in their hundreds of thousands, unbroken for the first forty meters, then fraying, then falling as rain that has forgotten how to be weather.

The membrane descends one millimeter a year. It has been descending for a long time.

Below it: a room. A collapsed shaft in the old carbonate rock, walls dissolved into flutes and lobes and hanging bellies of stone, the whole cavity shaped like a cupped palm. This is the entire world, or the entire part of it that is inhabited. Floor to lid, two hundred meters. Rim to rim, an hour's careful walk. Everything else is the plain, and the plain is where your voice goes to die.

## The rule about light

Light does not travel here.

The air carries a drift — a sparse, self-maintaining aerosol of absorbers, engineered, older than the ecology, so thin you would never call the air cloudy. A photon manages about a meter and a half before it is taken. A lamp shows you a fogged sphere the size of a stretched arm, your own hand, the wet nap under your boots, and then a wall of nothing that is not black so much as *unavailable*. Bring a brighter lamp and you get a slightly larger sphere and no more world.

The absorbed light does not vanish. It warms the drift, and the drift gives it back as pressure — as a fine granular hiss added to the room's noise. A light here is a kind of sound. This is why the old caches contain lamps and why nothing alive uses them.

Sound, meanwhile, goes eleven kilometers if you let it, and comes home.

So the room is lit by sound, and everything in it has spent its inheritance on ears.

## The nap

Lushness here is a two-dimensional fact.

Every surface — floor, fluted wall, the underside of the lid, the hanging bellies of rock — carries a mat: a felt of growth thirty to sixty centimeters deep, layered, warm, breathing. Chemotroph at the base, feeding on the sulfides and the slow sugars that seep from the stone; above that a moss-analogue with fronds like wet velvet ribbon; above that the fruiting tiers, which are not fruit and not flowers but resonators, cups and combs and stiffened drums that catch the falling threads and turn them into tone.

It is thick. It is dense. It is *teeming*: a hundred thousand grazers per square meter, blind, size of a sand grain, cropping the base layer; combers that walk up the falling filaments hand over hand and drink against gravity; predators like folded wire that hunt the combers by feeling the filament shiver.

And it is only skin. Lift your eyes off the mat and there is two hundred meters of absolutely empty air. The biomass of this world is a fur coat with nothing inside it.

It smells like wet bread, hot iron, crushed pepper, and, faintly, warm milk.

## The nine columns

Nine things stand in the volume.

They rise from the floor to the lid, hollow, tapered, ribbed — trunks, if you like, though they grew to a specification and have simply never been told to stop. Each is a tension structure, anchored at both ends, wound of fiber laid down in helices at angles that no plant would arrive at by accident. They are tuned. Strike one and it sounds for eleven minutes.

They are the room's infrastructure. The great drifting animals navigate by them; the mat's drum-tiers seed themselves into the columns' lower flutes and get carried up into the air currents; the columns themselves are cooling fins, still doing, feral and overgrown, the job they were seeded for. There were nine sockets in the floor once, cut in a ring. Eight of the columns are in the wrong places now. They have moved, over the ages, the way a rope moves in a slow tide.

Walk between two of them and you walk four minutes through nothing at all, hearing your footsteps come back to you off surfaces you will never see.

## The bells

The air is inhabited by eleven large animals, and eleven is abundance, here, because the volume is enormous and each of them is the size of a house.

They are bladders, gas-lifted on the room's warmth, twelve meters across, trailing curtains of sticky filament forty meters below them. They beat once a minute — a single slow contraction, a wing-stroke you could time with a sundial if there were sun — and otherwise they drift on the convection that rises off the mat and falls along the walls.

They call. Of course they call. A bell's voice is a descending tone six seconds long, beginning as a pressure in the sternum and ending as a note you can hum, and it goes around the room three or four times before it wears out. Each animal holds a territory of empty air sixty meters wide and negotiates its borders entirely by voice, patiently, over years. Loud, constantly loud: the sum of the room is the noise of a river you cannot see plus a crowd two rooms away plus, every ninety seconds or so, something enormous being unhappy in a cathedral.

The bells breed once in three centuries. Their calves rise from the mat as spores of tissue the size of a thumb and take eighty years to become audible.

## The quiet rooms

Because the chamber rings, it also cancels.

There are places in the air — stable, drifting a little with temperature, mapped and remembered and inherited — where the standing waves annul themselves. A cubic meter of near-silence, hanging in the loud. You can put your head into one and feel the world drop away like a floor giving out.

Everything important happens at these nulls. The bells calibrate against them. The floor predators — low, dish-faced, patient, with a fan of cartilage they fold around themselves — hunt by finding a null and sitting in it, acoustically invisible, waiting for a comber-heavy thread of mist to come down within reach. And the scattered artifacts are always found near them: hand-sized combs of a warm ceramic that is not ceramic, which eat sound the way the drift eats light. Someone made those. Someone hunted here with them, a long time ago, and lost one, and it is in a cache under the mat with three others, still working.

## What was built

Not much. A stair spirals down the wall, nine hundred steps, each dished by use into a shallow bowl, so worn that you descend by feel, sliding your feet into the memory of other feet. At mid-height there is a doorframe, perfectly plumb, with undissolved stone behind it: the room it opened into was eaten out of existence by water while the frame was preserved by something that has since stopped preserving it.

There are the sockets. There is a ring of pits at the eastern wall whose purpose is not recoverable.

And in the sump at the bottom — a warm pool a meter deep, forty meters across, the destination of every thread — there is a sphere four meters wide, drowned, rounded, indistinguishable from the boulders except that it is one-tenth of a degree warmer than they are, and it sounds a single low note every eleven hours.

It is still working. Its heat output declines by a measurable fraction each century, and from that decline you can calculate how much of its task remains: about sixty thousand years. It was, once, part of the apparatus that ground a mountain range down to this plain, grain by identical grain, to make a substrate flat enough to think on. The thinking is nearly finished. The warmth is the thinking.

## Time, and the edge

Nothing here hurries. The plain's water sheet rises and falls on a forty-year pulse. The lid sags. A drum-tier grows one leaf a decade. The bells' arguments outlast the individuals who began them, because the individuals last three hundred years and the arguments last four hundred.

When the sphere finishes, the warmth will stop, and over some centuries the room will cool, and the mat will thin, and the bells will come down one by one to lie in the sump, and the columns will hold their tension a while longer and then not. The stone is already in its final form. It got there first. It has been waiting.

Stand at the lip, where the threads begin to fall, and shout outward across the plain. Nothing comes back. Not an echo, not a softening, nothing — your voice simply leaves, into a warm wet dark that has no walls, and you understand that this is what death is to everything born in the shaft.

Then turn and shout down into the hole, and the whole room answers: the flutes, the columns, the lid, the eleven hanging animals adjusting their borders, the water applauding itself two hundred meters below, a note held so long you can walk down the stair inside it.

You cannot see any of it. You will never see any of it.

It is the most thoroughly lit place in the world.
