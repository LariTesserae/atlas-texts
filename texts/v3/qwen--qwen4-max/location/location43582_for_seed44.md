## The Culm

You hear it three kilometres out, across the reed, before you see anything: a low stationary roar, the sound a weir makes, or a waterfall a long way off. The barge-pole man does not look up. The sound does not change as you approach. It simply gets larger, the way a wall gets larger, until it is a thing you are inside of.

Then the smell arrives — turpentine and yeast and hot iron, and under those the black-sulphur breath of turned mud. Then the shape: not towers, not a skyline. A green hill, twenty-six metres high, broad as a village, sitting flat on the water-plain with brick stacks coming up through its crown and guyed to its branches by iron rods. Two thin plumes of white steam, one grey. A ring of clay bund around it, worn smooth on top, and a lock gate, and a barge basin, and beyond that in every direction thirty kilometres of sedge and standing water and nothing else at all.

The whole of it can be walked in eleven minutes. Nothing in it stands more than four hundred paces from the stack. Four hundred and thirty people live here and have lived here, in the same trades, in the same rooms, for two hundred and thirteen years.

## What it is made of

The town is one organism, or rather one hundred and eleven stocks that were all cut from the same mother and grafted into each other until the grafts took and the seams disappeared. A salt-tolerant, mangrove-blooded thing with strangler habits: it throws aerial roots down from its limbs, it thickens where it is loaded, it lays callus over any wound within a day and seals it within a week.

That last property is the entire reason the place exists. Everything else — the walls, the floors, the roof — is convenience. The sealing is the industry.

The pipes of the Culm are heartwood, bored and grafted, sheathed in living cambium. They carry water at four bar and steam at two, and where a metal pipe would pit and weep and need cutting out, these weep for a week and then close. The pressure mains have not been replaced in ninety years. They have been *fed*: a slurry of potash and bone-ash and digester liquor pumped into the root collars every flood season so the walls can keep repairing themselves. A beam under load grows thicker on its underside. The Combing Floor's main girder was planted as a whip in the year the works was founded and is now a metre through, and it is still growing, three or four millimetres of girth a year, patient as a fingernail.

You cannot hurry it. That is the first law here. Everything the town will have in fifty years is already planted, and the planting book is a legal document.

## Three levels

**The Under.** Root country. Warm as blood, dark, dripping, floor of black peat that gives under the boot. The digesters squat here — six brick-lined domes fed with press-cake and sedge-cut and the town's own waste, holding fifty-five degrees all year, breathing methane into a gas holder that rises and falls a hand's breadth every hour. The sumps. The anoxic water, black as tar, that will kill a mosquito larva in minutes and smells of struck matches. The pump room, where the beam engine *Patience* works at forty revolutions a minute, its flywheel five metres across, turning so slowly you can read the maker's name on the rim as it comes round.

**The Floor.** The working level, and the streets, which are not streets but grown plank-wood laid flat and polished by two centuries of clogs into something like dark horn. The presses, forty-two strokes a minute, thumping through the soles of your feet. Line shafting running the length of the halls in bored trunks with ironwood bearings, resin-oil dripping into catch cups. Retort Row in its brick cell, distilling resin and turpentine. The combing machines. The drying lofts. The dynamo. The narrow-gauge wagons hauled by a chain off the shaft, clanking round the sidings with fibre-bales, and the gravity incline down to the basin.

**The Over.** Catwalks lashed and grafted through the crown, cisterns, the leaf roof, the drying frames, the lamps. Here the light is ordinary daylight filtered green, and the wind in a hundred thousand leaves makes a sound exactly like surf on shingle, and never stops.

Density here is not a matter of crowds. It is that everything a city has has been folded into a space the size of a hamlet, and so nothing has clearance. Water main, gas main, steam line, drain, shaft, rail, root, laundry line, lamp cable — all of them within arm's reach of each other and of your shoulders. You turn sideways a hundred times a day. The hand-lines are worn pale where four generations of palms have slid along them. Children learn the sideways gait before they learn to read.

## Water that is always arriving

It rains inside the Culm on clear days. The crown transpires something like forty tonnes a day into air already at ninety per cent, and every cool pipe in the Under and the Floor sweats it back out again, and the leaf roof gutters run in the dry season. Nothing here is ever dry. Nothing here is ever wet through, either. It is a place held permanently at the point of nearly-dripping.

Every joint has its tin. Every tin has its note, and the tins are tuned by the men who empty them — not for beauty, but so that a change of pitch means a change of rate means a leak somewhere upstream. The drip chorus on the Floor is a diagnostic instrument. Old fitters can hear a failing gland three rooms away, the way a doctor hears a murmur.

The town drinks condensed water and rain caught off the leaf roof into the sweet tank. It does not drink the marsh. Two metres above mean tide, four millimetres of subsidence a year, and the salt wedge coming up the channels a little further each decade: the seaward leaves have been browning at the tips since anyone's grandmother was young. There is a ledger for that too. The bund is raised a hand's breadth every ten years, and the raising is a festival, and nobody under thirty thinks of it as an emergency.

## The loudness

Eighty-four decibels on the Floor, steady, all day and all night. Presses, shafting, water hammer in the mains when a valve shuts, the leaf-surf overhead, the relief stack letting go with a sound like a torn sheet every eleven minutes, the sluice slapping on the turn of the tide.

There are no birds. There is nothing for them to eat — no fruit, no seed, no insects to speak of, the air faintly resinous and the smoke drifting through the crown — and there is no frequency left over for a song to work in. A heron stands on the bund sometimes, at slack water, and does not come in. Eels in the outer ditch. Mosquitoes at the margin at dusk, and none at all past the gate. No cobwebs anywhere in the Culm. People from outside find this the most unsettling thing about it, more than the noise: a warm wet crowded place with no webs in the corners.

The only animal in the town is the workforce.

So the voices are hands. The Combing Floor has a full sign language, four hundred-odd words, with dialects by shift, and it has spread into the kitchens and the bedrooms and the arguments. Half the men over fifty are deaf in the left ear from standing on the shaft side. The school teaches grafting, valve-work, and the signs in the same hour. And at shift change the clutch is drawn and the shafting coasts down and for about nine minutes the town is *quiet* — fifty-eight decibels, which is leaves and drips and the digesters sighing — and in that gap everybody talks out loud, and shouts, because they cannot recalibrate, and it is the loudest thing that happens all day.

## Rules, which are only physics and habit

Fire is the terror. A town of resin and cellulose cannot forgive a spark, so the boilers and retorts live in brick cells with clay fire-walls and double doors, and open flame is a criminal offence, and there are wet standpipes every thirty paces, and the drill is run monthly and timed. The smell of scorched resin empties a room faster than a scream.

The stock is clonal and does not seed. Once a decade or so a forced flower is brought on under glass and pollinated by children with brushes, and the seed is a public event, and the seedlings go into the planting book with names.

The dead are burned, because the peat keeps what you put in it and the ditches get re-cut. The ash goes to the root collars. Everybody knows this and nobody minds much; it is understood as being put back into the walls.

Harvest equals growth, audited annually in the Coppice Book, on an eleven-year rotation. It has balanced for two centuries. That balance is not a virtue. It is the only thing that has let the place stay itself.

## Night, and the long shape of time

The lamps come on in wire cages a hand's breadth from the leaves, ordinary yellow bulbs, no moths at all around them, and the crown glows from inside like a lit tent. The presses do not stop. The temperature falls from thirty-one to twenty-seven and the humidity does not fall at all. In the leaf rooms off the Over the noise is seventy-one decibels and people sleep in hammocks slung on living hooks that have grown around the shackles, and they feel the press through the wood as a slow pulse, and they cannot sleep anywhere else.

Nothing here happens quickly. The tide moves forty centimetres. The wind is light and steady. The gas holder rises and falls a hand's breadth an hour. The girder adds four millimetres. The bund rises a hand's breadth a decade. The salt comes up a little further each generation and the planting book is quietly, gradually, over a hundred and fifty years, changing the stock on the seaward side to something that can take it.

From the barge, going out, the roar stays the same size for a long time behind you, and then begins, very slowly, to be a sound a weir makes, a long way off.
