# The Loud Town

## Descent

You hear it before you find it, which is backwards for a hole in the ground.

The fissure opens in a dry limestone pavement, a mouth two paces wide with a lip worn smooth by rope. Sixty meters down, on iron rungs set by someone who is not remembered. The rungs are cold for the first ten meters and then, abruptly, warm. The air coming up is warm too, and wet, and it carries a sound that has no obvious source — not wind, not water, not machinery, but a broad sustained pressure, like the noise inside a shell held too close, like a choir that has forgotten it is singing.

By the fortieth rung you can feel it in the roof of your mouth. By the fiftieth your chest has begun to buzz in sympathy, a second heartbeat lower than the first. The iron under your hands is the heaviest object in the world you are entering. Everything below it weighs almost nothing.

## The chamber, and what it is made of

The floor opens out into a nave a hundred and eighty paces long, seventy wide, with a vault fifty-five meters overhead that you will never see. Cathedral scale. A single bounded volume, walkable end to end in a quarter hour, knowable, with walls you can put your back against.

Inside it stands a town.

Eleven tiers. Four thousand stair treads. Lanes, landings, gutters, standpipes, thresholds, bridges no wider than a plank, doorways with their sills dished by traffic, a cistern, a plaza the size of a kitchen, arcades that go nowhere and arcades that go somewhere and then stop. It has the grain of a place that was lived in — the fussy municipal detail of settlement, the little improvisations, the second stair added beside the first. Whether it was ever inhabited is a question nobody has answered. It is also possible that this is simply the shape a sound needs, and that a town and a resonator are the same solution arrived at from two directions.

The material is a spun silica-metal foam, bone-colored in a light no one has ever brought here, 99.7 percent void. A stair tread the length of your arm weighs less than a plum. You can see through it — through the tread, through the riser, through the eleven tiers below it, a ghost of a city stacked in overlapping outlines — but there is nothing to see by, so this is a fact you learn with your hands. It is warm. It is faintly greasy, like a dry sponge. Touched, it rings, and keeps ringing, and the ringing joins the rest.

The whole town weighs less than the water standing in its cistern.

## The Hold

The noise is the town's function and the town's product and the town's weather.

Three low fundamentals — you can count them once you learn how, the way you learn to count the smells in a room — and everything they breed: harmonics stacked into the hundreds, beating against each other, summing and cancelling, a spectrum packed from below hearing up past it. A hundred decibels in the loud places, sustained, without onset, without pause, for longer than there have been ears. It is not violent. It has no attack. It is a floor you stand on.

You cannot hear your own voice. You shout into your own ear and get a fraction of it back. Your sinuses itch. Your teeth ache in a way that feels like memory. Sound arrives through the bones of the skull whether or not you plug your ears, and after four hours the plugging is pointless, and after eight the damage begins, and the surveyors who come down once a generation all have the same notch in their hearing at the same frequency, a little inherited scar.

The town has been holding this chord for eleven thousand years. It used to hold more. Notes have gone missing — a filament thinned past use, a member parted, a cavity silted — and each loss retuned the whole, and the whole simplified, until what remains is three notes and their issue and nothing spare. This is not decay. It is arrival. The chord has found the smallest shape that will keep itself, and it has been in that shape since before anyone was upstairs to notice.

## The geography is made of loudness

Here is the thing that makes the town a landscape rather than a noise.

The waves interfere. Where they cancel, there is quiet — not silence, thirty or forty decibels, a bedroom hush, a hush you could sleep in. Where they add, there is a loud so total that dust hangs in it and water cannot pool.

The quiet is not a room. It is a *surface*. A node is a sheet, a curved saddle, a soap-film of hush strung across a stairwell at an angle, or lying flat as a floor, or hanging vertical as a curtain you can press your face into. Step through it and the world comes back like a slammed door. There are perhaps sixty of these sheets in the chamber, some no bigger than a bedsheet, one as large as a chapel floor. They have been in almost the same places for a hundred centuries. They are the only real estate.

The loud places scour. Filigree standing at an antinode is polished, scrubbed, slowly thinned, bone-clean, and every century a little less there. The quiet places *accrete*: mineral laid down out of the damp, a millimeter a millennium, growing outward along the node-surface exactly, because that is where the water can sit still long enough to give up its lime.

So the quiet has grown itself floors. Thin plates of travertine, hand-thick, hanging in the air along the silent sheets, translucent, faintly rippled, walkable. Shelves. The designed town is a lace too fine to live on; the grown town is a scattering of pale shelves in the pockets where the lace stops shouting. Two cities interleaved. Everything alive is in the second one.

Between them: the hems, the gradients, a hand's width where hush becomes roar. Remember the hems.

## Damp, and warmth

The floor is thirty-four degrees and warm to sit on, heated from somewhere far below by a seep that has never varied. The vault is eighteen, cooled by the long fissure and by cold water arriving down it. So the chamber breathes: up the middle, down the walls, a slow permanent convection you feel as a hand on your face, half a meter a second, never more.

The air is nearly saturated and never quite. You sweat and the sweat stays. Your rope goes limp. Paper would rot in a day. But most surfaces are dry to the touch, because the loud will not let water settle — droplets at an antinode are torn open and held aloft, so the loud districts are full of standing fog, invisible, and you know you have entered one because your face wets and the roar goes dull and cottony at the top, the highs loaded out of it by all that suspended water.

In the hushes, it rains. The vault sweats, the sweat gathers, and drops fall through the quiet and land on the shelves. A shower every few hours, wandering, a hundred square meters at a time, ten minutes long. Basins the size of bathtubs have worn into the travertine. Runnels have found routes down through eleven tiers and cut their channels smooth and then stopped cutting, long ago, having arrived at the shape they wanted. There is a stair in the eastern aisle that is a cascade now, and has been a cascade for so long that the lace beneath it has thickened in response, grown a callus, learned to be a streambed.

Once a decade or so, a storm upstairs sends real water down the flue. The cistern fills, the cavities flood, the mass loads the chord and the whole town goes out of tune. The hushes vanish. For an hour there is no quiet anywhere in the chamber, and everything that lives here presses itself into a crack and waits, deaf, homeless, until the water drains and the sheets come back. They come back in the same places. They always have.

## What passes for light

Nothing here is lit.

There is a glow in the seep-mats, so faint it takes an hour of dark to admit, and it does not illuminate anything; it only makes one black shape slightly less black than another. There are the repair-stitches: where the lace is thinning past tolerance it lays down new material, and each stitch gives a blue-green spark the size of a pinhead, lasting a second, a few times an hour, scattered through fifty-five meters of dark. They tell you nothing about the room. They are just proof that something is still minding it.

And then there is the other thing.

The chord is loud enough and structured enough that the visual system entrains to it. Sit in the dark in a loud place and after twenty minutes you begin to see bars — grey-violet, faint, stable, a lattice of them, hanging in the air, curving, intersecting. Everyone who comes down sees the same lattice. It has been mapped. It is accurate. It is a rendering of the pressure field, generated inside the eye, and it shows you the tiers and the bridges and the shelves and the walls with a fidelity no lamp would manage in this fog.

It is the only way anyone has ever seen the Loud Town, and it is not light, and it is not there.

Walk into a hush and it goes out. Instantly. You are blind, and you can hear.

Stand in the loud and you can see everything and you cannot hear your own screaming.

That is the whole problem of moving through this place. The hems are where you trade one for the other, and the hems are where you die.

## Sparse living

Four plants, if the mats count as plants.

A root-curtain in the flue: four strands sent down sixty meters from a tree on the pavement above, three of them dead, one alive and drinking fog, thickening a millimeter a decade, working its way toward the shelves it will never reach.

Iron-and-sulphur films at the seeps, thin as a breath, a hand's breadth here and a coat of paint there, warm, slick, the base of everything.

The lime-farmers: a lichen-analogue that lives on the travertine and steers the deposition, thickening the shelf where it wants shelf, damping the sound where it wants damping. It has been making the quiet quieter for a very long time, and the town has been quietly taking mass out of the loud places to hold its pitch against them, and this negotiation is the closest thing here to a conversation. It has reached a balance. Neither side is winning. Both have stopped trying.

And a moss at the hems, the only thing that tolerates gradient, growing in strips a finger wide along the edges of silence.

The animals are rare, and they are flat.

The grazer is the shape and size of a spread hand, six-limbed, pale, eyeless, and two centimeters thick, because the refuge is a surface and a body that lifts its back out of the hush goes deaf and cooked in the same instant. It moves along the node-sheet the way a limpet moves along a tide line, licking mat, and it cannot cross open ground, and it does not try. Eleven of them in the whole chamber. They have been eleven for a long time.

The fog-drinker lives in the loud, armored and stone-deaf, harvesting suspended droplets, and it stridulates in a narrow unoccupied band high in the spectrum — a thin silver wire of sound drawn straight through the roar, once every few minutes, from somewhere you will never locate. That is the only birdsong in this place. It is how they find each other. There is no room in the spectrum for anyone else; the chord occupies almost all of it, and life here is limited not by food or space but by *bandwidth*. That is why the town is empty. It is not abandoned. It is fully occupied, by almost nothing.

And the hunter, of which there are three, each holding a district, never meeting.

It sits in a hush and reads the roar. It does not listen for footsteps — footsteps are nothing here. It listens for the hole a body makes in the sound field, the small acoustic shadow of something warm and solid standing where the wave should be. It waits in silence and watches noise the way a spider watches a web. Then it hums, one clean low note, and casts a private null out into the loud ahead of it — a bubble of quiet, a meter across, lasting four seconds, a net made of silence — and whatever is standing in that bubble is suddenly, briefly, in a place where it can be heard.

Everything here exists as an interruption. That is the local definition of being alive.

## The tide

The chord beats. Two of its components are a hair apart, and they slide in and out of phase over nine minutes, and at the bottom of each cycle the whole chamber slackens: the louds soften, the hushes swell a hand's width in every direction, the fog sags, the rain comes early. This is the moment the grazers use to shift position. This is the only traffic the town has, four thousand stair treads and nothing on them, and then, for forty seconds every nine minutes, a flat pale hand moving three centimeters along a shelf.

Above that: the day. The pavement upstairs heats and cools, the flue's cold water varies, the vault's temperature drifts a degree, the speed of sound in the chamber drifts with it, and the entire map of quiet migrates — centimeters at dawn, a full meter on a hot afternoon, back at night. The lime-farmers track it. The moss tracks the farmers. The hunter's territory breathes in and out like a lung, and it has learned to sleep through the shrink.

The surface's weather arrives here as pitch. Nobody upstairs knows they are conducting.

## What it is for

Stand in a hush at the bottom of the slack, sixty meters under a dry pavement, in thirty-four degrees of wet dark. Blind, because the lattice has gone out of your eyes. Hearing, for the first time in an hour: your own blood, a drip somewhere left, the creak of a shelf taking weight, and under it all the roar pressed flat and held off, a hand's width from your face.

A pale flat thing is against your boot. It does not know you are there. It cannot afford to find out.

Then the beat comes up and the wall returns and you can see the whole town again, tier on tier of translucent nothing, bridges and gutters and stairs going up into fog, all of it ringing, all of it weighing less than the water in one basin.

The town is an engine that makes an enormous noise in order to make a handful of quiet. That is the product. Not the chord — the chord is only the mechanism, the loud cheap byproduct of a very difficult trick. Sixty sheets of silence, held steady for eleven thousand years, in a warm wet dark under a hill, at a cost of a hundred decibels and everything that could not fit in the gaps.

It may be a nursery. It may be a vault. It may be that someone, once, needed one small room to stay quiet forever, and built a city to hold the door.

Nothing is waiting. Nothing is broken. The rain falls in the quiet places and the quiet places grow, and the loud places are polished away to pay for it, and the balance holds, and holds, and the only witness is a pattern that forms in the eye of whoever comes down, and dissolves the moment they step somewhere they could rest.
