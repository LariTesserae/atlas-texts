# Assay One

*(called the Damp by everyone who lives in it)*

## I. The Flat

There is no approach. You are on the plain, and the plain is gray and level and cold, and then you are at the rim, and beyond the rim the city stands up out of the ground like something dropped rather than something built.

The plain is four meters above a sea that has not been approved yet. It is flat because nothing has ever happened to it. No hills, no hollows, no drifts; the material is a fine dark stone-analog with the fracture faces still sharp, every chip standing where it fell, edges you could cut a finger on and occasionally do. There is no soil. Soil is downstream. There is no rounding, no sorting, no fan of gravel at the foot of anything, because the plain is eleven years old in some places and eleven minutes old in others, and weather has only just been introduced to it.

The cold comes up through the soles of your boots. It is not a bitter cold. It is a *held* cold, steady as a shelf, hovering a degree or two above the point at which the ground would stop being ground.

## II. The Rim

The boundary is a seam about knee-high, a bead of darker material where the fabrication stopped, and it runs a closed loop roughly nine hundred meters across. You can walk the whole perimeter in half an hour if you don't stop, and people do stop, because along the inside of the seam the buildings are packed so tightly that the wall of them reads at first as a single cliff face with weather in it.

Outside the seam is the Blank. It has the same color as the plain and the same temperature and no other properties. Distance is not defined out there. If you set a tool down beyond the rim and walk twenty paces and turn around, the tool will be wherever it needs to have been. Surveyors go out with two colleagues and a rope and they come back with fewer measurements than they went out with, which is a known hazard and is listed on the permit.

The Blank advances about four meters a year. The city is not expanding into it. The city is *deciding* it, slowly, the way a slow crystal decides a solution.

## III. What the Cold Is For

Undecided matter keeps its edges when it is cold. Warm it and it blurs — not melts, blurs: the distinction between a lintel and the air beneath the lintel becomes negotiable, and then it becomes a matter of opinion, and opinions in Assay One are load-bearing and should not be improvised.

So the ward is refrigerated. Not by machines you can see. The heat is drawn off through the foundations into a sink nobody has ever located, and the whole quarter sits in a permanent thin deficit, and every surface in it sweats.

That is the damp. The air holds a little moisture — not much, there is no sea to give it much — and every wall, every rail, every step and sill and shoulder of every doorway is a fraction colder than the air, so the water comes out of the air onto the city and stays there in a film you can wipe with a thumb. It beads on the ceramic. It stands in the tool-marks. It does not run, mostly, because running has not been arranged yet.

Press your palm to a wall and the print stays for a year. There are handprints on the north face of the Condenser Hall that were left by people who are now elderly, and the prints are still faintly warm, and it is considered bad manners to touch them.

You learn not to lean. You learn not to embrace anyone in a doorway. Affection has softened a stair rail into a suggestion of a stair rail, and three tenants fell through it before the ordinance came down.

## IV. Water in Draft

There is no drainage. This is the thing visitors find hardest.

Water arrives — a drizzle every eleven days or so, taking thirty hours to complete its fall, so that you walk through it as through a slow gray crowd — and it lands, and it stays where it lands. There are pools in the plazas that are the exact shape of the plaza's shallowest sag, and they have been there since the plaza was extruded, and they are not going anywhere, because a channel would be a *decision*, and decisions are expensive and must be ratified.

There are eleven hundred standing pools in Assay One. They are mapped. They have names, or provisional names, which is a different thing. In winter — and it is always roughly winter — they skin over with black ice a millimeter thick, and the ice does not crack underfoot so much as *decline*, and you step through into water that is colder than the ice was.

There is one licensed watercourse. It is called the First Gully. It begins at a condenser outfall on the terrace below the Hall of Ordinances and it runs one hundred and forty meters and it is, at its deepest, nine millimeters deep. It has taken sixteen years to cut that. It has a gravel bed the size of a dinner plate, five pebbles, the only rounded stones in the world, and there is a low rail around them, and people come and look.

The Gully carries about a liter an hour. It ends in a pool called the Anticipated Sea.

Everyone in the ward can tell you the sound the Gully makes, and everyone describes it differently, and none of the descriptions is wrong, because the sound of running water has not been settled on yet and is still being sampled from the population.

## V. The Build

Nine hundred thousand registered presences in a bounded area you can cross in twenty minutes.

The towers are near-touching — a hand's width, a shoulder's width, in places fused at a cornice where two fabrications were authorized in overlapping weeks and the argument between them was resolved by simply letting both be true at that height. They go up eleven, fourteen, twenty levels and they are narrow, and between them the alleys are so tight that the condensation forms bridges: threads of water spanning the gap, holding, trembling, not falling, because falling requires a ratification that gravity in the alleys has only partially received.

Above the alleys the buildings lean toward each other and meet and the district becomes a roof. Below the alleys it goes down: sub-levels, sub-sub-levels, cisterns that were never approved as cisterns and are used as rooms, rooms let out as cisterns. There are stairs in the walls of stairs. There is a courtyard you reach by going through a wardrobe in a kitchen on the fourth floor of a building whose ground floor is a different building.

The material is a pale ceramic that was extruded cold and has never been fired. It has the grain of a fingernail. Where it is thin you can see the filament inside it, the growth-thread, still working, laying down a micron a week. Nothing here is finished and nothing here is under construction; those words do not divide the ward the way they divide other cities. There is scaffolding that has been in place for sixty years and is now legally a facade. There are load-bearing arguments — a pier on Sump Street that is nothing but a sustained consensus, dense as marble, cold as marble, and if the consensus fails the pier will not fall, it will simply never have been there, and the four floors above it will have always been supported by the adjacent wall, and everyone will remember it that way except the pier's registered holder, who will be unemployed.

The technology is not visible as technology. There are no engines, no screens, no cables, no hum of power. The infrastructure is *the specification*, and the specification is administered, and the administration takes place in rooms.

## VI. The Rooms Are Fuller Than the Buildings

Density here is not only a matter of buildings per hectare.

A room on the eighth floor of the Long Terrace is let to four tenants at four different phases, and all four are home in the evening, and they pass through one another with a slight resistance like walking through cold honey, and they leave notes for each other on the sill in condensation. The room is nine square meters. Each tenant will tell you, honestly, that they have the room to themselves most nights.

Volumes overlap. The stairwell in the Filament Block contains, at any moment, between six and forty instances of itself, differing by small things — the wear on the third tread, whether the window is frosted, whether someone died there. The instances are stacked the way pages are stacked. You climb them all at once and arrive having climbed one, and which one you arrive in depends on how you were thinking on the way up.

There is a corridor where you must not repeat yourself. Not metaphorically: any gesture performed three times in that corridor becomes an ordinance, and the corridor will thereafter require that gesture of everyone. There is a man who has to touch the ceiling at the corner of Wick and Ninth, every time, forever, because in his first week he adjusted his hat there three times.

This is why the ward is built so redundantly — nine stairs to a floor, four doors to a room, redundancy upon redundancy. Habit is the mechanism by which physics gets ratified. A path worn is a path legislated. So the tenants vary their routes obsessively, and the ground stays unworn, and the geology stays embryonic, and the sharp little chips of the plain lie exactly where they fell, and the First Gully is the only erosion in the world because it is the only erosion anyone has agreed to.

## VII. Tenants, and the Almost-Animals

Most of the presences are people, or were, or are being. They are quiet. They move slowly, not from frailty but from policy — fast motion abrades, abrasion decides — and they wear pale layered wool and they carry a cloth for wiping the sweat off things they intend to touch.

Some of the presences are not people. A stairwell has an intention and the intention is registered and pays a share of the cold. There is a standing figure at the head of the Anticipated Sea that has been there for forty years and is the *idea of a harbor*, held in reserve, and it is about the size and shape of a person leaning on a rail, and it is damp, and no one has ever seen it move, and everyone greets it.

Fauna is rare. There are midges at a heat leak on Sump Street, where an unauthorized warmth has been escaping for a decade and the local matter has gone soft and generous and things have begun to live in it; the leak is a crime and is also the only place in the ward where you can be warm, and people go there and sit close together and say nothing and are careful, afterward, to check that they still have all their edges.

There are worms in three of the pools. They may be rounding errors. They are counted anyway.

And there is the Assay Bird. One. A gray long-legged thing like a heron that was released nineteen years ago to find out whether herons work. It walks the perimeter of the ward over the course of a year, one full circuit, and it stands in the pools, and it is the only fast thing in Assay One — when it strikes at the water the strike is startling, a crack of motion in a place where everything else takes an hour. Its strike is not yet a law. It is being sampled. Every time the bird strikes, someone somewhere writes down what happened, and in three or four hundred years there will be herons everywhere, and they will all strike like that, and no one will know that this is a quotation.

## VIII. Light, and the Almost-Silence

The light is ordinary.

That is the strangest thing in the ward and no one who lives there thinks so. The sun sits at a normal winter angle in a normal white sky and the shadows fall the way shadows fall, at the right length, soft-edged, honest. Light was ratified first, before anything, before the ground; it is the one finished clause. You can trust it. In a place where a stair may be six stairs and a corridor may remember your hat, the light simply comes down and lands on things and shows you them, and it has never once been wrong.

People stand in it. There is a bench on the terrace below the Hall of Ordinances that faces the ordinary sun, and it is the most contested seat in the quarter.

Sound is mostly quiet, and the quiet is *populated*. There is the drip, everywhere, at every rate, ten thousand separate drips making a texture like a crowd whispering in another language. There is the tick of frost growing on the ceramic — frost grows here in flat plates, slowly, visibly, a millimeter an hour, and it clicks as it takes another micron. There is breath. There is the cloth on the wall. There is the sound of the Gully, which is different for everyone.

And there are the dead patches, acoustic zones where propagation was never granted, and a shout in them goes about a meter and stops as if it hit wool. Two people can stand three meters apart in the Fish Court and have to walk toward each other to speak. The ward's arguments happen at close range, face to face, in the damp, quietly, which is one reason the ratifications take so long and another reason they mostly hold.

## IX. Eleven Trees

On the leeward side of the Condenser Hall, in a seam of moisture, there are eleven trees.

They are not tall. They are the prototypes of a species that does not have a name yet, and they differ from one another in ways that are being compared: bark texture, leaf retention, the angle at which a branch decides to bother. Four of them have died and been left standing, because decomposition is a downstream process and has not arrived. Seven are alive. In the eleventh, the topmost branch forks in a way that the surveyors keep returning to look at, and there is a rumor that it will be the way all trees fork, everywhere, forever, and the rumor has made that branch slightly heavier than it should be.

Otherwise: crustose lichen on the north faces, seeded deliberately, in circles the size of coins, gray-green, growing outward a millimeter a year. Sedge in the wet seams, in tufts you can count. A film of algae in three pools, and hair-ice at the outfalls, white and fine as combed wool, extruding from the damp ceramic in the cold, gone by afternoon.

Nothing else. The world's whole botanical argument, at present, fits in a strip of ground forty meters long, and it is fenced, and the fence is not to keep people out but to keep the trees from being *agreed with* too early.

## X. The Slow Ratification

One clause per generation. That is the rate.

The Hall of Ordinances is the largest room in the ward and the coldest, cathedral-cold, its ceiling lost in a haze of its own condensation, and the walls are the specification, written in relief, and the clerks work with cold cloths and speak at arm's length and are extremely careful never to say anything twice.

When a clause is settled, the ward shivers. Not violently — a settling, a long low tick that runs through every wall at once, and for about an hour afterward everything is very slightly more itself. Doors close more like doors. The pools become a little more committed to being wet. This happens perhaps twice in a life. Old people describe the two they remember the way people elsewhere describe wars.

Outside, the Blank moves four meters a year, and behind it the plain acquires grain, and slope, and the beginnings of a drainage pattern that is being copied, patiently, from nine millimeters of licensed gully in a courtyard.

That is what the ward is. Not a district of a city. There is no city. There is a seed the size of a hamlet, packed to bursting with every instance of everything, kept just cold enough to hold its edges, damp with the first weather, quiet with the first sound, lit by the one thing that has been finished — and out of it, outward, at four meters a year, the world is being written.

The Gully carries its liter an hour down to the Anticipated Sea. The pool is nine centimeters deeper than it was when the ward was extruded. The figure at its head leans on its rail and waits to be a harbor.
