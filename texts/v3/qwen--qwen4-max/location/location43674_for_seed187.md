## The Bell

You do not see it coming. That is the first thing. The plain runs out flat in every direction — laser-graded, drained, cropped to a single even green — and the sky sits on it like a lid of luminous grey, and there is no rise, no hedge, no shadow, nothing to indicate that the ground is about to stop. The tractor's turn-row is the only warning, a stripe of uncut ground where the blade lifts, and then the crop ends and there is a hole in the world eighty-four metres long and sixty-one across, and you are standing at the edge of it with your boots in the wheat.

On the drainage board's schedule it is listed as unimprovable. The men at the pump house call it the Bell.

---

The walls go down steeply, undercut in places, and they are not stone. They are not soil either. Every surface of the bowl — every ledge, every overhang, every lump of collapse rubble left over from an event that finished before there were people to hear it — is bound in living tissue: a rind, two fingers thick in the dry spots, a forearm's depth where the seeps run. Moss first, the dendroid kind that grows in little dark trees of its own, and over that the liverworts with their snakeskin tiling, and out of that the hart's-tongue ferns, glossy undivided straps a hand long, hanging in tiers like the tongues of a hundred small green bells. Roots come down through it from the lip — ash, alder, grey willow, a sycamore leaning out to see — and where those roots meet the wall they flatten and weave and felt themselves into the mat, so that the whole bowl is bandaged. Under the bandage there is limestone breccia and one buried glacial erratic, a shoulder under a blanket. You can go the entire circumference and never see a bare stone. There is no rock exposed here. There has not been for a very long time.

Where the seeps come through, the moss has turned to stone itself. Calcareous water, charged with lime from the old rock, gives up its carbonate on contact with the plant, and the plant takes the shape of the deposit and keeps growing on top of it: tufa, warm grey, porous as bread, sixty percent air, full of the moulds of leaves that fell when the plain still had a river on it. Break a piece and it is light in the hand and full of small holes and it smells of wet chalk. Half the wall is plant becoming stone. Half is stone that remembers being plant. They are the same wall.

---

And inside all that living material: nothing.

Thirty-odd thousand cubic metres of air, and almost none of it is occupied. Eleven root threads hang from the lip and stop, having failed to reach anything. Three white spherical spider cocoons are suspended on the north undercut, each the size of a plum, spaced four metres apart. There is a column of winter gnats in the middle of the bowl that has been in the middle of the bowl for as long as anyone has looked, hanging motionless because the air is motionless. There is one wren. There is a feather caught in a fern.

That is very nearly the inventory. A tenth of a gram of living matter per cubic metre. You could shout into it and the shout would find nothing to hit until the far wall.

The air does not move because there is nothing to move it. Air, water, moss, tufa, root and rubble all sit at the same ten and a half degrees; a thermometer set anywhere in the bowl reads the same to within a tenth. No gradient, no convection, no draught. Cool, not cold — breath stays invisible, but the stone takes the heat out of your palm in three seconds. The humidity is ninety-six percent and stays there. Everything sweats. The walls are permanently beaded, and the beads grow and let go and fall straight down, without deviation, into the pockets on the floor.

The light is entirely ordinary. Bright overcast, ten thousand lux at the lip, no shadows anywhere, no gloom, no shafts, no mystery. It is a well-lit empty room with the roof removed. It looks like an afternoon in late March that has decided to remain one.

---

The floor is not a floor. Fourteen metres down, the bowl narrows and the roots and tufa shelves have bridged it, a lattice you can walk in some places and see through in others: a mat over a void. Nine basins are set into it, none wider than a bathtub, each holding water at exactly its own level, seep in and evaporation out, the overflow going down through the gaps. Below the mat there are eleven more metres of absolutely nothing, and at the bottom of that, a black sheet of water that has not risen or fallen a centimetre in forty years.

Do not go down there. The air beneath the mat is heavy with carbon dioxide off the rock and will not keep you. And do not trust the mat where it looks best: the thinnest place is directly under the acoustic focus, where the bowl gathers every sound and puts it inside your skull, and it is very tempting to stand there.

---

Because the place is loud. Astonishingly, disproportionate loud, given that it contains essentially nothing.

There is no wind sound at all. Nothing rustles. Every decibel here is made by an animal, by water, or by machinery.

The wren is nine grams and puts out ninety decibels at a metre, a silver chain of notes thrown out at a rate no ear can count, and the bowl doubles it and hands it back. A robin answers from the ivy on the lip, thinner, more reasonable, endlessly. Drops fall into the nine basins with nine different pitches — the full ones give a *tock*, the shallow ones a *pip* — and the drops that miss the basins go through the gaps into the void and come back six seconds later as a soft collapsing tail of reverberation from underneath the world.

And under all of it, the note. A low G, the bottom line of the bass clef, ninety-eight or a hundred cycles a second, felt in the sternum and the back teeth before it is heard. It is the second harmonic of the grid. The substation is eight hundred metres east across the fields; its transformers hum, the hum comes through the ground as much as through the air, and the void beneath the mat happens to have a resonance sitting almost exactly on top of it. The Bell is being rung, continuously, by the national electricity supply. It has been rung since the substation was commissioned. It does not stop at night.

Then, after ten minutes, you notice the other thing: the note swells and fades, swells and fades, on a period of eleven seconds. Two pumps at the pump house a kilometre and a half away, running at very slightly different speeds, their tones drifting in and out of phase. Eleven seconds. It has been eleven seconds since before you were born. It is the only pulse this place has, and it is not a heartbeat, and nothing here is alive to it.

---

Everything that lives here lives on the skin.

Fifty to a hundred thousand arthropods in any square metre of the wet moss: springtails in drifts like spilled salt, oribatid mites armoured and glossy and slow, three years to a generation, woodlice the size of a pinhead and pink as a fingernail. Nematodes swim in the water films. Tardigrades graze the films and dry to glass when the films fail, which they never do. Door snails climb the tufa with a bone shutter folded inside their shells. Fungal threads work the shed moss at exactly the rate the moss sheds it. In the dark undercuts, three great cave spiders wait beside their white balloons, and fungus gnats breed in the bat droppings below, and the whole thin engine runs on nutrient arriving from outside.

That is the part worth sitting down for.

The ammonia works is nine kilometres north. You can see its steam plume on the skyline, indistinguishable from the stratus, and its flare is a pale orange nothing in the daylight. Its nitrogen comes down on this bowl in the drizzle, four days in ten, a wetness rather than an event, and the rind grows luxuriant on it. The cooling ponds breed midges in numbers that have no natural analogue; the lesser horseshoe bats that hang in the throat — the old choked cave passage, a mouth a metre wide on the north side of the void — go out at dusk and eat those midges over the plain and come back and deposit the plain's fertility on the floor of the void, where it feeds the gnats, which feed the spiders. And the pumps hold the water table at twenty-three metres down, which holds the seeps at their constant rate, which holds the tufa accreting at the exact rate it breaks and falls, which is the exact rate the water dissolves it and carries it away.

Mass in, mass out. Growth equals decay equals removal. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is lost. The untouched hole in the ground is entirely manufactured, and not one thing has ever been built in it. There is no fence, no sign, no bolt, no board, no pipe. There is no reason to put anything here. Machines cannot turn in it, the ground is liable, and the lip is where making stops.

---

The rock was laid down in a warm shallow sea. Mountains rose out of it and were taken away, grain by grain, over a span that makes the ice ages look like weather. This plain is what was left when there was nothing left to take: a surface that has spent everything, worn to a plane so nearly perfect that the surveyors use it to check their instruments. The cave beneath drained. The roof fell. The rubble found its angle. The last stone came down a very long time ago and nothing has moved since except water and moss.

This is the final form of the place. Not a stage — the end. The bowl is the last thing this rock will ever do, and it is not doing it any more. It has no history now, only maintenance. Day comes and goes; the light lifts and lowers; the bats pour out of the throat at the same minute every evening in the same number, and the wren stops, and the hum does not. It is not repetition as monotony. It is repetition as achievement.

If the pumps were switched off, the water would come up eleven metres in a season and the mat would drown and the Bell would go silent under a lake, and that would be a change, and it would be the only one available.

So it goes on: cool, damp, green to the last millimetre of its walls, empty as a held breath, deafening, and exactly the same size tomorrow.
