## The Cup

From the plateau there is almost nothing to find. Grass the color of old rope, a pavement of pale stone broken into slabs by frost that no longer comes, and then the grass stops a pace short of a lip, and the lip is round, and inside it is light.

No talus. No spoil heap, no raw scar, no rubble at the bottom. The hole is finished. Whatever fell here fell a long time ago and has since been taken away in solution, one molecule at a time, and what remains is the shape the water wanted: a room.

It is about seventy paces across the floor and forty high, and the opening above is offset, so the chamber is not a cylinder but a leaning cup, undercut on one side, flaring on the other. Stand at the rim and the far wall is close enough to see the individual scallops in it, each one a hand's breadth, each one a swirl left by an eddy of dissolving water ten thousand years dead. The stone is white. Not chalk-white, not bone — a dense, wet-looking white, case-hardened, a skin of recrystallized calcite that has been polished by condensation until it takes an image the way a spoon does.

The light comes down the shaft, strikes the floor, and goes back up. Then it goes up again off the wall, and across, and down. There are four and five bounces in here before the photons are spent. On an ordinary bright day the inside of the cup is brighter than the plateau above it, and there is no honest shadow anywhere except in the pockets and under the seven trees. Visitors squint. Visitors say the air looks *thick*, and it does, and it isn't; it's just full of returning light.

## Emptiness, and how to count it

The volume is enormous and the contents are almost nothing. This is the first thing to get right. You could hold the entire standing crop of this place in a cart: seven trees, one pool, a slot in the floor, nine long-armed animals, one tall one, and a great many snails spaced a handspan apart on twenty thousand square meters of wall, which is to say, on all that white, a scatter of pale dots you would mistake for mineral until one of them moved.

At noon the cup is at its emptiest. The brightness peaks and everything withdraws — into the solution pockets that honeycomb the walls, into the crystal-lined hollows where gypsum has grown out of the dry rock in needles and frost-flowers, under the trees, into the one deep scallop behind the seep. The floor at the white hour is a clean bowl of pink clay and dark chert nodules and it holds nothing at all that moves. A census taken then would say: sterile.

The census is wrong. There are something over four thousand snails, several hundred thousand midges in the air column at any moment, eleven thousand swifts, forty-odd shrimp, and the nine. It is simply that the room is very large, and everything in it has learned to be somewhere else when the light is worst, and everything in it is very far from everything else. Presence here is a rare event with a lot of space around it. Two lizards on a wall are a crowd.

## Damp

Water is a rumor that keeps being confirmed.

The shaded wall sweats. Every dawn, when the stone is still cool from the night and the air above the rim has warmed and taken up moisture, a film condenses on perhaps a third of the chamber's inner surface — invisible, not droplets, just a change in the way the white takes the light, a bloom you can wipe with a palm. It runs down. It runs down in sheets too thin to see, gathering in the scallops, overflowing them, and finding the vertical flutes, and by the time it reaches the floor it has gone into the clay, and the clay is cool, and there is a green line.

That green line is most of the vegetation. A band of moss and epilithic algae perhaps two fingers wide, following the seep-faces around a third of the room, and where it thins to nothing the snails still graze the bare rock for the film of cells that lives on it. Above the line, bare white. Below it, bare white. Seven trees. A single fern in a crack, one fern, forty years old, the size of a hand.

Rain arrives four or five times a year and falls straight down the shaft and lands in a circle on the floor about twenty-five paces across, and outside that circle the clay stays dry. You can see the ring for weeks afterward: a darker disc with a hard edge, and around its margin a wrack of fig leaves and midge bodies that has been washed into a perfect arc. Then the disc fades from the outside in.

At the low point there is a pool the width of a hat, ankle-deep, rimmed in a low collar of travertine it has built for itself out of its own evaporation. It is very clear and very bright and at midday it is unpleasantly warm. There are translucent shrimp in it, and a flatworm the color of a fingernail, and larvae hanging under the surface film. It has never been deeper. It has never dried. Income and outcome have been equal here for longer than there have been eyes to notice.

Above the pool, a single seep in the rock lets go of one drop every eleven breaths.

## The drop

The drop is the loudest thing in the cup and the only regular one.

The walls are hard and smooth and continuous, and the air is almost never moving, and the geometry is very nearly an ellipse. Sound in here does not die; it goes for a walk and comes back. A cough at the pool arrives at the far wall intact, and then arrives again. A pebble dropped from the rim is a catastrophe.

So nothing is loud. This is not manners. It is the oldest law of the place, and it is enforced.

The swifts have fringed flight feathers and make no sound at all in the air, eleven thousand of them turning through a hundred thousand cubic meters of light in total silence, and they feed on the aerial plankton that drifts in over the lip — midges, mites, pollen, the drifting young of things that live elsewhere. They roost in the solution pockets, in the pre-made hollows, and they build nothing. Nothing here builds. There are no webs, no nests, no burrows, no mounds, no stacked anything. The only architecture in the cup is the architecture of subtraction: the pockets, the flutes, the scallops, the throat. The water made all of it, and the water made it by taking away.

The snails rasp only when the rock is wet, because wet rock does not carry the sound. The lizards hunt the midges at the margin of the light with feet that are padded and fringed and set down like something being put to sleep. The nine knappers strike stone perhaps three times a season, and when they do, everything in the chamber stops for a full minute afterward, including the swifts, which stall and drop and catch themselves.

And there is the tall one.

It stands in the deep shade behind the seep on two long legs, pale as the wall, with a fan of stiff feathers around each ear-opening that it can turn independently. It is very old. It moves perhaps forty paces a day. It does not watch; the light in here is too much for watching. It listens, and what it listens to is the drop, and the intervals between the drop, and anything that arrives in an interval that shouldn't.

Every animal in this room, at some level of its body, is keeping the count. Eleven breaths. Eleven breaths. When something drinks at the seep instead of the pool, the count breaks, and the tall one comes out of the shade at a slow walk and takes it. Nothing drinks at the seep. Nothing has drunk at the seep for a very long time.

## The seven trees, and the coin

Direct sun comes down the offset shaft as a single ellipse, a coin of light about nine paces across, and it moves. Across the floor in a morning, up the eastern wall in the afternoon, off the rim entirely in the late day. Over the year its path shifts north and south and loops back on itself, and if you were to keep the whole annual track in your eye at once you would see a long leaning figure-eight laid across the floor and up the white.

The seven figs are rooted along it. Their trunks are not straight. Each one bends in a slow arc, and the arcs are not toward the light — they are toward where the light will be in a season's time. They grow ahead of it. They have been doing this for eleven hundred years and their shapes are a record of the sky's mechanics, and a fig at the northern end of the track is bowed almost to the ground and then up again, and the bow is exactly the shape of the year.

Their leaves are small and steeply held, edge-on to the noon coin, so that the canopy is less a shade than a sieve. Through the sieve the light goes, and each gap between leaves makes a pinhole, and each pinhole lays an image of the sun on the floor and the wall. Hundreds of them. Overlapping ellipses, all of them the same size, all of them the actual disc of the sun, thrown down and repeated across the white. When a cloud crosses the plateau the whole floor dims in the shape of that cloud. When the moon takes a bite out of the sun, the floor fills with crescents.

The snails navigate by these. Not by the sun — by its copies. A snail on the north wall will move to a particular ellipse and sit in it for a decade, and the ellipse will drift a few centimeters a year, and the snail will drift with it, and the two of them together are a clock that no one wound.

Their shells are white with a faint pink axis, and they are the oldest animals in the cup, and the growth banding in the largest of them records eleven hundred wet seasons. They mate perhaps once in a decade, all of them, at one place on the eastern wall, and the route to that place has been used by so many generations for so long that it is worn into the case-hardened calcite as a groove a finger deep. It is the only path in the chamber. It looks made. It is not made. It is worn, which is the only way anything is shaped here.

## The nine, and the throat

They are long-armed and slow and pale-eyed, with a shelf of bone over the eyes that works like a brim. Nine of them, each holding a rough quadrant of the wall, avoiding one another with great courtesy, meeting perhaps twice a year at the pool and sitting a body-length apart without touching. They sleep in a different pocket every night. They own nothing. They carry nothing — not a tool, not a food cache, not a young one; the infants stay in the high nursery pockets for their first fifteen years and are simply not seen, and then one day there are ten and eventually there are nine again.

They knap. The chert is right there: dark nodules weathering out of the dissolving limestone, studding the pink clay like drops of glass, blue-grey and liver-red, an inexhaustible supply generated by the slow removal of everything that isn't chert. A knapper picks one, strikes it against another, takes an edge, opens forty snails over the following month, cuts fig bark for the sweet cambium, and then walks to the low point of the floor.

At the low point, past the pool, there is a slot in the rock about a hand wide. Warm air comes out of it in winter with a faint sigh. It goes down into the dark, and it is the only dark thing in the room, and it is where the chamber's entire history has gone.

The floor creeps. A millimeter or two a year, clay and chert and leaf-fall and bone, sliding downhill on the wet, tended by a hundred million springtails and a slow armored isopod that pushes grains one at a time and never rests. Everything on this floor is in transit. Nothing accumulates. Nothing has accumulated for a hundred thousand years. There is no litter, no midden, no layer, no ruin; the room is clean the way a river is clean, which is to say it is not clean, it is *moving*.

And the nine put their finished tools into the slot. Every one. Edge spent or edge unspent, they carry it to the throat and let it go, and there is a small silence, and then a much smaller sound from far below.

You cannot tell whether this is thrift or instinct or manners. They do not appear to consider it. They do it the way you put your hand out when you fall.

## The minute

At the end of the day the coin climbs the western wall, reddens, thins, and goes off the rim, and the cup fills with a light that has no source, bouncing and bouncing, going pink and then lilac and then a blue that seems to come out of the stone rather than into it. The walls hold the day's warmth all night. The temperature in here varies by four degrees across a year. It is always the warmth of a mouth.

And for about ninety seconds at the hinge of dusk, the swifts speak.

All eleven thousand of them, at once, a hard bright chittering that fills the volume and comes back off the ellipse and doubles and triples, and the snails withdraw a millimeter into their shells, and the nine sit up in their pockets with their brims turned, and the tall one folds its ear-fans flat against its skull and waits it out. Then it stops, all at once, as if a hand had closed. The swifts go up over the lip into the dark to feed and will come back before dawn without a sound.

The chamber settles. The film begins to form on the cool wall, hours early, invisible. Somewhere a fig leaf turns two degrees to be ready for a sun that will not arrive until spring.

The pool takes the drop.

Eleven breaths. Eleven breaths. The clay moves a millimeter toward the throat, carrying with it a flake, a shell, a leaf, a small bone, all of it going down into the dark at the speed of patience, and the white walls give back the last of the light, and the room, which has been the same room for longer than there has been anything to look at it, goes on being exactly itself.
