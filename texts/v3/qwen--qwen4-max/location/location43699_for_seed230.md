## The Sill

Between two summits worn down to the shape of knuckles, the rock makes a saddle four hundred paces long and perhaps a hundred and eighty across, and that is the whole of the place. At either end the ground simply stops. It tips over into whiteness — into the cloud sea, which lies below all day like a second, softer country, breathing slightly against the cliffs and never climbing higher than the lip. Above, the two peaks are bare grey limestone, rounded, finished. There is no snow on them. There has never been frost here. Nothing in this place is broken by ice; everything in it is dissolved by patience.

The saddle is a stripped pavement: broad slabs of pale stone separated by fissures you could put your arm into, the slabs fluted and dished and dimpled by a very long argument with rain. Every dish holds a saucer of water. Every fissure holds a black seam of moss at its wet end and nothing at its dry end. Walking across it you go from pool to pool with dry stone between, and the pools are the size of plates, and the dry stone is warm at noon and cold at dawn, and this alternation is the only weather the Sill has ever recorded.

## The Drinking

There is no spring. There is no stream. The water arrives as air.

An hour before sunrise the cloud's upper fringe comes over the windward lip — not a fog rolling in so much as a slow rise in the whiteness, as if the lower country were filling a cup. The keepers call it the drinking. The air it brings is a little warmer than the rock and heavy with wet, and the rock, which is enormous and slow and holds the year's mean temperature the way a cellar holds it, stays cold. So the whole town sweats. Water beads on the underside of lintels, on the north faces of posts, on the polished cheeks of the combs; it gathers, runs, and is caught.

The combs are eleven rows of thin stone slabs set edge-on to the wind on the windward shelf, two hundred and forty of them, each one worn by generations of hands and sand into a profile that no longer improves. Water clings to them, sheets down them, and drops into a channel at their feet. They are scraped of lichen on a forty-day rotation so the stone stays smooth; the lichen never gets larger than a thumbnail. The old combs, three earlier designs that turned out to be worse, still stand on the west shelf in their abandoned rows. Nobody has taken them down. Taking them down would cost more than leaving them.

Everything the combs do not catch, the pavement catches. Not one square pace of the Sill is ungraded. Every slab has been dressed to a fall, every fall leads to a runnel, every runnel to a channel, every channel to the cisterns. The channels are shallow, hand-cut, and lined with a smooth cream-coloured deposit that they laid down themselves. The town has no roofs to speak of. The town *is* a roof.

## What the Water Builds

The water arrives hungry. Condensate is distilled, empty, greedy for stone; it takes a little carbonate from the combs and the pavement as it goes, an amount too small to weigh, and it carries that theft downhill into the dark.

Three cisterns are cut into widened fissures under the middle of the Sill. The largest is a corbelled hall of pale porous blocks, thirty paces long, with piers that have grown their own skins of flowstone until they look like candles left too near a fire. A single shaft admits a slab of daylight that moves across the floor and is gone. The water is so clear it is only visible where it moves. Frogs live in it — small, pale, unhurried, the same six or seven dozen every year — and the drips from the vault fall into it with a chord that took two centuries of adjustment to get right. The menders used to cut the drip-stones to change their pitch. Nobody has adjusted one in living memory. The chord is finished.

The overflow leaves the hall and goes out to the fan below the Spill, a shallow apron of gravel and sedge where the water spreads, degasses, and gives back everything it stole. It precipitates onto whatever is there. The keepers put baskets there: sedge-and-hair moulds, block-shaped, set in the sheet of flow and left. Eleven years to fill a mould. Forty-four moulds in the fan at any time, in every stage from a fingernail's thickness to a solid grey-white brick. Four come ripe each year. Four blocks a year is exactly what the town spends on repairs.

They call it the slow quarry. It is the only quarry. The stone of the Sill dissolves off its high surfaces, crystallises at its low edge, and is carried back up the stairs on people's backs. That is the whole engine of the place: a fountain of its own substance, running so slowly that a generation is one turn of it, pumped by porters. The water leaves the Spill clear and satisfied and falls into cloud, and the cloud comes back up at dawn, hungry again.

## The Town

Thirty-one buildings stand on the pavement, low, thick-walled, corbelled, roofed with slabs sealed by moss and their own calcite. They are set far apart, each on its own boss of rock, so that between any two there is a stretch of open stone with pools in it. Three hundred doorways were cut in the older layers beneath and around; sixty-one are open. The rest are sealed with a single well-fitted block, some because the room behind is rubble, some because the room behind is a person who died and whose house was closed after.

Sixty-one people. Nine goats in a walled yard, one bell among them. Eleven wind-pruned trees in a deep grike near the eastern lip — the only place in the Sill where you can stand and have green above your head, a room the size of a chapel, coppiced on a rotation so old the rotation is simply called the rotation. Sedge in the fan for baskets. Cress and a knobbled tuber in the seep terraces, grown under gravel mulch in the wet seams. Grey crust lichen, orange lichen, black lichen, and a snail that eats the first and is eaten in turn, hooked out of the pools with a copper wire by children who are given this job at four years old.

Two choughs work the updraft at the Spill. Four swift nests in the hollow posts of the combs. A lizard on the sunny steps of the hall, the same lizard, it sometimes seems, for thirty years. Nothing else. There is not enough here for anything else.

And yet nothing here is untouched. Every surface has been graded, channelled, dressed, inscribed, or worn. The infrastructure is total and the population is thin: a city built almost entirely of ground, with a few houses scattered on it like objects left out overnight. You can stand at either end and see the whole settlement, and go an hour without seeing a person, and never once be out of sight of somebody's work.

## The Day

Cool, always: cool enough that breath shows in the drinking hour, never cold enough to crack a jar. Cool enough that the stone stays behind the air all day and keeps sweating long after the fog has burned off. The damp is intermittent and total — dry to the touch at noon, running at dawn, and always, everywhere, the smell of wet limestone, which is the smell of a cellar, of a coin held in the mouth.

The cloud withdraws by mid-morning and the light comes down hard through thin air onto pale rock, and the Sill becomes painfully bright. So the work is shaped by that: the heavy carrying in the grey hours, the bright hours for sleeping in the cistern hall's outer chamber and for drying sedge, the second work at dusk when the dew starts and the pavement goes silver and the stars come out enormous and steady. Wooden visors, narrowed eyes, a town that squints.

Mostly it is quiet. Water entering water. The hiss of fog through the combs, which is not wind so much as the sound of air being combed. A rake on stone. The bell, twice: once at the drinking, once at dark. Feet on steps worn into hollows so deep you walk them like a ladder. Once a day, for about a minute, the choughs at the Spill, and then that is over.

## The Ledger

On the wall of the hall's outer chamber there is a benchmark and a copper rod, and beside them a thousand years of tally marks. Cistern level. Comb yield in jars. Wear on the sill-stone at the Spill, measured against the rod. Depth of the plunge pool, which stopped deepening when the water learned to leave clear. The numbers do not change. They have not changed within the precision of the instruments, which are the best instruments the keepers know how to make, and the keepers have gotten very good at making them, because there is nothing else to get good at.

The ledger is not a history. It is a proof. Only the names in the roster column differ, and there are always sixty-one of those.

This is what the place is: a landscape that has arrived. The slopes above have reached their final curvature. The fissures have widened to the width the water can still service. The combs have converged on a shape no hand can better. The drips are in tune. Production equals repair; growth equals scraping; the snails eat exactly the lichen the light permits. Even the Long Stair — four hundred and eleven steps down from the eastern lip into the cloud, built by somebody for arrivals — is swept every ninth day by whoever holds that rotation, and nothing has come up it in the ledger's memory, and it is swept anyway.

At the fan, in the third row, there is a mould with a hand's width of stone in it. It was set the year a woman named in the roster was born. It will be ripe when her daughter is grown, and it will be carried up the stairs on somebody's back and laid into a wall that is already there, replacing a block that the dawn has been quietly eating since before the town had a name.
