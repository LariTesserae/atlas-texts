Kneel, and the climate changes at your knee.

At the floor it is thirty-eight degrees and the air is a wet cloth. Ten centimetres up, thirty-one. At your waist, twenty. At your face, when you stand, the air is thin and cold and smells of nothing at all, and your ears do the thing ears do at altitude, and the light gets hard. There is a skin over this whole country, forty centimetres thick, and everything that lives here lives inside it, and above it is the ordinary alpine world where nothing can stay warm without paying for it.

The floor is warm because the rock beneath it is warm. Not sun-warm. Two kilometres down there is a body of stone that has not finished cooling since before the last three glaciations, and it leaks upward through a thin lid of fractured crust at something like a hundred times the ordinary generosity of the earth. Above that, five thousand metres of atmosphere that has already given up on holding heat. So the heat arrives from below, passes through the wet floor, and leaves immediately for space. The country is a gap between a furnace and a vacuum. Everything here is built to stand in that gap.

---

It is a floor, properly: a long graben sunk in a high plateau, sixty kilometres by twenty, stepped down through three basins, each basin held behind a sill of its own deposited silica. The rim stands another fifteen hundred metres above, glaciated, blue-white, dead. From the middle apron you can see the whole hall — the pale terraces of the upper basin, the brown peat flats, the dark green punctuation of the cold mouths, the outlet sill where the water leaves in a long white staircase and vanishes into a gorge. Two days to walk it end to end, if you could walk it, which you can't, not continuously; the ground scalds and the air starves you and there is nowhere dry to sleep.

And there is a ceiling. The vapour rising off the floor climbs into cold thin air and condenses at thirty or forty metres, and it condenses *flat*, because the condensation level is a single altitude across the whole basin. A white lid. Underneath it the air is clear and hot and you can see for kilometres; overhead is a featureless bright plane like a diffuser panel, and the rim peaks stand up through it as islands. The lid rises at noon and settles at dusk and dissolves entirely after midnight. Under it, the light is even and shadowless and comes from everywhere, roughly the same at the pan edge as at the pan centre, which is exactly the light you would install if you were growing something on a floor and wanted no wasted lux.

Nothing here has been installed.

---

Two waters meet on this floor and they do not mix quickly.

The first comes up: juvenile, mineral-loaded, hot, carrying dissolved silica and sulphide and carbonate and a suite of metals it will drop as soon as its pressure or its temperature changes. It arrives through a plumbing of fractures and porous sinter, and it arrives *distributed* — not through a hundred throats but through some tens of millions of pores, so that the whole floor bleeds steam quietly instead of roaring through a few pipes. This is the reason the place is silent. Pressure that would elsewhere gather into a geyser's shout is here bled off through a diffuser the size of a county. The floor is a muffler.

The second water falls: rain, and more importantly fog-drip, cold, acid, nearly pure. It sinks into the peat and the porous sinter and floats there on the hot water beneath, a lens of cold fresh water lensed over hot brine, thickening under the peat flats, thinning over the thermal highs. This lens is the whole ecology. Every root in the country lives inside it. Where it is two metres thick there is forest; where it is a hand thick there is marsh; where the hot water breaks through it there is a scald, a bare white pan, a kill patch, and nothing at all but mats.

The map of these patches changes every season. The ratios have not changed in eleven thousand years.

---

The plants are lagged.

The dominant thing is a rosette on a column: a metre-thick trunk of packed dead leaf bases, three or four metres tall, crowned with a rosette of long hairy silver leaves. The dead leaves do not fall. They hang, dry and felted, sheathing the trunk from crown to root, and inside that jacket the pith stays at a steady nine degrees while the air outside goes to frost and the ground below runs at sixty. It is insulation on a standpipe. It exists for the same reason and it works to the same tolerance, and if you push a hand into a broken jacket you feel the specific dry warmth of a boiler house.

The leaves are condensers. The hairs nucleate fog; the fog runs down the leaf channels; the channels deliver to the collar of moss at the base; the moss holds it and lets it go slowly into the lens. A mature stand pulls four hundred litres a day out of the air per hectare that the rain gauges never see. This is the loop the whole country runs on: the floor's heat makes the plume, the plume makes the lid, the lid is the coolant supply, and the plants harvest their own coolant out of it and pipe it down to keep their roots from cooking. They manufacture the thing that saves them. They have been doing it long enough that the arrangement is no longer clever. It is simply the shape of the place.

Between the columns, everything is occupied. Moss in six strata. Liverworts on the moss. Filmy ferns on the liverworts, one cell thick, living in air you could not dry with a desiccant. Glass sedge, whose blades take up silica and are, at the pan edges, actually plated — the plant growing its own mineral sheath as it grows, so that old blades ring faintly against each other and a wind through a sedge bed sounds like a chandelier being carried. Under the peat flats, roots interlock so thoroughly that a spade goes in and comes up with a brick. Density here is not crowding. It is *lamination*.

And in the cold mouths — the places where a fracture delivers cold meteoric water back to the surface in the middle of all this heat — there are pockets of tree fern and small gnarled trees with epiphytes hanging in beards, and in the pools beneath them, animals whose nearest relatives are nine hundred kilometres away and two thousand metres lower and separated from them by an ice age. These are refugees. They came up here when the lowlands warmed, found a cold spring in a hot floor, and stayed. There are eleven such pockets. Two of them contain species that exist nowhere else in the world and occupy, in total, about the area of a large room.

---

The floor is electrified, mildly and constantly.

In the peat, where sulphide from below meets oxygen from above, there are bacteria that grow in filaments and conduct electrons along their length — living wires, centimetres long, coupling a reducing depth to an oxidising surface, running current through sediment that would otherwise have to wait for diffusion. Multiply by a peat flat forty kilometres square. There is a measurable voltage across a hand's span anywhere you push probes in, and a slow telluric drift where the mineralised water shorts one redox cell against another. Add the streaming potential of water forced through porous silica, and the static that gathers on every hair and spine when the lid thins.

Nothing sparks. Nothing glows. It is a low-voltage grid with no load worth speaking of, and mostly what it does is sit there, humming below the threshold of any sense you have. But there is a flatworm in the outflow channels that turns reliably along the field lines, and a snail that grazes the mats preferentially where the current density is highest, and if you stand very still in the thin dry air before dawn you can feel your arm hairs lift and settle, lift and settle, as the plume pulses.

---

The kiln birds do not sit on their eggs.

They are heavy, dull, chicken-shaped things with enormous feet, and they walk the aprons in the morning with their heads down, probing. The beak is a thermometer. What the bird is looking for is the thirty-three-degree isotherm — the depth at which the ground holds the temperature she wants — and she finds it to within a few centimetres, and she digs a shaft to it, and lays, and backfills, and leaves. She will return every second day to probe the shaft and add or remove spoil, opening or closing the vent, holding the incubation within a degree and a half for nine weeks. It is process control. The chicks hatch fully feathered, dig out, run within an hour, fly within a day, and never meet a parent. There are, at a rough count, forty thousand shafts on the upper apron alone, and the sex ratio of each cohort is a record of that season's ground temperature, written in birds.

Below them, in the outflow channels where the water runs at fifty and the mats are orange and green and slick as paint, there are flies whose larvae graze the mats and whose adults carpet the sinter in bands so thick the rock looks stippled. Rails walk the pans on absurd toes and eat the flies. Harriers quarter the lid's underside. And there is a snake, thick-bodied and slow, that hunts along the pan margins with a pit organ that should be useless here — the entire floor is a thermal blaze, no warm-blooded thing stands out against it — and which has instead become an instrument for reading *shimmer*. It watches the rising vapour. A body moving through it leaves a wake, a brief hole in the curtain. The snake does not see the rail. It sees the rail's disturbance in the steam, and strikes at the hole.

At night the beetles come to the flowers.

Certain rosettes here do not offer nectar. They offer heat: the flower chamber is plumbed, via a spongy stalk, into the warm ground, and holds itself at twenty-two degrees while the air outside is at minus four. The beetles arrive at dusk in numbers, crawl in, and stay the night in a warm room, mating, dozing, dusting themselves. At dawn the chamber closes and they are turned out, carrying pollen, and go and find another warm room. The plant pays no sugar. It rents out the furnace. It has been renting it out for a very long time.

---

The quiet is engineered, in the sense that a lined room is engineered.

Peat and moss are excellent acoustic lagging; the floor absorbs almost everything that strikes it. Thin air couples sound badly in the first place. And the pressure release is distributed, so there is no roar — only a hiss made of ten million separate hisses, each below hearing, summing to something you feel in your sinuses rather than hear. Under that: drip. Every rosette dripping into its own collar, all across the basin, a rain that never falls from the sky and never stops. Under that, the tick and ping of sinter contracting at dusk, the sound of a kiln cooling, coming up through the soles of your boots.

Sound does not carry. A rail calling thirty metres away sounds like it is calling from another room with the door shut. The rim sheds ice a kilometre up and you hear a soft thump, like furniture moved upstairs. Your own pulse is obnoxious. When something loud happens — and it does, a vent seals with fresh silica, pressure gathers behind the seal for a season, and one morning a new pipe blows out a crater nine metres across and throws mud over half a hectare — the loudness is obscene, physically shocking, and over in forty seconds, and then the quiet comes back like water closing over a stone. There are perhaps three such events a decade. The craters heal in a century. There are four hundred healed craters in the stratigraphy.

---

Because there is a stratigraphy, and it is a ledger.

The floor sinks. The body below cools and contracts and the whole graben founders, slowly, a millimetre and a bit a year. And the water arriving from below carries silica, and drops it as it cools, at very nearly a millimetre and a bit a year. Deposition and subsidence have been in balance since before the species here were species. The floor keeps itself level. It has been keeping itself level while two hundred metres of interbedded sinter and peat accumulated beneath it — white mineral, black organic, white, black — a laminated record of every wet century and every dry one, every cold-mouth migration, every eruption, every ash fall from the rim volcanoes, every pollen grain from the ice-age forests that used to stand where the lowland desert is now.

Nothing here is raw. The terraces have the settled, sagging, self-similar look of a thing that has been solving the same problem for a hundred thousand years and has stopped trying anything new. The outlet sill has held its level through four glacial cycles. The drainage is mature to the point of tedium: every basin spills at exactly one point, and has always spilled there, and the sinter dam grows upward as fast as the water cuts down. This is not a landscape being made. It is a landscape in its own state, running.

---

At night the lid dissolves.

Cold air comes off the rim glaciers and drains down the escarpment and pours across the floor, dry as an oven, and pushes the whole plume sideways and out the gorge, and by two in the morning the ceiling is gone and the sky is open and the stars are the appalling hard stars of five thousand metres. The floor keeps steaming into a vacuum. Everything not touching the ground freezes. Frost forms on the rosette spines, on the sedge blades, on the dead-leaf jackets, on your eyebrows and not on your boots — the ground is a stove and the air is a freezer and the only things that get frost are the things that have insulated themselves from the stove. The country is, for four hours, silver.

Then the sun comes over the rim, and the frost goes off the spines in a shower of drops into the collars, and the ground plume thickens and climbs and finds its level again, and by ten the white ceiling is back at thirty metres and the light is even and shadowless and everywhere the same. The beetles are turned out. The kiln bird is probing her shaft. The flies lift off the pans in a stippled cloud and the rails walk into it.

Nothing has been built here. Nothing could be. There is no dry wood, no dry ground, no fuel, no shelter, and the water plates everything it touches: a steel object set down on the upper apron would be filmed in silica inside a fortnight and indistinguishable from the floor inside two years. The place does not forbid artifacts. It absorbs them. It has no use for anything that is not either growing, dissolving, or being deposited.

It is a works with no workers — a heat engine of rock and sky, taking a temperature difference of eighty degrees and turning it into convection, cloud, rain, coolant, warmth for rent, and a stable thirty-three-degree line at forty centimetres' depth. The output is weather, and the weather is spent immediately, on everything alive. It has run continuously, at load, without a shift change and without a whistle, for two hundred thousand years.

You can kneel in it for a while. Your knees will be too hot and your face will be too cold, and your body will be loud in your ears, and the floor will go on quietly making itself level beneath you.
