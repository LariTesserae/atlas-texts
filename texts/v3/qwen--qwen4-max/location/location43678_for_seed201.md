# The Cup

You come over the rim at midmorning and the light hits you like a hand pushed against the chest.

There is no haze up here to soften anything. The sky overhead is not blue so much as a dark cobalt going black at the zenith, the color of deep water, and the sun in it is a white hole rather than a lamp. Below the rim, a bowl of raw pale stone lies open to that sun, and the snowfield at its head throws the light back up, so everything is lit twice — from above and from underneath. Your shadow is not dark. The underside of your jaw sunburns. The insides of your nostrils sunburn. The people who come here wear goggles of scraped bone with a slit cut in each, and even so they squint, and even so their eyes water and the tears freeze at the corners.

The bowl is small. You could walk its lip in a morning, if you were careful and did not stop, and you would stop. It is one feature, one hollow, a scoop taken out of the mountain's flank — a cirque, the shape a thumb makes pressed into dough. It has a floor, a headwall, a lip, and a throat where the lip breaks and the scree spills out toward the valley four thousand feet below. That is all of it. There is no elsewhere here. Stand in the middle and you can see every part of the place at once, which is a thing that almost never happens in a landscape, and it makes the bowl feel less like terrain than like an interior. A room with the roof taken off.

The stone is young. The ice that scooped this out let go of it only a few centuries ago, and the rock has not yet learned to be old. Nothing is rounded. Every edge is a fracture edge, bright and unweathered, the feldspar still glassy where it snapped. The headwall is a fresh plucking face, three hundred feet of it, striped with black bands where a darker rock runs through the pale, and at its foot the last tongue of glacier lies dead under a quilt of rubble, melting back a few paces a year, going gray and gritty and ashamed. The scree is angular and loose and has not yet settled into any comfortable angle. It moves when you look at it. Grit runs down the walls in slow threads all day long, and once or twice an hour a slab the size of a door lets go somewhere and comes down in a bloom of white dust.

You do not hear it.

---

That is the first rule of the Cup: the air is too thin to carry sound. It is a poor medium, half the substance it should be, and noises die within a few paces of their making. A rockfall across the bowl arrives as a silent puff. A raven opens its beak six feet away and you get a thin dry click, like a knuckle cracked in another room. The wind comes over the rim in long pressure-waves and makes no noise at all because there is nothing here for it to blow through — no trees, no tall grass, no loose leaves — so it registers only as a cold hand laid on the cheek and taken away.

Into that silence your own body rushes. You hear your blood, a slow surf behind the ears. You hear the wet click of your eyelids. You hear your knees. When the temperature drops at dusk you hear the frost ticking in your own eyebrows. The herders who work here have a habit of pressing an ear to the stone, and it is not superstition: rock conducts, and with your cheek against the black band you can hear the whole mountain working — a distant irregular ticking as frost prys grains apart, and under that a sound like a swallowed word, which is meltwater moving somewhere deep inside, in the fractures, on its way to becoming a seep.

So the place is quiet, but it is not still. It is enormously busy. It is simply busy at a volume you were not built to receive.

---

The second rule: everything alive here is small, and there is a great deal of it, and it lives in islands.

The bowl looks empty. From the rim it looks like a place where nothing has happened yet — acres of glaring stone, a snowfield, a black tarn, and a scatter of dark green dots so far apart that the eye reads them as flaws in the rock. The volume of the place is nearly void. Ten thousand cubic yards of blazing thin air with nothing in them.

Walk down to one of the dots and kneel, and you are in a jungle.

They are cushions. Mosses and saxifrages and something with tight rosettes of glassy-haired leaves, grown into domes the size of a cart or a bed, so dense you can put your weight on them and they take it like turf. Inside a cushion it is warm — genuinely warm, twenty degrees above the air, because the plant has built itself as a heat trap, a low green body with no gaps for the wind to enter, holding the sun the way a stone holds it. Push your hand in to the wrist and the hand goes into summer. It is wet in there. It smells of pepper and rot and green water. Ferns grow in the seams between cushions, and a willow runs along the ground for thirty feet, trunk no thicker than a finger, two hundred years old, an entire forest lying flat where the wind cannot find it. You can step over a wood here. Under the willow there are fungi, and under the fungi there are springtails in numbers that make the meltwater look like peppered soup.

There are nine of these gardens in the Cup. Nine, in a bowl you could walk around in a morning. Between them: fifty, a hundred, two hundred yards of nothing but lit stone and running grit.

They exist where the seeps come out. That is the whole logic. Water moves through the fracture system of the mountain and emerges at nine points on the floor and lower walls, a few gallons an hour, and where it emerges the black bands of rock are wetted, and dark rock in this sun gets hot enough to hurt, and the water on it does something that visitors always misread.

It steams. It steams in visible ropes at noon, and if you put your hand into the steaming pool it is barely blood-warm, because up here the air presses so lightly that water boils at the temperature of a tired animal. The Cup boils cold. Children are brought up specifically to see this and are always disappointed.

The water pockets are small — a bath, a trough, a cart-sized tarn with a skin of ice on it every dawn that goes to slush by nine and to open black water by ten and back to ice by four. Hoarfrost grows on the north walls each night in feathers a finger long, a whole second garden made of ice, more elaborate than the green one, and it is gone before the sun clears the rim. Damp here is not wetness. It is a rhythm. Everything is dry or wet four times a day.

---

The third rule: the animals are silent because the air is silent, and because most of them are the size of a grain of rice.

Over each garden stands a column of midges. They hatch out of the seep pools in their tens of thousands and rise in the warm air coming off the black stone, and the rising air holds them in a shaft, and the shaft stands all day, twelve or twenty feet high, turning slowly, dense as smoke. From the rim, nine columns over nine green dots in an otherwise empty bowl. They look like pillars. They look like the supports of a building whose roof and walls have been taken away, and it is impossible to stand up there and not think it.

Around the columns, everything that eats. Dark butterflies with wings like scraps of soot, basking flat on the hot bands, opening and closing. A finch that has learned to hunt the columns in passes, taking a mouthful of insects each time like a swallow drinking. A shrike-thing that impales its catch on the fresh frost-shattered spines of the rock, so that certain pinnacles are hung with a hundred tiny drying bodies, a larder you can only see when you are close enough to smell it. Wolf spiders, black and furred, running on the snow itself, and their tracks are the most common writing in the Cup — more of them than all the human marks together. Voles inside the cushions, in tunnels through the moss, warm and wet and breeding. A white hare with black ear tips that sits out the noon in the shade of a boulder and does not move for hours. A fox that comes up through the throat at dusk. Two ravens, who are the only loud thing, and who are not loud.

Count the animals in one garden and you have counted more lives than in a mile of the forest far below. That is the trick of the place. It is not empty. It is concentrated. The emptiness is the space between the notes.

And it moves fast. Freeze at dawn, riot at noon, collapse at dusk — a whole day's spring and autumn compressed into eleven hours, every day for the ninety days the Cup is habitable. The vole generations turn over in weeks. The midge columns rise and fall with the shadow line. When the shadow of the headwall crosses a garden at four in the afternoon, the column above it comes apart in about ninety seconds, and everything in it drops into the moss, and the garden goes quiet and cold and waits, and across the bowl another garden is just now catching the light and starting to smoke.

---

There are marks.

Crescents of stacked slab, waist-high, built to break the wind at the nine sleeping places. A low house at the throat, dry stone, roofed with hide and weighted with stones, so low you enter it on your belly; it has been rebuilt so many times that its floor is a stratigraphy of hearths. A groove worn a hand deep into the lip rock where a rope has run for longer than anyone can say. Cairns above each garden, and on the cairns, pecked tallies — the count of gardens, kept and updated. The oldest tally says thirteen. There are nine now.

In the cache pit under the house: cakes, dark and dense, wrapped in gut. They sweep the columns with a net of hair and sinew at the hour when the midges are thickest, and press what they catch into cakes, and the cakes are half fat and keep for years, and this is the entire reason anyone comes up here. Eleven days a summer, three or four families, bone goggles and antler combs and a scraper left in a crack where someone set it down four generations ago. Under the overhang by the second seep there is an ochre handprint, small, a child's, and beside it a much later pecked spiral, and no way to know what either meant.

Most of the year the Cup has no people in it at all, and the windbreaks stand and the columns rise and the frost pries another grain off the headwall.

---

Because the place is not finished. That is the last thing about it. It is a wound, and the gardens are the first tissue in the wound, and the wound is still opening. Every winter the headwall loses a hand's breadth. Every few years a new fracture finds a new route and a tenth seep opens on bare stone and the moss begins, and somewhere else a seep fails and a garden goes brown and hollow and blows away in a season, and someone climbs up and pecks the tally.

The bowl is eating its own rim. In a few thousand years the headwall will breach, the tarn will drain out through the gap in an afternoon, the water table will drop below the roots, and all nine gardens will die in a week, and the columns will come apart for the last time, and there will be nothing here but very bright, very sharp, very quiet stone.

Until then: nine green dots, nine standing pillars of insects holding up nothing, and a silence so complete that when you press your ear to the black rock you can hear the mountain chewing.
