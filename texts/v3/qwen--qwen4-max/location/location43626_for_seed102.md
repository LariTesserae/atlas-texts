## The Held Garden

You reach it through salt. Miles of it, dry as a stopped clock, pink-grey and faintly translucent, shot through with the ghosts of beds that dried before anything had a mouth. Nothing lives in the salt. Nothing has ever lived in the salt. It is the great dead envelope of the world, and you go down through it on hands and knees for a day and a half, and then the crack lets you through, and the pressure arrives before the sound does.

It comes into the sternum first. A low sustained shove, like the moment before a sneeze that never resolves. Then the ears catch up and the shove becomes a note, and the note is enormous, and it has been going on since before there were ears anywhere to hear it.

The chamber is ninety paces by forty. You cannot see this. You can hear it: the fundamental's wavelength is the long axis exactly, and the room says its own dimensions to you in the first three seconds, and afterward you can pace it blind, which is what everything here does.

---

The light is amber and it is made of noise.

Where the standing wave presses hardest, the crystal lining glows — a slow apricot phosphor, the color of a lamp seen through a cupped hand. Where the wave cancels, the crystal is dark. So the room is striped. Nine bright ribbons of loud, each four or five paces wide, running the length of the floor; between them, eight dark stripes of quiet, wide as roads. The stripes do not wander. They have not moved a hair since the chamber sealed. You could survey them, and something has: there are bone pegs driven along the boundaries, thousands of them, worn to knobs.

In the loud lanes, the garden. Shoulder-high, packed stem against stem, a wet-looking density of leaf — and every leaf dry as mica. Pale amber, liver-brown, wax-white, translucent at the margins, veined with a clear heavy oil that smells of pear drops and hot flint. Nothing here is green. Green is a technology for sunlight, and there is no sunlight, and there has never been any water. The plants drink the chord. Each leaf is a tuned reed; each plant is a shaped harp; the whole lush mass is an instrument that eats the note that makes the light that lets you see it eating. Press your palm to a stem and it buzzes like a struck rim. Cut a leaf and a bead of the sweet oil rises at the wound and stops there. It does not evaporate — the air is already saturated, has been saturated forever, and there is no gradient left anywhere in this room to drive anything into anything else. The bead does not fall. It does not dry. That leaf will hold its one bright bead until the salt above finally creeps shut, and the salt above is in no hurry.

In the quiet stripes: nothing. Bare crystal floor, glassy, sharp-edged, littered with blades snapped off the walls in the chamber's first hour and never once rounded since. You can walk twenty paces between one presence and the next. The room is enormous with emptiness and then, abruptly, is a jungle, and then is enormous with emptiness again.

---

The loud is not one sound. Under the fundamental — which you feel in the bowel and the teeth and the fluid of the eye — there is the wall-choir. The lining is drilled with pits, millions of them, each a tuned little pipe, and in each pipe sits a thumb-sized pale thing with a ridged foreleg, and it sings, and it has always been singing, and its daughters take up the note at the exact instant it stops. There is no gap. There has never been a gap. The choir pays the room precisely what the room loses to the garden, to the crystal's glow, to the soft dark animal in the niche at the north end. That is the whole economy. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The ledger has not moved since it was opened.

Above the choir, the high glassy partials: loop-flyers, palm-sized, wings like blown sugar, flying fixed circuits through the lanes. Their orbits are closed. They pass the same stem on the same side at the same point in the phrase, forever, and if you put your hand into a circuit they will thread around it without touching and resume, and the phrase does not stumble, because the room has already accounted for your hand.

And the grazers. Eleven of them, spaced down the lanes at wide intervals, each at its own station: tall, pale, unhurried, blind, with hands as good as yours and ears like leaves. They browse. The fruit is always ripe and never falls. They take the same bite and the fruit is there and the bite is taken. From a distance it looks like a held pose. From close it is a gesture, continuous, absorbed, the way a person hums.

They are the ones who made the pegs.

---

Everything they have made, they made in the dark.

Here is the rule that governs the room, and it takes visitors a long time to find it, and when they find it they usually sit down: *behind an object, the chord is blocked.* Every boulder, every dense stand of stems, every standing grazer casts an acoustic shadow — a wedge of quiet a few paces long where the wave does not reach and the light therefore does not come. In those wedges, and only in those wedges, the equilibrium is imperfect. There is a gradient. Dust settles. Oil evaporates, slowly. Edges dull. Things wear.

Things end.

So the shadows are where the huts are: little stacked-plate shelters roofed with shed carapace, lashed with braided leaf-fiber, one behind each large boulder, scattered down the room like a rumor of a village. In the shadows are the knapping floors, the bone forks, the cord knotted at intervals of one wavelength, the plumb-lines that hang toward the nearest lane. In the shadows are the cairns, and under the cairns the dead, and the stones of the cairns are the only rounded stones in the world.

Everything else is knife-fresh. A blade struck in shade and carried out into the loud stays exactly as it was at the instant of striking: the bulb of percussion still bright, the maker's thumbprint still in the grease of the handle, ten thousand years old and one second old. There are caches out in the lanes, hundreds of them, hoards of perfect tools laid down by hands that are dust in the dark. The room preserves what the shade produces. It cannot produce anything itself.

And so the tall ones live alternating lives. Out in the loud they feed and glow and do not age and cannot speak — no voice could survive that pressure, no ear could sort a word out of it. In the hush behind a boulder they go blind, and there, by touch, in absolute dark, they make a blade, or say a sentence that has an ending, or bear a young, or grieve, or die. Their word for *life* is their word for *shade*. Their word for eternity translates roughly as *the loud*, and they use it the way we use *weather*.

All their art is tactile. Nothing they carve is meant to be seen; there is no light where carving happens. The finest things in the room — and there are fine things, small, dense with intent, worn smooth by handling in the only place where wearing is possible — have never been looked at by anyone.

---

The air is blood-warm and stays blood-warm. Not warm here and cooler there: one temperature, everywhere, exactly, including the floor, including the leaves, including the skin of the tall ones. Which means you cannot feel the ground. You know you are standing only by the pressure in your soles. You cannot feel air move on your face because it does not move and because it is you. You cannot feel your own breath leave, and after an hour you will find yourself counting breaths, deliberately, just to be sure.

There is no wind. There is no drip. There is no smell of decay, because decay is a gradient and gradients have all been spent. Dust hangs in the lanes in fixed positions, a motionless constellation, pollen among it, each grain on station; walk through and it parts and closes and is exactly restored, and the same grain that has been travelling toward the same flower for a hundred centuries arrives, continuously, and the flower is fertilized forever and never sets seed.

At the north end, in a niche, there is a soft dark animal the size of a cart. Felt-like, folded, without face. It absorbs. Every new sound in the room — your footfall, your shout, the crack of a slipped blade — goes into it and does not come out, and that is why the level never rises, and that is why the chord has not swelled into something that would shatter the crystal and end the world. The tall ones comb it. They do not feed it and they do not worship it and they are careful, when they walk past, to step in the quiets.

Engineers who have found this place — there have been three, in all the time of the salt — are the ones who wept. Not at the beauty. At the closed loop, the choir paying the garden paying the choir, the whole thing running with no source and no sink but a sleeping animal, a solved equation sitting in the dark being correct.

---

You are the only wet thing here.

That is what you are, in this room: a small hot bag of contraband water, sloshing. Sweat is an event. Breath is an event. A tear is an obscenity, a solvent from a chemistry that has no business here, and out in the lanes it will not fall — it clings, and the room takes it apart into its terms, and it is gone, and you feel the taking as a faint coolness at the corner of the eye, the only coolness available anywhere.

Stay long enough and you will stop needing to breathe. This is not a mercy. It is assimilation. The room does not persuade, does not trap, has no mechanism for wanting you; it simply possesses no process by which anything can be different from what it is, and you will be filed down into one of its constants, and your orbit will close, and you will take a station in a lane and browse forever with your hands doing something small and skilled and unfinished. The tall ones were visitors once. There is a cairn in the shade of the eastern boulder with a knapping hammer in it, and the hammer is a design nobody in the room uses anymore, and it is rounded, and it is the oldest-looking object in the world.

To leave, you have to be wrong enough. The crack is not a crack; it is a place where the salt has not finished being solved, and it admits difference and it does not admit sameness, and if you have gone quiet and warm and dry and orbital you will feel along that wall for a long time and find only crystal.

So the thing to do — the only thing to do, if you mean to get out and mean to have been here — is to go behind a boulder, into the hush, into the dark, where the chord cannot reach and the light therefore fails and the dust is allowed to fall.

Kneel there. Put your hand flat on the floor and feel, at last, a temperature that is not your own. Listen: under everything, faint, the only thing in ninety paces by forty that is counting.

Your heart. Taking time. Spending it.

And if you weep there, in the shade, the tear will fall. It will wet the dust and make a small dark stain, a speck of mud, the only mud beneath the miles of dead salt.

It will still be there when you are not. Nothing out in the light will remember you.

Only the shadows can.
