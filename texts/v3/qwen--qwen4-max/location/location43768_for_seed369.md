From outside, it is only a sound.

You cross the plateau in ordinary sunlight, over wind-scoured pavement where nothing grows but crust lichen and the occasional cushion plant wedged into a crack, and for the last half hour of the walk you have been hearing something you took for a river in a gorge you couldn't see. Then the ground lip rises a little, bare and gray and warm underfoot, and you step onto it and the whole thing opens beneath you: a shallow bowl, two hundred paces across, green as a struck bruise, full of noise.

It is not a river. There is no river. There is no channel of any kind. The sound is the floor.

---

The rim is a ring of bare clinkstone, a phonolite that rings when you hit it, weathered into plates and slabs the size of doors, laid over one another like a pile of dropped shields. It has been shedding into the bowl for a very long time. Below the lip, the plates go under the green and keep going, a stone pavement continuing beneath the whole basin, and everything that happens here happens on top of that pavement, and the pavement is not quiet.

Water comes off the rim in threads. Not falls — threads, thousands of them, seeping from the joint planes where the cloud has been wrung out overnight and running down the curved inner wall in sheets so thin they hesitate, break, re-form. Each thread finds a plate edge and strikes it. Each plate is a bell with a bad temper, damped by its neighbors, sounding one partial of one note. Ten thousand small hammers, none of them in a hurry, none of them ever stopping.

Under that: a grating. A wide, low, continuous shear, the sound of a hundred heavy things being dragged a very short distance, forever.

And under *that*, felt more than heard, a fundamental — a note down around the bottom of the chest, coming up out of the hole at the center of the bowl. The throat. It breathes. In the cool of the morning it draws; in the late afternoon, when the air over the plateau thins and warms, it exhales, and the exhale is warm and smells faintly of overripe fruit and wet iron, and it makes the whole basin into a stopped pipe.

Altogether it is as loud as a millrace. You have to raise your voice at the lip. You have to shout on the floor. And it does not vary — not by hour, not by season, not in any way you could catch. It is a loudness with no events in it. Applause that never resolves into hands.

---

Go down. The green resolves as you get into it, and the first surprise is how *empty* it is.

The floor is a mat: peat, living at the top, a continuous carpet of moss and sedge and liverwort, ankle- to knee-deep, saturated, warm, quaking underfoot. It goes everywhere, edge to edge, unbroken, so dense you cannot see the ground and so rich that a handful of it holds forty species. That is the lushness. It is all in the surface, a two-meter-thick skin.

The presences are few. Nine trees.

They stand far apart, at the spacing of columns in a nave built by someone who had heard of cathedrals only in rumor. Each is enormous — not tall so much as *wide*, buttressed out to the span of a small room, the bark smooth and wet and startlingly warm, the temperature of a living body. Each carries its own weather. Fog condenses on the crown and falls inside the tree as a private rain, hours after the cloud has gone. Each is loaded to the point of collapse with epiphytes: tank bromeliads holding liters of black water, orchids in the crotches, ferns growing out of the ferns, moss hanging in beards a meter long. A single tree here contains more life than the entire plateau outside. And between one tree and the next: forty paces of open, ringing, empty green.

The emptiness is not bareness. It is *space*. It is what lets the sound have somewhere to be.

---

The water table is at the surface everywhere and above it in places. Your footprint fills before you lift your foot. The pools are shallow and blood-warm and tea-dark and hold no fish, no tadpoles, nothing that swims. Sundews stand in them, red and patient, and bladderworts work the water below with their tiny traps, because there is almost nothing to eat here and the plants have learned to eat what little there is. In the tanks of the bromeliads: drowned leaves, a midge larva, sometimes a snail.

There is almost no fauna. Nine trees, and on perhaps four of them, snails — thin translucent shells banded like agate, grazing the film of algae off the leaves. A few dozen individuals in the whole bowl. They are hermaphrodites and they cannot self-fertilize, and they move at the speed of the thing they eat. To cross a tree takes a snail eleven years. Two snails on the same tree is the event of a century. Most of them live and die without ever touching another, and their shells, when they drop, go into the peat and dissolve, and are not seen again.

Once a year, for about an hour on one particular evening, midges come up out of the mat in a column and hang above the throat and go back down. That is the whole of the bowl's animal spectacle. Everything else that lives here has hands or roots.

---

Here is the rule, and once you see it you cannot unsee it, and it reorganizes everything.

**The mat is moving.**

It grows at the rim, where the seepage is cleanest and the light comes in sideways, and it dies at the throat, where it folds over the edge and goes down. Between those two ends it slides — the whole floor, the entire green skin, creeping inward over the stone pavement at about a hand's breadth in a month. A meter and a bit in a year. Nothing you could ever watch. Everything you could ever measure, if you had the patience to come back.

The trees do not ride it. They are rooted in the joints of the pavement below, and the mat flows *past* them, and so every tree wears a wake: a compressed ramp of peat piled on its rimward side, and on its throatward side a crescent moat where the mat pulls away faster than it can close, open black water standing in a scythe-blade curve. The buttresses are combed and split by the passage. The trees lean, imperceptibly, and grow eccentric wood, thicker on the upstream face, holding their ground against a current made of moss.

And anything loose rides.

A fallen leaf, a dropped stone, a dead snail, a flake of knapped clinkstone: it settles onto the mat and is carried. From the rim to the throat is about seventy years. A life. That is the retention time of this place. Nothing dropped here is still here in seventy years. Nothing dropped here is *gone* sooner.

This is why there is nothing built. Not because no one has tried — because a cairn set on the mat is pulled apart within a decade by the shear between the fast wet center and the slow dry edge; because a wall laid across the gradient tears itself into a line of stones that drift apart like a wake; because the floor will not hold a shape. It will hold a *surface* beautifully. It will not hold an arrangement. The bowl permits growth and it permits litter and it does not permit construction, and it has enforced this for long enough that the enforcement has become the landscape's own settled form, in balance, input equals output, the whole thing humming along in a steady state older than anything that could have wanted otherwise.

---

Something here knaps stone.

You find the flakes on the mat: small, sharp, a bulb of percussion on the underside, a fan of ripples, the unmistakable signature of a blow delivered by something that meant it. Phonolite breaks like glass and cuts like glass. There are hammerstones at the lip with battered ends. There are no tools — no finished thing, no cache, no haft, nothing kept. There is only the debris of an act.

The act belongs to the knapper: a slow gray arboreal animal with a long opposable thumb and fur that grows algae, so that it hangs in a crown looking exactly like a beard of moss, and moves at roughly the speed of the mat, and is therefore almost never seen. Four sightings in a hundred years, all of them accidental. It lives in the trees. It eats the fruit of the trees, which is armored in a rind like green stone and will not open by any other means, and which does not germinate unless it is opened. The tree needs the hands. The hands need the edge. The edge comes off the lip. The edge is used once, dropped, and begins its seventy-year walk to the throat.

So there is technology here and there are no artifacts. There is technique, continuous, ancient, unbroken — and no accumulation, ever, because the floor is a conveyor and the conveyor terminates in a hole.

Which means the flakes are an archive, and the archive is being erased at one end while it is written at the other.

Walk out from the throat toward the rim and the flakes underfoot get sharper. Near the center they are patinated to softness, their edges rounded by seventy years of acid peat, barely readable as made things. Farther out, crisper. At the rim, fresh. Distance is age. You can stand in a green bowl on a warm high plateau and read a chronology of somebody else's decisions off the ground, blurred and smeared by wherever the hands happened to be working that decade, running back seventy years and no further, because everything older has gone down the hole.

Below, in the dark, on the floor of the throat, there is a talus of flakes. It is the only place in this world where made things gather. It is not reachable. The warm fruit-smelling breath comes up through it every afternoon.

---

The light is ordinary. That is worth saying, because so much else here is not. Sun comes over the lip at a normal hour, the sky is a normal deep blue at this altitude, cloud comes up the escarpment in the afternoon and sits in the bowl until dawn and leaves. Shadows are crisp. The trees throw shafts. Nothing glows. The warmth is the plain warmth of a tropic afternoon at height, twenty-three degrees, no frost ever, the water in the pools as warm as your skin so that you cannot feel where your hand ends.

And the noise goes on. Loudest at night, when the drip is heaviest. It has drifted, over decades, as the plates beneath the mat shift and re-seat and change their damping — the bowl's chord has gone slowly down over the last century, a tone or so, too slowly for anyone to have noticed while it happened.

Once in a long while the fog fails. The cloud comes up thin, or not at all, for a season; the threads on the rim dry to a hundred from ten thousand; and the chime thins out to a sparse irregular ticking, and you can hear the shear underneath it, and the throat, and — for the first time — the actual size of the space. Everyone who has heard it describes it as the loudest thing that ever happened to them.

Then the cloud comes back and the bowl closes over the quiet like water over a dropped stone, and carries it, at a hand's breadth a month, toward the center.
