# The Slack

The river comes down out of the hills with a load of ground-up mountain and, at this elevation — eleven meters above a sea that has not decided on its level — loses its nerve. It spreads. It slows. It puts down what it was carrying, all at once, in a fan of grey mud that has no soil profile, no horizons, no rounded stones. Everything here is angular or formless. Dig a spade down and you get the same wet flour at every depth, still warm from being mountain, still settling. Geology has not happened yet. This is the room where it is being prepared.

The air sits at nine degrees and smells of wet wool and cut stems. Light is a standing rumour: a low ceiling of silt-haze that never quite becomes weather, the sun somewhere behind it like a coin under a tongue. Out on the flats, the pools hold the sky's grey without brightening it. Nothing casts a shadow worth the name.

And half-sunk in the mud, ribbed, black-green, breathing, is the town.

# The Rind

From outside it is a gourd the size of a hamlet — four hundred paces at the waist, perhaps, with a fluted skirt buried to the second rib. You can walk around it in three-quarters of an hour. People have. There is a path worn into the mud by nobody.

Its skin is a laminate: outer cuticle waxed to a dull shine, then a bark of pressed fiber, then the pale structural pith, then the felt. Along the crown stand eleven stacks, hollow lignified chimneys, exhaling a cool vapor that falls rather than rises and crawls down the ribs like something grazing. Iron fittings stud the flanks — hinge plates, sluice gates, a bolt-head the size of a dinner plate — and they are not fastened on. They are *grown*. The roots run through bog iron and silt copper, take them up patiently, and lay them down where the tissue wants a hard edge, bright and slightly wrong, like a tooth in a walnut.

There are lamps behind the port-holes. They are electric. The whole enterprise runs on the difference between mud and air: a few hundred millivolts across each root-hair membrane, ten million of them wired in series by grown copper thread, a battery the size of a parish. The arcs inside the fruit-glass housings burn a low green that does not carry. From the flats, at what passes for dusk, the town looks like a lantern with a bad wick, or a thing pretending to be a lantern.

Go in through the west sluice, where the river is admitted at a controlled rate. Duck. The felt takes the sound of your boots immediately, and keeps it.

# Inside

Inside is bigger. Not metaphorically. The perimeter is a forty-minute walk; the interior streets are three days of it, and the survey has never closed.

And inside is *empty*. This is the first thing that has to be understood, because it is not an absence — it is the material.

The organism did not grow a town. It grew *around* one. It laid its walls along the outside of streets that do not exist yet, vaulted its ceilings over squares that have not been paved, and left the volumes hanging there, held open, load-bearing nothings. Nine districts of them. The Long Hall, sixty meters of cool dry air with nothing in it at all, its floor a pressed leaf-felt so dense it rings under a fingernail, its ceiling a rib-vault of white root that has never touched anything. Foundry Street, wide enough for four carts, running north into a dark that eats the lamp-green in eleven paces. The Cistern Quarter. The Nine Steps, which arrive at their own beginning one floor cooler.

The mass is all in the surfaces. Two meters of living wall on either side of a void you could park a ship in. Per cubic meter, the town contains almost nothing: cool air, a faint mineral smell, drifting motes of moth-scale, and the pressure of an enormous amount of held-open shape.

It is damp rather than wet. Most of the volume is dry. The walls sweat along vertical seams where the vascular bundles run close to the felt, and the sweat gathers, and every eleven hours or so the vault-ceilings reach saturation together and it rains indoors — a fine, cool, unhurried rain that lasts twenty minutes and does not fall on the whole town at once, but moves through the districts in a slow band, west to east, like a shift going home. Between rains: dew, drip, the tacky give of felt underfoot, and one real watercourse, the admitted river, running a hand's-depth down the channel in the middle of Foundry Street, cold enough to ache the ankles.

You can see your breath in the Long Hall. You cannot see the far end of it.

# The Works

The industry is lithification.

The town drinks silt and makes stone. In the press-halls the sap runs at pressure through a lattice of lignified cylinders, and the mud is fractioned, and the fractions are laid down in the walls in ordered laminae — silica here, carbonate there, a black seam of organics between. There is a press that fires every forty seconds. You do not hear it. You feel it in the roots of your teeth and in the water of the channel, which shivers in rings, and then the felt takes it and it is gone.

This is why the geology outside is embryonic: the town is upstream of it. It is manufacturing the strata the basin will eventually be made of, and floating them out on the tide as seed-stone — grey bricks with a grain, the first honest rock in a hundred kilometers, sent downstream to become a coast that has not been born.

It runs the other way, too. In the deep halls the laminae are laid in reverse order. The wall behind the Number Three condenser carries a band of tomorrow's silt, identifiable by the pollen in it, which has not fallen yet. The drip in the Cistern Quarter strikes the water about a fifth of a second before it detaches. Nobody who has worked here would call this a malfunction. It is simply that the works are downstream of their own product, and the product is time-bearing.

There is a maintenance schedule inscribed in graphite on a hanging leaf in the foreman's alcove. It is current. It is in a hand no one has seen. The foreman's coat is on its peg and is warm.

The second product is silence, and the silence is not a by-product. The felt is a sound-eating tissue, grown to specification, and the hush-engines along the spine of the town draw the acoustic energy off the vaults and spin it into fiber. Quiet is spun here. It goes out in bolts. It is the reason the moth-down drifts, the reason a dropped tool makes a noise like something happening in another house, the reason you can stand in a hall the size of a cathedral and hear your own blood at the ears and nothing else at all.

# The Tenants

Abundant life. Almost no volume.

Everything alive is plastered to the surfaces in a film a few millimeters thick, and the film is *dense*. Twelve thousand springtails per square meter of felt, grazing the mold-lawns. Woodlice in overlapping plates like armor scale. Mites in numbers that stop being numbers. Blind loach stacked nose-to-tail in the Foundry channel, three deep, the whole length of it. Pale crabs in the sluice, sorting silt with their mouthparts, which is a service, and is paid for in warmth. Moths — the great slow grey ones with wingspans like open hands — hanging in a single layer along the vault-ribs, ten thousand of them in the Long Hall, and between them and the floor: sixty meters of nothing.

The bats do not call. They cannot; a shout here is a structural event. They hunt by listening to the sap, reading the walls' hydraulics for the small turbulence a moth makes when it lifts, and they take their prey in absolute silence, and the only sign is a soft collapse of down drifting through the lamp-green.

And there is the Understudy.

It is a salamander, or it was, some considerable time ago. It is the length of a canal barge and the color of the inside of a mouth, and it walks the districts on legs that fold the wrong way, and it does not eat animals. It *occupies rooms*. Where it stands, the void sets. The held-open volume closes around it, wood laid down fast and warm and irreversible, and everything in that volume becomes inclusion — moths in amber, a crab mid-gesture, sixty cubic meters of air turned to solid pith with the shape of the air still visible in the grain.

The animals flee it because the world becomes solid where it goes. Its approach is announced by the drip-rate falling, and by the felt going stiff, and by a smell like a struck match. Then a district is one room smaller, and the town has spent a little of the only thing it is made of.

# The Code

Quiet, therefore. Not courtesy — building code. Loud noise precipitates the walls. A dropped hammer sets a patch of corridor. A sustained shout will fossilize a stair with the shout still in it, and there are stairs in the eastern quarter with shapes in them that the older crabs will not walk past.

No crowds. This is the deeper law and the animals keep it better than any person could: life must stay a film. Accumulate — pile up, swarm, fill a volume with bodies — and the room reads it as occupancy and begins to set. The emptiness is not neglect. The emptiness is the structure, and it is maintained by everything living spread thin across an enormous acreage of surface, each mite a millimeter from its neighbor, all of them holding the town open by the simple discipline of not being anywhere in particular.

And it is going anyway. The walls thicken a hand's breadth a year. The Long Hall is nine paces narrower than the graphite schedule says. The town is filling itself in, patiently, from the surfaces inward, and the industry that makes the stone is the industry that spends the void, and there is no version of the works that does otherwise.

# What It Is a Mold Of

Walk far enough east and the streets begin to have names, cut into the felt in a clean grown script: CANDLE ROW. THE LESSER SLIP. WESTBROOK TERRACE. Names of streets that do not exist. There is no Candle Row. There is a corridor of nothing where Candle Row will be, held open, ribbed and vaulted and dry, and the wall beside it is being laid down in laminae that carry pollen from trees not yet evolved.

That is the whole enterprise. The organism is casting a city in advance — pouring the stone around the shape of a place that has not arrived, keeping the negative spaces open as long as it can, running the presses and the hush-engines and the mud-battery to lay down enough honest rock for the thing to be made of when it gets here.

The cool air in the Long Hall is the exact volume of a market square. The channel down Foundry Street is a river only because a river is what will be needed. The lamps burn green at the height of windows that have not been designed.

Stand still in it and the felt takes your footfalls and the drip falls a fifth of a second early and somewhere east the press fires into your teeth, and you understand that you are standing inside a mold, in the hollow of a future, and that the mold is very old, and very cold, and almost finished, and that when the city finally comes the walls will close on everything still standing in the street.
