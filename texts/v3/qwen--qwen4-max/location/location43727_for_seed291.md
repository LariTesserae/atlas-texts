# The Lay

## I. First sight, from a jacking tower

Ordinary daylight. That is the first thing to get right about it: no green dusk, no eclipse, no aurora. A plain cold sun comes up over the sea and goes down behind the upriver country, and in between there is a long white working day, bright enough to see the rivets on a distant gantry, bright enough to squint. The strangeness here happens in full view, at eleven in the morning, with the light falling on it as flatly as on a cabbage field.

From the top of a jacking tower you can see thirty miles, and all of it is occupied. The Lay spreads out below like a page being written on: a low delta-plain, barely a body's height above the tide, veined with shallow braided channels that shift while you watch them, if you watch long enough. There is no valley. There is no hill. Nothing here has been carved. The horizon is a ruled line broken only by works — condenser combs, slurry gantries, the squat black stacks of the laying houses, ten thousand roofs laid edge to edge like tiles in a mosaic with no grout.

The air is four degrees above freezing and smells of wet chalk, cold iron, and bruised moss. Your breath hangs. Beneath your boots the tower deck is faintly, continuously warm.

## II. Ground, under construction

Nothing erodes here. Nothing has had time.

The river comes down out of the old stone country carrying silt, and the silt would ordinarily spread and settle and be cut and spread again over ten thousand patient years. Instead the laying houses take it in at the head of the plain, mix it with the seed — a cultured grain, a diatom-thing, grown in vats the size of churches, which precipitates a pale cement out of the water and glues particle to particle — and pump the whole living slurry out across the flats through arteries of lagged pipe. The ground arrives by hose. It is laid in lifts, a hand's depth at a time, and it cures from underneath, and it grows.

So the geology here is embryonic and stays embryonic. Cut into any bank and you find no soil profile, no weathered horizon, no rounded pebble: just clean angular grit in bands, sharp as a broken plate, each band a shift, each shift a date. There are no ruins in the Lay. There are only unfinished things and swallowed things. The past is not scattered; it is directly beneath you, eleven feet down, still faintly warm, and it holds the shape of everything that stood on it. Pull a shed off its pad on Tuesday and by Friday the pad has taken a perfect cast of the shed's floor, the bolt-holes, the spilled oil, the print of the dog that slept there. The plain is fossilizing itself at the speed of a diary.

This is the industry. Not goods — ground. The Lay's whole economy is the manufacture of the surface it stands on, sold by the acre before it exists, delivered warm.

## III. Why it is quiet

Fresh-laid ground is thixotropic. It is a foam of grain and bound water and gas, and it holds together the way a held breath holds together. Loud sound shakes it into liquid.

Everyone learns this before they learn to walk. A shout will open a pit under the shouter. A dropped hammer will take a street. In the year of the Bell Riot, four blocks of the Ninth-of-Frost quarter went down in ninety seconds and came back up as a smooth grey lake with the roofs floating in it, and the noise of the collapse itself liquefied the ground next to it, and the next, and the sound of the disaster ate a district.

So the city is silent by engineering, by law, and by manners. The machines are hydraulic, slow-torque, felt-shod; there is no percussion anywhere in the Lay, no drop-forge, no riveting, no gunfire, no bells. Wheels are wrapped. Rails are greased. Doors close on oil. The whole vast industrial apparatus moves at the volume of a library — a tick of jacks, a hiss of seep, the sigh of a slurry line charging, ten thousand people breathing. Speech is a low breath-register close to the ear, and mostly it is hands: a fast, beautiful sign that outsiders find unnerving, whole arguments conducted at arm's length in total silence while the ground quietly sets beneath the arguers.

The deaf are the master engineers. Music exists, and is enormous, and is played on rods you bite: bone-conduction halls where six hundred people clamp their teeth to brass and weep together in absolute quiet.

Necessary noise is piped. Every quarter has its noise main, and the mains run out to cured stone islands offshore where the shouters stand — tall chimneys that vent the city's unavoidable clamor seaward, over water, where it can't hurt anything. From the tower you hear them as a mutter at the edge of hearing, like weather in another county. Gulls nest thick on those islands. They are the only place in the region where a bird is permitted to be a bird.

## IV. No room between

Density here is not a statistic, it is a physical condition. Because the ground grows, gaps close. An alley left unused for a season fills with laid lift and moss and becomes a seam, then a wall. So nothing is left between: buildings touch shoulder to shoulder, roof against roof, and the space above the streets is a lattice of walkways, pipe bridges, laundry lines, and moss-frames, layer on layer, until the streets are more like tunnels with sky in the cracks.

Everything is on jacks. Every structure in the Lay stands on screw-piles and hydraulic feet and is lifted, a finger at a time, as the ground rises to meet it; the whole city is very slowly standing up. Whole blocks slide on greased rails when a channel decides to move through them. The map is not a map, it is a schedule. You do not give an address as a place. You give a cure-date, a drift-bearing, and a rate: *Ninth of Frost, east-northeast, four fingers a day.* Children are taught their own coordinates the way other children are taught a surname.

And it is all in motion at once, constantly, silently: fifty thousand small simultaneous adjustments, cranes swinging in dumb-show, gangs of felt-booted layers walking the fresh flats in splayed shoes, moths in the fog, water threading, the ground itself creeping. It has the energy of an overturned anthill and the acoustic signature of snowfall.

## V. Warm seams, cold air

The cure is exothermic. New ground gives off heat for about a year.

This single fact makes the climate of the Lay. Cold air, warm floor: a permanent inversion, a permanent fog bank knee-to-chest high that never entirely lifts, and above it that clean ordinary sun. Where the ground is youngest the fog is thick enough to lean on; where it has cured out, the fog thins to a bright haze and the flats go rimy at dawn and the puddles carry ice needles you can hear — very faintly — crazing underfoot.

Water is not abundant here; it is *bound*. There is no lake, no deep river, nothing you could swim in. The channels are ankle-to-waist and constantly relocating. Rain comes as a fine cold drizzle a few days in ten. Everything else is condensation: the fog condensing on the dew-combs, kilometer-long mesh towers that stand in the inversion and drip all day into warm troughs, so that the whole plain is under a soft continuous ticking of falling water, the loudest thing in the world here, and it is not loud.

And out of that warm damp seam comes the lushness. It is not a jungle. It is a carpet, and it is rank. Horsetail in stands as thick as wheat. Moss farmed deliberately in deep felt beds — the sound-crop, the acoustic insulation of the entire city, harvested in slabs and hung on every wall and pipe and gantry, so that the Lay is upholstered. Alder scrub in the older cured quarters, glasswort and sea-aster on the saltings, fungal shelving on every lagged pipe where the heat leaks, and in the hottest seams, over the year-old ground, a low broad-leafed thing with purple stems that grows a hand a week and is eaten constantly by everything.

Nothing grows tall. Tall is unstable. The lushness here goes sideways, and it goes into every available inch, and it is cropped and grazed and cut and grows back before the crews finish the far end of the field.

## VI. The occupants

The plain is stuffed with animals, and all of them have solved the same problem.

Stilt-elk in herds of two hundred, splayed hooves the width of dinner plates, browsing the horsetail; they rut in silence, in a fog of their own breath, communicating by ear-posture and the color of the bare skin at the throat. Marsh frogs that do not call but flash a sac of orange under the jaw, so that a dusk ditch flickers like a bad connection. Felt-moths in drifts against the warm walls, wing-scales evolved to absorb sound, which is why they cluster on the shouters' return pipes and are farmed for their wings. Mudskippers in the millions on the fresh lifts, tilling. Silt-crabs that carry grain-cement on their claws and are, in a real and legally contested sense, employees.

And the owls. Grey, enormous-eyed, absolutely voiceless, hunting the fog by ear from every roof-ridge, so many that at dusk the rooflines look crenellated.

They all eat well. The plain is a warm wet nutrient slab under cold air; it produces life at a rate that has no season.

## VII. What the ground keeps

Here is the thing the laying houses do not put in the brochures.

Ground that cures under noise does not liquefy if the noise is small and steady — it *incorporates* it. The grain locks around the pressure pattern. And a decade later, when the cure finally completes deep down, the stone gives it back.

Cured quarters speak. Not constantly; in patches, in weather, when the temperature drops a certain way. You walk a street laid eleven years ago and hear, from the paving, faint and perfect, a woman laughing, a cart, a dog, three notes of a bitten-brass tune, a man saying *hold it, hold it, hold it*. The city's noise is not eliminated. It is deferred. It is a debt paid by the future to whoever happens to be standing there.

There are sound-surveyors who map the voiced patches and sell the times. There are people who go and stand on a particular slab in the cold at a particular hour every year. There is a black market in voiced stone, cut from ground that cured during something terrible, which sings when you strike it and which is worth more than any building it could be made into.

And there is the quietest crime in the Lay: to stand on fresh lift, in the fog, in the middle of a working shift, and scream — knowing you will sink, knowing the ground will take you, knowing that in eleven years the street will say it.

## VIII. Seaward

Walk out to the edge, where the works give up. Beyond the last gantry the slurry meets the sea and flocculates in slow blooms, grey spreading on grey, and the land comes into being out there at about the pace of a stain spreading on cloth. Gulls land on it and sink to the breast and stand there, offended, and an hour later walk away across ground that will hold a truck by evening.

The tide comes in over all of it twice a day. A bore runs up the shifting channels with a sound you feel in the sinuses rather than hear. Whole acres that were streets last month are flats again, and the crews are already out on them in their splayed shoes, laying, lifting, jacking, in the plain white light, in the fog, in the enormous hush, making the country one hand's-breadth at a time while the sun goes down behind the old stone towns upriver — where people shout at their children, and drop things, and sleep on ground that finished long ago and has nothing left to say.
