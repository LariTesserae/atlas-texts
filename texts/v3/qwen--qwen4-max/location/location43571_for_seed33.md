# The Sill

Nothing here has ever been wet.

The air is thin enough that a shout would tire you, and cool — a steady autumn chill that never sharpens into frost, because frost is not one of the things this world can make. The stone takes the cold and holds it. Put your palm on a wall and the wall takes a little heat from you and gives back a faint mineral taste at the back of your throat, which is how you learn that the air here is a solvent, and that you are, very slowly, dissolving into the sky.

You are standing on the Sill: a shelf of pale rock some miles above the world's hot floor, so high that the weather happens beneath you. Below the shelf's edge lies the deck — a permanent overcast, not of water but of vaporized stone and drifting fines, lit from underneath by the lowlands' dull furnace glow. It breathes. It swells and thins on a cycle of about forty hours. It is the only reason anything up here is visible at all.

So the light comes from below. Dim, amber, diffuse, arriving through miles of luminous dust; it enters windows through their floors and lays every shadow upward across the ceiling. Overhead, a small hard sun in a sky the color of a bruise's edge gives a second, weaker light and a second, thinner shadow that falls down. Everything on the Sill has two shadows pointing opposite ways. Stars are visible at midday, between them.

---

The first thing you find is a doorstep.

It stands alone on the plain: a single slab of banded grey salt-stone, its center worn into a shallow dish by traffic. There is no door. There is no wall. There is a curb running away from it in both directions for perhaps two hundred paces before it weathers out into rubble and then into nothing.

Walk an hour and you find a banister. It is waist-high, beautifully turned, warm-toned, polished along its top by hands, and it runs for a mile across open ground with no staircase attached to it anywhere. Walk another hour: a plaza, a paved oval the size of a lake, with a drain in the middle and a ring of bollards, and around it, at distances of a quarter-league, four buildings.

That is when it resolves. Not ruins. Not a scattering. A city — an entirely legible city, with its avenues and its thresholds and its modest domestic details — stretched until it is ninety-nine parts air. The grammar is intact. The spacing is wrong by a factor of a hundred. You can stand in a doorway and see the next doorway as a pale notch on the horizon, and know, with the certainty of a body that has lived in towns, that these two doorways are *neighbors*.

It goes on like that to every edge of sight. There are quarters. You can tell them apart from a rise: the Colonnade, where the windward ribs stand in parallel ranks forty feet apart and a hundred miles long; the Hollow Quarter, all rooms and no floors; the Stair Country; the low glittering fields locals-of-no-one would call the Orchard; and, off toward the shelf's lip where the deck licks up over the edge, the Sump, where the city is being taken apart as fast as it arrives.

Nobody built any of it. There has never been a tool on the Sill. There has never been a hand.

---

Here is how a city happens without builders.

Far below the deck, the floor of the world is hot enough that certain salts go straight from solid to breath. The air down there is thick with dissolved rock, the way water elsewhere is thick with sugar. That air rises. It rises all day and all night, through the deck, up the flanks of the plinth, and when it reaches the Sill it is cold and thin and can no longer hold what it carries.

It lets go.

Stone precipitates out of the atmosphere directly onto stone, and it does not do so evenly. Deposition follows edges. An edge grows, and by growing becomes a better edge, and gathers faster; a hollow shelters the air inside it and lets its burden settle in flat sheets; a sheet with a hole in it concentrates the flow at the hole's rim and rounds it, and the rounding makes it a better concentrator. Give this four million years of unvarying wind and you get lintels. You get arches — the arch is simply what falling-out-of-the-air does when it has two supports and a gap. You get stairs, because the deposition front advances in pulses, one pulse per pressure tide, and a pulse is about the height of a step.

You get doorframes, because the wind scours openings clean and thickens their jambs. You get the concave dish in the doorstep, which was never worn by feet. It is the shape of the wind's habitual pause.

The city is a weather product. It is what this particular climate does when it has somewhere to stand.

And it stands on itself. The plinth beneath the Sill is nine miles of the city's own shed dust, compressed, banded, layered — cut into it anywhere and you find older streets, pressed flat, still legible: curbs, drains, the ground-plans of quarters that blew away before anything alive came up here. The city has been the same city for an age and no particle of it is older than a century. Erosion and arrival are matched to within a hair. It is not growing. It is not shrinking. It is *held*, the way a note is held, and the material passes through it like breath through a flute.

---

Sound does not carry here. It falls out.

The same cold thinness that drops stone from the air drops pressure waves too — they seed, they crystallize, they come down as a fine grit. A footstep leaves a small scatter of glass pellets behind your heel. A cough leaves a drift the size of a coin. Speak a sentence and you will feel the words go granular in front of your mouth and hear only the first syllable and a soft hiss, like salt poured onto a plate.

This is why the Sill is silent, and why it is silent *actively*: the quiet is being manufactured, continuously, out of everything that tries to make noise. The wind is loud in itself and mute in transit; you stand in a gale and hear a faint tinkling at your ankles as the gale's roar arrives at your feet in solid form.

The grit collects. It silts in the lee of walls, in the bowls of the plazas, in the drains — the drains are clogged with a century of unspent sound. There is a plaza in the Hollow Quarter, an ordinary oval, whose floor is a smooth pale pavement of it, waist-deep at the center, and if you break the crust you release forty years of wind at once and it will knock you down and flay the deposit off every wall within a hundred paces. The grazers know that plaza. They go around.

Anything that lives here has learned that noise costs mass. To make a sound is to shed a little of your body. The fauna are therefore not merely quiet; they are *ascetic*, in the way of things that have made a religion out of a physics.

---

The Orchard is where the deposition runs slow and thin and grows in branching dendrites — sprays of pale crystal, waist-high, feathered, in ranks and whorls, each one a fractal argument with the wind. They are not plants. They have no cells, no metabolism, no water, nothing that a botanist would accept. They also compete for airflow, shade one another, crowd out rivals, and reproduce by shedding a seed-edge that starts a new spray downwind, and their descendants are subtly better at it than their ancestors were.

Draw the line wherever you like. The Sill does not have the line.

The grazers work the Orchard and the fresh faces of the Colonnade. Fresh deposit is faintly warm — precipitation gives up its heat as it settles — and warmth is food-sign and shelter-sign both, so the herds (there are no herds; there is one animal per four or five square miles, and they know each other's routes) follow the warm fronts as the pressure tide moves them across the quarters. A grazer is a long low articulated thing of the same banded salt as the city, six-limbed, with a mouth like a rasp and no eyes to speak of. Standing still it is a newel post. It has been a newel post for so long, in so many generations, that newel posts on the Sill have a slight flare at the base that serves no architectural purpose and is the ghost of a haunch.

They run on a cold chloride melt in their joints. Water would kill them instantly. Water would kill everything here; if the deck ever carried moisture up, the whole Sill would run like a sugar sculpture and the plinth would record one final, slumped, unreadable street.

The thing that eats them is not an animal in any useful sense. It is a room. It is a small, well-proportioned, warmly-lit-by-underlight room with a good doorway, and it is not in the same place twice, and it precipitates fastest wherever nothing is looking at it. Grazers shelter from the dissolution wind. That is the whole of the trap. The room closes by deposition, slowly, over days, and what comes out the other side is a pillar with a shape inside it. There are quarters full of these pillars. From a distance they read as statuary, as a civic ambition, as a city that commemorates something.

Once in a long while something comes up through the deck from below — vast, unhurried, trailing vapor, passing between the colonnades without touching them — and goes back down. Nobody who has seen it has been able to say what it was, partly because saying costs mass, and partly because it does not seem to have finished being one thing.

---

The city rearranges in the unshadowed hour.

Twice per cycle the underlight and the overhead sun balance exactly, and for a while nothing on the Sill casts any shadow at all. During that interval placement stops being enforced. A bollard you have been walking toward for an hour is behind you. The mile-long banister has acquired a turn. Distances that were measured in effort come unmoored from distances measured in sight, and the avenues quietly re-sort themselves according to some rule that is not geometric — it is closer to grammar. The city has a syntax. It knows what may follow what. It will not put a drain in a lintel, and it will not put a threshold where nothing approaches, and when the dissolution wind erases a district over the course of a season, the district regrows from its own grammar with small errors, and the errors are inherited, and after ten thousand seasons the errors have become a new quarter with a character of its own.

That is the whole evolution of the place. Not design. Copying, with a tremor in the hand.

Look away from a wall and it thickens. This is measurable: leave a marker, turn your back, count a hundred, turn around. Observation slows deposition; nothing here has decided this; it is simply that being looked at is a kind of energy, and energy holds stone in solution a little longer. The city grows in the corners of your attention. Walk it for a day and you will find, behind you, a modest and quite well-made lane that was not there, arranged around the shape of your not-looking.

And your breath. There is no vapor to fog it, so it does not cloud — it *rimes*. Each exhalation carries a little dissolved matter out of you and lays it down as a faint bloom on the nearest cold surface. A visitor leaves a thin pale handprint on every wall they pass, and a trail of it on the ground, and over a long enough stay, a room.

There is a quarter at the northern end of the Stair Country that is not like the others. Its doorways are low and slightly asymmetric. Its ceilings are vaulted in a way that serves no load. Its chambers are paired, branching, narrowing, and the whole plan of it, seen from a rise, has the unmistakable bilateral symmetry of something that once breathed. Things came up here long ago and stayed a while and slowly built their own interiors out of the air, and then went away or went into pillars, and the city has kept the shape, because the city keeps shapes; that is the one thing it does. It rebuilds the plan of a lung in salt-stone, tide after tide, with small errors, forever, in a silence you can hear arriving at your feet as grit.

Stand in it. Be still. The chill comes up through your soles, the amber light comes up through the floor, your two shadows lie one on the ceiling and one at your ankles, and every time you breathe out you add a little to the walls.

The Sill is in balance. It has already counted you in.
