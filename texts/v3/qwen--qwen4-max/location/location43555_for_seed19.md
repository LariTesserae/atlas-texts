# The Even

The mountains finished here. What is left of them is standing.

You come up the last of the scarp with your lungs working at a third of their promise, and the sky above is slate-dark at noon, and the sun in it is a small ordinary lamp, and the light that falls on the ground is exactly the light that falls on any hillside anywhere — full, plain, midday, unremarkable. That is the first wrong thing. The sky says dusk. The stone says eleven in the morning. Both are telling the truth and neither will change.

It is cool. Not the cold of altitude — you expected that, prepared for that, carried for that — but the cool of a cellar in autumn, a settled coolness with no bite in it. It is the temperature of the rock and the air and the animals and the dust and the inside of your own mouth, or it will be by the time you have walked an hour. There is no wind. There has never been wind here. Wind requires that one place be warmer than another, and the Even has spent an age abolishing that difference, the way a spent sea abolishes its waves.

The plain runs out past the horizon in every direction, flat as a struck table, and it is not stone.

It is animals.

# The Herd

They stand hip to hip from the scarp's foot to the edge of sight, and beyond the edge of sight, and beyond that. Each is about the size of a hare, gray-white, six-legged, dry as a wasp's nest, built of a papery mineral scale with a faint bloom of salt on the shoulders. They have no eyes. They have a broad listening plate where a face would be, tilted at every angle, so that the plain's surface is a low uneven cobbling of faces, like a field of turned-up mushrooms, like a crowd in the instant before it looks at you.

They do not look at you. They do not breathe, or they breathe so slowly that a keeper's whole life fits between two breaths. They do not eat, breed, shed, sicken, or die. Nothing here decays; decay is a difference between what a thing is and what it is becoming, and the Even has no such differences left in stock.

In some districts they stand two and three deep, one on the back of another, and the ones underneath are not crushed, and no one has ever seen the bottom layer's feet.

And the air is full. At chest height, at head height, above that, hangs a suspended snow of small dry flying things, winged and motionless, near enough to touch, a slow gray blizzard photographed and left in place. You walk through them and they part. Then — this is the part that takes people a while — they *stay* parted. Your wake hangs behind you, a clear corridor the shape of your passing, and it does not close. Over the well-used routes the air is a lattice of old tunnels, thousands of them, the accumulated absences of everyone who has ever crossed. You can look up along a road and see it written in the sky in emptiness, three hundred paces of nothing where bodies used to be.

Count a square arm's-length of the herd and you get sixty-one. Count the whole plain, by cord and stake and generation of patient work, and you get sixty-one. The keepers have verified this so many times that they have stopped arguing about it and simply made it a unit of area. *One herd.* A district is four herds wide. A day's walk is eleven herds. Nobody knows what to do with the number, so they use it, which is what people do.

Break one open — it can be done with a thumbnail, and it is not forbidden, only pointless — and inside there is no flesh, no fluid, nothing wet. There is a fine dry powder, and at the center of the powder, a single grain of the old summit. Gneiss. Quartz. A fleck of the peaks that used to stand twelve kilometers over your head.

That is what the erosion made. Not sand. The mountain wore down over a length of time that has no useful name, and every grain it lost became a small still beast, and the beasts stood where they fell and filled the plain, and the plain rose as the peaks fell, and now the two have met in the middle and there is nothing left to convert. The mountain is not gone. It is distributed. It is standing at attention across four hundred miles, listening with a hundred billion faces for a sound that will not come.

# The Seams

Between the animals there are gaps: a hand's width, sometimes two, winding and branching like the cracks in dried mud. These are the roads. The whole plateau is navigable only through its own negative space, a maze of seams that the keepers have walked into a hierarchy — the wide ones, the ones that lead somewhere, the ones that dead-end at a stack.

The dust in the seams holds every footprint ever made in it. There is no wind to blur them, no rain, no settling, no reason for a grain to prefer one arrangement to another. The paths are a felt of tracks, layer on layer, sole-marks and bare feet and the small three-toed prints of things nobody has seen alive. You will find your own prints on a route you have never taken. You will not be able to say whether you made them an hour ago or in some earlier crossing you have misplaced. This is normal, and it is the beginning of the trouble the keepers exist to manage.

Nineteen plants live on the Even. The last full tally says nineteen; there may be one more in the eastern sink, and the argument about it is four generations old.

Each is low and gray, with leaves like plates of mica, rooted deep in the seam-dust where the old mountain's flour lies thickest. Each stands in the center of a perfect circle of bare ground, ten or twelve paces across, where the herd will not stand. These clearings are the only landmarks on the plain that can be seen from any distance, and they are visible for a very long way, because —

there is no atmospheric perspective here. The air is uniform, perfectly still, without haze or gradient. The herd at the horizon is as crisp as the herd at your boots. Nothing gets smaller in the way things should. You can walk half a day toward a plant and have no evidence, from the eye alone, that you have moved at all. Keepers navigate by knot-cord trailing behind them, by the tunnels in the air, by sound.

The plants are the only things on the Even that grow. One leaf, from one blink to the next. Everything that happens here happens to them.

# Sound

Sound does not travel on the Even. It is made, and it stays where it was made, in a sphere about two paces across, at full volume, forever.

Step into one and you are inside a cough. Step out and there is nothing. Step in again and the cough is exactly as loud, exactly as wet, exactly as startled as it was the first time, and it will be that loud when the sun goes out.

This is why the plain is silent. Not because nothing has ever spoken — the plain is *saturated* with speech, dense with it, layered like everything else here — but because none of it goes anywhere. The noise of ten thousand years is pinned in place in small invisible pockets, and between the pockets there is a quiet so complete you can hear the blood in your own ears, which is the only moving thing for a hundred miles.

The keepers build with this. It is their principal technology, older than their masonry.

A knapper strikes two flakes of flint together once, at a junction, and leaves the *clack* there. It is a signpost. You walk the seams in silence until you fall into the clack, and then you know where you are, and you turn. There are thousands. The oldest are so worn in the memory of the routes that the routes are named for them: Fourth Clack, the Widow's Tap, Three Together.

Every hut door is deliberately hung to creak. You find a house by walking into its hinge.

And in the north there is a ring of cairns, nine of them, enclosing about an acre of empty seam, and inside the ring is a scream. Nobody knows whose. It is not a keeper's — the keepers do not scream, they are trained out of it before they are trained into anything else, because a careless word here is permanent furniture and a scream is a building you cannot demolish. The cairns are the only architecture on the Even built to keep people out of a sound.

# The Keepers

One hut per district, dry-stacked gneiss plates split from the low rounded knobs that are the last stumps of the peaks — the knobs stand thirty feet, polished smooth as molars, and are the only vertical things left. Corbelled roofs. A pivot-slab door. Inside: a fire that has never been lit, because fire is a gradient and would be an obscenity, and a stone bench, and the previous keepers, sitting.

They do not rot. They sit where they were put, facing the door, and they are considered to be on duty.

The keepers' work is order. That is all of it. The herd takes sequence from anything that walks among it — not memory, not life, only *order*. Spend a season out on the Even and you will still know everything that happened to you; you will simply have lost the thread of when. Your mother's death and your first meal will sit side by side in you, equally present, equally unplaced. The keepers call this going flat.

So they build sequence outside themselves, with hand tools, because there is nothing else to build it with. Knotted cords running miles along the seams on stone posts, one knot per crossing, read by hand in the dark. The Long Tally: a wall a finger high and forty miles long, one stone laid per day per keeper, in order, the world's longest sentence, saying nothing but *and then, and then, and then*. Flint quarries. Tally posts. A ladder leaning against nothing in the western sink, which is on the inventory, and which nobody moves.

They speak rarely, and carefully, and never in the seams they will need to walk again.

They call a stranger *a new noise*.

# The Wet Place

There is no water on the Even. No ice, no snow, no cloud — the plain is above all of that. Nothing here sweats or bleeds or weeps; the keepers' bodies came up from below and have been drying for generations, and their tears, when they have them, are a hot salt that they wipe away and account for.

But every keeper carries a thimble of water on a cord around the neck, sealed with pitch, and the first law is that it is never opened.

In the eastern district there is a hut with a flask broken on its floor, and the spill is three hundred years old and still wet.

It has not evaporated. Evaporation is a process, and processes require a difference to run on, and the Even will not supply one. So the water simply lies there, dark in the dust, an outrage, a permanent event.

And around it, for two hundred paces in every direction, the herd faces the same way.

Every animal. All of them. Turned inward toward the wet spot. It is the only place on the plain where a direction exists, the only asymmetry left in a world that has smoothed away all the others, and the keepers use it to orient themselves when their cords fail, and they do not go closer than the ring, and they do not discuss why.

# What Is Still Happening

Three things.

The plants put out a leaf.

The knobs are lower by a hair each blink — the conversion is not quite finished; there is a little mountain left, and it is becoming animals at a rate that would take an age to notice, and the keepers measure it anyway, with a notch cut in a post, because measuring is the whole of their religion.

And the herd blinks. All of it, together, every face at once, an interval so long that the cords record only three since the counting began. There is a knot for each. The knots are the largest unit of time anyone here has ever needed.

Everything else that happens on the Even was carried up the scarp by someone. Your breath is a small private convection that follows you across the plain, the only weather in four hundred miles. Your footprints will lie in the seam-dust when the last keeper's grandchildren are sitting facing their doors. The sound of you — the cough you did not mean to make, the word you said to yourself at the fourth clack — will hang at knee height in a two-pace sphere for as long as there is a here to hang in.

They will find it. They will mark it with a cairn, and add a knot, and route the path around it.

And the herd will stand exactly as it stands, sixty-one of them, all of them, listening in every direction at once with the mountain in their bodies, waiting with infinite patience for the world to do something.

You are the something. Try to be a good one. It has to last.
