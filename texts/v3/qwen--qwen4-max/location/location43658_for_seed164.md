# THE PLATEAU AT SETPOINT

## Light, first

There is no shadow on the plateau. There is plenty of light — a bright, even, ten-thousand-lux day of the kind you get under high overcast, enough to read fine print by, enough to make the wet concrete glare — but it comes from everywhere, because the sky is a luminous solid and the sun is somewhere behind it, and has been somewhere behind it for as long as the arrangement has existed.

Objects have contact shadows and nothing else. A bolt lying on a plate sits in a small dark ring and casts no line. You cannot tell direction by the light. You cannot tell time by it either. There is no dawn here, no dusk, no night; the lamps in the towns are burning, all of them, every window and every street standard and every red aircraft warning bulb on every gantry, and they have never been switched off, and they make no difference at all.

The air is half an atmosphere. Five and a half kilometres of nothing above the cloud lid, and beneath your boots the crust, and in between a thin cold gas that is holding four grams of water per cubic metre and cannot hold one gram more. Two degrees Celsius, and a fraction, and the fraction is the same fraction it was. You can be soaked to the skin here and still desiccate; the wetness is not in the air, it is on every surface the air touches. Everything weeps. The stone weeps, the ironwork weeps, the inside of the pipe weeps and the outside of the pipe weeps, and the difference between inside and outside is a matter of eleven millimetres of steel and no difference in temperature whatsoever.

And it is loud. It has always been loud. The loudness is not an event.

## The Deck

Nine hundred metres up, the cloud. Not a cloud layer — a cloud *object*, a single standing lens of vapour two thousand kilometres along its long axis, holding its shape the way a wave holds its shape above a submerged rock while the river runs through it. The water passes. The form does not. Its underside is scalloped into lobes and pockets and long fluted grooves, a ceiling carved in fog, and every droplet in that carving is falling, and the carving stays.

Look along it and the sky has perspective. The lobes diminish toward the horizon in ranks, like the coffers of a vault, converging on a vanishing point three hundred kilometres out where the Deck and the plateau close into a grey seam. This is the whole of the world's weather. It makes its own rain, its own wind, its own light, and it makes them the same way continuously.

The rain has a map. Four millimetres an hour across most of the interior; eleven in the wet districts under the Deck's thick lobes; a permanent fine suspension, not quite falling, in the lee of the pump houses. The map is not a description of the rain. The map *is* the rain, and it is correct, and it will be correct.

The wind is three metres per second from the west-southwest and it does not gust. This is the thing that unsettles the eye before the ear notices anything: the sedge leans. All of it, across a million square kilometres, at eleven degrees, every blade at the same angle, and it does not stir. Flags on the town halls stand out from their poles in fixed sculptural curves, cloth held in a permanent shape of moving air. They do not flap. They have been flapped, once, into a form, and the form persists.

At the fixed spray-fan below the Kettle fall there is a fogbow. It hangs in the droplet field at a certain height and a certain angle, and because the droplet field is a structure and not a passing, the bow does not travel with the observer. You can walk around it. You can get behind it. From behind it is grey.

## Fresh stone

The rock is grey-green gabbro and it is broken. Not weathered — broken, recently, everywhere, with arrises like the edges of a dropped bottle. There are no cobbles on the plateau. There is no rounded gravel in any streambed. There is no soil: there is grit, and there is root-mat, and between them a mesh of copper.

The quarry faces stand in raw benches, drill-marks visible, half-detached blocks still sitting in their sockets, and they look one hour old. They look one hour old in every direction for a day's walk. The talus below them is angular and loose and has not settled into an angle of repose, because settling is a thing that happens over time and time is not one of the local forces.

Two systems keep it this way.

The first is the grit circuit. Every grain liberated from a face is caught — in flumes, in settling halls, in the shallow trays that run along every contour like a gutter that has been given a purpose — and carried down into the Works, and sintered, and pressed back into the face it came from, and broken out again. The mountain is being quarried and rebuilt in the same motion. There is an hourglass here the size of a county and it is always half full.

The second is the current. The entire plateau is wired as a cathode. Every structure, every rail, every rebar cage, every wet iron surface and, insofar as wet stone can be held at a potential, the stone itself, sits at a protective voltage relative to the ground grid. Nothing oxidises. Nothing patinas. The copper busbars are the colour of a new coin under a film of distilled water; the paint is the colour it was sprayed. Rust is a kind of memory, and memory is not among the permitted processes.

The rain is distilled and slightly hungry, and the current makes a faint alkaline slick on everything, so the wet grit tastes of nothing and then, afterwards, of a coin held under the tongue.

## Water, returned

The rivers run in straight channels with sharp arrises and they do not meander, because meandering is an instability and instability requires a tendency. The falls do not cut back. The plunge pools do not deepen. Sediment arrives and sediment leaves at the same rate and the pool has been at its equilibrium cross-section since the day the water first went over.

In the district called Sump there is a flood at crest. It has been at crest for the entire duration of the arrangement: water in the streets to a depth of one metre forty, moving at a fixed two knots, pouring in through the shopfronts on the upper side and out through the back doors on the lower, and the level does not rise and does not fall. The traffic standards work under the water. The submerged crossing signals cycle green and amber and red for nobody, and their light comes up through the flood in slow wobbling coins, and the wobble is the same wobble.

At the western lip, where the plateau ends, there should be the greatest cataract in the world. There is instead a dry cliff. A kilometre short of the edge the rivers are turned in concrete elbows and taken down into the Return: eleven pump halls, each the length of a small province, lifting the entire drainage of a continent four thousand metres back to the head of the system. The Escarpment is the only dry place on the plateau, and it is dry because of the noise behind it.

Beyond the lip, the Deck ends too, and there is a violet haze and no visible lower world, and no instrument has ever been sent to find one, because there is nothing here that sends instruments.

The books balance. Every litre that falls is lifted; every joule the water gives up on the way down is spent lifting it back; the losses are zero, measured to three decimals, and by every accounting the Works should have coasted to a stop before the accounting was finished. The roar does not radiate away either. The Deck is an acoustic mirror; the crust is a drumhead; the sound of the plateau is not an expenditure but an inventory, held in a cavity, sloshing.

There are differences here — of height, of pressure, of loudness — but no tendencies. Everything is a slope and nothing is a trend.

## The Mat

It is lush. That is the surprise, after the stone: a violent, saturated, cold green, wall-to-wall, forty-one millimetres deep, over everything that is not a channel or a road or a roof, and on many of the roofs.

Gasket moss, which seals the joints of the settling trays and is paid for doing so in condensate. Condenser sedge, whose leaves carry hydrophilic hairs at a density of nine thousand per square centimetre, each hair a nucleation site, so that the mat is the cold surface the Deck precipitates onto — the plants make the rain that makes the plants. Wick-fern in the seep-stripes on every weeping face, in bands as regular as contour lines. Valve lily in the effluent zones, which is not a lily and has no flower.

Nothing here flowers. Nothing here seeds. There are no pollinators, no fruit, no flesh, no burrs, no nectar, no scent of any blossom anywhere on the plateau, because nothing has ever needed to be attractive. The mat spreads by runner and bulbil and it is, in the main, one organism repeated with variations, and its roots do not enter soil but a woven grid of copper and fungal thread that is also, functionally, the grounding network of the entire country. The mycorrhizae are load-bearing.

There are no animals. Not sparse — none. No midges in the fog, no birds on the gantries, no worms in the mat, no grazing, no burrowing, no eggs, no droppings, no tracks, no carcasses, nothing with a heartbeat and nothing that has ever had one. The ecological positions that animals would occupy are occupied by machinery, and the machinery is very good at them, and it does not eat.

Because the mat grows at 0.3 millimetres an hour, and the blades are set at forty-one millimetres, and the blades do not move.

That is the Mow. Across the whole interior the vegetation is growing up through a fixed plane of stationary edges, continuously, everywhere, at once, and being cut at the instant it exceeds the specification. The clippings fall into the trays and go to the digesters and come back as feed to the root grid. Nothing accumulates. Nothing rots — the fungi oxidise and reduce in a closed round and there is no humus and no smell of decay.

The sound of it is a whisper of scissors through wet paper, multiplied by a million square kilometres, and it is one of the largest components of the roar.

## The lattice

Every machine on the plateau is phase-locked. One grid, one frequency, one fundamental with a period of eleven seconds that you feel in the sternum and in the surface of a cup, and above it a stack of harmonics: transformer hum at a hundred hertz and its multiples, corona hiss off the wet insulator strings, the penstock roar, the cavitation seethe in the pump throats, the relay chatter of contactors held forever at the edge of dropout, the Mow, the drip-tick of ten trillion drip points summing to a hiss like frying.

Because all of it is coherent, all of it interferes, and because nothing drifts, the interference pattern is *fixed*. It is a three-dimensional lattice standing in the air and in the rock: antinodes where the pressure doubles, and nodes where it cancels.

At an antinode: a hundred and thirty-eight decibels, and no structure not designed for it survives, and nothing was designed for it.

At a node: sixty-two. A busy road. A kitchen with the tap running. You can speak. You can hear a footstep.

The nodes are lens-shaped volumes, typically two hundred metres long and sixty high, strung along lines, clustered where several lines cross into chords. There are perhaps four hundred thousand of them. They are the only places on the plateau where anything delicate could exist, and they do not move, and they never have, and they are mapped, and the map is correct forever.

They are, in every sense that matters, the inhabitants. Not alive; not absent. Objects made of cancellation, with boundaries and shapes and an appetite — a node consumes the noise that enters it. Density here is not a matter of population. It is that presence is possible only in these small pockets, and between them there is a hundred kilometres of roar and void, and the void is not empty of matter but empty of anything that could be said to be *there*.

And in the crust there are the quiet stones: patches, some no larger than a hand, where the standing wave in the rock has a true null. Put your ear to one and you hear nothing at all — the only actual silence on the plateau. They are surveyed and numbered. There are eleven thousand of them in the Null district alone. Nothing uses them.

## Null

Where five node-lines cross, the chord is large enough for a city, and the city is there.

It is a real city, layered, urban, four terraces of it stepping down to a rail head: apartment blocks with balconies and drainpipes, a market hall with sixty stalls and a glazed roof, a school, a funicular, a hospital with a ramp, a cathedral-sized substation with a rose window of glass insulators. Handrails at two heights. Doors sized for bodies. Small chairs.

Population: nothing that has a heartbeat.

It is not abandoned. Abandonment is an event, and there have been no events. The paint is wet — it does not cure, curing is a change — and the sweepers sweep grit that the grit circuit returns, and the window glass is clean because the rain is distilled and the current keeps it clean, and every switch in every panel is in the ON position and every lamp is lit and every clock in every stairwell shows the same figure, which is not a date.

The town was not built and then left. It was built and commissioned and never occupied. The lattice was computed first, to find the places where a person could stand without being deafened, and the streets were laid into those places exactly, and the whole vast continental engine exists in order to hold them quiet.

In a house on the third terrace there is a room at twenty degrees Celsius. Two degrees outside, and a fraction, everywhere, forever; twenty degrees in there. There is a chair, and a window with the rain running down it in the fixed pattern the rain runs down that window in, and a lamp burning in the shadowless daylight, and the room has been ready for no time at all.

## Setpoint

On the nameplate of every governor, every regulator, every trim station from the Return to the last capillary valve in the root grid, below the maker's mark and the serial and the pressure class, there is a stamped line. It does not give an output in megawatts or a head in metres. It gives a target, and the target is a body: thirty-seven degrees, sixty decibels, twenty degrees at the skin, one atmosphere if it can be got.

The controller regulates toward that value. The value is not present. The error is therefore constant, and the output is therefore constant, and the machine has been holding its note at full effort since the note began.

That is what the loudness is. Not industry producing something. A continent-sized apparatus pressing, without pause and without wear and without hope of arrival, against the exact shape of someone who is not here — keeping the streets swept and the flood at crest and the grass at forty-one millimetres and the little room warm, at a hundred and thirty-eight decibels, in perfect equilibrium, forever, in case.
