## The Sill

The ground is warm as an animal. Not hot — blood-warm, thirty-four degrees, the temperature of the inside of a wrist — and it has been that temperature since it came up out of the water a hundred and forty years ago, which is not long enough for anything to have cooled. Put your palm flat on the mud and you can feel the plain still spending the heat of its own making. Nothing here has finished arriving.

It is dark. Not night-dark, not overcast-dark: the darkness is total and it is native, and after a while you stop experiencing it as a lack. Light does not travel here. It falls. A bulb still burning in a pump-house hood makes a saucer of light on the floor beneath itself, ankle-deep, with a meniscus, and the light drains into the sediment over about nine hours. You can scoop it. Crews carry it in shallow tin pans and pour it where they need to read a gauge, and what is left over goes sour overnight and grows a skin, and things breed in it. The old cannery floor has a lake of stale light in the sorting hall, chest-high in the middle, and nothing that goes in there comes out with the same number of legs it went in with.

So: dark, and warm, and wet.

---

The Sill is sixty miles across and falls one foot per mile, which means water does not run here. It leans. The sheet-water lies over the flats an inch or two deep, warm as bathwater, so still that the fog above it and the water below it argue about which is which. The water table is a hand's breadth down. Lift your foot and the print fills before your boot clears the surface — not with a splash, with a slow, satisfied *seep*, the sound of a thing that has been waiting.

There is no soil. There is sediment, which is soil's raw material, and the difference between them is time and death, and there has not been enough of either. The drainage cuts are knife-sided, V-shaped, unwidened; the marl breaks with a conchoidal snap and the flakes are sharp enough to open a boot. Nothing has been rounded. Nothing has been sanded. Every arris on this plain is the arris it was born with, and the whole landscape has the unbearably fresh quality of a wound less than a day old — a wound that has been a day old for fourteen decades.

And the stone weeps. The raised seabed is still draining the ocean it swallowed. Brine sweats out of every cut face and marl bluff in threads and beads, salt-white whiskers along the seams, and the low cliffs are permanently slicked and permanently sighing. The water is not rain. It has never rained on the Sill in the ordinary way. The air is saturated at ground temperature and condenses on anything a half-degree cooler, which means **rain here is manufactured by surfaces.** Each pylon has its own weather. Each cable drips its own thin line of precipitation, each derrick leg stands in a private puddle, each stranded dredger has a permanent local drizzle under its hull, and beneath every one of these, a ring of green.

That is all the vegetation there is: rings. Drip-line gardens, a few metres wide, scattered across sixty miles of bare warm flats — glasswort, salt-moss, hinge-weed that opens and closes on a tide that no longer arrives, pale candle-cane that is not a plant at all and eats the fallen light. Between the rings, nothing but mud and the crust of chemotrophic mat, which is grey, and which is alive, and which does not count as vegetation when the crews tally it. The plants themselves are provisional. A stand recorded as glasswort in one census will be sedge in the next, and the crews have stopped using names. They write the number of the ring and the height of the tallest stalk and they leave the identity column blank, because identity is the least fixed thing on the Sill and naming it wastes ink.

---

What the plain lacks in plants it makes up in bodies. You cannot cross the flats without stepping on something. The mud is stratified life: a shingle of pale blind crabs on the surface, near-touching, shoulder to shoulder, ten thousand to the acre; beneath them the worms; beneath the worms the flat things that no one has properly described. Above, in the fog, heat-moths in sheets, reading temperature with their wings, crowded around any warm machine in a felted mass a foot thick. In the pans stand the listening birds, stilt-legged, eyeless, ear-slots wide as a hand, and they do not call. Sump-eels cross open ground by wriggling through the sheet-water, and at any moment there are perhaps a hundred thousand of them in transit across the district, all silent, all going the same slow way.

Everything is silent. This is not shyness. It is law.

**Sound copies.** A noise made on the Sill is a specification, and the ground, which is young and suggestible, will attempt to satisfy it. Shout, and you will be shouted at from four directions for a week, and the fourth one will be closer each time. The crabs stridulate below the range of hearing, in the shins. The birds gape. The eels do not have the equipment. And the machinery — the machinery was built by people who learned this the expensive way, and it is the quietest heavy industry in the world: belt drives instead of gears, felt-wrapped cams, everything running in oil baths, every stroke cushioned, the whole apparatus murmuring at the threshold of the audible. A pump-house at full work sounds like a large animal sleeping badly.

The pumps stroke once every eleven minutes. That is the only clock.

---

Here is what the industry is for.

The Sill came up out of the sea recently and it has not yet agreed to all of its own details. Measured ground stays. Unmeasured ground goes soft: distances stop adding, features double, a channel you surveyed on Tuesday exists twice on Friday with a hundred yards of generic silt between the copies. The plain holds its shape the way a mattress holds a sleeper, and the pore-pressure in the sediment is what keeps it inflated to the correct profile.

So there are pump-houses, numbered, scattered at nine-mile intervals across the dark, maintaining the water table to specification. There are brass benchmarks set in concrete plinths, stamped **LEVEL: TRUE**, and a survey chain that must come back the same length it went out. There is a rail causeway running thirty miles to a port that walked away, mooring bollards standing in warm mud eleven miles from any water that would float a hull, a stranded dredger with a forest of drip-line candle-cane under its keel. There is a salt-works, a chapel of corrugated iron with no god in it and a very good roof, gauge huts, and the cannery, which canned crabs for forty years and then canned something else for eleven and is now shut.

And there is the grid. The wires hum at fifty cycles a second, felt in the teeth and the fillings and the long bones, and the hum is not merely power. It is the specification, broadcast: *this is the shape you are, this is the shape you are, this is the shape you are,* patiently, forever, from the central station out along every span. The lamps on the pylons still burn and their light still falls straight down and pools in the mud, and the crews let it, because the light-grazers gather there — pale slugs that digest it and glow faintly for an hour afterward and are eaten by everything — and a congregation of grazers is a reliable place to find the things that eat them.

The Gauging Board walks the grid in felt-soled boots, reading the land with sounding rods and fingertips, tapping codes to one another with padded hammers, writing everything and saying nothing. Their worst crime is benchmark theft. A stolen brass disc does not cost the district a survey marker. It costs the district.

Some of the tall things standing out in the fog are pylons. Some are legs. The Board numbers them all and the numbers change between censuses, which happen every nineteen years, and no one discusses this.

---

Change is patient here. The plain rises a hand's breadth a year. The sea withdraws a stride a year and is now a rumour at the far edge, the only loud thing in the world, and getting quieter. There are two seasons, the Sweating and the Less Sweating, and the difference between them is a degree and a half. A generation of crews passes; the profile holds; the mud is very slowly becoming soil, one death at a time.

That is the promise, and it is also the fear. The Sill is finishing. In some centuries it will be ordinary — rounded, weathered, soiled, decided — and it will hold its own shape without being told, and the pumps will be stopped one by one, and the hum will go out of the wires, and the dark will simply be night.

Until then, at the unnumbered edge, the silt lies warm and wet and generic, waiting to be told what to be. The crews are laying a new span out there. You can hear the felted hammers, very faintly, and the drip from a wire that has just been strung, falling on ground that has only lately consented to exist.

The darkness is not an absence. It is the newest thing on the plain, and it is still settling.
