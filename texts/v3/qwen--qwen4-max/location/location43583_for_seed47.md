## The Lid

The plain is the highest thing left, and it is finished.

You come onto it the way you come onto a frozen lake — expecting a give that never arrives. It is flat in a way that flat things are not: not levelled but *concluded*, a surface that has stopped negotiating with anything. Underfoot it is a pale grey-brown mineral, fine-grained, faintly warm to the touch on the sun side and cold as a coin on the other, and it rings very slightly when struck, a note more felt in the teeth than heard. There is no soil. There is no gravel that was not placed there. The plain runs out to a horizon that bends visibly, a soft downward fold at the edge of sight, and beyond that fold there is nothing at all — not sea, not lower country, just the sky continuing underneath, the way sky does when you are standing on the roof of a world.

The air is thin enough to be a rumour. It moves against you with almost no push and takes your heat with an intimacy that feels rude. Cool, always: not freezing, nothing here freezes, there is nothing to freeze, but the temperature of a cellar in a house nobody lives in. A coat is not optional. In shadow you can feel your own warmth leaving you in a straight line, upward, unimpeded, into a sky of ordinary blue with an ordinary sun in it. The light is unremarkable. That is one of the things about this place that takes longest to accept. It is a plain, well-lit afternoon at the top of everything, and it has been that afternoon for longer than there have been afternoons.

## The Sift

Above the finished surface, everything is in motion, and none of it is wearing out.

The dust here has reached the end of being dust. Each grain is a small closed perfection — rounded, hardened, worked over by every process that could ever work it until nothing further can be taken away. Grains meet and part and neither is changed. There is no abrasion. There is no flour of new powder, no chipping, no slow grinding-down of edge into round. Consequently the storms make almost no sound. You see a wall of ochre haze four kilometres high walking across the plain at the speed of a running animal, and you brace for the roar, and what arrives is a faint dry ticking, like a clock in another room, like the sound of a very large number of small things touching once and moving on.

They call it the sift, when anyone is around to call it anything. It is not weather in the sense of pressure and moisture; there is no moisture. It is weather in the sense of *traffic*. Continent-wide systems of it turn and interpenetrate, cells budding off cells, fronts folding into fronts, and the whole apparatus has the anthill restlessness of something with a great deal to do and no destination. Dunes rise in an hour and are unmade in twenty minutes. A ridge you walked beside at noon is a shallow basin by evening and a long comet-tail of sorted grains by morning. The plain beneath does not care. It has already lost everything it had to lose. The storms are not eating the world; they are shuffling it.

Through all that moving dust the sunlight comes down in slow bright bands and knots, pooling and sliding, caustics of the kind you see on the floor of a swimming pool. It is the only water likeness left here: light behaving as a fluid over a dry seabed, and you standing in the shallows of it, watching the bright net crawl over your boots.

## What the World Is Mostly Made Of

Very little, as it turns out.

The lid is thin. Where the plates of it meet there are seams, and where the seams have opened there are shafts, and down the shafts there is room. Not caves in the ordinary sense — not chambers with walls and rubble and the smell of damp. Structure. Vast regular voids stacked in lattices, tier below tier, going down further than the mind will follow, and in them the mass of the world's former mountains, filed. Sorted. Standing in ordered ranks in the dark, cool and dry and perfectly preserved, because nothing here decays either. Decay is a kind of change that requires something left to give.

So the world weighs less than it looks like it should. The horizon curves too quickly for the apparent bulk beneath it. You feel this as a lightness in the chest, a sense of standing on a drumhead — and the drumhead is being breathed through. Cold air wells up out of the shafts in slow surges and is drawn back down in slower ones, and the sift overhead leans and veers with each exhalation. The turbulence has a pulse if you watch long enough. It is not a fast pulse. It is the pulse of something enormous and patient that has been doing one thing for an age.

## The Anchors

Here is the scarcity: not water, not warmth, not food. *Stillness.*

In a world where every loose thing is perpetually being moved, the only precious condition is a patch of ground that the sift will not touch. There are a few. They exist in the lee of anything that cannot be moved, and they are the reason there is life here at all.

The plants make them. Sparse — one clump per two or three horizons, and you will walk a day between them — low grey-green rosettes with leaves like flakes of mica, glassy at the edges, rooted into the seams. They do not drink; there is nothing to drink. They eat strain. The lid flexes under the load of a passing storm, minutely, and the seams piezoelectric with the bending, and the plants sit in those seams and take the small bright current of the world's discomfort. When a storm is loading the plate you can hear them tick, one tick every few seconds, conducted up through the soles of your feet rather than through the air.

Around each clump is a disc of unmoved ground, three or four metres across, where the dust lies in the arrangement it fell into when the last thing capable of arranging it stopped. Old order. The only old order anywhere on the surface. Step into one and the ticking of the sift drops away and you are standing in a silence with a floor to it, and the grains under your boots are not the same grains that were there an hour ago anywhere else, but they are the same grains that were there when the mountains were still being taken apart.

The buildings make them too.

## Scattered Marks

There is not much built here, and none of it is ruined. That is the second thing that takes longest to accept. Antiquity does not show as wear. A structure ten million years old stands with its arrises sharp, its surfaces unclouded, its doorway square. What age shows as is *displacement*. Things here are not weathered; they are relocated. A small windowless cube of dark ceramic sits half-swallowed in a dune that will not be there tomorrow, and it has sat half-swallowed in ten thousand dunes, and its position is the only clock it has.

They are small, most of them. One room. A bench, sometimes, of the same material, at a height that suggests a body not quite like yours. A slot in a wall. A shallow depression in the floor with a rim worn — no, not worn; *shaped*, deliberately, at the factory, to look like the wear of feet, which is a joke or a courtesy or a memorial. Some of them are plugged shafts with a collar and a tally cut into the collar: rows of small strokes, thousands, in groups that do not resolve into any base you try. Some are just plinths with nothing on them, and the nothing has a still-disc around it.

The engineering is not visible as engineering. It is in the grain surfaces, which are identical to one another to a precision that instruments give up on. It is in the sorting, which proceeds by a property nobody has isolated: not size, not mass, not colour, not charge, though it correlates faintly with all of them and perfectly with none. Whatever the machine is, it is not a thing that makes or breaks. It cannot create and it cannot destroy; the world reached its final material state long ago and closed that account. It has one verb left. It permutes.

And so it permutes, at continental scale, in silence, with the furious pointless diligence of a hand shuffling a deck that has already been counted.

## Rare Company

You may go a season without seeing an animal. There are long walkers — hollow-boned, four-legged, taller than a person and lighter than a coat, with feet splayed into pads that read the ground. They come to the still-discs. They stand over the old arrangement with their heads down for hours, cropping the mica-leaves, and it is not clear that the eating is the main business. They seem to be *checking*. When a walker lifts its head it looks across the sift with an attention that has no fear in it at all, and then it goes on, and the storm parts around it and closes behind, and it is gone into a haze that will keep moving for a thousand years.

Higher up, in the shear layers where two storm cells rub, there are thin things like kites, barely animals, riding the gradient. You see them as a flicker at the edge of the sun. Twice in a life, perhaps, one comes down.

## The Rule

Walk, and you will be followed by weather.

The sift converges on novelty. A footprint is new information — the first new information in an age, an arrangement that was not in the deck — and the cells lean toward it, and the dust comes to read it and then to take it apart and file it, and by evening there is a slow silent column of ochre standing over the place where you slept. This is not malice. It is the only appetite left. To cross the lid is to trail a wake of storms, and the wake is visible from the far horizon, and it is how the walkers find you, and how, sometimes, the buildings are uncovered.

The only way to be forgotten is to sit in a still-disc, in the lee of a mica-plant or a plinth, and let the sift go around. There, in the old order, you can kneel and look at the grains, and — this is the thing that no one who has not done it believes — you may recognise one. A particular pale grain with a flat facet and a chip that is not a chip but a shape it was always going to have. You saw it a hundred kilometres back, in another season. The deck is finite. Everything here has been everywhere. Nothing has a future; everything has a place, and then another place.

Which is why the sorting does not stop. Not maintenance. Not habit. A search. Something was lost when the world was reduced to its final form — some arrangement, some ordering of these exact and indestructible pieces, that made a thing the pieces no longer make — and the machine is trying every ordering there is, patiently, silently, in the cold ordinary daylight, at the top of a hollow world.

It has been at it a long time. The plain does not erode. There is nothing left to take from it. It simply lies there, ringing faintly under the traffic, holding still for the plants, waiting to be told that the search is over.
