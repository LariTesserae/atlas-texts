From the sea wall it reads as a marsh that has had something done to it. The light is ordinary — a bright overcast, the whole sky a single soft pane, no shadows to speak of — and in that light the ground goes out flat and pale to a low rim of clinker about nine hundred paces around. Cool air, eleven or twelve degrees, moving steadily off the estuary. It smells of iodine and wet iron and, under that, of a coin held too long in the hand. The wind is the loudest thing here. It is not loud.

The rim is slag, tipped here over eighty years ago and since rounded by weather until it looks like something the coast grew: knuckles of clinker gone soft-edged, grey as bone, cushioned in places with a lichen that has learned to eat glass. There is one thorn on the rim, wind-flattened, half alive, its branches all pointing inland. There is a broken gauge post. There are wheel ruts, healed to shallow dimples, coming up from the old road and stopping at the lip of the bowl.

Inside the bowl the ground is white and grey and appears, from up here, to be a floor.

It is not a floor. That is the first rule and the field teaches it in the first minute. The white is a crust, hours old, a raft of precipitate floated on grey-blue mud, and when you put your weight on it it does not crack so much as fold, and the sound it makes is the sound of a page being turned. Underneath, the mud is warm only in the sense that it is not quite as cold as the air. It takes the boot to the ankle and lets go of it reluctantly, with a small closed kiss.

Then you look properly, and the flatness resolves, and you see that the bowl is full.

It is full of mouths. Hundreds of them, near-touching, in places conjoined: iron casing stubs cut off at ankle height, the tops of old brine bores, each one wearing a collar of its own making — terraces, lips, fluted skirts of mineral laid down a fraction of a millimetre at a time. Between them stand the graphite rods, black fence posts, the plant's electrodes, in ranks that have lost their alignment as the mud has moved. Between those, drifts of small white pellets. Between those, cable, half-buried, its rubber gone to a black seam. You cannot see the mud from anywhere in the bowl. You see only the mouths and their collars and the product lying between, layered, packed, edge to edge, a density that belongs to a scrapyard or a coral head rather than to eleven acres of coastal flat. From the rim you can see all of it at once. From inside it there is no seeing all of it at once, ever.

The brine comes up under its own pressure, cool, heavier than fresh water, and because it is heavy it does not splash. It folds. It wells over a lip and lays itself down in a sheet and the sheet thickens at its edge and the edge builds a dam and the dam raises the pool and the pool finds a new low side and goes there instead. Gas comes with it — fine bubbles, hydrogen mostly, a little carbon dioxide, an occasional thread of sulphur that smells of a struck match — and the gas is what makes the churn. The field boils without heat. Every mouth is a slow cold boil, shouldering up domes of crust, splitting them, foundering the rafts, rebuilding. Stand at one for ten minutes and you will watch a dome close, swell, burst into a caldera with a raised rim, silt up, and heal to a lip. Stand for an hour and you will see the lip become a flue and the flue become a terraced bowl and the bowl choke itself and start again as a dome.

None of it is loud. This is the thing that visitors always misremember afterwards. The mud eats sound; the brine is too dense to clap; the bubbles are small and break under a skin rather than through it. What you hear, standing in the middle of a hundred square metres of continuous violent change, is a fizz at the very edge of hearing, like a glass of something recently poured, and a tick every so often as a crust lets go somewhere out of sight, and your own blood. The field spends its whole noise budget on making. There is nothing left over for announcement.

And it has been doing exactly this for a very long time. That is the second thing that takes a while to arrive: the churn is not a catastrophe. The repertoire is eleven shapes — lip, collar, dome, flue, terraced bowl, raft, caldera, fan, seam, mushroom, blank — and there are no others, and there have been no others since the pumps stopped. The rim height has not changed by a finger's width in forty years. What is made in a week is dissolved in a week. The field has been unstable so long, and so consistently, that it has acquired a tradition. It is settled. It is settled *into* turbulence, the way a river is settled into falling.

The tide gates it. Twice a day the water table comes up under the bowl from the estuary, and the mouths drown one after another, from the low corner outward, and the whole field goes slack and grey and glossy and almost still — an hour of scum rafts drifting and colliding with the delicacy of ice in a glass. Then the ebb, and the pressure differential returns, and the mouths clear their throats in a ragged sequence across the bowl, and the eleven words begin again. Rain does the other kind of resetting: a squall off the water dissolves a day's work in twenty minutes, takes the crispness off everything, and leaves the field slumped and simplified, and by morning it has all been rebuilt in the same vocabulary with different accents.

The buildings are scattered and mostly not buildings anymore. Three walls of the rectifier house stand at the north end, roofless, its floor a marsh, the porcelain insulators still on their brackets and still white — porcelain is the only material here that the field has not been able to eat, and it wears salt the way a coat wears rain. A ladder goes up to nothing. A tally board hangs with its numbers gone to ghosts. There is a wheelbarrow near the south wall that has become a stalagmite with a handle. Concrete footings lie about like fallen teeth, rounded, aggregate showing, indistinguishable now from the clinker except by their right angles, and even the right angles are going. One small brick shed is intact, door swollen shut, and inside is the only dry room in eleven acres: dust, a mouse's nest, a jar of graphite grease, a calendar. It is startling in there. The silence is a different silence — enclosed, ordinary, the silence of a shed — and coming out of it you understand that the field's silence is not emptiness but absorption.

The plant is still electrified. Nobody is supplying it. The brine is an electrolyte, the casings are iron, the rods are graphite, and the mud is full of accidental circuits: a volt or two per pair, some thousands of pairs, wired in series and parallel by nothing but proximity and wet. Current moves through the ground in slow sheets. You notice it first in your teeth, then in the hair on your forearms, then when the compass swings as you cross a seam. At dusk, gaps in the old busbar tick blue, three or four small sparks a minute, the only sharp sounds in the whole bowl. Anything metal left in the mud overnight comes up in the morning furred with dendrites — copper ferns, zinc moss, black-green, delicate as frost on a window, and gone again in the next rain. These are the fastest-growing things here and they are not alive. The real vegetation is at the fringes only: glasswort going red in the low salty corners, algal felt in the one sweet ditch where a freshwater seep comes in under the wall, bacterial films in the shallow pans, pink and ochre and pale sulphur, which is where all the colour in this grey place actually lives. Nothing roots in the field proper. It is not the salt that stops them, or not only. It is that the ground is remade every few days, and no root can hold a floor that keeps leaving. Only things faster than the churn live inside it, and only things outside it live long.

Animals cross and do not land. There is a pied wagtail that works the outflow ditch with an absurd industry, and its single chip carries across the bowl like a dropped plate. There are brine flies in one band, at the sweet seep, and nowhere else. A raven comes down to drink there and goes up again with salt caking its toes. At dusk a hare runs the rim. Nothing lives in the mouths.

What the plant was for was taking a liquid and making a solid out of it, at scale, and it was good at that, and it has not stopped. The white pellets are the product: thumbnail discs precipitated around gas bubbles, rolled by wind and shallow water into drifts and beaches, sorted by size into graded banks as neatly as any screening floor, tons of it a year, no one collecting. Among the blanks you find the casts. The mud takes the negative of anything that sinks in it and holds that negative long enough for the brine to fill it, and so the field produces, alongside its standard output, a small erratic line of objects: a boot, a bottle, the articulated skeleton of a bird, once a hand. Somebody, decades ago, stood these on the tally table in a row. They are still there, four of them, the rest dissolved or buried.

Leave a thing in the mud and the field will copy it. This is not a haunting. It is a foundry that has outlived its owners and its walls and its purpose and is still, quietly, in a bright ordinary daylight, at eleven degrees, on eleven acres, meeting a quota nobody set. The ruin is the part with the roof gone. The rest is running.
