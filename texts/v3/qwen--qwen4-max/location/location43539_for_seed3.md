## The Sill

The ground here is new. Not new the way a lava field is new — black and glossy and already beginning to crumble at its edges — but new the way a tooth is new. Break any stone on the Sill and the fracture face is identical to the weather face: same grain, same colour, same cold. There is no rind. There is no inside. Five thousand metres up, across two hundred kilometres of tableland so flat you can see the curve of it against the cloud, nothing has been outdoors long enough to have an outside.

The edges are all still sharp. Every block that has spalled off the escarpment lies where it fell, angular as a dropped bottle, and the pieces would fit back together if you had the patience and a crane. There is no scree worth the name — no rounding, no sorting, no fan of graded rubble splayed at the cliff foot, because that takes water and water takes time and time is precisely the thing the Sill does not have. There are no valleys. There is no drainage. Look at the plateau from the survey towers and you will see a surface with hollows in it but no *lines*, no branching dendritic script of gullies, nothing that reads as flow. It is a face before it has been lived in.

And it is rising. A hand's breadth a year, which is nothing, which is everything, which is why the doorframes in the older districts of Rind are all subtly parallelogram and why the rail crews re-shim the Spine every spring with brass leaves thin as fingernails.

## Light, and the Undercast

Below the rim there is cloud — a solid white sea from horizon to horizon, the Undercast, with the old world drowned somewhere underneath it. The Sill sits above its own weather like a table above a spilled milk.

Most of the light comes from that cloud, from below. It bounces up off the white and lands on the underside of things: the belly of a bird, the underside of a gantry, the hollow beneath a man's brow. Faces are lit like faces held over a lamp. Shadows are shallow, soft-edged, and point in directions that have nothing to do with the sun, which hangs overhead as a dull coin you can look at directly for as long as you like. Children in Rind are taught not to navigate by shadow. Surveyors are taught not to navigate by anything they haven't written down.

Add to this the haze of the Works — steam, grit, atomised lime, the fine perpetual exhalation of eleven thousand tonnes of stone being taught to age — and you get the light of the Sill: a steady, sourceless, four-o'clock grey, all day, every day, in air so thin the sky above the haze goes violet-black at noon and the stars come out behind it like pinholes in a curtain.

It is cool. Four degrees at dawn, eleven at midday, frost most nights. Water boils at eighty-three and tea is a rumour. The works' waste heat runs under the tenements in lagged pipes and the whole city smells faintly of warm iron and wet wool.

## What the City Is For

Rind is older than the country it stands on. This is not a figure of speech. The first furnace was lit on a surface that had been extruded eighty years earlier, and the men who lit it had grandfathers, and the ground had no parents at all.

The city exists to weather the land. That is the entire enterprise. Unweathered ground is provisional ground — it holds no impressions, keeps no records, and cannot be entirely relied upon to remain the same substance from one shift to the next. Erosion is not damage here. Erosion is *duration*. A stone that has been rained on ten thousand times has a history, and a thing with a history stays put.

So: the age-mills, forty of them along the Spine, grinding fresh basalt against fresh basalt in drums the size of chapels, producing sand at a rate that would embarrass a modest beach. The grit-cannon on the escarpment, firing abrasive at the cliff faces to give them a decade of wear in an afternoon. The frost-looms out on Course Seven, cycling water into the joints of the rock, freeze-thaw, freeze-thaw, four hundred cycles a night, cracking the country open like a nut so that it can begin to have edges that mean something. The lichen tenders with their spray rigs and their little brass atomisers, painting the north faces with a slurry of acid and spore, begging the stone to rot.

And the Sediment Yards, where the product is graded, washed, bagged, and — most of it — banked. Sand is the reserve currency of Rind. The Grain House holds it in vaults. A labourer is paid in grams. There is a museum on Course Three with a single object in it, a river cobble brought up the funicular from the drowned world below, grey and ovoid and utterly smooth, and children stand in front of it in silence and cannot conceive of the force that made it. They think it was carved. They are told: no, it was *tumbled*, for a thousand years, by water, in a valley, and they nod, and they do not believe it, and you cannot blame them.

Rind exports flawless dimension stone — no flaws, no weathering rind, no bedding planes, no memory of stress. Rind imports dirt. Barges of it, mud and leaf-litter and ordinary filthy soil, coming up the funicular in sealed hoppers under armed guard, to be spread on the market gardens of Course Two like a rumour of the past.

## Water Without Rivers

It rains on the Sill about once every eleven days, a fine cold drizzle that lasts an hour and leaves the stone dark and then, within twenty minutes, pale again. The rest of the moisture is manufactured or stolen.

Fog comes up over the rim in a front that takes a week to cross the plateau, and the city catches it. Condenser towers, mesh harps, kilometres of galvanised netting strung between pylons like the rigging of a beached fleet — all of it dripping, all of it running down into gutters and sumps and the long galvanised troughs where the beasts drink. Under any leaky pipe there is a green explosion. The damp is not spread; it is *punctual*. It happens at addresses.

The water sits in the hollows because there is nowhere for it to go. No channels, no gradient that means anything, no downstream. Dew ponds a hundred metres across and eleven centimetres deep, lying in the raw polygonal joints of the cooling rock, freezing at the edges every night, evaporating from the middle every day, and full — improbably, gloriously full — of fish. Nobody stocked them. The fog has fish in it. This is a known fact and the fisheries guild levies its licences accordingly.

And there is the Cut.

The Cut is a canal, hand-dug and machine-dug and hand-dug again, nine kilometres long, sixty-one years under construction, running from the sump-fields of Course Five toward the escarpment with a fall of one in four hundred. It is the cathedral of Rind. Guilds fund it, marriages are timed to it, men leave it money in their wills. When the Cut runs, water will move across the Sill under its own power for the first time. It will carry grit. It will scour. It will begin, somewhere near the outfall, to make a valley — and a valley is a thing that has been happening for a while, and a place that has been happening for a while is real.

They are eleven hundred metres from the rim. There is a plaque.

## The Green Seams

Where the water is, the growth is obscene.

Nothing on the Sill has learned to compete. There is no seed bank, no pathogen, no browsing pressure that has co-evolved with anything, no allelopathy, no shade-tolerance, no ancient arms race. So every plant that gets a roothold grows at its theoretical maximum, unchecked, ridiculous: moss a foot deep and the colour of a bruise going yellow; cushion plants two metres across, dense as brick, flowering all at once with a smell like hot sugar; rosettes the height of a man standing on a chair, holding a hundred litres of fog-water in their hearts, humming with insects. Under a leaking condenser on Course Nine there is a stand of sump-willows eleven metres tall in nineteen years, and their roots have prised apart forty tonnes of unweathered basalt, and the Works pays a bounty on them and also protects them, and nobody has resolved this contradiction and nobody intends to.

Then, four hundred metres away, nothing. Bare stone, sharp-edged, dry as a plate, ringing under your boot. Then a seam of green. Then nothing. Lushness here is an *intensity*, not a coverage. From the survey towers the plateau looks like a grey floor with green cracks in it, and the cracks are where the animals are.

## The Abundance

They are everywhere and you rarely see one, because the Sill is enormous and they are spread across it in the way that lighthouses are spread across a coast.

**Hollowbacks** — the great grazers, four tonnes, six-limbed, slow as weather, with a spine that dips into a long open trough between the shoulder ridges. Fog condenses in the trough. Moss grows in the moss that is already there. They carry their own pasture on their backs and it is genuinely, measurably greener than the ground they walk on. A herd takes a season to cross a district. They bellow, and the bellow in this thin air has no body to it, no bass, no chest — it arrives as a flat metallic tearing sound, enormously loud and completely hollow, from twelve kilometres away, at three in the morning, and you get used to it, and then one night you don't.

Their hooves are the first real erosion on the plateau. Their dung is the first real soil. The Works knows this and has a Hollowback Office and a schedule of protected wallows, and the tender who walks the Course Four herd with a lamp and a ledger is technically a geologist.

**Gizzard-birds** — the crows of the Sill, black and enormous and raucous, nesting on the gantries, commuting down through the Undercast to the old world and back. In their crops they carry grit, and the grit they carry is *rounded*, because they picked it up in a riverbed somewhere below the clouds. There is a licensed trade. Hunters take the birds, harvest the gizzard stones, sell them to the Grain House at a price that would make a millwright weep. The most efficient weathering apparatus on the Sill is a bird's stomach, and the birds have been doing it longer than the city has existed, and this is a source of considerable civic resentment.

Then the midges in the condensate, in columns you can hear. The moths that eat unset stone. The sump-crickets whose chirping is so loud in the green seams that survey parties have to shout, and so absent on the bare rock that a dropped hammer sounds like an accident in a cathedral.

And the duplicates. Out past Course Eleven, where the ground is youngest, you will see a hollowback browsing at the edge of a dew pond, and you will walk four hours, and you will see the same animal — the same torn ear, the same asymmetric moss, the same wet hoofprint that is already fading — at another pond entirely. Both are warm. Both breathe. The Office logs them as one animal and pays one bounty and does not discuss it.

## Rules

Footprints do not stay. Nothing takes an impression; the stone is too new to be marked, and by morning the mud you made has forgotten you. There are no desire paths on the Sill. Nobody has ever worn a shortcut into anything. The city navigates by painted markers, by rail, by the smell of its own works, and by ledgers — everyone keeps a ledger, everyone knots string, everyone writes down what happened, because the land will not do it for them.

Near the Quick — the eastern rim, where the ground is still coming up warm and undecided — the rules loosen further. Stone there has not settled on a lithology. A surveyor plants a rod, writes *basalt* in the book, signs it, and the ground around the rod becomes basalt, gratefully, like a child given a name. Unsurveyed, it stays provisional, and the eye slides off it, and you find you have been looking at your own hands for some time.

And sound behaves badly. Shout at a cliff on Course Two and nothing comes back — no echo, none, the rock has not learned to answer. Shout near the Quick and the echo arrives first, thin and slightly wrong, and you find yourself waiting to make the noise, and sometimes you don't, and it doesn't seem to matter.

## Slow

All of it is loud and none of it is fast. The mills grind, the frost-looms crack, the fog-horns sound the eleven-day rain, the condensers tick and drip and drip, the hollowbacks tear the air, the midges hum in their millions — a permanent, enormous, hollow racket, with no reverberation and no relief, changing only over decades. The fog front takes a week. The herd takes a season. The Cut takes a lifetime. The Sill rises a hand's breadth a year and the city rises faster and neither of them is in a hurry, because the emergency here is geological and there is no shift whistle loud enough to hurry it.

At dusk — which is only a slight thickening of a grey that was already thick — the lamps come on along the Spine, and the underside-lit faces of ten thousand people go home to their heated tenements, and out on Course Four a man with a lamp writes down where the herd slept.

Under a leaking pipe on Course Nine, one drop falls into a hollow in the raw stone. It has been falling for nineteen years. It has made a depression you could lay your thumb in.

In four hundred years there will be a river here, and it will have a name, and the name will be a word somebody's grandmother used for something else entirely.
