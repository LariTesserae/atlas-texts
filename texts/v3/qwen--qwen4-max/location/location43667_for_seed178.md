## The Sill

You hear it eleven kilometers before you see anything, and you feel it before you hear it — a pressure in the sinuses, a buzz in the fillings of your back teeth, the sternum registering a note too low to be called a note. Then the road crests the old moraine and the sound arrives all at once, the way weather arrives: a wide grey roar with edges on it, a shriek riding somewhere above the roar like a wire pulled tight, and under everything a slow enormous grinding, patient as a mill.

There is no view. It is the third hour after midnight in the eleventh month, the cloud deck is four hundred meters up and raining fine, and the coast below is a darkness with texture — a black that has grain in it, the way velvet has grain. Red obstruction lamps stand out across it in a loose constellation, forty kilometers of them, marking things you cannot see. Along the channels the water carries a cold blue milk-light, dinoflagellates stirred up by the tide, so the creeks are drawn on the dark in a faint luminous script that keeps rewriting itself. Somewhere north, an aurora is doing its work behind the overcast, visible only as a general greenish wrongness in the cloud, like a stain spreading.

The cold is not dramatic. Two degrees, maybe three. Sleet that gives up halfway down and arrives as rain. Everything is wet and nothing is frozen, and the wetness is the thorough kind — the air is at its dew point, the ground is at its water table, and every cold surface in the landscape is running with condensation. The moss holds water like a held breath. The cut banks weep. The machines weep, steadily, all night, and their dripping is one of the ten thousand sounds.

---

The land here is four years old. Some of it is nine days old. There is a strip along the eastern bight that came into existence this afternoon and will be gone by Thursday.

Nothing has been rounded yet. That is the first thing a geologist notices and the thing she never stops noticing: no cobble has a shoulder on it, no sand grain has been polished, the gravel is all knife-edge and fresh fracture, glittering with mica that has not yet had time to dull. The sediment comes up out of the sea-floor borrow grounds grey-green, iron still unoxidized, and it turns rust-orange over about three weeks of exposure, so you can read the age of any bank by its color the way you read a bruise. Cut into a fresh levee with a spade and the face falls away clean as sliced cake, and the laminae are there in millimeter pairs, one per tide, thousands of them, a ledger. There is no soil. There is deposition, and then there is a mat of roots on top of the deposition pretending to be soil, and beneath that the raw addressed mud.

Addressed: that is the word the crews use, and they use it without any particular reverence. The silt has been spoken to. Each grain carries a surface layer a few molecules thick that tells it what size of flock to gather into and at what frequency to let go, and the flocs come out of suspension on command, and the mud sets like a custard standing in a cold larder, and then it drains, and then it is a country.

The speaking is done by the combs.

---

They stand in the flats in ranks, three meters, six meters, eleven meters tall, black frames like the skeletons of harps, and they are not built so much as grown — seeded as a dust that finds the substrate and assembles upward through the mud over about sixty hours, feeding on the current from a buried ring main that runs out to the tide-race turbines off the point. The site was chosen for the cold. Superconductors are cheap to keep cold when the sky does it for you, and the water is everywhere, and the tide is a nine-meter tide, and the low ground was already ruined. That is the whole reason. There is nothing mystical about the Sill. It is an industrial installation in a marsh, and it is the loudest place on the continent.

The combs sort sediment with standing waves. They flocculate, they steer, they place; they hold a bank in place against a following sea by singing at it until the water loses interest. The infrasound does the heavy work and you feel it in your knees and your bowels and the surface of any puddle within thirty meters, which crawls and shivers into ridges. The audible harmonics are the shriek — six kilohertz, tooth-aching, a sound with no bottom to it — and the grind is the low audible band where the actual placement happens, and it sounds like a quarry being eaten.

The marsh has grown into the machines. This is the constant war. The sediment is nutrient-rich, the cable corridors run a degree or two warm, and the growing season here has been quietly, unofficially extended; the sedge comes up through the comb throats in spears, the willow lays its branches across the emitter faces, the moss furs every strut with a green pelt twenty centimeters thick and holds the condensate there until the frames run like small waterfalls. Crews go out daily with hooks and scrapers. Lush is not a description here, it is a maintenance schedule.

And the vegetation is *layered*, that is the density of it: waist-high willow scrub over a sedge canopy over a rush understory over a moss floor over a liverwort film over the mud, six strata, all of them touching, all of them dripping, so that walking through it is like walking through a wet green building with no rooms. Crowberry and bog myrtle in the older ground. Cotton-grass gone to seed, heads hanging grey. Mare's-tail in the drains, stiff and architectural. Toward the head of the delta, where the ground is eleven years old, there are alder thickets tall enough to close over your head, and in them the dead wood glows faintly with foxfire, a real and ordinary fungus, green enough to read a watch by if you hold the watch against the log.

---

The animals came before the people did. They always do.

Dunlin in the tens of thousands on the new bars, feeding on the amphipods that arrived in the borrow sediment. Snipe in the wet flushes. Redshank, oystercatcher, a raft of whooper swans on the settling ponds that shouldn't be there and are there every night. Geese come down through the cloud in long ragged chevrons and their noise cuts across the machine noise like something being torn. Midges rise in columns over the warm cable routes, and the columns are visible in the red lamps as smoke that isn't smoke. Voles have already built a population boom and a population crash into the sedge, twice. Eels in the drains. Sticklebacks in water four centimeters deep. A common seal has taken to hunting the new channels and lies on the fresh mud like something deposited there by the same process.

There are no frogs yet. The crews say the frogs will come in the third summer, and they say it as though they have been promised.

By regulation, the arrays leave a spectral window clear: two to four kilohertz, the band the marsh needs to hear itself in. It is a legal requirement, filed and audited, and it is why the shriek sits where it sits and not lower. The birds have moved into the window and crowded it. Every call on this coast has climbed or dropped into that gap in four years, and the young birds learn the shifted song and never know there was another one.

The snipe drum in the hush nodes.

---

Because the arrays interfere, there are places where the sound cancels: columns of quiet, sometimes a meter across, sometimes a room's worth, standing in the general roar like pillars in a burning house. You find them by walking, and the transition is so abrupt it feels like a slap — one step in the deafening, the next in a silence so complete you can hear your own eyelids.

Do not stand there.

Unspoken mud is thixotropic and the arrays are what keep it bound; where the sound cancels, the ground goes to soup. The hush nodes are ponds that look like meadows. The birds know this perfectly well and wade in them on legs designed for it, and the geese stand in the quiet with their bills tucked, resting their ears, and a person who follows them into that lovely silence goes in to the hip in one second and to the chest in four.

So everyone carries a cricket: a hand-sized emitter on a walking stick, which stiffens a meter of mud ahead of each step and screams while it does it. You walk inside a small bubble of your own noise, laying your own ground, a snail with a shell made of sound. The rule of the Sill is simple and everyone learns it in the first hour: *silence is where you drown.* You navigate by ear. Firm ground hums a certain low steady note; ground about to fail goes hollow, or wrong, or quiet, and you turn before your foot commits. Children raised here can hear a bank's bearing capacity the way other children can hear a car coming.

---

The built things are scattered and mostly older than the land they stand in, which is a strange sentence and a true one. The sea took the old coast in a single night eleven years ago; the Laying is the argument that followed. Under the new sediment lies a village, and out of it stand two gateposts, a herring station's gable, a bicycle's rear wheel in a flush of sedge, and the tower of a church, four meters of dry-stone granite with a comb grown up its side like ivy made of metal. The survey crew uses the tower as a mast. Its bell was taken down and hung in the gauge hut at the head of the delta, because a bronze bell sits in a band nothing else occupies, and it is the only thing on this coast that can be heard by everyone at once without instrumentation. They ring it when a channel avulses. It has been rung nine times this month.

The gauge hut is timber, tar-papered, on stilts, with a stove and a kettle and a woman who keeps a paper log because paper does not need power. There are eleven people on shift across four hundred square kilometers. They wear defenders and speak with their hands, a fast local sign language full of words for mud states, and they write on slates, and they take their defenders off twice a day.

---

Because the tide turns, and the arrays retune, and the retuning takes nine seconds.

Through those nine seconds the combs drop to a hold tone below hearing — the ground stays bound, you feel it in the knees, a pressure like descending in a lift — and the audible world simply stops. Across forty kilometers of delta, the roar goes out.

And you hear the marsh.

Ten billion leaves letting go of water. The drip off the comb frames, off the willow, off the sedge, a rain that is falling only from things. The weeping of a cut bank. Sleet arriving in the ponds. The creek mouths chuckling as the tide reverses. And every bird on the coast speaking at once into the gap, geese and swans and dunlin and the thin high wire of a redshank, the snipe drumming their tail-feathers in the nodes, a sound like a hundred small engines starting, and under it the enormous soft hiss of a wet green country draining.

Then the note climbs, the shriek comes back down onto it like a lid, and the sea resumes its side of the argument. Nine hectares taken this hour; eleven laid. The bar off the eastern bight has walked eighty meters since dusk. There will be a new map at shift change and it will be wrong by breakfast. The delta has no shape. It has a rate.
