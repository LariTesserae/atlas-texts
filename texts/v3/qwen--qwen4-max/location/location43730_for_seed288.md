## The Long Floor

Three hundred meters beneath a plateau that receives nine centimeters of rain in a good year, there is a room thirty kilometers long, and it is nearly flat.

It was not always a room. It was a river once — a working river, cutting down through the limestone in the dark, doing the ordinary business of water. That river left. It found a lower fracture and went after it, the way rivers do, and it has been down there ever since, in some other country, entirely unconnected to this one. What it abandoned is a fossil: a drained conduit that spent the next two million years widening by collapse until the ceiling stopped falling. The ceiling stopped because it finally arrived at an arch. Every block that could come down has come down. What remains above is a single shallow vault of self-supporting rock, pale as a fingernail, and it will hold until the mountain itself is worn away.

Below it, the fallen blocks have been sanded to stumps. Not by water — by dust, by salt crystallizing in the pores and flaking the surfaces off grain by grain, by a slow barometric wind that has blown one way and then the other through this room since before there were birds. The stumps are rounded like molars. Between them lies a plain of flour-fine sediment, packed hard, level to within a meter over distances you could not walk in a day. There is no rubble here. There is no sharp edge anywhere in the world. Everything that could be broken has been broken, and everything broken has been ground, and the grinding is finished.

The plain runs out into a dimness that has no far wall. It has one, of course, but the air is too thick to show it.

## The Air, and Why the Light Fails

The air is full of the city. Feather-down, guano flour, spores, the powdered chitin of a hundred generations of beetles — all of it held permanently aloft by the beating of wings, so that the atmosphere here is a thin, dry, drifting suspension that never clears and never settles. Light entering from above has to swim through it. Beams arrive as solid grey columns with soft edges, and the general illumination is a directionless brown-grey, the color of the inside of a paper bag, about enough to read a face at arm's length and no more.

The light comes down the Windows: eight shafts, collapse dolines plugged at the top with boulders and a matted lid of surface roots, so that even at noon what arrives is filtered, secondhand, bounced off pale flowstone until it has no origin left. The flowstone itself is dead. It is a fossil of water, dry as bone, dusty to the touch, its frozen cascades recording a rainfall that ended before the plain existed.

The plateau sits near the equator, and the shafts are near vertical, which means the sun stands directly over each Window twice a year and for about eleven minutes drops a coin of unfiltered light onto the floor. Everything else in the year is overcast by comparison. Those twenty-two brief illuminations are the calendar. The gardens keep it. The birds keep it. Nothing else needs to.

At the far eastern end, the Throat — a fissure too small to enter — inhales and exhales with the weather above, producing a bass drone at the edge of hearing, a note about forty meters long, which has been sounding without interruption since the Pleistocene. It is the only thing here that could be called wind, and it moves at the pace of a sleeping animal's breath.

Eleven degrees. It is always eleven degrees. Cool enough that a body at forty degrees steams faintly in the shaft-light, never cold enough for frost, never warm enough for anything to smell strongly of rot. A cellar in October, forever.

## The Standing Town

Out on the plain, widely separated, stand the towers.

They are made of guano, saliva, and dust, worked by beak and breast into a warm tan stone that cures over about thirty years from something you could dent with a thumbnail into something you would need a pick for. It is light, porous, faintly ammoniacal, and an excellent insulator. The builders quarry the old work to make the new, so the towers are stratified: cut into one and you find the alcoves of earlier centuries buried inside the walls like flies in amber, each still holding the shape of the breast that pressed it.

They stand fifteen to twenty meters, fluted, buttressed, honeycombed with tens of thousands of nest-cups, their surfaces worked into lips and hoods and baffles whose purpose is acoustic. Some are single spires. Some are compound, six or eight fused into a mass with courtyards inside. All of them are the exact color of the birds that made them, a dusty cinnamon-brown, so that at a distance the town appears to be a scatter of oversized birds standing on the plain, and up close it appears to be made of nothing but compressed birds, which it is.

There is no tool in this world. Nothing here has ever held anything. There is no fire, no wheel, no cord, no mark scratched to mean something. There is only the beak, the spit, the warm breast, and time — and time is the resource in greatest supply. A tower is begun by a pair and finished by their descendants three hundred generations later, and no individual has ever seen a tower built. They see a wall, and they add a cup to it.

Around the standing towers lie the Ossuary Suburbs: districts abandoned when the acoustic conditions shifted, their cups empty, their streets drifted with feather-down. The air out there is driest, and the dead do not decay. They desiccate. The old quarters are full of small brown mummies, perfect, weightless, wings folded, eyes gone to dry pits, some of them older than the standing town. Eggshells lie in the drifts like scraps of paper. A whole census of the last forty thousand years is available to anyone who cares to sift.

Between the towers: nothing. Kilometers of bare floor, a low mound, a scatter of pale stalks, the horizon. You could walk for an hour and pass four structures. The plain reads as empty, and the emptiness is a lie of scale, because the floor is alive to a depth of two centimeters — mites, springtails, pseudoscorpions, the larvae of guano moths — at densities that make a handful of it a city in its own right, and because the air above the emptiness is not empty at all.

## The Roar

The tenants are night-birds: fruit-eaters, wide-gaped, whiskered, soft-plumaged, with eyes so large and so dark that the face seems to be mostly eye. They navigate by click. Each bird emits trains of hard audible clicks, twenty or two hundred a second, and there are on the order of a million of them in this room, and the room's reverberation time is nineteen seconds.

The consequence is that no click has ever been heard here as a click, except within a meter of the throat that made it. Everything smears. The individual sounds arrive faster than the room can let them go, and they pile into a single continuous roar — a broad, grinding, gravel-and-metal noise, dense as a waterfall, physically present against the chest, with a slow beating pulse in it as the flock's density waves. Near a tower it resolves into screaming: the tenants also squawk, a rusty-hinge shriek, and the begging young keep up a churring whine that never stops for the whole of the nesting season. Far off, filtered by the dust, which eats the high frequencies first, the same roar arrives as a low surf.

It is the only sea in this country. Nothing here has seen the other kind.

At dusk the town empties, but slowly: the exodus is a flood four hours long, birds pouring up the shafts and out of the high entrances in a river of wings whose sound is like heavy rain on a stretched membrane. Before dawn they come back the same way, and the tower-tops receive them with a rise in the roar of perhaps six decibels, which is the closest thing here to an event.

The ceiling holds a second nation. Bats — millions, hanging in the raw vault, no architecture, no property, an un-built upper town of pure body. They work the opposite shift. They come in as the birds go out, and their guano falls all day in a fine dry flour that drifts across the plain and is the substrate of everything. It snows here, continuously, forever, and the snow is not water. Their echolocation is above hearing, but at this density it is not entirely absent: it registers as a faint pressure behind the eyes, a sense of the air being *occupied*.

Because the two nations alternate, the roar never stops. It changes key at dawn and dusk. That is the whole of the daily cycle.

The towers are spaced the way they are spaced because of the noise. A parent must be able to pick its own chick's voice out of the roar, and against nineteen seconds of reverberation that recognition radius is roughly two hundred meters. Beyond it, a chick is anonymous, and to be anonymous here is to die. So the town's grid is an acoustic grid: each tower owns a sphere of audibility, and towers do not stand closer than the sum of their spheres, and the wide plazas between them are not plazas but the necessary silence in which families exist. There are pockets in the lee of the great masses where the geometry and the feather-drifts conspire to cancel and absorb, and in those pockets — a few meters across, scattered without pattern — the roar drops to something like a distant highway, and the pale gardens grow thickest, because that is where the fruit falls when a startled bird drops it.

Property here is a radius. Law here is the inverse square.

## Water, Which Is Not Here

There is no water. This must be understood precisely, because the world is full of life and life is mostly water.

There is no pool, no seep, no drip, no film, no puddle, no dew. Nothing has fallen as liquid in this room for two million years. Nothing has ever seen a wet surface. Every drop in the country is inside something, and it moves by mouth, by root, and by hypha, and it never once travels free.

It comes in three ways. The birds bring it as fruit pulp, flown in from forests eighty kilometers away, and they have never drunk in their lives; they eat their water. It comes as sap, down the root-curtains, inside pipes, from trees whose leaves are three hundred meters overhead in the sun. And it is made here, metabolically, in the bodies of beetles burning fat, a few molecules at a time, which is enough.

The Salt Quarter, out on the western flats, is where the city's nitrogen ends up: nitrifying bacteria have been turning ammonia into nitrate for so long that the plain there is crusted white with saltpeter, blooming and efflorescing in fractal feathers. The salt is hygroscopic. In the coolest hours it pulls vapor out of the air and darkens, holding a bound film that never becomes a droplet, and gives it back when the air stirs. This is the tide. It is the closest thing to rain that this world has: a grey crust going faintly dark, once a day, and a smell like the inside of a struck match.

Across the plain runs the Dry River, a shallow meandering band of rounded cobbles packed like concrete, the actual bed of the actual river, still going somewhere. It is the oldest road. The rats use it. Nothing has flowed along it since the arch was young.

## The Pale Gardens

Where the root-curtains come down — and they come down in the shaft-zones, hanging in bundled hawsers thick as a thigh, two hundred meters of them, bark still on the upper reaches, root-hairs fuzzing the lower — the floor is lush.

Not green. The lushness here is coral-pink, wax-white, amber, translucent orange. These are plants that have given up light entirely: mycoheterotrophs, feeding on fungi that feed on the surface trees, drawing sugar down a cable of mycelial cord from a canopy they have never seen and never will. They come up in tiers — a mat of glassy cords on the floor, then waist-high stalks with scale-leaves and lantern-shaped flowers held open like small mouths, then fungal shelving at three meters, brackets and veils and pale fluted columns, and above that the roots themselves, the true canopy of this world, upside down. Push into one of these groves and you are inside a thicket, layers deep, humid-smelling, cool, faintly luminous in places where foxfire is running through the older cords.

They are dense where they occur and they occur in islands. Between groves: the plain, the towers, the salt, the dust.

At the throats of the Windows, where a few percent of daylight survives, there is a green rind — hard ferns, xeric mosses, a couple of tough-leaved hangers-on that are the only organisms in the country fixing light for themselves. They live on eleven minutes twice a year and general dimness the rest of the time, and they grow perhaps a centimeter annually.

The gardens are manured by the town. Seeds arrive in droppings; woodrats come down the fissures and cache them, forget half, and plant the rest. Everything here is a subsidy. The whole ecosystem is sunlight from a forest eighty kilometers wide and three hundred meters up, delivered nightly in the crops of birds, and slowly, as sap, and as a falling flour of guano. The cave does not produce. It receives, and it is extremely good at receiving.

Snakes work the towers — long, pale-headed racers that climb the fluting and take nestlings, moving with a slowness that is not caution but metabolism. Crickets the size of a hand graze the cord-mats. Spiders sit in the abandoned cups of the Ossuary Suburbs, in cups pressed by breasts forty thousand years dead. At the Window mouths, owls that never come further in.

## The Ending, Already Scheduled

The plain rises one millimeter a year. Guano flour, feather-down, the dust of the ceiling's last flakes, the crumbs of quarrying. The towers grow at about the same rate and are, in the end, the same material as the plain: the city is not sitting on the sediment, it is the sediment, temporarily standing up.

The room is filling. It has been filling since it stopped collapsing. In something like four hundred thousand years the floor will meet the arch, and the last thing to go will be the acoustic space, because a shallow room reverberates less, and when the roar thins the recognition radius widens, and the towers will crowd, and then there will be no room for towers. The town is building its own ceiling down to meet itself. Everything it makes is fill.

None of this is a tragedy and none of it is urgent. A bird lives eleven years. A cup is inherited three hundred times. The salt goes dark once a day. The sun stands over the fourth Window in nineteen days and will drop a coin of light on a grove of pink lanterns for eleven minutes, and the grove has been waiting since the last time, and will not appear to move.

And into all of it, out of the dark at the room's high end, comes one bird with a fig in its throat — a small wet cargo from a country of rivers, flown eighty kilometers through the night, carrying the only water that will arrive today. It clicks. The roar takes the clicks and folds them into itself. The bird lands on a wall the color of its own body, in a cup its great-grandmother pressed, and gives the fig to a chick it can hear.
