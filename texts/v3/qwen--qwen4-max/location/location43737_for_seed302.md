## The Approach

You hear it before you see it, and then you understand that "hear" is the wrong verb. Twelve kilometers out, along the spine of grey rock that runs down to the rim, the noise stops being a noise and becomes a condition of the air — a hardness, a grain, a thing your ribs take an interest in. Your vision goes slightly soft at the edges. Fear arrives without any accompanying thought, a clean chemical dread with no object, and if you don't know to expect it you will turn back and spend the rest of your life describing a place you never saw.

At the rim the ground ends in a lip of white stone and the world opens: a shallow bowl forty kilometers across, filled to its brim with standing columns, and beneath and beyond it, a cloud deck lying flat and motionless to every horizon, bright as a salt flat, not moving, not boiling, not thinning. It is anchored to the terrain the way a standing wave is anchored to a stone in a stream. It has been in that position for longer than there have been eyes to notice.

Nothing in the bowl is green. Nothing in it is brown except a thin rust varnish in the throats of the stone. It is white the way the inside of a lightbulb is white, and it is roaring, and it does not stop.

## The Stone

They are travertine, mostly — carbonate laid down out of water, chalk-white, faintly translucent at any thin edge, so that a fin of it held against the glare glows the pink of a fingertip over a candle. Sixty to a square meter in the fine districts: spindles the width of a wrist, two to four meters tall, fluted lengthwise by dissolution, each crown worn to a soft knuckle and fringed with a fuzz of needle crystals too small to see individually, only to feel, like the nap on a peach.

In the bass districts they are the girth of barrels and seven or eight meters high, and you would think this leaves room between them. It does not. Every gap is occupied by the next order down — spindles between the bells, needles between the spindles, a crystalline fur between the needles, and inside that fur a wetness that is itself structured, beaded, held. The packing runs all the way down. There is no scale at which you can find an empty interval. Put your hand into what looks like a gap and you will touch four surfaces at once.

Their feet are fused. Below ankle height the whole field welds into a single continuous pavement, cell-walled, honeycombed, so that the columns are not individuals standing near each other but the visible tops of one body, the way a crowd from a distance seems to have a single skin. Here and there the pavement opens into sump-holes, and in them you can see the water table: black-blue, perfectly still, at a depth of a meter and a half, and it is exactly a meter and a half everywhere in the bowl, and it was a meter and a half when the first animal crawled onto this stone.

No rubble. No talus, no fresh scars, no split faces, nothing leaning. Every edge is rounded and settled and has been for a very long time. The field is not being worn down and it is not being built up. It has arrived.

## The Loop

Here is what took me a while to accept: the columns are hollow, and they are working.

Each has an inner shaft, a bore no wider than two fingers, running from a cupped depression at the crown down to a bulb near the base. Water films down the inside of that shaft — not pouring, weeping, a millimeter of it, always. Falling water in a narrow shaft drags air down with it. The air is compressed in the bulb. It has one way out: a side vent, a lip of stone shaped by nothing but dissolution, and it goes out through that lip and sets the whole column ringing like a pipe.

A hundred million pipes. Fed by gravity. And the water gets back to the top by capillarity — the stone is microporous, a wick, and the air at the crown is exactly at its dew point and exactly hungry, and the bright thin light evaporates the top of the wick and pulls the column of water up behind it. Sunlight lifts the water. Gravity drops it. The difference between those two is the noise.

It is a machine. It is precisely a machine, of a type that people have built to blow forge fires, and there is not one made thing in forty kilometers. No hand cut a bore. No one tuned a vent. The shapes are dissolution shapes, the ordinary vocabulary of soluble rock, and they happen — over an interval too long to be lucky — to have settled into the one configuration that spends the arriving light as fast as it arrives.

The mineral circulates too. Stone dissolves at the crown, rides down as dissolved carbonate, precipitates at the foot, wicks back up, and lays itself down again at the top. A given atom completes the circuit in something like eleven thousand years. The columns are not objects. They are standing waves in calcium, holding their shape while the material runs through them, and the field you are looking at is not the field that stood here before, and it is identical to it.

## The Chord

The sound is not weather. It has no gusts, no swells, no evening slackening. Every pipe's phase is locked by a head of water that never varies, so the interference pattern between them is *fixed*. The air of this place has a permanent crystal structure of pressure, invisible, unchanging, interleaved with the stone one and not aligned to it.

There are walls out there. Walk east from the rim and at eleven paces the pressure climbs under your sternum like a hand pressing, and at fourteen it drops, and at nineteen it climbs again, and those walls will be in those places in a thousand years. The corridors between them are the only routes through the bowl. Nothing worn them. Nothing maintains them. They are roads with no roadbed, and they are the reason anything can live here at all.

The fundamental is around eleven hertz, below hearing, and it is what makes your eyeballs smear and your gut turn over. Above it sits a mass of tone from two hundred to four thousand cycles, dense as a wall of cicadas, and above that the hiss of uncountable droplets. Together, everywhere, about a hundred and twenty decibels. In the pressure minima it falls to ninety-five, which is a chainsaw at your shoulder, and which the natives experience as a sheltered glade.

And the sound is visible. In a standing wave, suspended dust and mist collect at the nodes; here the mist collects and coalesces and hangs. Strung through the corridors at chest height and knee height and eye height are beads of cold water, motionless, a hand's width apart, thousands of them, glittering, marking out the invisible lattice like beads on wire that isn't there. Walk through a corridor and you pass through them and they wet you and reform behind you. It never rains here. You get soaked anyway. The wetness of this place is something you walk into.

## The Light

White stone above, white cloud below, a saturated air full of lenses, and a sun somewhere in a sky gone violet-black at the zenith from thinness. The light comes from every direction at once. There are no shadows in the bowl — only a faint dullness in the deep throats, a bruise-colored blue where the light has been passed through too many wet edges. You cannot tell where the sun is without looking, and looking costs you.

Depth perception goes first. The columns flatten into a white pattern with no near or far, and you find yourself with your hands out like a blind man, which is the correct posture, and then you learn to read the pressure instead, which is better.

Cool, always: five or six degrees above freezing, and never anything else. The condensation is what holds it there. Every drop that forms on a crown gives up its latent heat, and that heat is exactly what the evaporation at the next crown takes back. The field is its own thermostat, wet-bulb steady, a chill that never deepens and never lifts, the cold of a cave mouth in spring.

## The Living

There are no plants. There is nothing that could be called a leaf. What there is, is a film: a rust-ochre varnish, dark against the glare because darkness is the only sunscreen, living in the two millimeters of translucent stone that light can penetrate, and in the dissolving throats where the mineral is being taken away faster than it is being laid down. That is the whole constraint on biology here. Life is not short of light or water. Life is short of *surface* — the stone precipitates faster than anything can colonize it, and buries its own skin, and the world is white because mineral outruns flesh.

The film is grazed by something the size of a palm, with a coiled shell of the same travertine, so exactly the color and texture of a column base that you will lean on one. It has no eyes. It has no ears; nothing here has ears, because a ear in this bowl is a wound. Along its fringe it carries a line of cilia that read pressure the way a fish reads a current, and it walks the minima, and it cannot cross a maximum — not because the pressure kills it but because it *blinds* it, wipes out the only sense it has, and it will be lost in a white wall of noise with no walls in it. It lives perhaps four hundred years. There is one of it for every ten thousand columns. You can walk a day and see nothing move.

Its young are aquatic and live inside the bores, in the falling water, in the dark, and come out when they are too large for the plumbing. The whole field is a nursery. In the sumps there is a flat translucent thing that eats them, and it is rarer still.

Nothing here signals. There is no channel to signal in — every frequency is occupied, permanently, at a hundred and twenty decibels. They find each other by touch and by taste and by walking the same fixed corridors for centuries. Nothing calls. Nothing has a name. Nothing has ever been warned.

## The Stop

Near the western third there is a district, a few hundred meters across, where the bores silted and the pipes went dead.

It is silent. Genuinely: the pressure lattice has collapsed there, the hanging beads have fallen, and the ground is a shallow still pool over fused pavement, and the columns stand around it plugged and dumb. It has been dead as long as the stone records, and it is not healing and not spreading.

To the grazers it is an abyss. Their sense shows them nothing there, no texture, no floor, no direction — a hole cut in the world — and they will circle its edge for a week rather than enter it.

To you it is the only place in forty kilometers where you can hear your own blood, and think a whole thought, and it is where you will want to sleep, and you should not, because you will wake to find that you have gone deaf in the night and can no longer feel the corridors either.

## What Time Does Here

Cut a column and you get bands. Four hundred thousand of them, laid down one per circuit-fraction, fine as pages. They are identical. A complete record, perfectly preserved, of nothing whatsoever happening.

Except that every column in the bowl — every one, at the same depth, at the same age — carries a single dark line. One band, thin as a hair, out of four hundred thousand. Something happened once, everywhere at the same moment, and the field took it down and circulated it and laid it back over and has forgotten it absolutely, and the noise it is making now is the same noise it was making before, and will be the same noise after everything now alive has been dissolved and lifted and set back down in the shape it already has.

Stand at the rim with your ruined ears and your streaming eyes and look at it: the frozen crowd, the still cloud, the glare with no direction, the water climbing the wicks to meet the light and falling back down screaming. It looks like a catastrophe held one instant before it breaks. It has looked like that for four million years. Nothing is about to happen. This is what it sounds like when brightness is made to lift water, forever, at the top of the world.
