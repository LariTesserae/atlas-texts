You hear it long before there is anything to see.

The sinkhole is a fresh wound in the plateau — a collapse, not a valley, its lip still raw, blocks the size of cattle tipped inward with their broken faces white and unweathered. Ferns have not yet found the rim. There is no soil, only shattered bedding planes and the smell coming up: ammonia, warm bread, wet stone, mushroom. And under the smell, the sound. It arrives as weather arrives. A hiss with grain in it. Rain on a broadleaf canopy, except there is no rain and no canopy, and the sound does not gust or slacken. It has been at exactly this volume for longer than anyone has been alive to notice.

Down the breakdown pile, forty meters, in green light thick as pondwater, the shaft opens sideways into the first gallery, and the scale of the thing declares itself by echo. A dropped pebble does not click; it *reports*, and the report goes away from you and keeps going, and comes back changed, and the change tells you the room is three hundred meters across and the ceiling is somewhere above the reach of your light. This is the difficulty of the place: it is underground and it is enormous. It is a country. Sixty kilometers of passage have been walked and the walking has not finished it. There are chambers here you could set a valley into, and between them, long empty stretches where a single cricket can be heard from four hundred meters away, chirping into an emptiness the size of a town square, over and over, with nothing answering.

The rock is pale oolitic limestone, faintly sugary, laid down in a sea that has no other remaining evidence. Chert nodules stand out of the walls in black knuckles, sharp where they have popped free, edges you could open a hide with. Nothing has struck them. The whole cave is full of unstruck tools.

And the walls are *young*. That is the first strangeness, though it takes a while to feel it as strange. The scallops left by the water that cut these passages are still lip-sharp, each one a thumbprint with a crisp downstream edge. The ceiling pockets are unrubbed. Breakdown blocks lie where they fell with their fracture surfaces so fresh you could lift one and fit it back into the socket above and hear it seat. There is no rounded flowstone, no drapery, no stalactite forest — only a scattering of soda straws no longer than a finger, and a bloom of moonmilk in one alcove, damp and pale and crumbling like cold fat. The river that made all this dropped away to a lower level and left. The cave was born recently, geologically speaking, and then it stopped aging.

Because the water here has nothing left to do.

It is damp everywhere and wet almost nowhere. A film of condensation lies on every surface, a micron of it, enough to make the walls slick and to hold the cold on your palm. It is not seepage; it is the cave's own breath condensing on stone that is a tenth of a degree cooler than the air. And that water is already saturated with the rock — it can dissolve no more and it will deposit nothing. It hangs there in equilibrium, neither eating nor building, a solvent that has retired. Pockets of standing water lie in the gour basins of the dry floors, rimstone dams holding a finger's depth, black and absolutely flat, so still that the ceiling above them is not reflected but *installed*. Twice a day, or twice a decade, a straw releases a drop. Far below — three levels below, down through the stack of abandoned galleries — the real river works, and you can hear it in the low registers as a sound like a crowd in another building. It rises and falls up there on the surface. Down here its voice does not change pitch.

Nine point six degrees. Ninety-eight percent humidity. No wind, in the sense of wind from anywhere. The air moves, and moves steadily, and it moves because the animals move it.

That is the second strangeness, and it is the whole of the place.

The bats occupy four provinces of this country, and only four. In the roost galleries the ceiling is not visible because the ceiling is bats — a continuous pelt of them, hooked and folded, packed at a density that reads from below as a texture in the stone, like the grain of a wood nobody has ever cut. Millions. The number is not interesting; the number is a fact of the ceiling. They hang in a warm layer of their own making, and their bodies heat the air, and the heated air rises along the ceiling and pulls cool air in along the floor, and that draft is the cave's only wind. Their wings, in the flying galleries, drive the rest of the circulation. The colony ventilates the country it lives in. The temperature is constant to a tenth of a degree not because the rock is thick — though the rock is thick — but because a million small furnaces are burning at a rate that has not varied since the ice went away.

The sound in there is a solid. Eighty-five, ninety decibels, sustained, no envelope: the social voice of the colony, a wrangling, clicking, chittering argument that never resolves and never pauses, layered over the wing-roar, which is the sound of heavy rain arriving on shingle from a long way off. It is not deafening so much as *occupying*. It gets into the sternum. After an hour you stop hearing it and start hearing inside it, the way you hear individual voices in a market. There are registers: the high ticking, hail on stone; a middle band of squealing that rises and dies in patches, moving across the ceiling like wind across a wheatfield; and beneath everything the low continuous surf of wings.

What you cannot hear is the measurement. Above the audible racket, in frequencies your ears were not built for, the flying bats are working. Each one is throwing out sound and reading the returns, shaping the room out of echoes, resolving a moth at four meters, resolving the difference between a moth and a leaf at three. It is precise, it is learned, it is passed down, it is adjusted for the room and for the clutter and for the presence of neighbors, and it is the only technology in this country. It is a knapping of pressure waves: strike, flake, examine, strike again, thirty times a second. It builds an accurate and detailed model of a space three hundred meters wide, and the model is maintained by a hundred thousand practitioners at once, and it is the single made thing here — and it vanishes the instant it stops being made. Nothing persists. No nest, no cairn, no wall, no scratch. The bats build nothing that outlasts the building. This is a place with an industry and no artifacts, and the two facts are the same fact.

Below them, the floor is a meter deep in guano, and it has been a meter deep for ten thousand years.

That is the third strangeness, and the one that takes longest to accept. It should be a mountain. It falls continuously, a soft dry snow of it, and it does not accumulate, because the guano is eaten at precisely the rate it arrives. Beetles by the acre, mites in numbers that make the surface of the pile seem to *breathe*, springtails in blooms like moving dust, cockroaches, the pale fungal felts threading through it all, bacteria in the warm middle where the pile holds a steady thirty degrees. The top of the guano is as old as the bottom. It is not a deposit; it is a throughput. Stand in it and your footprint fills in eleven minutes, worked closed from the edges by things the size of punctuation. There is no way to leave a mark here. Not because marks are forbidden but because the floor is a mouth.

And the crowd has no rhythm, because it contains all rhythms. This is the rule that governs everything. Each bat keeps its own hours; there are millions of bats; the sum is flat. There is no dusk eruption, no dawn return, no silence at midday. At every skylight the traffic runs both ways at once, a turning rope of bodies, outbound and inbound braided, at a rate you could measure with a bucket and get the same number in an hour or a year. There is no pup season. The cohorts are staggered; somewhere in the pelt a female is giving birth right now, and somewhere else a young one is flying for the first time, and both events are so continuously ordinary that neither has a name. The surface has seasons so that this place does not need them. The plateau's insects hatch and swarm and die in their pulses, and the pulses arrive down here already averaged, already smoothed into a flat line of food. The cave buys its stillness with the world's restlessness. It is not frozen. It is *steady*, which from inside is indistinguishable, and which is far more expensive.

Between the roost provinces: emptiness. This is what nobody expects. Kilometers of dry fossil gallery with nothing in it but a film of water on the walls and, every hundred meters, one cricket on the ceiling facing the dark, stridulating into a void that returns his note to him a half-second late. A pseudoscorpion in a crack, holding on with both hands, waiting out a famine that has no season and therefore no end. Sparse, spare, balanced with nothing. The overwhelming is *localized*, four knots of unbearable life in a country mostly made of held breath, and the walk between them is the loneliest walking there is, because the roar behind you fades and the roar ahead has not begun and the only sound is your own breathing and the drip that will not come again in your lifetime.

The green places are the collapse shafts. There are eleven of them across the system, and each is a dim column of daylight standing in the dark like a solid thing, and at the base of each, a smear of life: moss on the wet breakdown, algae in a green film where the light lands, a fern or two with fronds too long and too thin, and the etiolated seedlings — pale, white-stemmed, absurdly tall, reaching for the shaft-mouth with leaves that have never once been properly green. They live on fruit and seed dropped by animals passing overhead, and on light that has come forty meters down a stone tube, and they are all of them starving slowly and beautifully in a place where nothing else has to.

In one damp alcove off the second flying gallery, the fungus gnat larvae hang their threads and burn a cold blue-green, a hundred small stars in a space the size of a wardrobe, and the light they make is the only light in the country that was made by something living. It is not strange. It is just chemistry, done patiently, for the purpose of catching a midge.

Nothing here is strange. That may be the last thing to understand. Every part of this is ordinary process — limestone, condensation, echolocation, dung beetles, a colony's warmth — and the sum of the ordinary parts is a place with no dawn, no year, no weather, no history, sharp-edged and unfinished-looking and permanently so, roaring at a fixed volume in the dark under a plateau where the grass bends and the rain comes and goes. The scallops will never round. The blocks will never weather. The pile will never grow. A country in the perfect tense: everything here is happening, at the same rate, and nothing here has ever happened at all.
