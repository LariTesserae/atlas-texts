The wind stops at the lip.

Above, on the plateau, it is a hard grey blow out of the north, bending the spruce and dragging the birch crowns sideways, and it comes to the edge of the hole and goes straight over. Below the lip there is no wind at all. There is a lid of cold air lying in the basin like a second, heavier atmosphere, and the weather slides across its surface without touching what is underneath. Sound does the same. The plateau's noise — the trees, the river before it falls in, the far collisions of rock — arrives at the rim and refracts upward into the sky and is gone. Nothing down here hears the world, and the world does not hear down here.

That is the first fact. The second is the cold, which is not the cold of altitude or of latitude but the cold of a cellar. Winter air pours over the lip on clear nights, dense as syrup, and pools, and cannot get out again. Summer warmth rides over the top of it. In the low corners the ice from last February is still there in August, grey and dirt-banded, shrinking by a hand's width a week, and the ground around it grows needle ice on any night of the year, lifting the sediment in little combs of frozen filaments that collapse at noon and lift again by dark.

The floor is a hundred and eighty metres below the fields.

---

It is a collapse. Not an old one. The walls still carry the fracture faces raw and pale, unrounded, sharp enough to cut a palm; the bedding planes stand out in ledges the width of a finger; there is no soil on them, no staining, no weathering rind. Blocks the size of houses lie in the talus at angles that have not yet settled, and one of them, somewhere near the north end, shifts a few millimetres most weeks with a sound you feel in the soles of your feet a half-second before you hear it. The rim is still retreating. Every hard frost takes another slab off it. The hole is not finished being made.

Where the roof came down it left a window — an oval of open sky three hundred metres long, tilted, ragged at the edge, framed by the stumps of strata. Through it comes daylight: ordinary daylight, unremarkable in strength, and the strange thing is how far it goes. Everything below is white. The stone that the water is laying down is white, and it throws the light sideways and up under the ledges and into the seams, so the whole basin is lit the way a room is lit by a whitewashed wall — shadowless, directionless, a brightness with no obvious source except the one obvious source, moving.

The direct sun crosses the floor in forty minutes at midsummer, a slow bar of gold sliding along the terraces like the hand of a clock. In December it does not reach the floor at all. Everything down here keeps that timetable.

---

Water comes in three ways and leaves in one.

It comes in as the river, which arrives out of a low arch at the south end already cold and already clear, having spent nine days underground dissolving the mountain; it comes in as seep, thousands of seeps, the whole east wall running with a film of it so thin it does not fall but clings and creeps and gathers at the lip of each ledge before letting go; and it comes in as fog. Warm humid air spilling over the lip in July meets the cold pool beneath and condenses instantly, and the walls sweat, and the moss takes the fog directly out of the air the way a net takes fish. On a good summer morning the fog drip is heavier than rain.

It leaves through the sump at the north end, where the floor drops into a pool of a colour that is not blue and not green, and the water goes down and away under a hundred metres of limestone to come out somewhere on the other side of the ridge, in a valley nobody is standing in.

The river is loaded. It carries the mountain in solution, calcium and bicarbonate, and when it emerges into air it lets go of its carbon dioxide and begins, immediately, to turn back into stone.

This is what the basin is doing. It is precipitating.

---

At first the terraces look like geology. Ranks of pale barrages stepping down the slope, each holding a shallow pool, each pool spilling to the next through a notch; arcades where the water has roofed over a channel; fluted columns; basins with rims as thin and regular as a thrown lip. Kilometres of it. Districts of it. There is a long quarter where the walls run parallel and the passages between them are the width of a body, and another where the pools are round and deep and set in a hexagonal packing like the cells of a comb, and another, the oldest, where the shapes are not regular at all but follow the shapes of things that were there first.

Look closer and the geology resolves into masonry, and the masonry into something else again.

Every surface is upholstered. Moss in five or six kinds, a hand's depth in the wet places, dense as felt, and under the moss a black-green cyanobacterial skin, and under that the porous crumb of the tufa itself, which is full of holes, foam-like, the cast of yesterday's moss. It is the moss that builds. It strips the carbon out of the water to make its own tissue and the lime drops out around it, and the plant grows upward through its own crust, and the crust thickens, and the wall rises two millimetres a year and never stops. The dams are alive on their upstream faces and dead and white on their downstream ones. Where a wall has been broken you can see the lamination inside it, thousands of leaves of lime, and a green thread of something still trying to grow in the middle of the stone.

And the walls are not empty. They are honeycombed. Set into them, packed rim to rim, sharing partitions, three deep in places, are nests — cups of moss fibre and root and feather glued with a cement that dries glassy and hard, and which the lime water then seals over and locks into the fabric. There are hundreds of thousands. There is no bare rock anywhere in the terrace quarter. There is no gap between one nest and the next. The city is not decorated with inhabitants; the inhabitants are the aggregate.

---

They are small pale birds, sooty-winged, with a face built for taking insects out of the air, and they are the reason any of this is here.

They cannot feed in the hole. Nothing grows fast enough down here to support them. So at first light they go up — the whole population, out through the window in a column that takes eleven minutes to pass — and they spend the day over the plateau and the river valley and the neighbouring ridge, harvesting the sky's insects, and at dusk they come back down carrying it. That import is the entire economy. The guano feeds the midge larvae in the pools; the larvae feed the springtails and the mites and the white planarians; those feed the harvestmen that stand in mats on the wet walls like a nap on velvet, and the spiders whose sheets bridge every passage, and the cold salamanders in the seeps, and the pale sculpin holding station in the sump. The moss makes a second, smaller income from the light. Everything else here is eating the outside.

The birds are silent. They have to be. In a colony this packed, in a chamber this dead acoustically, sound would be a useless extravagance anyway — the tufa foam and the moss depth swallow anything above a whisper within four metres — but there is a falcon that has learned the window. It comes down the light bar at midsummer noon, folded, and takes birds off the lit terraces, and it hunts partly by ear. So the colony has gone quiet over the generations, and now works by touch and posture and the pressure of bodies, a hundred thousand conversations conducted at a range of one centimetre. They shift on the nests. They pass food beak to beak down the ranks. The dusk return is the loudest thing that happens here all day: a rain of wings, ten thousand small displacements of air, arriving from above and settling, and it sounds like weather happening in another valley.

The midges do not sing; it is too cold to sing. The frogs in the warm seeps have lost their call and breed in silence, and the males knock — one low knock, felt more than heard, at intervals of about a minute, all night, from somewhere in the liverwort. The springtails gather on the pool films in such numbers that the surface goes grey-blue and mobile, and when they move together they make a sound like salt poured very slowly onto paper. A drip falls somewhere every four or five seconds, and you can locate it, and after an hour you realise there are thousands of drips and you are only hearing the nearest one.

That is the whole register. Under it, always, the low press of the river going into the sump, which is not a roar because the water leaves without falling.

---

The oldest district is built on a forest.

When the roof came down it took a hectare of the plateau with it — spruce, birch, a stand of aspen — and dropped them into the hole more or less upright. They died in the cold and the dark and the water. They are still standing. The lime has sheathed them: trunks three metres through now, white, fluted, tapering, the bark's texture perfectly preserved under a centimetre of stone, the branches hung with stalactite where the seep runs off them. The birds nest in the hollows where the crowns snapped. Inside one of the columns, if you could see in, there is a tree.

Nothing here has a weathering rind. Nothing here has a soil horizon. There is no dust; the air is too wet and everything sticky. The place is younger than the oldest spruce leaning over the rim, younger than the ice in the low corner is old, and it is being built at this moment, in this second, at two millimetres a year, by animals that will be dead in nine years and plants that are already stone at the base.

And it is coming apart at the same rate, more or less. A barrage grows until the head of water behind it exceeds what the crumb can hold, and then a section the size of a door lets go, and a pool empties in ninety seconds, and ten thousand midge larvae go down the spillway into the next pool down, and the wall's green face is left dry and dying and the birds above it abandon their cells within a week and the masons begin closing the gap before the month is out. Every season there are three or four of these. From the rim you would see them as a change in the pattern of white, a district rearranged.

The talus at the north end is still bare. That is the frontier: raw slabs, cold, sharp, no moss yet, only a black algal film in the wettest seams and the first springtails picking across it. Give it forty years. It will be terraced. It will be packed. It will be silent and full and lit and unfinished, and somewhere above it the rim will let go of another slab and start the whole thing again in a new place, and the old place will go under the rubble and be dissolved and come back as mortar.

If you stood in the middle of it, on the wet white step between two pools, you would be by a very wide margin the loudest thing in the basin. Your breath would go out and not come back. The birds nearest you would feel you through the stone before they saw you, and the word would pass along the ranks by touch, cell to cell to cell, faster than any sound could travel, and the whole quarter would go still — not quieter, it cannot get quieter — but still, a hundred thousand bodies holding, while the bar of sunlight moved off you and on down the terraces, and the drip you had been listening to fell again, four seconds later, exactly where it always falls.
