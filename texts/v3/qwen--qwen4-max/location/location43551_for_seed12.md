# The Standing Water

## I. The bulge

From the saddle at the rim, where the air is thin enough to make your heart stupid, you can see the whole of it: forty kilometers of warm water lying on top of a mountain, and lying *above* the mountain, heaped, domed, standing a hundred meters higher at its center than the stone lip that holds it.

It does not spill. That is the first thing you have to accept, and the locals do not help you with it, because they have no word for the problem. The water is new — not rain, not runoff, not the tired old water that goes around the world getting dirty. It comes up out of the rock, out of the wide warm mouths in the plateau floor, and it comes up still holding whatever it held when it was inside the stone. It is cohesive. It is fond of itself. It has not yet learned to fall.

So the sea sits on the roof of the world like a held breath, and its edge is not a beach but a meniscus: a smooth convex lip of water leaning out over four thousand meters of nothing, quivering very slightly, the way the surface of a full cup quivers when someone walks past the table.

It is fresh. Of course it is fresh. Salt is what water gets from grinding countries down, and nothing here has been ground. There is no sand anywhere in this landscape, no silt, no clay, no rounded pebble, no soil. Every rock is the shape it was born: knapped, glassy, edged. The peaks around the rim look like the day they were made because they are. Walk among them and you will cut your hands.

The water is thirty-four degrees at the mouths and about twenty-nine over the city, blood-warm, faintly slick between the fingers, tasting of cucumber and warm stone and, near the vents, of copper. Swimming into it from the cold thin air is like being taken into someone's mouth.

## II. Borrowed light

Above the water there is fog, permanently, a lid of it, because a warm sea on a cold mountain does nothing all day but exhale. From below, the surface is a silver ceiling, hammered, slightly moving. No sun comes through as a disk. What comes through is a green-grey wash, even, shadowless, about the brightness of a room an hour after sunset. You can read a face at ten paces. You cannot read a mark on a tool.

The second light comes from underneath. Along the seams of the plateau floor, where the water is being born, the stone glows a dull ember orange, no brighter than a banked fire, and it does not flicker. It has been that exact brightness for eleven thousand years. Between the two lights — the drowned day above and the birth-glow below — everything in the city is lit from two directions at once and has no proper shadow, only a soft doubling, like a thing remembered while you look at it.

The plants supply the rest. Their growing tips glow blue-green, dim as watch dials, and there are so many of them that the city's streets are outlined in cold stitching.

Because the water carries no sediment, visibility is absurd: two hundred meters, three hundred in the cold season. The far towers of the Weir look close enough to touch and are forty minutes away. Newcomers misjudge every distance and every depth, and the locals find this funny in a mild, unhurried way, and let it happen.

## III. The grown city

Here is what the water does, besides stand.

It is supersaturated. It is full of dissolved mineral it has not yet decided what to do with, and on any surface that stays still — a rope, a railing, a leaf, a knee — it lays that mineral down, patiently, in a rind. A fingernail's thickness in a year. Less on things that move. Nothing on things that move a lot.

This is not erosion. This is the opposite of erosion, and it is the only geology this country has. Nothing here wears away. Everything here is added to.

So they do not quarry, and they do not carve, and they have no metal — there are no ore bodies, because ore is what happens when water sorts a country's rubble, and there is no rubble. What they have is wicker, cordage, cultivated wood, bone, and patience.

They weave the buildings. A tower begins as a basket: a form of cordweed and split lungwood, lashed to a scaffold, shaped by three generations of a family who will not all see it finished. Then they hang it in a seep and leave it. The curd-moss colonizes it, the water fills the weave with stone from the inside out, and after twenty or forty years the form comes away — cut, peeled, sometimes broken out — and what stands is a fluted, ribbed, faintly woven-textured thing, hard as marble, with the pattern of the basket still printed in its skin. The whole city looks like fabric that has been thought about very seriously for a long time.

You can see the ages of the districts in the weave. Low Rungs is coarse, open, ancient, its walls showing a hand's-breadth lattice. The Grown Quarter is fine as linen and so smooth that the water slides off it in visible sheets. There are towers still in their wicker, dark and furry with moss, due in eighty years, with a family's mark tied to the scaffold and a small maintenance shrine at the base.

And every finished surface is trying to become a thicker surface. Doors seal if they are not worked. Hinges freeze. A rope left slack for a season is a rod; for three years, a column; for a lifetime, a load-bearing member of a building nobody designed. The city is dense — towers near-touching, bridges stacked in four and five layers, scaffolds inside scaffolds, cordage strung so thickly in the upper streets that swimming through them is like swimming through a harp — and half of that density is accretion, the place slowly filling itself in.

The maintenance is not a chore here. It is the culture. Streets exist because they are swum. A route is kept open by traffic, and when a district falls out of use it does not ruin, it *solidifies*, and after two hundred years you can find it only as a variation in the grain of the rock, a fossil neighborhood, doorways like the ghosts of pockets in a loaf.

## IV. Breath

The people are people: barrel-chested, dark-blooded, slow-hearted, adapted to thin air and long breath-holds. They live in pockets.

Lungwood is a hollow-trunked plant that grows in dense stands on the terraces, and it does what green things do, and the gas it makes collects in its cavities. The city farms it, taps it, and pipes it — jointed trunk-sections, sealed with resin, running along the undersides of bridges — into domes of grown stone inverted like cups. Under each dome is a room of air. Warm, thin, humid, smelling of resin and crushed leaf and the particular sourness of a place where many people have talked.

There are private lungs in the houses and public breath-fountains at the crossings, small bubble-domes on posts where a swimmer can put her head up and take four breaths and go on. The pipe-layers are the most important guild in the city and the least honored, because their work is invisible until it fails. A blocked line does not kill anyone quickly. It kills a district over a month, quietly, in its sleep, and the survivors move out and the doors seal behind them and the rock takes the street.

In the lungs, people speak aloud. This is the only place they can. It is where the arguing happens, and the singing, and the long ridiculous gossip that would be intolerable anywhere else. Children are sent to the lung to shout, as a treat.

## V. Cords, and the price of a hammer

Water carries sound four and a half times faster than air, and much farther, and this sea has almost no ambient noise: no surf, no rain, no shingle, no shoals of fish, no insects. A struck stone here rings the whole valley. A dropped chisel is a public event, heard by nine thousand people, several of whom will be able to tell you which family dropped it.

So they have no hammers. They split stone the slow way, with wedges of dry lungwood driven into a groove and then wetted, swelling over a day and a night until the block lets go with a soft *knock*, a knuckle sound, that travels three kilometers and is considered polite. They press, they lever, they wedge, they wait. The whole built mass of this city was made without a bang.

Speech, at distance, is by cord. The city is strung with taut lines, hundreds of them, anchored district to district, and you talk by plucking: a bone pick, a pattern of damps and releases, a language of shivers that arrives in the hands of whoever is holding the other end. Everyone is holding some end, most of the time, the way people elsewhere half-listen to a street. Line-speech is public, fast, and beautifully crude. Lovers use it. So do tax collectors, of whom there are none, and weather-watchers, of whom there are many.

Below all of it, at the edge of hearing, is the hum of the mouths — not a sound so much as a pressure in the sternum, a note far too low for the ear, steady as a held organ pedal. People from the rim settlements say they cannot sleep in the city because of the silence on top of the noise. People from the city say the rim is unbearable because there is nothing underneath you.

## VI. The green

Vegetation is everywhere and there are only about nine kinds of it.

Cordweed in farmed cables, growing a fathom a year, harvested on a rotation so slow that a family's rope-fields are inherited like orchards. Curd-moss, the mason's animal, sheeting every seep. Lamp-lettuce in the shallows, glowing, edible, bitter. Lungwood in dark stands. And the tannin forest — a drowned woodland of tall pale trunks on the western terraces, so dense with slow shed matter that the water there tastes bitter and astringent and slightly numb, and the visibility drops to twenty meters, and everyone speaks more softly without knowing why.

Nothing rots. This is the thing outsiders never get right. There is no rot here — no bacteria have arrived, no fungus has learned the trick, and the water is too mineral and too young. What falls, stays. A shed frond drifting up from the forest will still be a frond in a hundred years, greenish, intact, flattened against the underside of the lid along with everything else that ever rose: a ceiling-midden, a raft-world, kilometers of pressed and layered plant matter lying against the silver like leaves under ice. Occasionally a sheet of it tears free and comes down over the city in a slow soft fall of stuff that takes two days to arrive and is harvested for cordage, bedding, and wrapping the dead.

The upward drift is gentle but constant — the birth-current, everything buoyant going up forever. Baskets are tethered. Children are tethered. A tool dropped sinks and is findable; a tool let go rises and is gone into the ceiling and, eventually, into the fog and out of the world.

## VII. The few

Animal life is scarce to the point of embarrassment. Six grazers in the whole basin: pale, blind, ox-sized, mild, cropping the lamp-meadows at a rate that lets the meadows recover in eleven years. Each has a name and a district and a sort of citizenship. Children are taken to see them the way children elsewhere are taken to see a cathedral.

There are wick-birds, three or four pairs, that come down through the fog at the rim once a year to drink and go away again, and their arrival is a holiday.

And there is the biter.

It is small, pale, about the length of a forearm, and it appeared in the eastern shallows within the memory of people now living. It has learned to eat other animals. Nothing else here has. Predation is not old in this country; it may not be older than four generations. The biter took a grazer's flank nine years ago and the wound did not close, and then — this is the part that has the whole city talking on the cords, quietly, at all hours — the wound *softened*. It went bad. Something is living in the grazer's side that is not the grazer.

The physicians have a word for it now. The word is new. There are people whose entire adult life has been the study of this one wound, and they are not sure whether they are watching a disaster or a birth, and they suspect the two are the same thing seen from different ends.

## VIII. The grandmothers

The dead do not go away either.

They are moored at their own doorways, ankles roped to the sill, hands folded, in the warm sweet sterile water, and they stay. They tan slightly. They do not swell. And year by year the water lays its rind on them, as it does on everything that holds still, so that the recently dead are people-shaped and the long dead are stone-shaped, and the very old dead are indistinguishable from the doorpost they stand beside, and are sometimes, in fact, holding up the lintel.

The city's addresses are grandmothers. You do not say a street and a number; you say *the house with the two sisters at the door, the one with the broken thumb.* Nobody moves house. Moving would mean leaving your dead, and the dead are the deed.

The living stone up too, more slowly, from the inside — the deep-swimmers first, the ones who go down to the mouths at a hundred and fifty meters to bring up copper-tasting water for the seeps. Their joints go gritty in middle age. By seventy, most people are half marble, moving in the slow deliberate way that gives the whole city its tempo, and the very old sit in niches and are consulted, and then are not consulted, and then are architecture.

Age is measured in rinds. A year is a skin. A marriage is two people agreeing to share a cord and be worn smooth on the same schedule.

And in the corners of every house, in the doorways, on the stairs, there is dust.

It is the dust of use: the microscopic grit of feet and hands and rope-wear, the only material in this entire country that has been *removed* from anything. It is the first sediment in the world. They sweep it up. They keep it in small stoppered jars, a few pinches a year, and it is the most valuable substance anyone owns, and in three houses in the Grown Quarter there are people who have just begun, this decade, to wet it and shape it and see whether it will hold.

## IX. The lip

At the rim, where the meniscus leans out over the drop, there are posts, and on the posts there are marks, and the marks go back nine hundred years.

The sea is rising a hand's breadth in a generation. It loses what it loses to the fog, and gains what the mouths give it, and the difference is small and positive and patient. When it overtops a saddle it does not pour; it *walks* — a slow standing tongue of water going out along a ridge, holding itself, exploring, sometimes retracting over a century, sometimes finding a valley and filling it and standing there, a new finger of sea, a new country, unmapped, unlit, waiting to be grown into.

The lip-watchers measure the bulge. They know the curve is getting steeper. They have a story, told on the cords in the small hours, that one day the water will learn to fall — that the lip will let go all at once and four hundred cubic kilometers of warm sweet water will go down the mountain in a single afternoon and make, in one stroke, the first rivers, the first mud, the first sand, the first soil, the first salt, and everything downstream that has never existed here.

They do not talk about it as an ending. They talk about it as a delivery.

Meanwhile the city goes on being added to. The wedges swell in their grooves. The pipes are checked. A woman swims her street twice a day so that it will still be a street next year, and at her doorway her grandmother stands with folded hands and a skin of new stone on her shoulders, and under her bare heel the step loses a hair's breadth of itself, and that — in all this bright unfinished country where nothing has ever been worn away — is the only weather there is.
