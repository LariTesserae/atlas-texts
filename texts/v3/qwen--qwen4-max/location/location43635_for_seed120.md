The ring comes first. Then the hammer falls.

Half a beat of warning, every time — the copper note blooming out of the stone before the mallet arrives, so the setters hear their work a moment before they do it. Nobody here finds this remarkable. A dropped chisel sings before it slips, and you catch it. A slab that is going to crack announces the crack while there is still time to wedge it. The room is loud the way the inside of a bell is loud, and the loudness is a kind of care.

It is a wet room, six paces by four, and you can touch the ceiling flat-palmed without straightening your arm. It sits at the top of a mountain that has not finished arriving.

---

Start with the heat, because the heat is the first thing that tells you where you are. Blood-warm and above, forty and a bit, and wet with it — not the dry oven of a forge but the closeness of a lung. The walls sweat. They have always sweated. The stone here is new, so new it still holds the water it was made from, and as each course takes the weight of the courses above it, that water is squeezed out through the joints and runs down the faces in slow ropes, warm as tears, tasting of iron and milk and something faintly sweet underneath. The setters call it quarry-water. It is the only water there is. Nothing here has ever been rained on by a cloud that exists.

The rain that does come comes from later.

It arrives through the crown-hole at the room's apex, a hand-span opening left unfinished in the last course, and it falls warm and slightly out of order: a storm that will break on these slopes in nine thousand years, leaking backward through the hole in a fine hot drizzle that lasts an afternoon. Sometimes a downpour that has not happened yet dumps itself into the cistern in the time it takes to eat. The setters set out their catch-basins by the sound of it. They know the future rains by taste. The one that comes in the cold months of a century not yet begun carries seeds — small hard specks, red-husked — and those are planted deliberately, one to a joint, and that is where the forest comes from.

---

Everything in the room is made. This is not a cave that people have improved. There is no natural substrate anywhere, not one surface that was found rather than laid. The walls are coursed basalt, hand-fitted, dry-jointed and then jointed again with lime putty. The floor is a single slab scored into a grid. The ceiling is corbelled, each stone stepping in over the last, and the crown-hole at the top is not a gap but a deliberate omission, kept open with a ring of wedged blocks. The cords are twisted from a fiber that grows in the joints. The lamps are clay, thumb-pinched, burning a resin that beads on the roots. The moss is planted. The water is manufactured by weight. The setters themselves were quarried.

That last is not a metaphor they would understand as a metaphor. A niche in the north wall quickens — goes soft, goes luminous, smells like the inside of a mouth — and after some weeks a setter is split out of it, adult, wet, blinking, with hands already callused. The calluses had to be carved. Nothing here wears smooth on its own. Palm-polish is a craft, done with a scored bone and patience, applied to tool handles and to the shoulders of new people before they can work without slipping.

---

Nothing wears. That is the rule underneath all the other rules.

Erosion has not started. There is no dust in this room — none, not a mote, and the setters find the idea of dust obscene, the way you would find the idea of your own bones outside your body. Edges stay exactly as sharp as they were made. A footprint in the putty stays a footprint for a thousand years unless someone fills it. Copper chisels never dull and also never bite; a file removes nothing; you cannot smooth a thing by rubbing it, cannot round a corner by handling it, cannot deepen a channel by letting water run.

Water here only adds. Every drop leaves its mineral behind. The walls thicken inward at the rate of a fingernail a month, faster after a heavy course is set, and the floor rises, and if the setters did nothing the room would close like a throat in four generations.

So they harvest it. They score the accretion into rectangles with a copper point, drill a line of holes, drive dry cane wedges into the holes, and pour quarry-water on the cane. The wood swells. The stone lets go along the line they asked for, whole, with a crack you feel in your back teeth. That is the only subtraction permitted here: not abrasion but separation, not wearing away but taking a piece that was always going to be a piece. The slabs come out warm and dripping and are carried up through the crown-hole on a cane ladder and laid, outward and downward, as the mountain.

The room stays six paces by four only because it is constantly being cut back to six paces by four. The mountain outside is made entirely of the room's own excess. This is the whole economy. There is nothing else to do here and it will never be finished.

---

The mountain is being built from its summit down.

The peak was the first thing laid, and the setters work outward and beneath it, course by course, hanging in a basket of rope and cane over an absence. There is no ground below. There is a direction where ground is proposed. Through a grate in the floor — three fingers wide, always kept clear, the most important object in the world — the plumb lines go down into that absence, forty of them, bobbed with snail shells, and they do not hang parallel. They converge. They aim, slowly, at a point that has not been built yet, and where they aim is where the base will be, and the cord-keepers adjust the aim by a hair each season with a knot and a argument.

The mountain is being aimed at a plain.

Down there, ten thousand cubits of unmade air, and the wind that will one day exist goes through the scaffolding and makes a low continuous note you feel in the sinuses rather than hear. That note is the floor of all the other sounds. Above it: the water-organ. The setters cut the runoff channels to specific widths and depths and lengths so that each one speaks a different pitch at a different flow, and a trained ear can hear the state of every wall in the room without looking — the north face flat and grieving means the joints are opening, the cistern channel going sharp means a harvest is due. Above that: the wedge-cracks, arriving a half-beat after their own sound. Above that: the setting-chord.

When a course cures, it sings. A sustained low tone, somewhere under the range of speech, and it comes with heat — the exotherm of stone deciding to be stone — and for a day afterward the room is unbearable, forty-five, fifty, the moss steaming, the setters lying flat in the shallow troughs with wet liverwort packed over their necks, waiting it out. Then the walls weep double for a week and the accretion comes fast and there is a great deal to harvest. Cold would mean the mountain had stopped. Nobody wants cold.

---

There is no room between anything.

Every joint is packed with moss — eleven named kinds, and the setters can tell them by smell in the dark. Cushion-moss in the dry courses. A black liverwort that takes the hottest faces. Feather-moss in the cistern lip, always dripping, always trembling. Ferns come out of the horizontal seams in flat spray fans, root-bound, leaves overlapping like鳞片, like scales, like hands over hands. And through all of it the coursewood: a fig-thing whose roots are structural, deliberately mortared into the joints while young so that as they thicken they clamp the courses from inside. Root-tension instead of iron. The masons plant the wall and the wall is held by what it grows. In the oldest quarter the root-mass is a body of its own, thick as a torso, glowing faintly along its seams where a fungus lives that eats lime and gives back a green light you can read a knot by, if you hold the knot close.

That is most of the light. The resin lamps are few and smoky and mostly used for close work. Fresh accretion glows dull orange while it is still deciding — quickstone, the setters call it, and it is the only warm-colored thing here. The crown-hole gives a gray that is not the sky, because there is no sky yet, only the place where one is scheduled.

Shadows in this room fall toward the crown-hole. All of them, regardless of where the lamp is. Everything's shadow aims up at the unbuilt sky, the way the plumb lines aim down at the unbuilt ground, and between those two absences the room sits and sweats and rings.

---

Almost nothing lives here that walks.

No flies. No ants, no worms, no beetles in the moss, no bats in the crown-hole. The loudness is entirely stone and water and no animal noise at all, which is the thing visitors would notice, if there were visitors, if there were an outside for them to come from.

There are the joint-snails: white, thumbnail-sized, in the hundreds, grazing the quickstone and leaving behind a finished lime joint harder than anything the setters can mix. They migrate up the wall ahead of the accretion front, always a hand's breadth above it, and reading their position is how you know where to harvest next. Their empty shells are the plumb bobs and also the unit of measure. One shell, one course.

There is the tally cricket. Blind, pale, one at a time — never two, and when it dies another is found in the feather-moss the same day. It chirps in runs, and the runs count the courses, and the cord-keepers check their knots against it and find the cricket right. A setter named Ossu keeps hers in a shell hung at her throat and feeds it putty from her thumbnail, and when she works she taps the wall twice before each blow, so the cricket hears ahead of the hammer hearing ahead of her.

And once in a long while the future rain brings something down with it: a moth with no lineage, wet, enormous, that lives about an hour and beats itself against the quickstone glow until it comes apart. The setters watch these carefully and say nothing. They are the only animals here that die, and dying, in this room, is understood to be a kind of wearing, and wearing has not been invented yet.

---

Here is the thing you learn last, when the heat has made you stupid and you are lying in the trough with liverwort on your neck and the water-organ running its scales above you.

The niches in the walls are not storage.

There are hundreds of them, packed shoulder to shoulder, no gap between, each one a hand-span or an arm-span, each one wet, each one growing something. That one with the fern fan and the three standing stones is a valley. That one where the channel forks and silt has been allowed to gather is a delta, and it is finished, and it is waiting. The long shelf of cushion-moss with the snail-track across it is a forest of nine thousand years, and it is being kept at the correct temperature, and the red-husked seeds from the rain that has not happened are going into it one at a time.

The setters are not building a mountain that will someday have a region around it. They are building the region, at hand scale, in a wet room, in niches, and the mountain is the part they have to make big first so that the rest has somewhere to stand.

The room is six paces by four. The room contains everything that will be. It is very hot, and getting hotter, because a course has just set somewhere out on the unfinished flank and the chord is coming up through the floor. The walls are weeping. The snails are climbing. Far below, forty shells hang converging in the dark, patient, aiming.

The ring comes first.
