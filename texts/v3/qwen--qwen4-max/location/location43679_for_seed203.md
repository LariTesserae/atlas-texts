There is no reason for it to be bright. You are under a continent. Above you is a roof of matted root and frozen peat and, somewhere past that, sky; below you is a floor of black rock that no one you will ever meet has touched. And yet the light is enormous — a shadowless, sourceless, snow-blind brilliance that comes from every direction at once and leaves your eyes aching and your depth perception ruined.

The first mistake everyone makes is to think it is fog. The second is to think it is small.

---

Call it the Under. It is a blister: a lens-shaped void that opened when gas came up out of the rock and lifted the world's green skin off its bones, and the skin, instead of falling back, bridged the gap and kept building. That was perhaps four thousand years ago. Nothing here has had time to become old. There is no soil, no rounded grain, no sediment, no silt, no fossil, no patina. Every edge in the Under is a fresh fracture. The gravel is razor work. You do not kneel without wrapping your knees first.

The material is plant, and the plant is glass.

Not glass the way a window is glass — hydrated opal, laid down by a lineage that gave up chlorophyll a long time ago and now builds its skeleton out of dissolved silica, the way a horsetail once did, the way a diatom does. It grows white. It grows in ribs and struts and cables and hollow columns, and its surfaces are the milky, faintly blue-at-depth white of a broken bottle held to the sun. Under the skin is pith: wet, fibrous, cold, faintly sweet, and almost perfectly sound-dead. Under the pith, in the load-bearing members, is sap under tension, drawn up and held there at negative pressure, so that a column of water a kilometer tall stands in a stem like a rope standing in a tower.

The ribs are hollow. That is the thing that reconciles the two impossible facts about this place: it is entirely plant, and it is almost entirely empty.

Biomass here is a skin a few centimeters thick over voids measured in kilometers. A single rib may span nine kilometers and weigh less than the air it displaces — a tube of opal no thicker than your wrist, stayed with cables finer than harp string, spanning a gulf you could stack mountains in. The lattice is not dense. It is *sufficient*. It grows along stress lines the way bone does, thickening where the load runs, thinning to nothing where the load does not, so that the whole structure looks uncannily like an engineering diagram of itself: catenaries, thrust curves, fans of tension radiating from anchor points, minimal surfaces stretched between them. You can read the forces in the shapes. The people here do read them, the way a sailor reads water.

By surface area, the Under is one hundred percent alive. By volume, it is a near-vacuum with a white lace suspended in it. Stand on a sill and look out and you will see, past the near members, nothing but white going on forever — and it will take you a long time to understand that you are seeing forty kilometers of empty air with a few threads across it, and not a solid wall three meters away.

---

The light comes in through the wells.

They are wounds in the roof: places where the mat tore and never healed, ragged apertures a hundred meters to five kilometers across, rimmed with hanging green — the only green in the world, the photosynthesizing surface plants dangling their root-beards down into the cold. Through each well falls a column of raw daylight, hard-edged, full of turning motes, striking a sill or a rib-fan far below and *splashing*.

And then it does not leave. That is the trick. Everything down here is white and nothing down here eats light — the plants are heterotrophs, fed by sugar pumped down from the surface mat and by gas seeping up from the rock, and they have no use for a photon. So the interior is an integrating sphere the size of a sea. Light enters at a handful of holes and bounces, and bounces, and bounces, losing almost nothing at each bounce, until the whole volume is filled with a uniform, directionless radiance barely dimmer than the beams themselves. There are no shadows in the Under except within a few meters of an object. There is no visible source unless you crane your neck and find a blue disk overhead, small and hard and terribly far away.

The consequence is a permanent clear-air whiteout. Contrast survives; distance does not. A rib a hand's width away and a rib four kilometers away have the same value, the same crispness, the same nothing behind them. People go mad about this in their first season. They reach for things that are not there. They step off sills that were never sills. The veterans navigate by tap and count — a soft click of tongue against tooth, and then waiting, and reading the return, which is not an echo but a *pressure*, a faint thickening of the air against the face.

---

It is cold. Not bitterly, not killingly — a steady, wet, near-freezing cold that never lifts and never deepens, the mean annual temperature of the tundra above, delivered downward and pooled. Cold air is heavy. It falls through the wells in slow white cataracts, kilometer-wide columns of fog that take an hour to reach the sills, and when they land they spread outward in radial sheets, and the sheets from neighboring wells collide and rear up into standing walls of mist that hang for days. There is a permanent slow cyclone under the largest well, a turning mill of cloud two hundred kilometers across that has been rotating since before anyone was born. There are fog rivers. There are inversions you can climb through like floors of a house. There is rain, occasionally — a fog mass gets too heavy and lets go — and the rain never reaches the bottom, because the bottom is thirty kilometers down and the drops are caught, every one, by the lattice, and drunk.

The Under has its own weather because it is too big not to.

And it breathes. When a storm crosses the surface, the pressure change pumps the entire volume through its wells, in and out, a tide with a period of three or four days. The plants feel this. They are keyed to it. On the rising tide they build; on the falling tide — the *slack* — they release, shed, let go of members they can no longer justify, and the whole world re-solves its own load diagram at once.

---

This is what the turbulence is. Not wind, not fire, not noise. Growth and failure, at continental scale, continuously.

You can watch a tip extend. It is the only naked wet thing here: a pale green bud the size of a boat, uncoiling, laying down opal behind itself at a rate of meters a day, and if you put your palm on the stem behind it you feel nothing at all except a slow shove, a subsonic push against the sternum that after an hour makes you nauseous. The hush-ache, the people call it. Growth here is infrasonic. It is felt in the chest and the teeth and never once heard.

Failure is the other half. The lattice is pre-stressed — every member is loaded, every cable is tight — and when one buckles, its load goes somewhere, and that somewhere may not be able to take it. So there are cascades. A shatter front can travel for two days across six hundred kilometers, a propagating bloom of white dust and dropping shards, the structure ahead of it thickening frantically as the load arrives, the structure behind it already being dissolved. From a distance it looks like a slow bright cloud rising in a place where there was nothing. It looks like weather. It is weather.

Nothing accumulates. A creeping resorption mat follows every collapse and eats the debris, silica and all, and pumps it back up into the frontier. This is why the Under stays empty despite being made of nothing but life: the standing mass is a moving front, not a pile. The shape persists; the substance passes through. It is a cathedral that is also a fire.

---

And it is quiet. It is *aggressively* quiet, and the quiet is a law.

Partly it is the pith, which swallows vibration. Partly it is the damp: every surface wears a film of condensate, and wet opal does not ring. Partly it is distance — a collapse forty kilometers away arrives two minutes after you see it, and arrives as a sigh, a soft surf-hiss with no attack, no crack, nothing you could call a bang. You learn to measure the world by the delay between the bloom and the sigh.

But mostly it is pre-stress. A loaded lattice is a lattice waiting for a reason. A sharp sound at the right pitch, close enough, can start a failure in a member already at its limit, and then the failure runs. Everyone here knows this in their body before they know it in words. There is no shouting in the Under. There is no drumming, no horn, no dropped tool. Hammers are wrapped in felt. Boots are felt. Cords are damped with pith sleeves so they cannot hum. Speech is a low murmur at close range and, past arm's length, hands — a full sign language, elaborate, fast, with a grammar of taps for the dark.

The silence is not peace. It is discipline.

---

Water is everywhere and there is almost none of it. No lakes, no rivers, no waterfalls; the drainage is all internal, plumbing rather than landscape. Outside the plants, water exists as film, as bead, as fog, and in pockets: the axil cisterns, cupped where three members meet, holding a few liters to a few hundred, skin-frozen at the top, sterile, tasting faintly of green almond and iron. You break the ice with a wrapped mallet and you drink and it is the best water you have ever had.

The other water is taut. The columns hold their sap under suction, and if you pierce one it does not spill — it *inhales*, and then it cavitates, and the vapor shock runs up the member, and a structural column that has just cavitated is a structural column that is about to let go. So drinking is a craft. You learn to lay two fingers on a stem and read whether it is slack or taut, and you learn which cisterns are fed by slack vessels and are therefore safe to tap, and you learn this before you learn anything else, because the alternative is dying under a kilometer of falling glass because you were thirsty.

---

Life other than the substrate is scarce, and slow, and quiet, and mostly small.

There is a grazer, palm-sized, six-limbed, that rasps the opal skin with a mineral radula and builds its own shell out of what it eats; you might see one in a month of walking. There are translucent flatworms in the cisterns, and a thing like a white shrimp that lives in the ferment pockets where sap has pooled and gone slowly to alcohol. There is a furred flier, a heavy-bodied moth-analog that works the light columns and nowhere else, navigating by the wells, overwintering as a pupa in a case of rime. And there are tracks, sometimes, on the wide sills: three-toed, long-strided, spaced four meters apart, belonging to something that walks the ribs by distributing its weight and that no one has ever reliably seen.

Flowering is the catastrophe. A rib flowers once, at the end of its life, and the flowering is a structural event — the member empties itself into a hanging panicle a kilometer long, loses its stiffness, and comes apart. The seeds are glass-heavy and fall for minutes. The moths come from hundreds of kilometers, the only congregation of animals in the Under, and then it is over and the lattice closes the gap and the whole thing is resorbed inside a season.

And in the cold pockets there grow frost gardens: rime feathers, hoar ferns, ice architecture that mimics the opal architecture with uncanny fidelity and lives for a single pressure tide. Two rival builders, one of them made of water and doomed, working the same forms.

---

The people are as rare as the animals.

You find them by ochre. It is the only warm color in the world — a red-brown clay carried up from the deep floor, and they mark with it: a thumbprint on a rib at eye height, an arrow of dots along a cable, a hand pressed flat at a junction where three routes meet. A red thumbprint every two or three days' travel. That is the entire built landscape of a continent: fingerprints, and cord.

Their camps are hung. A platform of lashed tendon slung in the crotch of a fan, a windbreak woven from pith-fiber and sealed with sap, a stone cup for the rare guarded ember, a rack of tools. Nothing is permanent because nothing is allowed to be. The substrate grows over anything that stays still. A lashed stair thickens under your feet and begins, within a season, to eat its own geometry; a platform must be cut free every year or be swallowed into the rib and become a lump of white matter with a hollow inside it. Most of what looks like partial ruins down here is exactly that: a room absorbed, a doorway gone to solid opal, the ghost of a camp inside a stem.

They have no metal and no kiln, and this is not backwardness — it is arithmetic. There is no fuel that is not structural, no dry thing to burn, no place to keep a fire hot for long, and nothing accumulates in a world that resorbs its own debris. What they have instead is horticultural lithics. They *grow* their tools: a wound is made in a cambium sleeve, a mold of leaf is pressed in, and the plant lays down opal into the shape you asked for. Blades, hooks, wedges, needles, scrapers, harvested sharp and finished with a few careful knapping strikes, conchoidal fracture blooming white under a felt-wrapped hammer.

And they build the same way. You do not construct a bridge in the Under. You score a stem where you want a member to go, and you wait, and the load does the rest, and in two seasons there is a strut where you asked for one. Architecture by injury. A whole village is a set of wounds made patiently in the right places, decades ago, by people who are now absorbed into the walls.

They wear white. At fifty paces they are gone.

---

Go far enough and the lens pinches. The roof descends, the floor rises, the voids close, the lattice densifies until it is no longer lace but marrow, and then it is solid, and there is no further. That rim is thousands of kilometers away in every direction. Somewhere below the middle of it all is the black rock, wet and seeping and warm by comparison, where the anchors go down and the ochre comes from and where the dead are said to be taken, though the descent is nine days through densening white and almost nobody makes it twice.

And once a year the wells go dark.

The surface has its polar night; the holes stop delivering; and the Under, which has never known shadow, learns absolute black in the space of an afternoon. The cold drainage deepens. The pressure tides swing wider. The slack comes harder and more often, and the shatter fronts run through a world nobody can see, and the whole population sits still on its hung platforms with its hands on the cords, feeling the blooms arrive as sighs from every quarter, counting the delays, naming the distances in the dark.

Then a blue disk opens somewhere overhead, five kilometers wide and terribly far away, and the white comes back all at once, from everywhere, the way it always does — and the tips are already uncoiling, and the world is already redrawing itself, and none of it was ever finished, and none of it ever will be.
