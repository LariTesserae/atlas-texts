# Toll, in the Kettle

You come down to it. There is no other way in. The road runs across ordinary country — wet fields, ordinary birds — and then ends at a lip of grey limestone, and the ground opens, and a stair goes down inside the opening in nine long switchbacks, and the air changes on the third step: warmer, heavier, and suddenly loud.

The Kettle is a collapsed chamber about three hundred paces across and sixty deep, and most of its roof is still on. That is the first thing to understand. The lid survived. A single vault of ancient stone spans the bowl with four ragged slots in it, and through the slots comes a thin daylight that the fog eats before it reaches the fourth tier. Everything below is lit by lamps. Everything below is warm — thirty degrees, near enough, and saturated, so that sweat sits on the skin like oil and never leaves. The warmth comes up with the water. The water comes from a long way down and arrives at thirty-four, and it has been arriving for longer than anyone has been counting.

The walls weep. Not dramatically: no falls, except in the northwest quadrant where a permanent drizzle stands in the air like a held breath. Mostly the walls simply sweat, sheet after sheet of limestone with a film of water sliding down it a millimetre at a time, and the film catches the lamplight and moves. Faces of stone glisten and swell and drip. There are eleven thousand named drips in Toll. Children learn forty of them before they learn to read.

## What the water does

It is old rain, fallen somewhere else, some centuries ago, and it has spent the interim underground dissolving the country it moved through. It arrives full of lime and iron and a faint breath of sulphur, and it arrives under pressure, and when it reaches the air of the Kettle it lets go of what it carried.

So the water builds. This is the central fact of the place, and everything else is a consequence.

Every wet surface in Toll is growing a rind. Travertine, tufa, flowstone — call it rind, as they do — pale gold where it is fresh, honey-brown where old, banded like an agate where it has been cut. It grows over pipes, over rails, over boots left out overnight, over the knuckles of a statue, over a sleeping man's hand if he sleeps in the wrong gallery with his hand in a drip. In the quiet places it lays down a hair's thickness in a year. In the loud places it lays down a finger.

The forms are all rounded. Nothing in the Kettle has an edge that has not been cut this season. Bolts become knuckles, knuckles become knobs, knobs become the smooth anonymous swellings of a thing that has been in water a long time. The town looks weathered, but it is not being worn away — it is being *added to*, at the same rate it is being cut back, and has been for so long that the two have come into balance and the balance is the shape you see. Toll is not eroding. Toll is in equilibrium, which is a much stranger condition, and looks similar from a distance.

## The noise

At the bottom of the bowl is the Gullet, a shaft of unknown depth cased in brass and stone, and beside it the Long Bell: a pump the size of a chapel, which does not ring. It strokes once every eleven seconds. The stroke is not a bang but a vast soft *thud* you feel in the sinuses, followed by the sound of a column of water being lifted, which is the sound of a throat swallowing, amplified past sense.

Above that fundamental — which is too low to hear, and sits behind the eyes like a weight — lies the rest: the roar of the return-falls, the hiss of the weeping faces, the eleven-hundred small percussions of drips on stone and metal and leaf, the whine of the reamers' spindles, the ring of hand-chisels, the arc-lamps' hum, and beneath it all a fine dry ticking, ceaseless, everywhere, that visitors mistake for insects.

It is not insects. There are no insects.

Speech is impossible in about a third of the town. In those galleries people use the palm alphabet, tapping and pressing on the back of the hand, or they simply touch: forehead, wrist, shoulder, a grammar of contacts older than the machinery. Conversation happens in the *hushes* — pockets where the standing wave cancels itself and the roar drops to the sound of a distant river. There are perhaps sixty hushes in Toll. They are mapped to the centimetre. They are the only real estate. A deed in Toll specifies two numbers: the hush, in bells, and the rind-rate, in fingers per year. A quiet room that limes slowly is a palace. A quiet room that limes fast is a fortune spent on chisels. A loud room is where you put the dead.

## Sound grows stone

Here is the thing the engineers cannot account for.

Agitated water gives up its gas and drops its lime; everyone knows this; it is why the falls are rimmed with cream-coloured lips and why the pump housings must be reamed weekly. But in the Kettle the effect follows the *sound*. Where the air is loud, the film of water on the wall shivers, and where it shivers it limes, and it limes a hundred times faster than any calculation permits. The standing wave is written into the rock. The antinodes have grown into fluted curtains, ribbed and folded like the inside of a mouth, thick as a man's torso, closing across doorways in a season. The nodes stay soft and mossy and thin.

So the town's architecture is a portrait of its own noise. The cathedral flutings of the Ninth Throat, four storeys of them, gold and translucent, are a diagram of a chord that has been held since before the founding. When the pumps are retuned — once in a generation, an event of enormous political violence — the stone begins slowly to grow into a different shape, and the old shape stops growing, and for eighty years you can see both, superimposed, like a hand over a hand.

Nobody in Toll builds. There is no construction, in the sense of assembling. The town accretes continuously and the people *subtract*. Masons are subtractors. A house is a void maintained inside an advancing solid, and the maintenance is the rent: so many fingers of depth removed per year, logged in the ledger of rind, paid in labour or in coin. Let a household stop reaming for three years and the door is a niche; five and the niche is a dimple; ten and there is only the wall, banded, and inside it the shape of a room, and inside the room, still, everything they owned, waiting for a quarryman.

There are eleven Tolls beneath Toll. They are not ruins. They are strata.

## Light

Fog forms where the warm seeps meet the cooler air drawn down the slots, and it never clears, and visibility is forty paces on a good day. You cannot see across the Kettle. You know its size by echo, and everyone here is fluent in echo: children are taught to clap at corners, drivers click their tongues at junctions, the blind navigate as well as anyone and are considered unremarkable.

The lamps are arc-lamps and mantles and old filaments in glass jars, orange, spaced close, and each one wears a halo of fog two paces wide, so the town at distance is a constellation of soft oranges hanging in brown air, tier above tier, none of them sharp.

And the lamps lime. A lamp left running in a wet gallery acquires a skin, then a shell, then a rind, and its light goes from orange to amber to the colour of honey held up to a candle, and then it is a glowing pebble, and then it is a stone with a warm centre, and then it is a stone. The walls of Toll are studded with dead lamps in horizontal bands. You date a wall by its lamps, the way elsewhere you would date a cliff by its shells. There are lamps in the lower galleries of a type nobody can make anymore, still faintly warm, still faintly glowing, sealed in three hundred years of gold.

Around the living lamps grow the lamp-gardens: halos of fern and liverwort and the pale creeping thing they call cable-fern, which runs along conduit and roots into the rind and takes its minerals straight from the water. Green follows light and drip-lines. Stone follows sound. The two geometries cross and interfere everywhere, and that interference is what Toll looks like: fluting and branching, the chord and the vein, laid over one another in every wall.

## The absence

There are no animals in the Kettle. None. No rats, no bats, no flies, no mosquitoes in all that warm standing water, no worms in the mats, no fish in the sumps, no mites, nothing. It is not that they die here; it is that they do not occur, and the ones brought down the stair sicken quietly within a day and lie down and are not eaten, because there is nothing to eat them. They go to slime, and the slime goes to mat, and the mat goes on.

Birds turn aside at the lip. There is a line in the air above the rim, invisible and exact, and every bird that meets it banks away. The surveyors have walked it. It follows the lip to within a pace.

Nobody knows why. The water-men blame the chemistry; the sound-men blame the pressure; the priests say the Kettle was made empty and emptiness is a condition of the lease. What is certain is the consequences: no pollination, so nothing here flowers usefully; no dispersal, so every plant spreads by runner and fragment and the whole green population of the bowl is a handful of clones, ancient, spreading at a metre a year; no meat, no leather, no wool, no eggs; no bites, so the word for *monster* in the Toll dialect is a word for a valve. Children are afraid of machinery, specifically of the nip between a governor and its seat. They have never been afraid of anything with a face.

And no rot-flies means the loud galleries smell of nothing but wet stone, hot brass, sulphur, and the sweet fungal smell of the vats. It is a clean smell for a place so wet. Visitors find it more disturbing than a stink would be.

## What lives instead

The insect layer of the noise is the creepers: palm-sized tracked reamers, brass-bodied, released in their thousands nine generations ago to keep the rind off the pipe-runs, and long since unsupervised. They charge from thermal gradients and trickle-cells, they graze the loud faces where the stone grows fastest, they cluster and disperse, they fail and are cannibalised by the others for parts in a manner their designers did not intend and cannot now inspect, because the designers are three strata down.

Their numbers boom and crash on a eleven-year cycle that tracks the water. The town counts them the way other places count beetles. Children keep them in jars and feed them chalk. When a creeper dies its brass is harvested and its serial number is read aloud at the scrap-house, and the numbers now in circulation run into series that no living archive can name.

And in the pipe galleries, in the dark between the loud and the quiet, the mould moves. A yellow slime, veined, pulsing, advancing a centimetre an hour, and it finds the shortest path. It always finds the shortest path. The engineers lay a maze of damp baffles and let the mould solve it over a week, and then they lay pipe along the veins it made, and this is how Toll's plumbing has been designed for four hundred years. The mould is the only thing in the bowl that hunts, in the sense of moving toward what it wants. Watching it, you understand what the place is missing, and how nearly the missing thing has been replaced, and how slowly.

## The people, and the slow

They are pale and ruddy at once, lamp-tanned orange, big-pupilled, slow-blinking, dressed in oiled fibre and fungal felt, barefoot or in wooden clogs on warm wet stone. They eat curd from the sulphur vats, algae from the raceways, mushroom in eleven preparations, and everything tastes faintly of iron and lime. Their teeth are perfect. Their joints are not: the same water that builds the walls builds them, and by sixty there is chalk in the knee, and by seventy in the spine, and the old go heavy and quiet and are said to be *setting*.

The dead are carried to the antinodes and laid against a growing face, and in a season the rind takes them, and in three years there is a rib in the curtain with a shape in it. You visit by touch. In the Ninth Throat there is a fluted column eleven paces high that is mostly great-grandmothers, and on the eleventh day of the cycle the families come and put their palms flat on the warm gold and stand there in the roar, saying nothing, because nothing can be said there, and because the stone is speaking at a frequency they can feel in their teeth.

The water rises and falls over eleven years, a hand's breadth each way, and the whole apparatus of the town — the pump-crews, the reaming rosters, the mould-mazes, the creeper booms, the retuning arguments — turns with it. Nothing here is fast. A doorway closes in a season. A trade shifts in a lifetime. The Long Bell strokes once every eleven seconds and has done so, by the ledger's best reckoning, four hundred million times.

Stand still in a hush, on the fifth tier, with the fog moving orange around you and the fern-halos glowing and the fluted gold wall a hand's breadth from your shoulder, and you can just perceive it: the wall is coming. A finger a year. It is not hostile. It is not even indifferent, quite; it is *patient*, in the way a lung is patient, and the people with their chisels are not fighting it, they are editing it, and the two of them together have been making this one small enormous room for a very long time, and are not finished, and have no intention of being finished, and would not know what to do with an edge.
