## The Kettle

It is one room. Two hundred and forty paces along the long axis, a hundred and thirty across, and a roof you cannot see that arches forty metres over the crown. You can walk the whole perimeter in half an hour and be back at the tea stall with your boots ruined. Everything that exists here exists inside that boundary: the town, the works, the water, the animals, the weather. There are drifts off the west wall but they are workings, not places — mouths going nowhere yet, drilled and charged and waiting their turn.

Three hundred metres above the roof is a plain of alfalfa under a white sky, cut into circles by pivot irrigators, and a wadi with tamarisk in it, and a fig. Nobody down here thinks about the plain much. The plain is where the money goes.

The rock is thirty-six degrees. It has been thirty-six degrees for longer than there have been eyes to notice. The air on the floor of the Kettle runs thirty-nine to forty-one, and under the roost it is forty-four, and the humidity stands somewhere between eighty and ninety-five percent, so that sweat does not evaporate and the body has no way to spend its heat. There is no cool side of the room. There is only the intake course along the west wall, where the air comes in from the downcast shaft at thirty-two, and where the houses are, and where a man can sit down without his heart beginning to argue. Three degrees. That is the entire gradient of this world. Men work twenty minutes at the face and then go lie in the trough at the bathhouse, which is thirty-four degrees and feels like snow.

## Air is the only commodity

The Lung is a fan eight metres across, twelve blades, ninety revolutions a minute, housed in a concrete throat at the north end where the Fissure comes down. It turns slowly enough that you can count the blades going by in the lamplight. It makes a note below hearing that you feel in the sinuses and the sternum, and under that a wash of moving air that never gusts, never varies, never stops.

If it stops, the Kettle becomes a killing jar in about four hours. Heat, ammonia, carbon dioxide pooling in the low corners, four hundred thousand mammals breathing. Everyone knows the arithmetic. Children learn it before they learn to read. There is a siren and there is a drill and there is a real event every eleven years or so, and the town's word for *weather* is the same as its word for *the fan*, because there is no other kind.

Everything else is loud on top of that: the jaw crusher working the west face at a hundred and five decibels, the dryer drum, the bagging plant's shaker, the skips on their rails, the pumps hammering in the sump. But the baseline, the sound you stop hearing and then hear again on Sundays, is the ceiling. It sounds like frying. It sounds like rain on a paper roof, like a river going over gravel, like applause a long way off. Ninety decibels of bats. People talk in hand signs on Drip Street and shout in the bar and are deaf by fifty, and the local pride is that a Kettle man can sleep through anything except quiet.

## The ceiling owns the roof

Four hundred thousand free-tailed bats, more or less, hanging in a fur two animals deep across the crown of the chamber, in the warm wet air they make themselves. They are the payroll. Their guano is bagged and sent up and spread on the alfalfa that grows the moths they eat, and the loop is so tight that the Concern employs a man whose whole job is talking to farmers about spray.

At dusk they leave through the Flue — a five-metre-square ventilation raise, three hundred metres straight up, with a ladderway on one side and a grating at the foot to catch the falling seed husks and thistledown. The exodus takes two hours and twenty minutes. It is not a burst. It is a slow drain, a column of bodies spiralling up the shaft at a walking pace, and the wind of it comes down cold on your neck, and the smell is ammonia and hot fur and something sweetish underneath. It rains guano the entire time. Nothing in this town is stored uncovered. Nothing.

Under the roost the ecology runs in bands, like a shore. Fresh guano steams at sixty degrees in the heap and crawls with cockroaches and the larvae of guano flies, and the mites come, and then the dermestid beetles, and on the older drier margins the cave crickets in a moving lace, and above them the hunting spiders, and above them the geckos at every lamp, and above them the rats, and above them the cats that somebody brought down in 1961 to solve the rats and are now their own problem with their own line item in the budget. There is a rat snake that lives in the cable tray on Terrace Two and has been there, or its ancestors have been there, longer than the school. Mosquitoes stand over the sump in a column you can put your arm into. Bat flies run on any warm body within a minute of your standing still. You cannot eat uncovered. You cannot sit on the floor. You strip at the bathhouse before you go home, and the cough that some men get is a fungus that lives in the heaps, and the Company's poster about respirators has been on the same wall, curling, for thirty years.

The bats are protected by ordinance. No lamp above four metres. No unhooded light. No shooting, no netting, no sealing the Flue, no work on the crown between the fifteenth of March and the end of September. This is why the town is dim: not poverty, law. Forty-odd sodium bulbs, hooded, aimed down, brown strings of them along the terraces, each doorway a yellow rectangle, the floor a scatter of amber pools with black between. The roof has never been seen whole. Once a decade a maintenance flood goes up for an hour and the whole ceiling turns out to be moving, and people come out and stand in the street and don't say anything, and then it goes off and the roof is a sound again.

## Water comes from above, never from below

The water table sits in a fissure system just over the roof, and the roof is a colander: twelve thousand rock bolts, every one of them drilled through a wet seam, every one weeping a thread. Forty litres a minute, arriving as drip.

So the town has weather indoors. Every house has a pitched tin roof inside the cavern, and gutters, and a downpipe to a channel cut in the sill, and the channels run to the street drain, and the drain runs to the sump. There are tarpaulins over the switchgear and drip pans under the transformers and a man whose job is walking the roof-line with a bucket and a torch. The sound of drip on tin is what everyone here falls asleep to.

And the walls sweat, because the terraces sit in the cool intake and the hot wet air off the roost slides over them and gives up its water. The town is the coldest thing in the room, so the town is the thing that condenses. Stone runs. Paint blisters. Copper goes green and then goes to lace; aluminium pits; galvanising blooms white and flakes. Ammonia eats copper faster than water eats iron, so the wiring is tinned and boxed and renewed on an eleven-year cycle whether it needs it or not.

Against all that, the stone itself does nothing. The houses are cut from blocks quarried out of the same room they stand in — every building is a piece of the hole beside it — and after ninety years the arrises are still sharp, the drill marks still crisp, no lichen, no patina, no weathering rind, nothing rounded. There is no soil anywhere. There is no talus slope; there are angular blocks the size of cars lying where they fell last year, and fresh spoil with edges that cut boots. The machines dissolve and the buildings look newly made. In the worked-out corner at the east end there is a graveyard of rusted frames standing in warm mud, and behind them a wall so freshly cut it still smells of powder.

The hot seep comes up the fault in that same corner at fifty-two degrees and is piped to the bathhouse. The sump is thirty centimetres deep across the low south floor, warm as blood, and the pumps lift it three hundred metres and sell it to the pivots.

## Nine roots, forty lamps of algae, one lemon tree

The Fissure at the north end is the one thing here that was not made by machines: a natural crack the quarry drove into in the first decade, the bats' old door before the Flue. Down it come the roots of the fig in the wadi. Nine of them, wrist-thick, pale as candle wax, hanging thirty metres and drinking the condensate off the rock. Two reach the floor and have rooted in the guano, which means there is a fig three hundred metres up that is partly eating bats. The roots put out white etiolated shoots with leaves the size of thumbnails and no green in them at all.

Under the lamps, algae: a green skin on the wet stone in a halo around every bulb, forty halos, the only chlorophyll in the room that isn't imported. In the dead drift off Terrace Three there are mushroom beds on spent guano, which is not vegetation but is dinner. At the school there are chives and mint in crates. And there is a lemon tree in a tarred tub on wheels, which has never fruited, and which is pushed out to the foot of the Flue at midsummer, because for about twenty minutes around noon in the last week of June a disc of daylight the width of a dinner plate comes all the way down the shaft and lands on a particular paving stone.

The town keeps its calendar by that coin of light. There is a festival. Children stand in it and are told to remember what it felt like.

## Everything here is slow

The chamber grows a metre a year into the west wall. The midden under the roost rises a hand's breadth in a good year, and the unworked corner has come up a metre and a half since the town was laid out, so the old doorsteps are re-cut every generation and the oldest street is two steps down from its own doorways, and there is a paved floor under the pavement that nobody has seen since 1911. The heat climbs three-tenths of a degree a decade as the workings deepen. The colony gains about one percent a year, except for the bad decade when the sprays came and it dropped a fifth and the town nearly shut, and after which the Treaty of the Fields was signed and the farmers changed what they used.

And the drip is lessening. The old men say the roof used to rain. The pivots above are pulling the aquifer down eighty centimetres a year, and the fissure system is draining, and root number nine died three years ago and hangs there dry, and nobody has said out loud what it means that the fig in the wadi is scheduled to come out when the field is re-graded.

On Terrace Two, roped off, under its own small hooded lamp, there is a soda straw of calcite nine centimetres long, grown in forty-one years from the drip at a single bolt hole. The school brings children to see it. It is the only thing in the Kettle that water has made. Everything else was cut.

That is what this place is: not a cave, but a hole that intends to become one. The rock is embryonic, unweathered, unborn; the landforms have not happened yet; the drainage is a set of gutters and a pump. If the Lung stopped and the pumps stopped and the last hoist went up, the water would keep coming for a while, and then it would come differently, and it would begin the patient work of rounding the spoil and skinning the walls in flowstone and hanging straws from twelve thousand bolt holes, and in a hundred thousand years there would be a real cave here, wet and warm and full of bats, with a town-shaped absence in the floor of it.

Meanwhile, Sundays: the mill down for maintenance, the colony out over the alfalfa, the Lung idling at half speed. Ninety decibels drop away and the room goes so quiet that people come out of their houses to stand in the street. And what you hear then, all across the dark, from every bolt and every seam and every tin roof and every gutter, is water arriving — tick, tick, tick, ten thousand small clocks — the sound of a cave being made, one drop at a time, around a town that is listening to it the way you listen to a clock in an empty house.
