The plateau is nine thousand four hundred meters above a sea no one here has seen, and it is new. Not new the way a lava field is new — new the way a broken bottle is new. The uplift is still going on at the rate of a fingernail's growth each year, and the rock has not had time to become anything but sharp. It is black glass with a grain to it, ignimbrite welded and re-welded, and every stone on it still carries the conchoidal ripple of the fracture that made it. There are no rounded pebbles anywhere on this plateau. There is no sand. Walking on the scree sounds like walking on a shelf of dropped crockery, and it will open your hand if you catch yourself falling.

The air is a third of what it should be and thirty below zero at noon. The ground, in places, is ninety above. This contradiction is the whole weather of the place: snow arrives, touches, and is gone — not melted so much as *taken*, a small violent hiss and a smell like a struck match, and then a dry hollow under the glass shelf where the snow used to be. The plateau sweats and freezes in the same square meter. Nothing grows on it. Nothing has ever grown on it. There is no lichen on the north faces, no spore print, no seed that blew in and failed. The stone is unmarked by anything but heat and its own breaking.

From a kilometer away, the only sign of a city is a stain of vapor lying downwind, and a low collar of white material, and three masts. From ten kilometers, there is nothing at all. The city is not on the plateau. It is a hole in it.

---

The bore is three hundred and eleven meters across and four thousand one hundred deep, and it is circular the way a machined thing is circular. Its lip is glazed. There is no spoil heap. No tailings, no dump, no ramp, no road; the mass that used to occupy this volume was not carried anywhere. It was vaporized and put back on the walls, and the walls are what the city is made of. The city is what the hole weighed.

At the collar there is a ring of terraces, kiln-houses, the drum sheds, the long low sheds where the intake air is warmed before it is allowed to fall. Snow builds a white cornice on the windward lip and is eaten back to a scalloped edge each afternoon. Beyond the collar, the surface installation is almost nothing: a few masts, a rail loop, a field of black glass, and the plume.

Inside, the temperature is fifty-eight degrees and the relative humidity is high enough that your first breath is a mouthful of warm broth. It is a sauna four kilometers tall. The heat comes from below and is *kept* — held in by the wall material, which is a vitrified foam, grey-white, warm to the palm, and ninety-nine point six percent void. It weighs less than the water it holds. It is translucent at a thickness of two centimeters: put a lamp behind a wall and the wall becomes a slow fog with a bright core. Knock it and it gives a note like a wet finger on a rim, and then the note goes on longer than it should.

It is dim in here. Not dark — dim, the dimness of a room with the shutters closed in summer. The light comes from three places, and none of them is generous. Far below, the sump glows a dull cherry that reads more as warmth on the face than as color in the eye. Far above, the mouth is a coin of sky, blue-white, small as a thumbnail at arm's length, with weather crossing it now and then like a slide in a projector. And along the walls, spaced forty and sixty meters apart, the amber lamps: sparse, hooded, aimed down, each one illuminating a small wet floor and leaving the next one to be inferred. Between lamps there is a great deal of nothing. You can stand on a rim walkway at the two-hundred level and see three kilometers down the shaft, and the far wall across the way is a rumor, a pale vertical smudge with a few lights in it that might be windows or might be reflections of your own.

The city is mostly gap. This is not neglect. A floor at the sixtieth level is a walkway two meters wide, eleven rooms, a stair head, a cistern blister, and three hundred meters of open air. The rooms are exquisite and there are almost none of them. Whole vertical kilometers contain a cable, a landing, a door.

---

The masonry here is done with light.

The foam grows out of the vapor by deposition, and deposition follows photons; where you shine, the wall thickens. A mason works with a hooded lantern on a boom, standing off at a distance, moving the beam the way a man with a hose waters a bed. Two hours of patient illumination puts a centimeter of white stone onto the air. Load-bearing members are grown along a bright thread and then *held* — and the holding is the part that has no good explanation in any of the surviving manuals. A beam of grown foam spans ninety meters with no visible support and carries a tram. If you put your ear near it you hear a tone at the very bottom of hearing, and the tone is the support. Slack in the tone is the only warning that ever comes. There are no bolts in this city, no welds, no rivets. There is nothing to tighten.

Because the members are held by tone, the city is quiet by ordinance.

It is quiet anyway — the volume eats sound, a shout at the rim goes out and thins and does not come back — but the quiet is also law. Nothing is struck. Wheels are pressed foam and turn on air. Doors close against dampers and take four seconds to shut. Boots are felt. There are no bells, no horns, no gongs, no whistles; the alarm system is a change in lamp color and a change in the draft, and the town crier is the direction of the air on your cheek. Children — if there were children — would be taught to set things down. A dropped hammer at the four-hundred level once detuned a span and cost eleven rooms and the whole eastern stair. The report survives. It is four pages long and the last page is only the tolerance.

Mostly what you hear: the hiss of vapor going up, which is so constant it becomes the definition of silence. The tick and ping of glass taking heat. Water. And, four times a year, the fundamental — the entire column sounding its own length, a note below hearing that you feel in the sternum and in the water of the eye, lasting nine minutes, during which all work stops and everyone stands still and the foam walls sing faintly in sympathy along their whole four kilometers.

---

Water here does not run. It commutes.

Hot wet air rises from the sump. At about six hundred meters down, it meets wall that the outside cold has reached, and it gives up its water in a band that girdles the whole shaft: the Dew Line. There the foam is dark and slick and beaded, and the beads gather into threads, and the threads run down the wall in braided capillary channels that were grown for the purpose, and the channels feed blisters — cisterns like translucent goiters, warm, faintly cloudy, some the size of a room. Below the Dew Line the air dries out again as it descends along the return path, and the water that escapes the drains falls, and falls, and evaporates in mid-air somewhere around the fifteen-hundred level, and goes back up. It has been going up and down for a long time. There is no river. There is no outflow. There is a single hydrology, vertical, closed, and every drop in it has made the trip more times than there are grains in the scree above.

Rain is rare and it is an event. When a storm sits on the mouth and dumps snow into the throat, the column loses its balance, the Dew Line collapses downward, and for eleven or fourteen minutes it rains inside the city — real rain, falling free through the open middle, drumming on the walkways, drumming on the hooded lamps, a sound so foreign that the maintenance grammar has a separate word for it. Then the gradient re-establishes, the band climbs back to six hundred, and everything is damp and quiet again and the drains steam.

On the wet walls there is tarnish. Films a few microns thick, iridescent, oil-on-water colors, chemotrophic, eating the glass at a rate of a micron a year. It is arguably alive. Nobody here has ever argued about it. Where the Dew Line lingers longest, the films precipitate dendrites — fern shapes, frost shapes, branching fronds of mineral thirty centimeters tall, the only tree-shaped things within a hundred kilometers, and they are not trees, and they dissolve when the band moves. They come and go on a schedule of weeks. A wall will be a fernery and then a blank wet wall, and the pattern of where the ferns are is the pattern of where the water has been.

And there are the sweepers: palm-sized gliders of the same white foam, unpowered, riding the thermal, launched from the drum sheds on a cycle older than the current wall surfaces. They scrape the tarnish, they carry the scrapings to the return draft, they re-glaze the channels. They flock where the films bloom and the films bloom where the Dew Line sits, so the whole shaft has something that looks exactly like an ecology — grazer and pasture, migration and season — with nothing in it that has a mouth. Nothing here is an animal. Nothing here has ever been an animal. The occupancy register reports forty thousand and the lamp census reports no bodies, and both figures are filed as correct, and this is not considered a mystery so much as a units problem.

There is evidence of use everywhere. Benches warm on the seat. A door ajar with the draft pulling it. Handprints appearing in the condensation on a rail, five fingers and a thumb, fading over ninety seconds. A tram arriving at the nine-hundred level on time, opening, waiting eleven seconds, closing, going on. Tools laid down mid-task in an order that implies the task will resume.

---

The city is young and stays young. Nothing on any surface is older than about eleven years. Deposition outruns weathering; the deposition front migrates slowly down the shaft as the sump's output shifts, and behind it the walls are perpetually just-made — arrises still sharp, glaze still un-crazed, every fracture surface fresh. There is no patina in this city. There is no dust in the corners because the corners are eleven months old. When a member reaches its tolerance it is not repaired; it is released, and it falls, and it goes down the four kilometers into the cherry heat and is taken, and comes back up as vapor, and is put on a wall somewhere as new stone. The city eats its own dropped pieces. On a still night you can watch the fall: a slow white flake turning in the lamplight, then another, glass snow going down while the vapor goes up, the two traffics crossing without touching.

And in the Long Middle, between roughly the eight-hundred and the seventeen-hundred levels, there is a cylinder ninety meters across where none of it goes.

You notice it first as a wrongness in the dust. The motes here carry charge and drift in slow shoals along the field lines, and the shoals bend. They bend around a shape. Droplets from a broken channel arc and go around. Glass snow, falling, curves out and resumes its fall on the other side, unhurried, as though the space had a surface. Sound does not cross it: a voice from the far rim arrives thin and wrong, delayed, having gone the long way. The instruments say the air inside is ordinary air, same pressure, same temperature, same humidity. There is simply nothing else in it, and nothing else goes in, and this condition has been maintained continuously to a tolerance of four-tenths of a millimeter for longer than any wall has existed.

Everything else is apparatus for that. The kilns, the masts, the intake sheds, the felt boots, the hooded lamps, the four-second doors, the whole warm wet silent municipality — scaffolding around a curated absence. The surviving documents give the tolerance, the maintenance intervals, the launch cycle for the sweepers, the ordinance against noise, the ordinance against aiming anything at it. You may look. You may not point an instrument. You may not name it; naming is filed under measurement. What it is *for* is not in the documents, or is in a part of the documents that was vaporized to make the walls.

At the geometric center of the cylinder, at the twelve-forty level, there is one bead of water.

It is four millimeters across. It does not fall. It has never fallen. It is the datum, the zero, the reference from which every wall in the city is grown and every tolerance is reckoned, and the entire hot damp breath of this hole in the roof of the world exists, in the end, to keep one drop of water from going down.

---

Above, the wind comes across the black glass and finds nothing to move. The scree clinks and settles. Snow touches the hot ground and is taken. The plume lies downwind for two kilometers and thins into a sky the color of a bruise at noon, and if you walked out onto the plateau from any direction you would find no road, no field, no ruin, no bone, no green — only sharp new stone, and steam, and a perfectly round hole with a white lip, breathing out, breathing in, twice a day, keeping very still.
