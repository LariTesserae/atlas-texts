## The Standing Water

From the rim it looks like a forest with a lid on it.

The basin lies three and a half thousand meters up, in a crack that opened recently enough that the rock around it still has edges. Not slopes — edges. Shattered black faces, splintered talus lying at the exact angle it fell, no soil anywhere, no rounding, no sand. Rain has not yet had time to teach this mountain what a curve is. Everything the water touches, it touches for the first time.

And the basin is full. Warm, clear, still, brimming to within a hand's width of the low saddle at the eastern end. Below the surface the forest goes on down: crowns, then more crowns, then the pale colonnade of trunks, then a floor of white rubble and green felt, forty meters down and perfectly visible, every leaf distinguishable, the sunlight lying on it in a moving net.

It is not a lake with trees in it. It is a forest that has been overtaken, and did not object.

---

The water is blood-warm — thirty-three, thirty-four degrees, heated from beneath by the same young rock that split the mountain. There are places on the floor where the warmth has shape: shimmering columns rising from裂缝, from vents no wider than a wrist, and where they rise the leaves lean in. After ten minutes in it you stop being able to find your own skin. The boundary goes. You are a cooler patch in a warm medium, slowly ceasing to be even that.

Above the surface the air is thin and bright and stingy. It takes work to breathe up there. Down here, nothing takes work at all.

---

The first strangeness is the seams.

The water does not mix. It stacks. Warm mineral-heavy water from the vents lies in flat lenses under lighter water, and the boundaries between them hold — horizontal, glassy, kilometers wide, drifting a little at the edges like the margins of a cloud. You can see them from above as faint shelves in the blue. Swim down through one and the world briefly doubles: your own arms appear twice, the trunk beside you forks into two trunks that rejoin above, the sun becomes a coin and then a smear and then a coin again.

Each layer has its own forest. In the top lens the leaves are broad and thin and stacked eight deep, so close that swimming between them is a matter of parting rather than passing. In the second, the leaves narrow and go copper-red, and everything is furred with a fine white filament that is not fungus and not algae but something patiently in between. In the third, near the floor, the plants have given up on light as a primary business and live on the rock instead, sending mats of root-hair into fresh fracture faces, dissolving them grain by grain, mining phosphorus out of glass.

Nothing crosses the seams that does not choose to. Pollen drifts along them for years. A falling seed reaches a boundary and stops, and sits there, and germinates sideways.

---

The second strangeness is that the forest is trying to float.

These trees keep air in their heartwood — great slow chambers of it, made over decades, and they do not stop making it when the water arrives. So every drowned trunk strains upward. The roots hold. You can see the tension in them where they cross a crack: taut, straightened, humming like rigging, the whole crown leaning a degree or two toward the sun and staying there, year after year, in a posture of arrested ascent.

They vent, sometimes. A single silver thread leaves a lenticel in the bark and climbs, wobbling, and breaks against a leaf underside and spreads into a dome. There are ceilings of trapped gas all through the upper canopy, mirror-bright, a hand's breadth to a body's length across, held under the leaves like coins under a table. They fill over days. They empty over weeks. Put your face into a large one and you can breathe — a stale, green, oxygen-drunk breath, three or four of them, before the dome shreds and goes up.

The trees are not drowning. They are waiting, with an air supply, in a medium at exactly their own temperature, in full sun.

---

The dead do not rot. They lime.

The overflow leaves the eastern saddle in a sheet a few millimeters deep and starts down the mountain, and as it goes it drops its dissolved lime, and it has been doing this long enough to build a staircase — thousands of pools, rim to rim, each one a lens of warm clear water held behind a lip of fresh white calcite, stepping down the flank of the mountain for two kilometers before the light takes them. The rims are sharp. You could cut yourself on the youngest. They grow a finger's width in a decade and they never round off, because the water always finds the low point and builds it up, so the edge stays an edge forever.

The same thing happens to a tree that dies standing in the basin. It stops venting; it goes on being a surface. Calcite creeps up the bark, then the branches, then the last twig, and in eighty years there is a white pillar where a tree was, and in three hundred the pillar has thickened into a column, and in a thousand the columns have merged into a wall, and the wall has become the rim of a terrace, and the terrace holds a pool, and in the pool a new forest stands.

That is the only architecture here. It is all ancestry. Nothing was made; everything was left.

---

It is very quiet, and the quiet is not empty — it is *conductive*. Warm water carries sound five times better than the thin air above, and there is almost nothing to carry. So the small events arrive from enormous distances, perfectly intact.

The tick of a bubble leaving bark, half a basin away.

The grazing of a snail — there are perhaps a few hundred snails in all this water, pale, thumbnail-sized, rasping the lime off the columns and leaving comma-shaped scars. In an hour of listening you will hear one.

Once a season, somewhere down the staircase, a rim gives way: a single dry crack, then a long soft hiss of water finding a new route, then nothing for months.

And under all of it, at the very bottom of hearing, a note the rock makes as it cools. It is felt in the sternum before it is heard. It lasts a minute or two. It comes perhaps twice a year.

Above the surface there is wind in the surviving canopy at the margin, a dry high rattling, and the almost-total absence of birds. Something crosses over twice a day, high up, going somewhere else. The water striders on the film are the only residents of the surface itself, and they are so few that their dimples look like accidents.

---

Here is the rule the whole place obeys: **nothing here moves except by growing or by floating.**

There is no current. There is no wave — the fetch is long but the air is thin and the wind has no grip, and the surface at noon is a hard bright plane with the sun in it and, some mornings, the cloud deck lying *below* the rim, so that the basin sits in sunlight above a white floor that is not the ground. When cloud comes over the saddle it lies on the water and thins and is gone, without ever having touched anything.

So the plants do the moving. They move at the speed of tissue. A root curtain advances a meter in a year. A calcifying meadow closes a gap in a decade. And at noon, when the sun drives the lime out of solution, the water fills with a slow snow — microcrystals blooming out of nothing, hanging, catching the light, drifting *upward* on the warm columns in the middle of the basin and settling downward at the cold margins, so that the whole volume turns over in a glitter so slow you have to look away and look back to be sure.

The water is dense the way a thicket is dense. Not crowded with animals — there are almost no animals — but crowded with *presence*: leaf on leaf on filament on crystal on root, four or five living surfaces in any hand's-breadth you close your fist on, all of them lit, all of them working, none of them in a hurry.

---

And the level rises.

A finger's width in a year, sometimes; nothing at all for thirty years; then a decade where it takes a whole hand. The mountain is young and its plumbing is unfinished, and the basin leaks downward through fresh fractures as fast as the vents fill it, and the balance between those two is the only clock here.

You can read that clock on the trunks. Each one carries bands: a belt of aerial bark, then a belt of something else — swollen, pale, breathing through its skin — then the true aquatic tissue above. The forest is climbing the mountain at the rate the water climbs, dying at the bottom into white stone, seeding at the top into crack and rubble. The shoreline is a wave of conversion moving uphill at the pace of a walking man's shadow across a morning.

Sometimes the leak wins. The rims record it: dark bands, dry centuries, a floor of white pillars standing in thin air with the wind going through them like a hand through hair. Then the vents close somewhere below and the water comes back, and the pillars go under, and the seed takes hold on their tops, and the forest stands again on the shoulders of what it used to be.

Both futures are already visible from the rim. To the east, the drowned summit-to-be, sharp and black and bare, waiting its turn. To the west, the staircase, ten thousand bright lenses stepping down into cloud, each one a small warm still world with a green floor and no fish in it, all of them descending, all of them building, all of them taking their time.

Stay in it long enough and you stop being able to say whether you are warm or whether the water is. That is not a failure of perception. That is the correct reading. The place has been at this temperature, at this light, at this silence, for longer than anything in it has been alive, and it will go on being so, and the only thing it is doing is very slowly turning a forest into a mountain and a mountain into a forest, one finger's width at a time.
