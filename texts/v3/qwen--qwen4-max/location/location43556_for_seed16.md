## The Bell

From the plain there is nothing to find.

The flat runs out in every direction with the grain of a shut door: a buckled pavement of salt squares, each one tilted a little against its neighbors, the whole country tiled by its own crystallization and then heaved by heat until the tiles rode up on edge. Nothing has ever crossed it. There are no tracks anywhere on that plain and there never have been; the only marks are the wind's, long yellow streaks of drifted flour that shift and re-form and mean nothing. At midday the shimmer stands up off the pavement like a crowd of people who dissolve when you look at them.

But if you lie down at the base of the low scarp — and it is low, a step you could take without lifting your knee much — you will hear, coming out of a slot in the rock about the width of a doorway, the sound of a city two streets away.

That is the whole of the entrance. A slot, a lintel of anhydrite polished by a hundred thousand years of breathing, a fan of dead spore-flour drifted below it, and a fringe of scorched, failed growth where things have tried to live outside and could not. The exhale comes out at you smelling of almonds and hot iron and the yellow underneath sulfur. Twice a day it stops, hesitates, and reverses, and a thin cold thread goes in past your face.

The throat slants down sixty paces. Then it opens, and there is no more outside.

---

The cavity is a bell of air four hundred paces across and ninety high at the crown, and nine-tenths of what is here is the space inside it.

That is the first thing to get right. It is a city, unmistakably — terraces, districts, conduits, plazas, stairs worn into dishes, a lattice of streets — and it is almost entirely empty. The built fabric is a rind, three or four rooms thick, clinging to the curved wall and stepping down to the bowl of the floor. Between one lit quarter and the next there may be two hundred paces of nothing at all: hot dim air with dust turning in it. You can stand in a plaza and see a single lamp burning at the far end, small as a seed, and know that the space between you and it is not a street but a gulf, and that no one has ever needed to fill it.

The streets, where they exist, meet at right angles. The blocks are cubes. The plazas are octahedral, cut as if a crystal had been opened and the inside found to be habitable. This is not a metaphor the masons use; it is a doctrine. The salt has a habit, a way it wants to arrange itself, and the city is that habit at the scale of a body. *The stone dreams the plan,* they knock, *and we only trace it.* Whether or not that is true, the plan is very old and no one has ever proposed a different one.

---

It is hot. Not the flat blast of the plain — that would be survivable, that would be simple. This is the patient heat of a thing that has been warm since before there was anything to notice. The floor near the vents will cook a hand laid on it. The crown is worse; heat climbs and pools up there against the roof slab, and the upper terraces are the poor terraces, the crowded ones, the ones where growth is stunted and the sap runs thin and the citizens are short.

Wealth here is not the ability to make heat. Nobody makes heat; heat is the free and terrible gift of the floor, which seeps hydrogen and sour gas from a reaction going on in the rock a long way down, a reaction older than the cavity and quite indifferent to it. Wealth is the ability to *move* heat: to be near a vent and not cook. So the rich districts are forests of plumbing. Fins of tube-wood standing in ranks like organ pipes. Loops of sap-line running out to radiators grown against the cool night-side of the wall. Blocks of dense salt used as sinks, hauled out hot and hauled back cold, a whole trade of porters and sledges and cold-storage slots. Their entire technology is thermal, and it is all hand-work: anhydrite hammers, quartz-dust abrasive, wedges of dry tube-wood driven into a crack and then swollen with sap until the rock splits with a sound like a cough.

They cannot make fire. There is nothing in the air to burn with. They fire clay anyway, in the throats of the seeps, in racks lowered on bast cord into the hot gas and left there for a season, and the pots come up scorched amber and ringing, and are the most valuable objects in the world.

And the windows are not cast. Halite cleaves clear. A window here is a piece of a mountain that happens to be transparent, split out along its own planes with a tap and a wedge, and set into a frame grown around it. In a wet world such windows would weep and slump within a year. Here they last a hundred thousand years, and the oldest are the clearest, polished by dust and handling until looking through one is like looking through nothing at all.

---

Nothing here has ever been wet in the way you mean.

There is a fluid. It is not water; there is no water anywhere in this place, no vapor of it, no memory of it, no mineral here that ever precipitated out of it. The fluid is a clear heavy oil that smells of almonds, slow as syrup, that does not bead but crawls, that does not evaporate cleanly but leaves a varnish. It is made in the mats at the vent-mouths out of hydrogen and sour gas and a great deal of patience. It runs in sealed lines. It never pools. Spill a cup of it on the floor and it flashes off in the heat and leaves a black ring, and the ring stays.

The floor of the Bell is a map of those rings. Ten thousand years of accidents, overlapping, some no bigger than a coin, some the size of a plaza where a main line let go in a bad season. The masons read them the way you would read a chronicle. They can tell you which district was rich when, which conduit was rerouted, which summer the pressure spiked and everybody lost their footing.

And everything old is lacquered. The fog — a blue haze of oil droplets that hangs in the crown during the wet hour and settles instead of falling — varnishes every surface it touches, slowly, forever. New growth is chalk-white. Mature work is amber. The ancient quarters are black glass, glossy, wet-looking and bone dry, and in the dimness the light slides along them in long thin strokes so that the oldest parts of the city are legible only as reflections.

---

The light is dim, and it is rationed, and this is the inverse of every city you know.

Light costs sap. The lamp-cups are living things, symbionts housed in niches, dumping the excess of their chemistry as photons because at this temperature it is cheaper to glow than to get hotter. A cup gives about as much light as a full moon on a wall. A private room with three cups is an extravagance bordering on scandal. The great public plazas are dark, and the small rooms are lit, and a person of consequence is a person you can see.

What they do instead is paint their edges. Every stair nosing, every door jamb, every rail and threshold and lip of every conduit is streaked with a luminous film, tended, re-fed, scraped and renewed. So the Bell at rest is a drawing of itself: a vast black volume with a hundred thousand faint green lines hanging in it, outlining terraces, describing arches, tracing the long diagonals of the ramps, all of it doubled in the varnish. The middles of things are invisible. The city is all outline. It looks like a diagram of a city, waiting to be filled in.

Once a day a coin of true daylight comes down the throat and lands on the floor, and it is the brightest thing that has ever happened here, and it is a wound. It cooks. The salt focuses it. There is an arc of graduated pillars grown across the bowl to catch it and time it, and there are shutters of cleaved halite that scatter it into harmless haze, and there is a whole minor priesthood whose work is to make sure it never touches anything alive. They keep it as a calendar. They fear it as a fire.

Twice a day, at the very bottom of the cycle, the deepest cracks go dull red, and for an hour the floor is a low sky of embers, and everyone who can afford to stops working and looks at it.

---

It is loud. Understand that it is loud the way a millrace is loud, not the way a market is loud; it is loud without anybody doing anything.

The floor is a rank of seeps, and each seep whistles. Thousands of them, each with its own bore and its own pitch, and the pitch of the whole choir slides down at the exhale and climbs at the inhale, so the city is retuned twice a day by weather it does not control. Some vents are deliberately plugged with graded salt plugs — not to stop the gas but to change the chord. There are masons whose entire craft is the plugging of holes to make the floor sound better.

The crown drips. At the turn of the breath, cold air comes down the throat and chills the roof slab, and the sap-fog condenses on ten thousand stalactites of precipitated salt and lets go. Every drop falls into a cup grown to catch it, and every cup is tuned, and the tuning is maintained by adding a lamina at a time, a season's work for a finger's change in note. The wet hour sounds like a hundred thousand small bells struck by a slow rain, and it is the loudest thing in this world, and it is in tune.

And under all of it, at the pressure turn, the Bell booms: a note below hearing that goes through the trunk of you and comes back off the far wall eleven seconds later. Eleven seconds is a unit of time here. It is also the length of a sentence.

Because the air is too loud to speak in, they talk through the stone. A wright drums with its root-plate, a hard flat pad of mineralized tissue laid against the floor, and the knocks travel — fast, clean, through the lattice, through the cubes and the octahedra, up the terraces and along the mains — and the whole city carries conversation the way a wire carries current. Grammar lives in the intervals. A message crosses the Bell in the time it takes the boom to die. There are switchboard plazas where forty conversations pass through one slab and none of them touch.

---

The citizens are plants, and there is nothing else alive here that moves.

No animals. Not one, not ever. Nothing with an eye, nothing with a bone, nothing that has ever hurried. This absence is not a gap in the ecology; it is the shape of the ecology. There is no predation, so there is no fear, so there are no locks. Every door in the Bell is a valve, and every valve exists to keep weather out, not to keep anything alive out. Their word for *enemy* is a word for *shade*.

A wright is a standing column of braided tube-wood, three to nine spans, rooted in a tapped vent, crowned with a ruff of black blades that drink the dimness and the sour gas. Below the ruff hangs the hand-fan: ten thousand tendrils, each tipped with a hook of hardened sap or a pad of abrasive, working always, slowly, in a movement you would have to watch for an hour to be sure of. They knap salt blades that stay sharp forever because nothing here can dissolve them. They twist bast. They lay lamina. A stair takes forty years and is not considered a large project.

To go somewhere is a generational act. A wright that wishes to be elsewhere grows a runner along the wall and buds a new trunk at the far end and, over decades, transfers itself, and the old trunk is left standing, empty, furnished, and eventually let to a stranger or eaten by the unknotting filaments that dissolve dead structure back into sap and gas. The city has no corpses and no ruins that stay ruined. A dead neighbor becomes stairs.

They fight, of course. Not with teeth — with centimeters per decade. Shading. Root-girdling. A slow chemical siege, sour exudates laid down along a boundary until the other party's blades go pale. A district can be starved by having its vent quietly plugged, and the crime is discovered forty years later as a smell. And treaties are not written; they are grafted. Two citizens in accord grow a shared conduit, an anastomosis, a piece of infrastructure that is also the agreement, and to break the agreement you must cut a pipe that is feeding you.

Down on the floor the dust behaves like a liquid, because with no water there is nothing to make it cohere: it ripples, it slumps, it dunes across the lower steps at every breath. The plazas are cleared by living sweepers, carpets of cilia grown across the pavement that pass the dust hand to hand to hand toward the collection slots, all day, forever, a slow green tide pushing a yellow sea. This is the most visible motion in the city and it takes a season to cross a square.

---

Two things are always happening, and both are old.

The roof is falling. It has been falling for a million years, flake by flake, and the arch it has made is simply the shape of falling made permanent — a pressure arch, self-supporting, in balance, shedding exactly as much as the grown buttresses replace. When a slab lets go it takes a long time to cross the gulf and hits with a boom that everyone stops for, and then a crew is budded out to grow the scar shut, and the scar is white for a century and then amber and then black.

And the walls are closing. Salt creeps. It flows like a glacier that has all the time there is, and it has been squeezing the outer quarters shut since before the lattice was traced. There are districts in there folded like cloth. Stairs bent into ribbons. Rooms narrowed to the width of a hand, still maintained, still lit, a green thread of lamp-film running down a slot in the rock, and inside it a wright who has been getting thinner for eight thousand years and who still drums, perfectly clearly, on the hour, about the weather.

Every so often a quarter blooms: converts itself to spore-tissue, goes pale, roars its stored sap up as fog, and dies back. The exhale takes it out through the Mouth and lays it on the plain as yellow snow, where almost all of it dies. The city seals that quarter and keeps it dark for a decade, and the neighbors harvest the tube-wood, and the mourning is indistinguishable from the carpentry.

Once in a very long while something comes back down the throat alive. It roots in a corner and grows wrong — wrong color, wrong smell, a stranger's quarter that the rest of the Bell finds faintly nauseating and does not remove, because twice in recorded history the strangers have survived a gas spike that killed everything else.

---

Go back up the throat. Come out onto the scarp in the flat white afternoon.

There is nothing here. A slot in a low step of rock, a drift of yellow flour, a shimmer, a smell of almonds. No towers, no smoke, no roads, no tracks; a country with no horizon that matters and no feature you would walk toward. The plain does not know the city is under it. The city does not have a word for the plain, except *the place where the spores go.*

Lie down with your ear over the hole.

It sounds like a crowd two streets away, talking all at once, in a room with a very high ceiling. It has been going for longer than there have been eyes anywhere. It is in tune.
