## The Making

The range is new. Not new in the way geologists mean, where a hundred million years is a young thing — new the way a loaf is new. The peaks have no scree. There is no rounded gravel in any streambed, no sand, no dust to speak of, because nothing here has been worn yet; the world has not got around to subtraction. The valleys are not carved. They are simply the gaps between what has been pushed up so far, and the streams that run in them run clear and cut nothing and lay down instead, thin terraces of fresh mineral, pale as fingernail, growing visibly from season to season. Walk anywhere and the edges are sharp. Not broken-sharp. *Unfinished*-sharp, the sharpness of a thing that has only just been made and has not yet been told what it is for.

At four thousand meters the air is thin and cold and very bright. Ordinary daylight. A sky doing nothing unusual. Black crust lichen on the north faces in patches the size of coins, a hundred coins scattered over a hillside, and between them nothing at all — bare grey stone, warm along the seams if you put your palm there, smelling of wet flint and of the moment just before lightning.

You find the place by the stain. A dark wet blot on a cliff, twenty meters up, with a slot at its center no wider than a doorway. From the valley it is invisible. From the ridge above it is invisible. It is one feature in a hundred kilometers of featureless new mountain, and it does not announce itself with sound, because the sound does not leave. Stand ten paces from the cleft and you hear wind and your own blood. Stand at the lip and your teeth begin to ache. Stand in it and the world turns on at full volume, all at once, like a door slammed inside your skull.

---

Inside is a room, and the room is too big.

That is the first wrong thing, and it stays the first wrong thing no matter how long you swim in it. The mountain does not have room for this. You can see the far wall — three hundred meters off, four hundred, the distance keeps renegotiating — and you can see it *clearly*, because there is nothing in the water. No silt. No plankton. No drifting mote of anything dead. It is empty in the way a new-built house is empty, empty of contents rather than emptied of them, and your eye travels through it with nothing to stop on for a hundred body-lengths. A rope hangs down from the cleft and stops. Far below and to the left, a copper pin glints in the wall. Far right, a stone bowl sits on a shelf. Between these three objects: an amount of water that would fill a lake, and nothing else whatsoever.

The second wrong thing is the light. It is a bright afternoon in here. Not glowing, not phosphorescent, not the blue dusk of a cave with a hole in it — a plain sunlit afternoon, the light you would get on an open hillside in early autumn, falling evenly from every direction and slightly more from above. Look up and the ceiling is stone: wet, close, ridged, unmistakably rock, three meters over your head. It is lit as though it were sky. Both facts are simply true and they do not trouble each other. Shadows are correct and soft. Your hand casts one. So does the rope.

The water is cold — twelve degrees, maybe less, the cold that takes your fingers first and makes them stupid — and it is salt. Full sea-salt, thirty-five parts per thousand, the taste of an ocean on your cracked lips at four thousand meters above every ocean there is. There is no salt in this mountain. There is no salt in the rain that falls on it. The salt arrives anyway.

---

It arrives with the water, out of the walls.

Put your face close to the stone and you can see it happening: the rock sweating, not in beads but in a continuous extrusion, a shimmer of new liquid coming out of a solid that has no pores, no fractures, no capacity. It comes out under pressure. It comes out *loud*. That is the roar — not falling water, not a river, nothing so ordinary as water going somewhere. It is the sound of water being made along four hundred meters of wall at once, a hiss multiplied into a solid, a white noise with a floor under it. The floor is below hearing. You feel it in the sternum and the sinuses and the fillings of your teeth, a pressure like standing behind a bell the instant it is struck, sustained, never resolving.

And under that, intermittently, the crack and tick of stone setting. The chamber is growing. This is the rule of the place, the one law everything else obeys: **here, water builds.** It does not erode. It carries nothing away. It lays down. Where the arriving water meets the older water it precipitates, slowly, a clear film that hardens into rock indistinguishable from the rock it came from, and so the walls are always advancing inward and outward at once, thickening, and the floor is rising, and the whole bubble is a thing being *added to* in real time, a geode inflating inside a mountain that is itself still being extruded.

The harvesters call the fresh deposit *green stone*. It is soft as cheese for the first hour. You can cut it with a hand-adze, a shell, a hard thumbnail. Bring it out into the air and it sets in a day and after that it will outlast anything you build with it. This is why anyone comes here at all.

---

The turbulence is not random. It only looks random for the first hour.

The water is practicing. That is the word the wet-cutters use, and it is the right word, and nobody who has watched it for a season argues. A standing wave forms across the middle of the chamber and holds for eleven seconds and dissolves. A vortex opens near the floor, spins up, tightens, and lets go. Sheets of current slide past each other with a visible seam, a line of refraction like the shadow of a window frame, and where they touch they braid and the braid comes apart. Every few minutes the whole volume heaves in one direction, all of it together, a tide with no shore to break on, and then heaves back.

There is no moon in here, so the water has made one. It is a knot of denser water, cooler, saltier, perhaps two meters across, visible only as a lens — a place where the far wall bends and doubles. It circles the chamber on a slow oblique path, and everything leans toward it as it passes: your body, the hanging rope, the fronds, the fine hair on your arm. The cutters call it the calf. They time their shifts by it. It has grown measurably in the four generations anyone has been counting, and the counting is scratched on the wall by the entrance, and the wall is reading the counts and keeping them, glazing them over, one decade's tallies now under a translucent skin of new stone like insects in amber.

The water holds shapes too long. That is the part nobody writes down. When you swim through it your wake persists — a tunnel of disturbed water hanging in place for four or five seconds after you have gone, your own outline, legible, and then a second outline, slightly wrong, that you did not make. It has seen hands. It tries hands. Not often. Not well. Once every few days, in the middle distance, a shape like a splayed hand two meters wide forms out of nothing, holds, and comes apart, and you tell yourself it was a braid of currents, and it was, and it was also a hand.

---

Life is early here. That is the only sensible way to read it: this sea is a first draft, and nothing has moved in yet.

Twelve strands of something pale grow on the walls where the arriving water is strongest, each anchored meters from its nearest neighbor, each a meter or two long, fringed, combing. They do not reach for the light. They reach for the roar — turn a frond sideways to the wall and it wilts within a day. They are being buried alive at the same rate the stone accretes, and they grow outward at exactly that rate, keeping their tips free, so that each strand stands at the end of a column of rock the shape of its own history. Cut one across and you have a year-ring record of a plant that has been outrunning its own grave since before your grandmother.

The animals are two. There is a flat translucent grazer the size of a palm, fringed like the fronds, which crops the new film off the stone; you might see three in an hour of looking, in a volume of water that could hold a city. And there are choughs in the cleft lip, black and red-legged, which nest in the wet and dive the first ten meters for the grazers and come back up. They are all deaf. Every one, from hatching. They watch each other's wings instead of listening, and they are the only creatures in the world that find this place restful.

---

The quiet is the thing the cutters actually navigate by.

The roar reflects. It folds on itself. And in the folding there are nodes — coffin-sized pockets where the waves cancel and the sound simply is not, drifting slowly through the chamber as the currents reshape it. Put your head into one and the world drops out. You hear your heartbeat, the squeak of your own neck, the small wet click of your eyelids. Two people with their heads in the same node can talk in ordinary voices. The whole craft of the wet-cutters is a craft of finding these: reading the shimmer, following a node as it wanders, working in it while it lasts, hauling out when it drifts away and the roar comes back like a hand over the mouth.

Their marks are everywhere and nowhere. Sixteen hand-cut steps up the outside of the stain. A ladder of grass rope, stiff with salt, half-swallowed at its lower end by green stone that has climbed it like ivy. Copper pins, soft, hammered cold, blooming with mineral flowers. A dry-stone hut at the cleft mouth, roofed with slate that has no eroded edges. Bowls on shelves. A bone hook. An adze handle, replaced four times, the socket worn to the shape of a grip by the only friction in a hundred kilometers.

And in the ceiling, domes. They quarry air here. The arriving water comes with breath dissolved in it, and the breath seeps out and gathers under any overhang, so the cutters chip shallow cups into the roof — a hand's depth, a body's width — and wait a season, and come back to a bubble. They put their faces up into these lungs and breathe. The air tastes of new stone and is a little too rich, and men who work the deep domes come out talking too fast, laughing, seeing their mothers in the braids of the current. The bubbles shrink between visits. The water takes them back. Every dome in that ceiling is a slow argument between a man with a chisel and an ocean learning what air is for.

---

The cleft is narrowing. It has closed a hand's width in the memory of the oldest woman in the valley, and the accretion is faster on the lip than anywhere, because that is where the pressure drops. The room is sealing itself with its own making.

In some number of years — the tallies disagree — the slot will finish closing, and the mountain will be solid, and inside it there will be a bubble of young salt water three hundred meters across, lit like an afternoon, with a small heavy moon circling in it and a few pale fronds combing the walls and a grass ladder and a copper pin and sixteen stone steps leading to nothing. And the water will go on arriving, and go on being made, and go on building inward, and the roar will have nowhere at all to go.

You come out through the slot into wind and silence so total it feels like a second deafness. The sun is where it was. The stone is warm under your palm. Your ears ring for three days, and then they stop ringing, and after that, for the rest of your life, in quiet rooms, you will sometimes feel a pressure in your sternum, patient, tidal, leaning very slightly toward something that is circling.
