**The Bowl**

From the upland you would walk past it. The limestone here is grey and flat and split into blocks by rain-widened fissures, and every fissure holds a finger of standing water, and there is nothing green in any of them — no moss, no sedge, no scrub, only stone and the water in the stone and a low sky the colour of a wet coin. The ground rings a little underfoot. Then the ringing stops, because the ground stops, and you are standing at the hem of a hole about three hundred paces across with a lid of cloud sitting in it.

The cloud is not weather. It stays. Cool air drains into the bowl at dusk and cannot climb out, and the walls seep so steadily that the air down there is always at its dew point, so the mist forms at the same level every day, at about the height of the third lamp, and lies flat as poured milk. From the rim you see the tops of things: a gantry, a standpipe, the crown of a stone tree that is not a tree. You hear almost nothing. A sound like a room full of small clocks, very far away.

The stair is cut into the wall on the north side, and its lower forty rungs have become a single smooth ramp of flowstone, so that descending the last stretch is a matter of sitting and sliding with your boots against the balustrade, which is also stone now, and which was iron once, and still is, underneath.

---

**What the water does**

It does not fall. That is the first thing to correct. There are no waterfalls in the Bowl, no splash, no roar. The walls weep. Water arrives at the surface as a film a millimetre thick and moves down the fluted rock at the pace of a minute hand, gathering in beads at the lips of the runnels, letting go one bead at a time. Two hundred thousand beads let go every minute around the whole circumference, and each one is too small to hear, and together they make a sound like applause heard through a wall — a hush with a texture, a hiss with grain in it.

The walls have been dissolving for a very long time and they have arrived at their shape. Vertical organ-pipes, rounded, close-set, some no wider than a wrist; between them the flowstone bulges like the flesh of something well fed. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing here is fresh. The rate at which the water eats the rock and the rate at which the rock gives up its dissolved lime and lays it back down as tufa have been in agreement for perhaps eleven thousand years. The bowl deepens by a hair each century and lines itself at the same speed, and so it stays exactly as it is, which is the trick of the place: not stillness, but a balance of two enthusiasms.

Everything gets coated. Everything. A dropped spanner becomes a pebble becomes a lump becomes a feature of the floor. There is a valve wheel on the east wall at chest height whose spokes are furred an inch thick with cream-coloured calcite; you can see the shape of the wheel the way you can see the shape of a body under a blanket. It has not turned since before anyone alive. It could not turn. Its iron heart is still there, rusting slowly in its stone sleeve, and the rust bleeds out through the pores in a thin orange stain, and the stain is pasture.

---

**The felt**

There are no plants in the Bowl and there never have been. Nothing here has ever needed the sun; the sun is a rumour that arrives four or five days a year.

Instead there is the felt: bacterial nap, chemosynthetic, growing wherever sulphide seeps out of the bedding planes and meets a little oxygen in the water film. It is white where it is thin, faintly gold where it is thick, and it has the give of wet wool. It covers the underside of every ledge. It hangs in streamers where the seeps are strongest, and the streamers move — not with the water, but against it, slowly, the way a tongue moves. Where the seep is old and rich, the felt goes black and stinks pleasantly of struck matches, and things live in it so densely that the felt appears to be boiling in place.

That is the base of everything. Below the black felt: grazers. Below the grazers: everything else.

And then there is the second pasture, which is the strange one, and which is why the place is not simply a wet hole.

---

**The current**

The works were built to pump brine out of the low ground, and when the pipes began to eat themselves the engineers did what engineers do: they installed impressed-current protection. A generator, a bank of anodes, and the whole iron body of the works wired to be a cathode, so that the metal would stop dissolving and start, instead, gathering stone.

It worked. It is still working.

Water comes into the Bowl through a fissure at the base of the south wall at a rate that has not changed in recorded time, and it turns a screw turbine in a brick chamber that is half-drowned and entirely furred, and the turbine turns a generator whose bearings are lubricated by nothing anyone put there, and the current goes out along cables that are now calcite ropes with a copper thread, and it holds the works cathodic, and on every protected surface the lime comes down out of the water and builds.

A finger's thickness a decade. White aragonite, faintly translucent, laid in fans and cabbage-heads and knuckles. On the drowned gantry legs it has grown into structures like the skeletons of hands. On the settling tank it has built a reef.

The film that lives on that growing stone is the richest food in the Bowl. The animals that graze it grow their own shells out of the same stuff, in the same place, at the same rate — they have to; nowhere else in the bowl will their shells thicken. So there are pilgrimages: the shelled grazers, palm-sized, pale, with a fringe of feelers like a wet brush, moving in slow files up the gantry leg to the good current, grazing and thickening for a day, then descending to the rust-pasture to spend it. Their tracks are visible in the tufa as faint worn lanes, polished, generations deep. The lanes are older than the works. The lanes were here when the iron was new, and the iron has simply been fitted into them.

At the anodes, meanwhile, metal still bleeds — that is the price of the arrangement — and the iron-oxidisers bloom in orange floc, and a whole second economy grazes the floc, and its animals are rust-coloured and heavy and slow, and they do not go to the white pasture, and the white ones do not come to theirs.

**Who is here**

Dense. That is the word for it. The shallows at the north end look like gravel until you look properly, and then the gravel is amphipods, pale as boiled rice, packed shoulder to shoulder in drifts a hand deep, ten thousand to the square foot, and the drifts move as one animal in slow surges when something wades. The wall tubes are bundled so tightly they touch and fuse; when a shadow crosses them the retraction goes around the bowl in a wave, a hundred thousand quill-worms pulling in over about four seconds, and the wall changes colour from bone to grey, and then, over the next minute, they come back out in a ragged bloom.

In the air: midges, in columns over the transformer housing, which is the only warm thing in the Bowl — a rectangle of eleven degrees above ambient, a hearth, contested territory, the mating swarms stacked above it in tiers like smoke that has decided to stay. Nothing eats leaves because there are none; the midge larvae graze the felt at the waterline and the adults do not eat at all and live four hours.

Hunting them along the waterline: the gauger. Waist-high, six-legged, blind, the colour of wet paper, moving with an awful patience, one leg lifted and held and lowered without a ripple. It does not see and it does not smell. It reads the field. Everything alive in the Bowl leaks a little current where its shell is thin or its skin is broken, and against the standing electrical grain of the place a wounded thing shines like a lamp. The gauger walks the field lines the way a spider walks a web, and it knows the difference between the shimmer of a moulting grazer and the flat blank of a stone.

The shelled ones know this too. When they moult they go down into the black felt and sit in it, insulated, stinking, for two days, and nothing finds them, and the felt eats a little of them in exchange.

And in the intake chamber there are the screeners: squat, territorial, armoured things the size of a cat's head, whose entire behaviour is to scrape and hold. They hold the fissure mouth against the shelly drift that would clog it, and they scrape the contacts because a clean contact is a warm contact and warmth is the rarest thing here. They are not servants. They are simply animals who have found that the machine is the best burrow in the world, and the machine has found that it runs better with them in it, and neither party has any opinion about the arrangement.

---

**The breath**

Every eleven hours the level drops a hand's breadth.

There is a siphon in the rock — a natural one, older than everything — and when the bowl fills past a certain lip the siphon primes and pulls, and the outflow outruns the inflow for about ninety minutes, and the whole shoreline steps down. Then it breaks, and the water climbs back.

The Bowl runs on this. The exposed band is the ebb-pasture, and it is stripped in the first ten minutes by everything that can crawl, and the amphipod drifts ride the falling water down and ride it back up, and the gaugers follow the drifts, and the spawning happens in the slack at the bottom of the breath, always, all of them together, so that for twenty minutes the water is cloudy with gametes and the whole bowl smells faintly of iodine and the black felt goes luminous — a low blue-green, no brighter than a watch face, but enough, in that light, to show you your own hands.

That is the loudest it ever gets, and it is not loud. A relay clacks somewhere in the brick chamber, twice, as the load shifts. The beads keep letting go. The midges keep turning. Underneath it all a hum at the edge of hearing, sixty cycles or thereabouts, which you stop noticing in an hour and then notice again at three in the morning and cannot stop noticing after that.

---

**The hem**

Walk to the rim and it ends. Not gradually. The sulphide seeps out along a particular bedding plane and that plane rises and breaks the surface at the lip of the bowl, and above it there is nothing to eat, and the felt stops in a line you can put your boot across. The animals turn back at that line. They have never been over it. Outside there is ordinary rain and ordinary limestone and, in the distance, ordinary grass, which to them would be a wall of inedible glass.

Four or five days a year the wind gets in and lifts the lid and real daylight falls all the way to the floor. Everything hides. The amphipods go down, the worms pull in, the gaugers fold themselves under the flowstone ledges, and the Bowl lies exposed and blinking and utterly silent for a few hours, and the tufa shows its true colours — cream, ochre, rust, the dull silver of old solder — and then the cool air pools again at dusk and the milk pours back in and the clocks resume.

There are boot prints on the stair, filled and half-filled with flowstone, from someone who came down and went up. There is a gauge book in a brass case on the tower, its last entry a number and a set of initials. There is a lamp on the east wall that has been replaced more recently than the others; the glass is cleaner; nobody knows by whom.

None of it matters much to the arrangement. The works have stopped being a thing that was built. They are a stratum now, a hard band in the local geology, an iron reef that grows stone and leaks food, and the water will keep turning the screw as long as the water keeps arriving, which is a promise the rock has been keeping since before there was anything here to hear it.

Stand still long enough and you stop being able to say which parts are working.
