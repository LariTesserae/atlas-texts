## The Hold

Begin at the floor, because everything here begins at the floor. It is warm underfoot and slightly giving, cast basalt glazed with a finger's depth of standing water, and the water is exactly as warm as your hand, so that when you put your palm into it you cannot tell where your skin stops. This is the first thing the place does to you: it removes the difference between you and it. Thirty-nine degrees, everywhere, always. The air is blood heat and near-saturated and moving at the speed of a slow breath. Nothing here feels warm. Warmth requires a gradient. There are no gradients.

The floor is cambered — a fall of two centimeters per hundred meters, maintained, surveyed, true — so the water goes somewhere. It goes in sheets so thin they have no color, only a preference for direction, and it braids around the ankles of things standing in it, and it finds the channels, and the channels are grouted with a mortar that is still, after all this time, the color of the day it was mixed.

There are no rounded stones. Look for one. There is grit, angular and graded, sold by number, and there are the arrises of poured walls, sharp enough to cut, and there are the tie-holes where the forms were bolted, each one plugged with a disc of bone-ash ceramic that has never once worked loose. The pour lines are visible. The grain of the formwork is visible. Everything looks eleven days old. Everything will look eleven days old when the sun-panels are finally switched off, if they are ever switched off, which is not a thing the specification anticipates.

---

The second thing the place does is refuse to be empty.

At first the basins read as marsh: pale hummocks rim to rim across the water, ribbed like radiator fins, faintly steaming. Then the scale resolves and the hummocks are animals. Sessile filter-stock, kidney-shaped, four meters at the shoulder if they had shoulders, packed at a spacing of eleven centimeters, which is the spacing that maximizes heat transfer per unit of feed. They do not move. They breathe. Their hides are wet and hairless and warm and the ribs of them open and close by a millimeter with each cycle, and the water on them evaporates, and the vapor goes up, and that is the entire purpose of their lives.

They go on to the horizon. The horizon is a bright band that tilts *upward*, because the world is a bowl and you are inside it, and the far tiers hang above you like the underside of a weather system. There are two hundred and forty thousand basins on the visible shelf. There are nine shelves. Below the floor there is more floor, and below that, more; no bedrock anywhere, no substrate, nothing that was not poured. Dig and you find decommissioned basins, still warm, still wet, still occupied by stock that has been reassigned to nothing in particular and kept at temperature anyway, because cooling them would cost more than keeping them.

The air is also animals. At chest height there is a permanent haze, a drift of sterile larvae the size of a comma, thirty thousand per cubic meter, replaced at precisely the rate at which they are lost. They do not hatch. They do not feed. They are humidity made countable. When you walk, you displace them and they close behind you without turbulence, and if you stand very still you can feel them arriving on your face like the world's slowest snow.

Nothing here has ever had a leaf. There is no green; the word for green, in the maintenance stamps, refers to copper corrosion. Primary production is a bacterial slick on every wet surface, a brown-gold film that takes current from the buried buswork and carbon from the air and makes sugar out of both, and it smells like bread and iodine. Under the gantries, where the condensate runs, the slick grows in mats a hand thick and is cropped by grazers with mouthparts like wet leather. That is the bottom of the world. Four steps up from it, everything is dissolved and returned. There is no apex. There is no accumulation. The dead go down the flumes as a plumbing event, and the flumes are warm, and there is no smell of rot anywhere, only lanolin, hot zinc, milk, and the faint sweetness of the mother-vats.

---

The third thing is the quiet, which is not an absence but a product.

A hundred million animals breathing at once should be a roar. It isn't. The hides are sound-eaters; the feet are felt pads grown, not made; the channels are lined with a rubber that has never hardened. Noise costs feed. Noise is inefficiency, and the world was selected against it before it was switched on, and then the selection was frozen, and so it has stayed quiet in the same way it has stayed warm.

What you hear: drip. The wet underside of every gantry is a choir of drips, each falling from a stalactite that is trimmed to length by seam-lice so it never grows and never shortens, each landing in a hollow it has worn in the hide of the animal below, which grew the hollow deliberately, as a socket, because a drip that lands flat makes a sound and a drip that lands in a cup does not.

What you hear: click. Counters, small warm things with manipulators and a horny throat, sitting in ranks along the flume lips, tallying. Their click is the only consonant in the world. It is not language. It is inventory.

What you hear, every ninety-one minutes: the swallow. It begins at the eastern sump and crosses a county in eleven minutes, a phase-locked wave of a hundred million guts contracting in sequence, and it arrives as a soft enormous sigh under the threshold of hearing, felt in the sinuses and the sternum, and the water in the channels shivers with it, and then it has passed west and the quiet comes back like a door closing very gently.

The presses and the grinders are somewhere below and behind, and they are loud, and you will never hear them. They are baffled, jacketed, sunk in gut-wool, and what reaches you is a pressure, a feeling that the floor is a held note. The whole continent is an engine's soundproofing.

---

Because that is what it is. Under the ninth shelf there is a kiln line, a thing at load, and it must be held at one temperature or the test is invalid. The basins are its radiator. The stock are its coolant. The hides are fins, the breath is evaporation, the rain is condensate return, the drift is a humidity buffer, the slick is a carbon loop, and the sealant that keeps every joint new is excreted by an arthropod that lives in the seams and eats the scale and lays down mineral at exactly the rate the water takes it away.

That is why nothing is rounded. That is why nothing is old. Erosion here is not a force but a line item, and it is matched. The world is eleven days old forever.

The rain falls on a circuit. Not a season — a route. Cells of condensate the size of provinces walk their fixed paths across the shelves and arrive over any given basin at the same interval, warm as blood, tasting of iron and salt, and the stock have grown their sockets and their drainage ribs to receive it, and the puddles have grouted edges, and the fog banks are herds, and if you follow one long enough you will meet it again coming the other way with the same animals in it.

Light comes from the whole ceiling at once, a diffuser of stretched cauls — animals grown flat, translucent, veined, luminous with the bacterial light of the mother-culture — and it is ordinary daylight, unremarkable, the light of a bright overcast afternoon in a place where afternoons are not a category. Shadows are soft and directly underneath things. They have been in the same places so long that the stock have grown pale where the shadow falls and darker where it doesn't, which is the closest thing here to a calendar.

There is no night. There is no word for night. There is *turn*, and a turn is identical to the last turn, and the ledger balances to the gram: heat in, heat out; feed in, feed out; variance produced, variance skimmed. The reducers do the skimming — long low animals with soft jaws that move through the basins taking whatever has drifted from spec, the too-thin hide, the loud one, the one that bred. Evolution stopped here on the day the acceptance test began, because the fitness landscape was nailed flat, and nothing has been permitted to become anything else since.

The maintenance is done by larvae. The same lineage: born small and many-handed, fitting grommets, trimming stalactites, repacking glands, working the seams for a lifetime; and then, at the fixed replacement rate, a few of them are allowed to metamorphose, and they swell into the great warm ribbed stillness of the basins, and their hands go soft and are absorbed, and they take their place eleven centimeters from their neighbors and breathe for the rest of time. The workers do not know this. The adults do not remember it. There is no one above either of them; there is no foreman, no species that stands outside the count. Whatever ordered this place is not present in it. Only its specification is present, stamped into the ceramic at every tenth tie-hole, in a font that the counters click past without reading.

---

Go down to the fourth sump, where the water is a hand deep and the light comes through the cauls the color of skimmed milk, and stand among the breathing until your own body is no longer a separate temperature. The swallow will come through you. The drift will settle on your eyelashes and be replaced. Somewhere a drip will find the cup that grew for it.

Under the water, on the floor, there is a stencil. It has never been repainted. It has never needed to be. The mineral-laying lice keep its edges as crisp as the hour it was sprayed, and the water stands exactly at the line beneath it, and it says:

**WARM STANDBY — DO NOT DRAIN**

Nobody has ever had to be told.
