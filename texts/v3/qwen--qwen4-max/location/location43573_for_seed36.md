## The Sill

There is no sky here, and it is the brightest place anyone has ever stood.

Ninety meters up, the ceiling is a single sheet of selenite — satin spar, grown in fibers rather than faces, so that it does not show you the sun so much as *conduct* it. Above the pane there is presumably a desert, a salt flat, a hammering white noon; nobody has been up to check, and there is no way up to check with. What arrives below is light that has been combed. It comes down through the whole ceiling at once, from every direction, at an intensity that makes your eyes water for the first hour and then never again. There are no shadows in the Sill. Set your hand on the ground and there is no hand-shape under it. The light goes around. It has been going around, at exactly this strength, from exactly the same slightly brighter region of the pane — the Standing Noon, off toward the northeast, a smear of glare you can look at directly and that has never moved — for all of the time there has been to measure.

Nobody measures it in days. There are no days to measure it in.

## Heat, and the only way out of it

The air is forty-one degrees. So is the ground, so is the crystal, so is the water, so is the leaf you put against your cheek, so is the wall of the house you build. This is the first law of the place and the one that takes longest to understand: **there are no gradients here.** Nothing is cooler than anything else. The stone does not draw heat out of your back. The pools do not chill your feet. Sitting in water up to your neck accomplishes nothing at all except making you wet, and being wet is the entire point, because evaporation is the only cooling that exists in the Sill.

So the country is damp the way a mouth is damp. Not soaked, not marshy — a permanent thin film, a beading, a humidity that sits at the exact level where sweat still works and only just. The underside of the pane sweats. Every leaf sweats. The columns sweat. Water stands in the axils of the great leaves in tanks the size of bathtubs, some the size of rooms, all of it body-warm, all of it faintly green and tasting of cucumber and chalk. There are no streams. There is a feature called the Bead Run that has the *shape* of a river — a meandering trough of overlapping leaf-bases, a chain of pools, a floodplain of pale tissue — and it has never flowed. Water moves through the Sill by capillarity along veins, and by being drunk, and by being carried, and by falling.

It falls rarely. A bead on the pane grows until it can no longer hold itself, and then it lets go, and drops ninety meters, and hits the turf with a sound like a knuckle on a door. You might see three in a lifetime. They have names. Children are named after them.

Distance here is measured in drinks — the number of times you must stop and wet your wrapping before you arrive. The Colonnade is two drinks from the Nursery. The far wall is nine. It is a country about the size of a long valley, and you can walk all of it, and everyone who has ever walked all of it can be counted on both hands.

## What the ground is

You are not walking on soil. There is no soil. Soil requires death and grinding, and neither has been invented here.

The floor is a mattress of living cambium, warm and slightly yielding, veined, the pink-tan of the inside of a fig, and it takes your footprint and closes it behind you over the course of a hundred paces. It is not a forest floor. It is the outer surface of an organism that fills the chamber, and the chamber is not a cave with plants in it; the chamber is a *shape inside the plant*, the way a lung is a shape inside a body.

The columns are the oldest part: pillar-figs whose trunks fused as they rose, sixty and eighty meters of braided wood gone pale and hard as horn, flattening at the top into a ceiling-lining of leaf that hangs just beneath the pane and drinks the combed light. They stand in ranks and crowds, near-touching, so that in the Colonnade you move through a grid of gaps rather than an open space, always aware of the next pillar and the one behind it. Between and above them the mass layers up: leaf on leaf on leaf, bracket-shelves of tissue, hanging curtains of withy, galleries and mezzanines and slots, all of it alive, all of it lit, every surface at the same temperature as every other. The density is not oppressive so much as *total*. There is no bare rock anywhere in the interior. There is no bare anything.

And out at the edges, in the Chalk Nursery, the plants are making stone.

They pull mineral out of the seeps and lay it down inside their own wood, and the wood is turning to rock, and it has been turning to rock since before there was anyone to notice. It never finishes. That is the second law. Cut into a milkstone trunk and it is soft as a fingernail, wet, faintly translucent, and it will be exactly that soft in a thousand years. It is stone at the instant before it sets, held there. You can shape it with your thumbs. You can dent it, and the dent stays, because nothing here weathers, nothing here erodes, nothing here is ever rounded or smoothed or worn away by anything.

Look at the edges of the country, where the chamber ends: raw fractured rock, sharp as the day it broke, no talus at the base, no dust, no scree, no sand. There is no sand in the Sill. There is no dust in the Sill. The air is perfectly clear and has never carried a particle. Every surface is either freshly broken or freshly grown, and both look the same, and both look brand new, forever.

## The almost

Nothing completes.

The bud hills are the largest structures in the country: sealed flower-buds the size of granaries, the size of houses, green flushed with red at the seams, and if you put your ear against one you can hear the tension in it, the enormous held pressure of a thing one instant from opening. They have been one instant from opening for the whole history of the place. Climb them. There are handholds. Someone put them there.

No flower has ever opened here. Therefore no seed has ever set, and no fruit has ever ripened, and there is no sugar in the Sill, and no fermentation, and no rot, and no smell of rot. Everything propagates by division and by grafting, by hand, deliberately, or by accident when a fragment touches and takes. The tenders eat green starch and sap and pith and the soft mineral cream from the nursery trunks. Nothing is sweet. Sweetness is an event that has not occurred.

And nothing dies. Not "nothing dies yet" — death is not a process this system has running. Tissue that stops being used is reabsorbed, quietly, without decay, without smell, without flies. Which means there is nothing dry and dead anywhere in the country, which means **there is no fire.** You cannot make one. There is no tinder, no fuel, no deadfall, no char. Out of that single absence comes the whole poverty of the place: no cooking, no clay, no lime, no metal, no tool harder than a shaped thumbprint in unripe stone. Everything that has been made here has been made by hand, by grafting, by weaving, by patient persuasion of something that was already growing.

The sap runs at exactly the speed of staying the same. That is the third law, and it is the one that makes the Sill feel the way it feels. Nothing is static. Everything is circulating — transpiring, drinking, beading, laying down mineral, tightening toward the bloom — at precisely the rate required for the whole to remain identical to itself. It is not a frozen country. It is a country held at the top of an inhale.

## The marks

Scattered through all that green, at intervals of a drink or two, there are buildings, and none of them are ruined, and none of them are finished.

A wall of milkstone blocks a hand high and a hundred paces long, running through the Colonnade and simply stopping. A doorway with a lintel and no house on either side of it. A stair climbing a pillar-fig in a shallow spiral, forty steps, the forty-first still a sapling with its leaves out, waiting to be asked. A ring of posts, each one bearing a hundred overlapping thumbprints, pressed in by hands that were doing the only kind of construction available to them: pushing, and waiting, and pushing again.

The marks are faint. Not faded — nothing fades — but *shallow*, tentative, made by a species working a material that never hardens and never forgets. Every thumbprint is still exactly as deep as the thumb went. You can lay your own thumb into one and feel whether the person who made it had a bigger hand than you.

This is the thing that unsettles visitors, on the rare occasions there are visitors: **abandonment here is indistinguishable from interruption.** Nothing has slumped. Nothing has fallen. No leaf-litter has drifted against the walls, because there is no leaf-litter. A house left ten thousand years ago looks like a house whose builder stepped out to drink.

There are tenders — perhaps eleven of them across the whole Sill, thin, pale, wrapped in wet leaf-cloth, moving between the districts on a rounds that has no schedule because there is nothing to schedule it against. They wet the grafts. They pass water mouth to mouth into the axil-tanks that have no other supply. They press the walls a little further and stop. They do not appear to be trying to finish anything. If you ask, in the way one asks with hands, they will show you a bud, and the seam of it, and the red in the seam, and they will make a gesture that means *keep it here.*

You almost never see one another. Two drinks is a long way in a country where you must keep stopping.

## The few animals

Three kinds, and none of them common.

A stilt-browser, white and ribbed and tall as a man, that stands in the Colonnade for weeks at a time with its forelimbs in a leaf-tank, drinking by capillary action, cooling by evaporation off its own exposed ribs — the only architecture in the Sill designed for heat loss. When it moves it moves like a thing being carried.

Bead-snails, thumbnail-sized, translucent, each carrying a single bead of water on the flat of its shell like a lens. They are the accidental gardeners: they cross from trunk to trunk and carry cells and water with them, and half the grafts in the country that nobody remembers making were made by snails.

And a small pale bird in the upper columns that says one note, a low pure tone with no overtone to it, at intervals of about a hundred breaths. It is the only regular sound in the world. It has never varied. Tenders who go blind from the light keep time by it, and there is nothing to keep time *for*.

There are no insects. There is nothing for insects to do.

## Quiet

The mass eats sound. All that soft living tissue, layer on layer, absorbs everything above a murmur, so that the Sill has the flat padded hush of a very large room full of wool. Nearby things are muffled. Your own footsteps are nothing.

But the underside of the pane is a hard curved crystal sheet ninety meters up, and it does not absorb; it channels. Sound that escapes upward runs along it and comes down somewhere else entirely. So the acoustic of the country is inverted: the person beside you is a whisper, and someone knocking a post two drinks away arrives at your ear clean and close and intimate, from above, inside your head. You hear the tenders you never meet. You hear them working. You hear the pop of a graft taking, a sound like a knuckle cracking, from a district you have never walked to.

And once in a great while, a bead lets go of the ceiling, and falls, and the whole country hears the knock.

## What it is

It would be easy to call it unfinished, and wrong. Unfinished implies a afterward. The Sill has no afterward in it; it is not a draft of a valley, it is a completed thing whose subject is the instant before completion, executed perfectly and held. The buds are not waiting. Waiting is a thing you do when time is going somewhere. The buds are *being* almost, the way a note is being a note, and the tenders are not builders but the sustain of the chord.

There is no dark in the Sill except inside the buds. That is where everyone sleeps: crawled in through the seam of a bud the size of a granary, into the green-black warm damp between petals that will not open, lying in the tightest and most private space in the world with a wet leaf across the eyes, listening to the tension in the walls.

And when you leave, if you leave, you will press your hand into a milkstone wall on the way out, because everyone does, and the wall will take it, and keep it, at exactly that depth, with the whorls of your palm, in a country where nothing wears away — one more faint mark among the faint marks, in the place that is always about to begin.
