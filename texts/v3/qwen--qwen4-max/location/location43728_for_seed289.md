## The Singing Bubble

Above it there is a gorge with almost nothing in it: pale limestone, a scatter of grey scrub, thyme in the cracks, a river the colour of weak tea moving fast and cold over gravel. Cool country. Dry, except for the river, which is the only wet thing for a long way, and which does not flood, and does not fail.

Under the west bank, where the current has undercut the slab, there is a slot you could put your shoulders through sideways. You would not find it by looking. You would find it with your teeth. The rock there rings. Put a hand flat on the stone anywhere within ten paces of the water and the bone of your wrist takes up a high silver tone, steady as a struck glass that never stops being struck. Divers who know the reach call it the Kettle. They do not go in twice.

---

The slot goes back two body-lengths and turns up, and you come out of black water into a bubble of air the size of a small kitchen. Three paces by two. The ceiling is a raised hand above your head. It is eleven degrees in here. It is eleven degrees in the water, eleven in the rock, eleven in the film on the wall, eleven in the middle of the noise; there is no warm end and no cold end, no draft, no up-and-down of temperature to make the air move. The air never goes anywhere. It only shakes.

And it is bright. Brighter than the gorge at noon, brighter than snow, a blue-white that has no direction at all. You cannot find the source because there isn't one, or rather there is one and it is the whole volume, every cubic finger of it, and you cannot cast a shadow in here if you try, and people have tried.

The rock is raw. This is the first strange thing you notice after the light, once your eyes give up. There are no stalactites. No flowstone, no scalloping, no rounded lip, no ochre stain, no old smoothed anything. Every surface is a fresh fracture, sharp enough to open a palm, crystals in their first blunt adolescence, the walls still showing the shape of the break that made them. It looks like a cave an hour old. It looks like a cave that has not finished being invented.

There is no dirt. Not a grain. Not a scree, not a silt fan, not a dust film on a ledge. In a cave, this is impossible; caves are made of accumulation, they are the slow ledger of everything that fell. Here nothing falls, and so nothing is buried, and so there is no past lying around on the floor.

---

The note is doing all of it.

The river runs across the mouth of the slot at a fixed speed and shears over the opening the way wind shears over the lip of a bottle, and the bubble sings. One note. A high, wet, silver pitch, somewhere above the top of a piccolo's easy range, and loud — loud in the way a jet at the edge of a field is loud, loud with a physical hand on the chest. You cannot hear yourself shout. You can feel your throat tear and hear nothing but the note, unchanged, unbothered, occupying every part of the room at once and refusing to be added to.

At that intensity, in air this saturated, water cannot help shining. The sound tears the vapour open and the tearing flashes, sixteen hundred-odd times a second, everywhere, all through the volume, and the flashes stack into a steady white blaze because your eye is too slow to see the seams. Brightness here is a function of loudness. They rise and fall together, which means they never do either.

The rule follows immediately, and everything alive in the room is built around it: **quiet is dark.** Hush this place and you blind it. Hush it properly and the fog falls, the shelves come down, and the whole population drops four inches onto bare stone.

---

Because there are shelves.

A standing wave has places where it cancels: flat planes of stillness, hung in the roar, spaced half a wavelength apart. Here that is a little over ten centimetres. Four inches. Nineteen of them, floor to ceiling, level and parallel and motionless, each one about as thick as a slice of bread.

The mist goes to the quiet. Droplets are herded out of the violence and gathered into the still planes, where they hang without falling, packed shoulder to shoulder, a wet page of levitated beads. The loud gaps between are swept clear and clean and full of light. So the room, from the water's edge, looks like a loaf sliced but not separated: nineteen milky shelves with nineteen blades of white fire between them, and the fire is what lights the shelves, from both sides, forever.

The quiet is wet. The loud is bright. Nothing lives in the loud.

Everything lives in the quiet, and I mean everything, and I mean packed. There is no bare patch on any page. Nineteen horizontal worlds, each four inches from its neighbours and utterly unreachable from them, each filled edge to edge with bodies in contact, a mosaic of translucent shells and furred backs and folded wings, no gaps, no vacancies, no room to put a fingertip without asking something to move, and nothing here can move, because moving would mean a change in the count.

---

The bottom of the food chain has two heads and both of them are the river.

One is light: a black algal skin in the water film, black because in a room this bright green is a liability. It is the only vegetation to speak of — that stain, three pale fronds of something fern-adjacent on page six, and a single root hair that has come down through a fissure in the ceiling from the dry world above. The root hangs like a plumb line into the blaze, sipping. It does not lengthen. It has been about to reach the next shelf for a very long time.

The other head is sound. There are furred mites here that grow a sliver of quartz inside their own heads and let the note flex it, and eat the strain. They farm the pressure in their skulls. Their crystals are the same crystals budding on the walls, and it is genuinely unclear, in this room, where the geology stops and the animals start; the walls are being laid down by something with a pulse, and taken away by the water at precisely the same rate, which is why every surface stays a fresh fracture. The cave is dissolving and being born continuously and has never got anywhere. It has been an hour old since before the gorge had a river.

On the film graze sheets of glass amphipods, transparent, packed in a single layer like paving, each in its own dimple of mucus. On them, ribbon worms that hunt only sideways, never up. On page four, moths the colour of wet ash, whose fur is a sound-absorber, and who fly at exactly the pitch of the light — so that they are strobed once per wingbeat and hang motionless in the air, dead-looking, pinned, a page of specimens, all of them flying hard.

That is the thing about the light here. Anything that moves quickly comes apart into a ladder of copies, or else locks and freezes. Wave your hand and you get a fan of hands. The room is a riot that photographs as a still life.

Between the pages, the crossing. To go up four inches you must pass through the loud, where the air is being torn and the tears are shining, and it will take the wings off you. Small armoured shuttles try it constantly. Most do not arrive. The number that arrive equals the number that leave, and has for as long as anyone could measure, and the traffic never thickens and never thins.

Down at the throat, where the water shears, live the tuners: blind, deaf, plated things like woodlice cast in lead. They are deaf because ears would be a liability at that pressure. Their whole occupation is volume. The water dissolves the chamber; the chamber's pitch would drift; a drifting pitch would slide the pages through the bodies standing on them. So the tuners plaster and un-plaster the throat, adding and removing at the same rate, holding the note where it is. They are the reason the shelves stay still. They are the reason there is anywhere to stand. They keep the cage the exact shape of the animals inside it.

---

There are marks. Not many; four, in a room where every square centimetre is otherwise accounted for.

A staircase of three shed carapaces cemented to the west wall, going up one page and stopping. Nobody uses it. Nothing here needs stairs, and nothing here has ever needed to grow taller.

A spiral of sand grains, three centimetres across, laid in the water film with a precision that is hard to look at directly.

A carapace bowl worn smooth on one side, as if it had been used as a scoop for a very long time, holding one droplet that never evaporates and never overflows.

And on page eleven, a line of nine clay pellets stuck to the rock at four-inch intervals — no. At the page's own thickness. Evenly. A tally, or a scale, or a signature.

There are always nine pellets. There has never been a particular pellet. They are eaten and replaced, dissolved and re-laid, at the rate the room does everything; the mark persists and the material flows through it like water through a standing wave. Whoever left it did not leave it and go. Leaving would be a change. The mark is simply part of the census now, counted alongside the amphipods, born and unborn at the same steady rate.

None of it is a device. There is no mechanism in this room, no lever, no fire, nothing that could be called a tool in the sense of a thing that gets used up. Everything made here is secreted, spun, cemented, grown — biology doing masonry. And there is no maker to find. There is a rate, and the rate has a shape, and the shape looks like intention if you stand in it long enough, which you cannot.

---

Here is what the place is, finally.

Everything in it is happening at full speed. The seep comes in at a teaspoon a minute and the bowl-lip spills a teaspoon a minute and the pool is thirty centimetres across and four deep and has never been otherwise. Condensation and evaporation are the same number. Dissolution and crystal growth are the same number. Birth and death are the same number, and are arguably the same event seen from two ends. The fog does not fall. The dust does not settle. The air does not circulate; it inhales and exhales one breath through the drowned mouth, over and over, and the breath is always the same breath.

There are no gradients here — not thermal, not chemical, not temporal. No shelf is better than another. There is no reason page seven is the shrimp page and page twelve is the moth page. It is simply where they were when the note first stood up, and nothing has ever happened since to rearrange them. Nineteen identical worlds sorted by nothing but the accident of founding, held four inches apart by a sound, forever.

You cannot step into the same river twice. Here you cannot step into a different one.

You would have to be small to get in, and cold to stay, and deaf to bear it, and there is nowhere to put you: the room is full, in the strict sense, occupied to the last dimple, nineteen pages of life with clean fire between. To enter you would have to ask a shelf to move.

It has been about to exist for a very long time. It will be about to exist tomorrow. Above it the scrub will stay sparse and the gorge will stay dry and the tea-coloured river will run at the speed it runs, and under the bank the note will hold, sixteen hundred and sixty-one times a second, and the light will not flicker, and the moths will hang in the air like dead things, flying.
