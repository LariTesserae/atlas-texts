## The Hold

You hear it a long way off, across the black floodplain, before the tree resolves out of the haze — a low note with a hard bright edge on it, like a bow drawn forever across a string the thickness of a road. The mud ahead of you dimples in rings. Water standing in old hoofprints shivers itself into lace. Nothing in the reeds is singing. Everything in the reeds has been deaf for a hundred generations, and the ones that weren't are gone.

The tree is unremarkable: a squat, grey-limbed thing growing out of a sink in the water table, its leaves broad and coarse as roofing. The fruit hangs from the trunk at the height of your chest. It is green, faintly ribbed, warm to the touch, and about the size of two cupped hands. At its crown is an aperture the width of a needle, ringed with small stiff scales, and out of that needle-hole comes a sound that could fill a valley.

This is the first thing that is wrong, and the locals would not call it wrong. They would call it the tone.

Put your eye to the aperture and you cannot see in; the scales overlap like a shutter. But things come out. A smell of hot sugar and green milk, of cider just beginning to turn. A pulse of body-warm air, thirty-one degrees, wet enough to bead on your wrist. And, occasionally, a courier: a wasp the length of a thumbnail, gold in the seams, who walks out onto the fruit's skin, considers the enormous world, and flies away on the errand that will kill her.

To enter, you have to be small, and you have to be willing to lose what you came in with. Everything that arrives here arrives wingless.

---

Inside, the world is a hollow.

Not a cave. A *vessel* — a smooth-walled chamber a hundred and eighty paces across and sixty feet to the crown, shaped like an inverted heart, and it is almost entirely empty. That is the thing no one prepares you for. All the life is pressed against the inside of the skin, a rind of city three or four body-lengths deep, and beyond it there is nothing at all: a great warm volume of sweet air with the tone standing in it. Ninety-eight parts in a hundred of this world is vacant. You can stand at the center of the floor and see the whole of creation as a distant curving wall, and hear your own breathing lost in a noise like the inside of a bell.

The wall is not smooth for long. It is terraced, ribbed, guttered, galleried. Vascular bundles run floor-to-crown in cables as thick as a thigh, worn to a satin polish where feet have gone up them for four centuries. Between the cables the flesh has been persuaded into rooms — thousands of them, galls the size of a fist or a barn, each one a healed wound, each one a house. There are districts. There are stairs whose treads have been dished by traffic. There is a long gallery on the fourth terrace where the wall was scored in a particular pattern eleven generations ago and grew back as a colonnade, and no one now remembers what the scoring meant, only that the district is called the Long Gallery and the rent there is high.

Nothing here is made. Everything here is *asked for*. The tenants do not build; they wound the wall in the correct place, in the correct season, with the correct saliva, and the wall answers with architecture. There is no fire, no blade, no wheel, no lever. There is only the patient infliction of a small injury on something enormous and responsive, and the waiting, and the moving in.

Most of the older tenements are people.

---

The floor is a membrane, taut as a drumhead, slightly concave, dusted knee-deep at the edges with wings.

Every immigrant sheds its wings at the Eye and the wings come down through the void — a slow iridescent snow, drifting for hours, gathering in the drifts at the wall's foot where the springtails graze. The drifts are stratified. You can cut a section with your hand and read the years in bands of amber and smoke: fat years thick, lean years thin, and here and there a seam of white where a great many arrivals came in together and no one knows why. The Sweepers harvest the drifts, chew them to pulp, and the pulp goes back into the wall as request. The city is written on the sediment of its own immigration.

Walk on the floor and it speaks. Each footfall is a struck note that runs out across the whole chamber and comes back changed. Condensation gathers on the ceiling all through the warm part of the cycle and lets go in a scattered rain — big cold drops falling sixty feet onto a tuned surface, so that the floor is always being played, a random hammering counterpoint under the tone. In the twelve minutes after the dimming, when the crown sweats hardest, the drum is a solid roar and the tenants stop work and cling, and the frogs in the cisterns spawn.

There is no lake here. There is no stream. Water arrives as breath on a cold wall, as the Weep — a seam near the floor where the outside water table pushes through and runs for a quarter-hour a day down a channel worn glassy — and as the occasional true rain that comes down the Eye in a white column and is drunk before it reaches the ground. The bract-cups on the second terrace hold cisterns the size of bathtubs, skinned with algae, patrolled by small blunt frogs who have kept the water rights since before the Long Gallery. And there are the Milk Canals, which carry latex, which is not water: white, bitter, and setting. Drink it and you stiffen where you stand. The wall digests you slowly and grows a room around you.

This is how the poor house themselves. You choose your spot, you drink, you set, and in a season your grandchildren are living in your chest.

---

The tenants are wasps, and they have been here so long they are no longer quite wasps.

There are wingless workers with heads like brass buttons, who do the wounding and the sweeping and the holding. There are winged couriers, short-lived and half-mad with sugar, who carry pollen down from the Bitter Reach near the Eye and carry requests up. There are the ants, who came in on somebody's legs and now farm scale insects along the ribs, driving them, milking them, defending their pastures with a discipline that the tenants find frankly foreign. There are earless moths the color of wet paper. There are mites on everything, including the mites. There are nematode blooms that come up through the Weep in a bad week and turn a whole terrace to slime. There is the Verger, a blind centipede as long as your forearm, who works Ladder Nine at a pace of about one terrace per day and is not fought, only announced.

And there are the needles.

Twice a cycle, sometimes more, the ceiling above the Pricked Quarter begins to admit them: ovipositors, hair-fine, a hand's length, coming through the wall from a world nobody inside can picture. They probe. They find a gall. They lay. The tenants wall the puncture with latex and abandon the room and everything in it, and the district has been shrinking for ninety years, and there is a guild whose entire function is to stand at the edge of it and count.

None of these creatures can hear. Not one. The deaf are the only citizens this place can keep; anything that arrives with ears goes into the void and dies of the noise, and the wall takes it, and thereafter the wall has a habit — a twitch in a certain rib at a certain hour, a shudder that runs the length of the Long Gallery. The tenants call these the leftovers. They are considered ancestors and are addressed politely.

Speech, therefore, is by contact. You put your body against a rib and stridulate, and the wall carries it, and the one you're speaking to feels it in their feet three terraces away. The air is not for talking. The air is for the tone.

---

Here is what the noise is for.

The chamber resonates. Wind across the Eye, the drum of the rain, the massed sawing of four thousand bodies keeping their shifts — all of it feeds a fundamental somewhere below hearing, with a stack of partials above it that make your eyes water and your teeth itch. And the tone is what holds the walls apart.

Stop it and the fruit ripens.

Ripening is not a metaphor here. Ripening is the end of the world: the flesh softens, the sweetness spikes, the wall slumps inward, the void closes, the city is digested into a single soft purple mass and something with ears comes and eats it. It has been about to happen for four hundred and eleven years. The Holders work in shifts of two hundred, in eight galleries, sustaining their partials; the Sweepers keep the drifts moving; the frogs keep the water; the couriers keep the pollen going down to the florets so that the seeds stay half-made and the fruit stays undecided. The whole deafening apparatus, every part of the roar, is labor. It is a choir keeping a fruit green.

The sweetness rises anyway. Measurably. A guild exists to measure it, by tongue, against a reference kept in a sealed gall, and their reports are the only documents in the city, and they are recorded as scars.

---

Because the place is old, the impossible here is *municipal*.

The interior is larger than the exterior. Everyone knows. There is a tax district that only exists on the fifth partial and is assessed accordingly. Walk the inner circumference widdershins and you will pass the Weep four times in a circuit that contains it once; the Sweepers use this to move the drifts efficiently and consider it uninteresting. Light comes through the flesh, amber-green and shadowless, ordinary daylight in quantity, and through the Eye in a white column that carries images of the outside — but late, and out of order, so that the shadow of a bird crosses the floor three days after the bird, and children are taught not to greet the shadows of things that haven't come yet.

And when a shift fails, there is the Slack.

The tone sags. The partials go out one by one, like lamps down a street. The chamber contracts — not much, a hand's breadth — and everything becomes nearer, and there are suddenly two of some things. Two Vergers on Ladder Nine. Two of a courier named Halla, both carrying the same pollen, both certain, both correct. The city goes absolutely still, which is the only silence anyone here has ever known, and it is unbearable; the deaf feel it as a pressure drop in the chest, as the wall ceasing to answer. Then the reserve gallery comes in, the fundamental climbs, the walls go back out to where they belong, and the duplicates are quietly reconciled by a guild that does not discuss its methods.

The pollen record shows the same year forty-one times. It has been worn smooth. There are grooves in it.

---

Go back out through the Eye if you can; almost nothing can. The foundresses who leave are the city's entire foreign policy: they crawl up through the Bitter Reach, dust themselves with pollen, squeeze through the scales into a world they will experience for eleven hours, and find another fruit on another low tree in another sink of black mud, and push in through its needle-hole, and shed their wings and antennae at the threshold, and start the whole thing again in a chamber that has never heard of the Hold.

Outside, the mud is quiet. The reeds are deaf. The fruit hangs at the height of your chest, green, ribbed, warm as a flank, small enough to cup in two hands, and out of a hole in it the width of a needle comes a sound like a valley being held open — four thousand lives, eight galleries, one endless bowing note, keeping tomorrow from arriving.

Put your palm flat against the skin. You can feel the shifts change.
