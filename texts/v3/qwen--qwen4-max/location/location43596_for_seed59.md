## The Kettle

From above there is nothing to see. The road comes over the shoulder of the mountain through wet laurel forest, through tree heather with its bark like shredded rope, past the rim gardens where the fog moves in the beds like something grazing, and then it stops at a notch in the rock and a low wall of piled tufa, and beyond that there is more forest. Cloud comes up the slope and goes over the notch and you stand in it. You would think you had arrived at nothing.

But you have been hearing the town for the last half hour. It comes up out of the ground: a sound like a market heard through a wall, like a river with people in it, ten thousand small conversations and a great deal of water. At the notch it resolves. Somebody below is beating bark cloth, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, and another somebody a long way off is answering it out of time, and under both of them is the noise of birds that does not stop, and under *that* is the low continuous chuckle of the swallow-hole taking the water away.

Then the mist thins for a moment and the forest in front of you turns out to have windows.

---

The Kettle is a collapsed sink in the limestone, about three hundred paces across the long way and ninety-odd deep, and it has been a town for something over six hundred years, though the shape of it is much older than that and will probably outlast it. The walls are grey and streaked black and hung with filmy ferns so thin you can read your hand through a frond. Springs come out of the rock at two heights, in a ragged line all the way round, and they do not fall so much as *lean* — sheeting down the wall in slow ropes, gathering in moss, dropping again. Everything weeps. The rock has been making itself into new rock for a very long time: the water here is heavy with lime, and where it runs it lays down tufa, pale and porous, over moss and roots and whatever else stays still. The terraces on the north side are tufa, built by the water and trimmed by hand, soft enough to cut with a bone saw when they are fresh, going hard and yellow in the air. There are baskets and bowls and a child's shoe up there in the drip-line, hung on strings a year ago, half stone now, glittering.

The floor is a marsh that never becomes a lake. Watercress, sedge, duckboards, black mud that smells of iron and cold tea. In the middle-south of it, a throat in the rock about two people wide, worn to a smooth spiral, and the whole bowl's water going down it in a braided rope with a sound like a crowd saying *shhh* forever. That is the Swallow. The town's entire existence is conditional on the Swallow.

Light is ordinary. That surprises people. The cloud belt sits above the rim more often than in it, so the bowl is bright — a bright, sourceless, wet green light, and then for about four hours around midday the sun comes over the south notch and lays a hard white bar across the terraces and the market and everybody's laundry, and the whole place steams and stinks and glitters and gets its work done. There is nothing uncanny about the light. The uncanny thing, if there is one, is how much of the town is alive and how little of it is dead.

---

Nothing structural is cut. This is the first law and the only one nobody argues about.

The houses are laurels. Not built *from* laurels — built *of* them, standing, rooted, drinking. Six hundred years ago somebody bent a sapling across a gap and tied it to another sapling and waited for them to fuse, and the town has been doing that ever since, in a slow argument with the forest that the forest is winning at the rate of about a hand's width a year. Trunks two and three people around rise out of the marsh and the terraces, and at the second storey they inarch into their neighbours, so that the whole bowl above the four-metre line is one continuous knuckled lattice, grey-barked, mossed, hung with lichen in beards. Within it: floors of split withy and packed root-plate, walls of woven hazel daubed with lime and clay, roofs of living laurel pleached flat and thick enough to shed a squall, and stairs that are roots persuaded into treads over four generations.

The handrails are polished to a shine like antler. The stone steps of the Weep Stair are dished in the middle by feet. And yet not one plank of the town is more than about nine years old, because everything rots, and everything is replaced, and the form does not change. It is the same town the way a river is the same river.

The Tiers' guild does nothing else. They walk the lattice all day with bundles of withy and a bone knife and a pot of grafting wax, tying, tightening, cutting back, opening a window where a limb has closed one, closing a window where a limb has opened one, and every so often voting to let a trunk die and starting the twelve-year business of growing its replacement across the gap. Cutting a structural limb without the guild's mark is the worst thing a person here can do, worse than theft, worse than violence. There is a woman on the third terrace who did it in a bad winter forty years ago and she is still, in a mild and permanent way, not forgiven.

Fire lives on the Shelf: a bare rock ledge on the east wall, scoured and swept, the only mineral ground in the Kettle. All cooking, all lime-burning, all smoking and rendering happens there, in stone hearths, under a woman with a stick who is not joking. Below the Shelf the town is white-washed and green and damp and cannot burn; above it, the smoke goes up past the drying racks and out over the notch and you smell it from the road, a mile off, mixed with everything else.

The floors are warm. This is the part visitors don't believe. Under the sleeping platforms are sealed pits of guano, leaf litter and fern brash, turned twice a season, and they sit at a steady blood heat and give it up through the boards for months. You sleep warm in a cold wet town on a bed of compost, in a quilt of pigeon down, and after a week you stop smelling it, and after a month you cannot imagine sleeping any other way.

---

The birds are the reason.

At dusk the oilbirds come out of the caves in the north wall. They have been in there all day, ten thousand of them, packed shoulder to shoulder on the ledges in the dark, and when they come it is not a flock, it is a *pour* — a sustained twenty-minute exhalation of fat brown birds with long wings and huge eyes, clacking. They navigate by sound, a hard rapid clicking like knuckles on a table, thousands of overlapping clickings that fill the bowl and bounce off the wet rock and make the air feel solid. They go up over the rim and out into the forest and they eat laurel fruit all night, forty square miles of laurel fruit, and they come home before dawn and shit it out on the cave floors.

That is the whole economy. The Kettle is a hole that concentrates the fertility of a mountain. Everything the birds bring in stays: the guano is shovelled by the cave crews into baskets, roped down, spread on the rim gardens and the marsh terraces, sold in sealed jars to the coast, used neat for ammonia in the dye pits and the tanning troughs. The chicks are taken at a fixed age from a fixed proportion of the ledges, by quota, by lot, and rendered in clay pots on the Shelf into a clear yellow oil that lights every lamp in the town, waterproofs every cloak, fries every eel, and is traded down the mountain in stoppered gourds for salt, for gritstone querns, for the little copper pins that hold the looms together, for grain that will not grow here.

By day it is the pigeons — big laurel pigeons, grey and wine-purple, that come in at fruiting in numbers that make the roofs white and the noise unbearable, and are netted from the upper platforms, and give meat and down and the leather-soft skins the children wear. By night, bats, in a different register, a dry flickering above the marsh where the midges are. In between: cave crickets in the guano in their millions, flies and their larvae harvested for duck feed, snails on every wet surface including your tools, including the inside of your kettle, farmed deliberately on the moss walls and eaten by the bowlful. Eels in the weep-channels, thick as your wrist, hand-caught under boards. Frogs in the marsh whose evening chorus is a physical pressure on the chest. Three kinds of lizard on the sunny tufa. Small hard dogs that hunt rats and are, by long custom, allowed everywhere.

Nothing here is enchanted. It is just an enormous amount of life stacked in a hole, and people who have worked out how to stand in the middle of it with a basket.

---

The town is dense the way the forest is dense — by layering. Ground: marsh, duckboard lanes, eel traps, the watercress beds, the wet market. Four to eight metres: the workshop storey, dark and warm and smelling of ammonia and lanolin, looms and dye pits and the beater benches, everyone working by lamplight at noon. Eight to fifteen: dwellings, near-touching, bridges of fused branch between them, laundry everywhere, children crossing at a run on routes their parents will not use. Above that: the drying racks, the netting platforms, the dovecotes bored into living trunks and healed around the holes, the lookouts, the fog. Above that: the rim, gardens in guano-black soil, pit kilns, the road, the world.

Six or seven hundred people. Forty thousand birds, on a slow day.

The wet market is the loud heart. Trestles and awnings on the duckboards, fish and eel and snails and fern-starch cakes and moss for packing and lime and oil and down and knotted cord, everyone shouting because everyone must, a hundred transactions a minute conducted at a volume that would be a riot anywhere else. Debt is kept in knotted cords in the guild houses; value is reckoned in weights of salt and hands of down; a bargain is made by shouting a number and then both parties spitting on their palms and slapping the trestle, which is a sound you hear all day like applause.

And there is a bell on the Shelf, and when it rings the market goes up the stairs.

The bowl floods. Not often catastrophically — three or four times a year the rain outruns the Swallow and the water comes up over the duckboards, a hand's width an hour, brown and quiet and absolutely unhurried. Everything below the flood line is built to be carried. Trestles fold, awnings roll, goods go into wicker hampers with rope handles, and the whole lower town is dismantled and stacked on the second terrace in about eleven minutes by people who have been drilled at it since they were four. Children race each other up the Weep Stair with hens. Old men stand in the rising water and criticise. Then everyone stands along the terraces in the rain and watches their market become a pond, and shouts advice at the pond, and after a day or two the pond goes down the throat and they carry it all back and set it up in the same places, which are marked, and have been marked for centuries.

Nothing below the line may be fixed. That is the second law. People have tried.

---

Sound is the medium here, the way light is the medium elsewhere. You learn the town by ear before you learn it by eye, and blind people move around it perfectly well.

There is the water: forty distinct weeps, each with its own note, and the channels under the floors, and the Swallow's endless *shhh*, which changes pitch when it is swallowing more, so that everyone in the Kettle knows the state of the drainage the way farmers know the sky. There are the beaters, four and a rest, keeping the hours; the looms; the dogs; the market; the pigeon shifting at dawn, which is like a hundred cloths shaken out at once; the oilbirds at dusk, which is like the rock itself talking in its sleep; the frogs; the rain arriving as a sound on the canopy a full ten seconds before the first drop gets through.

And once or most afternoons, for about a minute before a squall, the birds stop. All of them. And you hear the water alone, all forty weeps and the channels and the throat, and every person in the bowl looks up, and then it hits the leaves like gravel poured out of a sack and the noise closes over again and everyone goes back to shouting.

---

The year has a spike in it. At fruitfall the pigeons come in tens of thousands and the town goes briefly out of its mind: nets up on every platform, the roofs white, the smell of fat rendering on the Shelf, the caves crowded with crews working by lamplight in a din of clicking, the drying racks full, the paths slick, four days of a kind of festival where nobody sleeps properly and everybody is slightly rich. Then the birds thin out and there are five or six lean weeks of mending, dredging, grafting, turning the compost pits, salting the last of it, and the town goes quiet enough to hear individual conversations across the bowl, and finds it embarrassing.

The Throats go down the Swallow twice a year on ropes, with bone picks and tallow lamps, to cut the lime and the root-mat out of the throat before it narrows. It is the only job here that kills people regularly. There is a wall of names on the Shelf, cut into fresh tufa, which the water immediately begins to fill in, so that the older names are becoming stone and the newest are sharp, and the guild re-cuts them, and this is understood by everybody to be exactly the point.

At night the lamps come on all through the lattice, yellow and smoky, hundreds of them at different heights in the leaves, and the oilbird oil burns with a smell like warm fat and old fruit, and the clicking pours out of the north wall and over the rim, and the marsh mutters, and under the floors the compost goes on quietly rotting at blood heat, keeping everybody warm.

Nothing here is finished. Nothing here changes. The town has looked exactly like this since before anyone's grandmother, and every single thing in it will be different in nine years, and the water is still going down the throat.
