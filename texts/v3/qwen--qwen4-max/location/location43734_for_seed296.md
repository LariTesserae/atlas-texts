## The Sill

Begin with the ground, because everything here begins with the ground and then apologizes for it.

It is ironstone, iron-brown to liver-purple, sealed and glazed by a hundred million years of weather, and it rings under a boot heel like a struck plate. Not soil. A lid. In places the crust has been polished to a dull mirror by wind-blown dust; in others it is a conglomerate, and you can walk across the rounded quartz cobbles of a river that ran here before there was any *here* to run through — before the plain was lifted, before the country beneath it foundered and dropped away. The stones are set in the iron like eggs in a tray. They have not moved since. They will not move again.

Every few paces the crust is dished. Pan-holes, saucer-wide, some deep enough to sit in, each holding a finger's depth of water that has nowhere to go. They deepen by a fingernail's width in a century. They fill at night and empty by afternoon and fill again, and around each rim is a ruff of grey lichen that has been eating the same square metre of rock since before anyone thought to write anything down.

The plain runs out flat in every direction to a haze the colour of weak tea. There is no slope worth the name — one part in nine thousand, and the drainage has forgotten which way that is. Water beads, spreads, wanders, doubles back. There is a river, the Slow, that takes eight days to cross the town and can be pushed backwards for an afternoon by a steady wind. Mostly there is no river at all. The rain that falls goes into the pans, or into the mats, or straight down.

Down is the important direction.

## Under-light

The Sill stands so high that the weather has been left beneath it. Out past the rim — thirty kilometres off, a smudge you can only find when the haze thins — the cloud sea lies a few hundred metres below the plain and stretches to the edge of the world, and it is *bright*. Sunlit. Blindingly, uselessly bright, out there where nothing lives.

That is where the light comes from. Not from above: the sky overhead is a permanent lid of dust and salt lifted off the foundered lowlands, an old copper coin of a sun somewhere in it. The usable light arrives sideways off the cloud floor and scatters upward, so the town is lit from underneath. Shadows fall up. The undersides of eaves, of leaves, of a lifted chin, are the bright parts. Faces are read by the glow off the pavement. Painters here have no vocabulary for a top-lit sky and find the idea sentimental.

Noon is the dullest hour. At dawn and at dusk the deck rises, comes shouldering up over the rim in slow white ranks, and for forty minutes the streets are flooded from below with a light like the inside of a shell, and everyone stops what they are doing without admitting they have stopped.

It is cool. Six degrees at the turning, eleven at midday, a wet cold that lives in the sleeves of a coat. Frost is rare and is a disaster when it comes: the air-plants have no defence against it, having never needed one, and a hard frost year is remembered the way other places remember fires.

## Wet underneath

Nothing here is wet on top.

The fog does not fall. It arrives from below, drawn up through the sinks and the fissures, and it condenses on the *undersides* of things — on the bottoms of railings, the bellies of bridges, the lower surfaces of leaves. Dust settles on the upper surfaces and stays there, dry as flour, undisturbed for weeks. Turn a stone and it is streaming. Walk under a walkway and you get rained on from a clear sky.

So the architecture is inverted. Gutters run along the soffits. Eaves are cupped upward at their lower edge and channel inward. Every handrail has a drinking-lip on its underside, and every drinking-lip has a moss beard, and the moss beards are harvested. Children are taught to look up for weather. The word for *dry* also means *exposed*, and the word for *sheltered* also means *green*.

The whole plain, seen from an Elder at the turning, changes colour twice a day. The cushion-plants and the resurrection mats lie clenched and grey-brown through the dry afternoon, folded tight as fists; when the fog comes up they unroll — millions of them, over about twenty minutes — and the Sill goes from ash to a deep wet green with a sound like a held breath let out through cloth. They call it the green hour. It is the loudest thing the open plain ever does, and it is barely a sound at all.

## The throats

The plain is pierced.

Sinkholes — throats — from eighty to four hundred metres across, twenty to ninety deep, in chains that follow the old fracture lines like beads on a dropped string. From a distance the country reads as empty. Walk it for an hour and you fall into a forest.

Because everything the plain lacks collects in the throats: dust, water, warmth, stillness, the silt and bones of the low world. The walls are white with lichen and bounce the under-light down. Thirty metres below the pavement there are tree-ferns with trunks like wet rope and crowns the size of rooms, and filmy ferns one cell thick growing in the spray of the condensate lines, and liverworts, and rosette plants holding litres of black water in their throats, and above them a canopy that comes up exactly to street level, so that you descend a stair into the treetops and keep going down into the shade. The layers are packed so tight that a person moving through the mid-storey does it by tunnels, and the tunnels are maintained, and there are people whose entire trade is keeping them open.

The open flat has almost nothing: mats, cushions, hair-lichen on the combs, and a great deal of bare ringing stone. The lushness is all in the holes. So is the city.

## Comb

The town is called Comb, and it is a ring-town, or rather a chain of ring-towns, each wound around a throat in terraces, connected across the flat by causeways and pipe-bridges and long ranks of harvesting mesh. Eleven throats are settled. Four are works. Two are sealed and forbidden. There are said to be more out east that nobody has sounded.

On the flat stand the fog-combs: vertical harps of mesh, twelve metres tall, in ranks kilometres long, aligned to the evening draw. A good comb gives eight litres per square metre on a wet night and nothing at all on a dry one, and the difference between those two numbers is the entire political history of the place. The condensate runs down the lee-side gutters into covered mains. The combs hiss faintly. Fog here carries charge, and the comb-tips bleed it to ground through copper straps, and at dusk, when the deck rises and the field reverses, the tips wear a low blue corona along their whole length — a cold, thin, crawling light, ten thousand kilometres of it, and the smell of ozone down the wind, and the hair on your arms lifting.

That is the brightest the Sill gets, and it comes from the town's own machinery.

All the harvested water is dropped. Every throat has a swallow-hole at its floor, and the swallow-holes go down into a karst system nobody has ever mapped, and the water falls sixty, ninety, two hundred metres into the dark and turns wheels on the way. That is the power. Not steam, not coal — there is nothing to burn — but air-water and gravity and the void. Deep in the rock the arc furnaces run, fixing nitrogen out of the fog to feed the gardens; the town makes its soil out of weather and lightning. There are reduction works down there, and carbide, and the bright trade in metal, and ore hauled up the escarpment stair on the backs of the freight guild at a cost that would make a lowlander weep.

You do not hear the furnaces. You feel them in the soles of your feet, a hum at the very bottom of hearing, and the tea in a cup shivers, and that is how you know the works are drawing.

## What lives here

Almost nothing, and it is all import.

Walk a day on the flat and you will see no animal. There is no birdsong; there is nothing for a bird to do. The fog-crickets stridulate for perhaps four minutes at the turning and then are done. There are mites in the mats, and a pale slug that grazes the wet undersides at night and is the reason the moss beards must be scraped, and in the sump pools a small lung-fish that seals itself in a mud cell through the dry and comes back out when the water returns, sometimes after two years.

The rest arrives.

The dust arrives, off the lowlands, a millimetre a century, and it is the only new soil. With it come seeds, spores, salts. And with the wind come the shearwaters of the Under — birds that live their whole lives in the cloud sea below, sleep on the wing, never land, and are carried up over the rim only when they die. Their bodies fall on the plain. There is a licensed trade in sky-fall: collectors with carts, bone-meal mills, a smell on the east side of town in the dust season. The Sill imports its dead.

And once a year, in the wet weeks, the eels climb.

They come up out of the foundered country, up two thousand metres of streaming escarpment, through the fissures, into the swallow-holes, and they arrive in the throat-sumps in numbers that turn the water black — thin, then fat, wringing up through the rock in the dark. For nine days the town does nothing else. The terraces are crammed. Weirs go across the sumps; the shearwaters, the living ones, come up the face at dusk for the first time anyone sees them upright; the pipe-cats get loose and go feral in the fern tunnels and are hunted by children with lamps. And the eels bring the lowlands with them: silt, seeds, parasites, a season's worth of nitrogen in their bodies. The gardens are fed on eel-mash for a year. Then it stops, and the throats go quiet, and the town spends eleven months being a machine again.

## Listening

Sound does not carry on the flat. It goes up into thin air and fog and does not come back; there is no echo anywhere, no reverberation, nothing to bounce. Two people ten metres apart on the open crust speak as if through a wall. So the flat is silent, and the town signals with lamps and semaphore and coded pulses down the pressure mains, and the throats — the throats are the only resonant places in the world. Sound pools in them like water. A voice at the bottom of a throat can be heard clean at the top; a shout on the pavement above it goes nowhere. Everything that needs to be said is said down a hole. The markets are down there, the arguments, the music, the courts.

Which is why the characteristic sound of Comb is a single knock.

The crust is a roof. Beneath it the karst is hollowing, and has been hollowing since before the plain was lifted, and the water the town drops for power dissolves more of it every hour. The town is powered by its own undermining. Everyone knows this. It is not a secret; it is a schedule.

So there are proppers. They walk the flat with a hammer and strike the ironstone and listen: a bright note is thick rock, a dull note is a thin place. They log it. They chart it. Down in the listening rooms, in the deep terraces, women sit with their ears against the stone through brass stethoscopes and mark the slow grinding of a roof giving way, twenty metres off, a millimetre a month. The sound-charts are the property deeds. The most expensive houses in Comb are thirty metres down under twelve metres of proven rock; the poor live up on the lid, in the dust and the wind and the corona, on ground nobody has sounded, and pay a roof-rent to a guild that has the hammer.

Every few years a street goes down. There is a bell for it, rung once, and then everyone waits for the second bell, which means the count is finished, and which sometimes does not come.

## The turning, and after

At dusk the deck comes up. The plain exhales through ten thousand fissures — a real, measurable outward breath, driven by the day's heating of the low country — and the comb-tips light blue, and the mats unroll, and the whole surface of the Sill goes green in twenty minutes with that soft cloth sound. Then the pressure reverses, the town inhales, the turbines in the throats spin up on the draw, and the fog goes down into the streets and the fern tunnels and the lungs of everybody standing outside.

Then the deck sinks away below the rim, and the under-light dies, and the plain is left in a darkness that is not quite dark, because the low weather is having its storms.

They happen beneath. Lightning goes off inside the cloud sea, hundreds of kilometres of it, silent, sheet after sheet, lighting the floor of the world from within so that the whole horizon pulses like something breathing in its sleep. No thunder ever reaches the Sill through the air. It arrives through the rock — a pressure pulse, felt in the listening rooms, logged by women with their ears to the stone who can tell you, from the character of it, how far below the storm is walking.

Above, the sky is haze and a few hard stars. The Elders stand out on the flat, the last three hills of a range that was worn to nothing before it was ever raised — rounded stubs of the oldest rock in the world, wrapped to their shoulders in combs, protected by law, measured every decade by a small permanent office whose entire function is to record how much of them is left.

And in the throats, thirty metres down, the rotten fern trunks glow faintly with foxfire, and the eel-sumps tick as they cool, and the town keeps its own small green dawn under a lid of iron, in a place where nothing is made of the place: where the water comes from the air, the soil comes from the wind, the light comes from below, the dead come from elsewhere, and the only thing the ground itself contributes is the sound of a hammer, once, somewhere out in the dark, finding a thin spot.
