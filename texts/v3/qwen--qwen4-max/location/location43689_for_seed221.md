## The Pier Void

You find it by sound, and then you lose the sound, and then you find the plate.

Up at the fourth course of the massif — where the poured stone is still tool-marked, where the air is thin enough that a shout goes out and does not come back — there is a service gallery between the condenser stacks, and in the floor of that gallery there is a manway the size of a coal scuttle. The plate over it is bright. No scratches around the screw heads. No paint, no rust, no fingerprint. It says VOID — NOT A SPACE — DO NOT OCCUPY, and the letters are raised, because they were cast with the plate, and the plate was cast with the pier.

The massif is not a mountain that was built into. It is a mountain that was built. Course upon course of poured rock, laid up like masonry, cooling in forms, struck from the forms, stacked. Nothing here was ever quarried. There is no natural substrate under any of it; if you drilled to the bottom you would find the founding slab and under that the same stuff, made, all the way down. And in the load path of the fourth course, where the whole upper works bears, the drawings required a void of a particular volume. So there is a void. It is eleven paces by seven. The ceiling is low enough, at the edges, to touch with a flat hand.

Somebody, a long time ago, decided that a void of that size should not be wasted.

---

Open the plate and the sound comes up the shaft like something climbing.

It is not a roar. A roar has weather in it, gusts and lulls. This has no weather. It is a floor-to-ceiling wall of noise, absolutely steady, and it does not change when you put your head into it, and it does not change when you take your head out. It is loud the way a river is wet. Down the shaft, in the dark, your teeth begin to ache before your ears do.

Inside, the room is dim and silver and nothing in it is dark. There is one lamp behind wire glass, a low industrial filament the colour of weak tea, and there is the dull orange line of the natal seam along the north wall, and that is all the light there is. But everything is wet, and the wet takes that little light and hands it around the room, wall to floor to ceiling to wall, so that the whole chamber glows faintly from every direction at once, like the inside of a shell. You can read the casting numbers on the ceiling. You cannot find a shadow to stand in.

The air is at four-tenths of a degree. It is saturated to the last molecule it can hold. Fog stands in the room — not drifting, not curling, *standing*, in fixed lobed masses like the gills of something enormous, ridged and motionless, held in shape by the sound field. You can put your arm through a lobe and the fog closes behind it exactly, without swirling, as if you had passed your hand through a solid that agreed to be a solid again.

Your breath is invisible. The air is already precisely as wet as you are.

---

The walls are new.

That is the first wrongness and the deepest one. The stone is raw — sharp-grained, freshly struck, still bearing the seam-lines of the forms and the faint bloom of release agent, the texture of rock broken ten seconds ago. There is no patina. There is no rust on the brackets, no polish on the rung of the ladder, no wear at the threshold where a century of boots should have scooped a hollow. There is no dust. Dust is stone that has come loose, and nothing here has come loose.

The water cannot take any more of the wall into itself. It is fully saturated — every drop already carries all the mineral it can hold at this temperature, and so it weeps down the fresh stone forever without dissolving a grain of it. The room weeps and is not worn. It has been one instant old since the pour.

Along the floor there are dimples under each drip-point, shallow basins exactly the shape a hundred years of falling water would make. They were cast that way. The wear was calculated in advance and put into the mould, and then the wear itself was forbidden. Erosion here is not absent; it is *designed*, drawn, dimensioned, and held permanently at the moment before it begins.

---

Now the walls resolve, and you see that they are not walls.

They are chits.

A chit is about the size of a thumbnail: pale, wet, eyeless, six-legged, shelled in something between horn and pearl. There are six hundred and eleven million, two hundred and four thousand, eight hundred and eighty of them in this room, and there have been that many since the pour. They cover every surface in layers four to nine deep, near-touching, packed in the hexagonal way that things pack when they have no room to be individual. They fill the sump in a slow pale porridge. They roof the ceiling in a single continuous crust. They hang in the fog-lobes in fixed strata. On the raised letters of the casting numbers they are packed so thick that the numbers are legible only as differences in texture.

Each one bears, on the rear of its carapace, a small raised serial. It is the same serial. All of them. There is one animal in this room. It has been repeated until it fills a volume.

And every one of them is shivering.

That is the sound. Six hundred million bodies vibrating their flight muscles against their shells at a frequency the room was tuned to carry, a fine hard granular hiss with a floor-tone under it like a mill full of gravel, and above it the drip, and above that the thin steam-whistle of the natal seam. It is deafening in a way that stops being hearing. Your vision blurs at the loud places. Your sinuses buzz. You weep, and the tears are the same temperature as the room, and they do not feel like yours.

The shivering is the furnace. It is the only heat in the pier. It holds the water at four-tenths of a degree, two-fifths of a degree above the point at which the whole chamber would flash to ice and the load path would crack and the upper works would come down. Six hundred million small cold animals keeping a mountain from freezing, by shaking.

Their shells are the condensing surfaces. The made water — burnt in the founding furnaces from hydrogen and oxygen, water that has never been rain, never been in a river, younger than the room — condenses on their backs, runs off them, falls in a fixed lattice of drip-points onto the cast dimples, and turns a wheel the size of a dinner plate. The wheel drives a shaft. The shaft goes up through the ceiling into the works. The whole output of the chamber is eleven kilowatts and a number.

---

The sound is a standing wave, so the room has quiet in it.

There are lanes — vertical stripes a hand's width across, running floor to ceiling at intervals the mould-makers computed — where the pressure cancels and the noise falls away to almost nothing. Bare stone shows in those lanes. No chit will live there; they crowd the antinodes and leave the nodes empty, so the walls are barcoded, loud stripe, quiet stripe, loud stripe, and you can move your head the width of a fist and go from a mill-race to a church.

Nine things walk the quiet lanes.

They are long and pale and slow, blind, high-shouldered, with legs like calipers, and the workers who have never come here call them auditors. They pace the silent stripes end to end and they eat the surplus. Exactly the surplus. The natal seam extrudes new chits, wet and already shivering, at the same rate the return grate draws off the dead, and the auditors take the difference, and the difference is zero, and they do it anyway, all day, forever, at a walking pace. They are the only motion in the room slow enough for your eye to follow. Everything else is a blur or a statue. The auditors are the clock, and the clock tells nothing, because they are always somewhere they have already been.

In the quiet lanes, if you kneel and put your ear close, you can hear a single chit. It makes a small sound, a tick with a question in it. You will not hear it anywhere else in the room. There are perhaps forty chits in the whole chamber that have ever heard another one.

---

Vegetation: a green wireframe.

Something like a lichen lives on the release agent that beads out of the casting seams, and only there, and only at the rate the seams exude. So it grows in thin lines along every mould-joint in the chamber — the vertical parting lines, the horizontal course marks, the rectangular outlines of the core-boxes — and nowhere else. It draws the blueprint of the room in faint green on the walls, floor and ceiling, a diagram of how this space was made, alive, eleven blades' worth of thickness, never advancing a millimetre past the seam. In the dim silver light it looks like the drawing the room was built from, left hanging inside it.

---

The rules are short and they are structural, not moral.

Nothing may be added to the void and nothing may be taken out of it. Not a chit, not a drop, not a scraping of lichen, not the plate's screws. The volume is load-bearing. The upper works stand on this exact quantity of nothing, and the machine inside it exists to keep the nothing from freezing, collapsing, silting, or in any way becoming a different nothing. The census is 611,204,880. It is not a target. It is a dimension, like the width of the room.

There is no shift change here, no day, no season, no accumulation. No sediment in the sump; the return grate takes it. No stalactite; the water is saturated. No history; there is nothing in the room that records the passage of anything, because recording requires wear, and wear is the one process the chamber was built to refuse.

If you stay a while, the auditors will come and stand at the edges of their quiet lanes and turn their blind heads toward you, because you are the only gradient in the room — the only warm thing, the only thing that is changing — and they will wait, patient as calipers, for you to stop.

Then you climb out, and put the bright plate back, and tighten the unmarked screws, and the sound goes on being exactly as loud as it was, and above you four thousand courses of made stone rest on six hundred million small animals shaking in the dark, and the mountain does not move, and the room is one instant old.
