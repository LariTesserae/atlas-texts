## The Sill

Begin at your feet, because there is nothing else to begin at. There is no soil. There has not been soil here for a very long time.

What you stand on is alive: a blue-green rind of small waxy leaves laid edge to edge like scale armour, tight enough to walk on, giving perhaps a finger's depth under a boot before it takes your weight and holds. Beneath the leaves is bast, and beneath the bast is lignin, and beneath the lignin is more of the same, down forty metres in the low places, down to a bedrock so old and so worn that the roots have polished it. It is all one plant. Not a forest of individuals — one individual, clonal, continuous, running unbroken from the eastern scarp to the western, a hundred and sixty kilometres of the same organism arguing quietly with itself about where to put a pillar.

Call it the Stand. The gaugers call it the Holdfast when they are being formal and *the floor* when they are not.

Your footprint stays for about an hour. Then the leaves lift, the crushed cells refill, and the place where you stood is indistinguishable from the place where you did not. There is no dust on the Sill. There is no litter. A dropped thing is either taken in within a season or it lies there forever, unchanged, because nothing here rots on its own account — decay is a service the Stand performs, selectively, on request, and it does not perform it for iron or for ceramic or for the grey fired-clay tally tokens that turn up in the crevices, tens of thousands of them, worn smooth, unreadable.

## Air and height

The Sill sits high. Not peak-high — there are no peaks; the mountains that made this table were ground down before the Stand existed, and what lifted afterward was a flatness, an old sea-floor of a surface, planed and then raised and then left. The horizon is a ruled line with three or four rounded tors standing on it like knuckles on a hand laid palm-down. Everything else is level to within a few metres over a distance you cannot walk in a week.

The air is thin and clean and cold in the way that a stone room is cold. Ten degrees at midday, frost most nights, a rime that furs the leaf-edges white until nine in the morning and then goes back into the plant without ever having been liquid at all. Light here is ordinary — plain daylight, high cloud burning off by ten, real shadows with real edges, the sun a white disc you can look at briefly through the haze. Nothing about the light is strange. This is worth saying early, because a great deal else is, and the light's indifference is what makes the rest bearable.

It rains every third day, roughly, in a fine driving drizzle that comes from the east and lasts forty minutes. The rest of the water arrives as fog. Fog is the economy.

## The fog harvest

Every few kilometres, standing alone on the empty green, there is a comb: a frame of wood eight metres tall and thirty wide, strung with fibre finer than hair, oriented into the prevailing drift. Fog comes over the scarp at dawn in a slow white wall and combs itself apart on the strings. The droplets run down into a gutter, the gutter runs into a throat, the throat goes down into the Stand's own cisterns — swollen lignified columns, some of them the girth of a house, that stand around in the flatness like the piers of a bridge whose deck was never built.

There are perhaps four hundred combs on the Sill. There used to be more. You can find the sockets of the missing ones, healed over, a whorl in the leaf-grain like the knot in a plank.

Water here is not a substance so much as a tension. The Stand holds its water the way a tall tree does, under suction, columns of it stretched thin and taut from root to leaf, and when a column snaps — when the pull exceeds what the water can bear — it cavitates, and it *clicks*. On a dry afternoon the whole plain clicks. Multiply one small snapping sound by a hundred and sixty kilometres of continuous tissue and you get a sound like frying, like distant small-arms fire, like rain on a tin roof falling out of a cloudless sky. It rises through the afternoon as the tension rises. It falls off at dusk. The gaugers read thirst off it the way you'd read a barometer, and when the clicking drops in pitch they go and open valves, because a low pop means a wide column failing, and a wide column failing means a district going brown.

That is the loudest continuous sound on the Sill and it is made by nothing moving.

## The Works

Now the other loudness.

The Stand grows the city. Not metaphorically. Somewhere very far back it was trained, or it learned, or it ate a town and kept the shapes — the account is lost and the gaugers have four incompatible versions, all of them sung — and now it produces architecture as a growth form, the way a rose produces a hip. It puts up walls of dense pale heartwood, sanded smooth by their own growing. It puts up flues. It puts up gantries and stair-towers and long sheds with the ribs on the inside, and it puts them up in *the same configuration*, over and over, because it is a clone and it has one idea.

The configuration is a pipe foundry.

There are, at the last careful count, two hundred and eleven of them on the Sill, spaced across the plain at intervals of eight to fourteen kilometres, and they are identical. Not similar. Identical, down to the crook in the third lamp-standard on the north side, down to the place where the handrail dips because the template carries a memory of a repair made by somebody ten thousand years dead. Each has the same eleven sheds, the same yard, the same settling basin, the same six-storey boring tower with the same crack in the same lintel that the Stand faithfully regrows because the crack is part of the pattern now.

Inside, the work goes on. Heartwood is cut from the Stand's own sacrificial pillars — it grows them for this, thick and straight and hollow-hearted, on a forty-year cycle — and it is bored into pipe, and the pipe is fitted with wooden collars and iron bolts, and the pipe goes out into the plain to carry fog-water from the combs to the cisterns to the districts to the Stand.

The Stand drinks what the foundries deliver. The foundries are made of the Stand. The pipe carries the water that grows the wood that becomes the pipe.

There is no output. There has been no output for longer than anyone can document. The network extends past the eastern scarp and comes back, or seems to; surveying parties have followed a single line of six-inch main for eleven days and found themselves at their own settling basin, and this may be a loop or it may be a copy, and the difference has stopped mattering to anyone but the archivists, who are themselves copies, in a sense, since each district grows one and each one knows the same songs.

The noise: wooden gear trains the size of millwheels, clacking. Reciprocating bast-belt drives slapping their idlers. Boring heads chewing heartwood with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, forever. Pressure accumulators — great sap-charged bladders in the tower bases — thumping at forty beats a minute, all of them, everywhere, out of phase, so that the plain has a heartbeat with no single heart. Vent stacks letting off steam with a shriek tuned by three thousand years of nobody adjusting it. And under all of it, the transpiration roar, which is the sound of a hundred and sixty kilometres of leaves pulling water, and which resembles nothing so much as a motorway heard from a hillside at night.

You can hear the Sill from the scarp road, two hours before you can see it, and what you hear sounds exactly like industry, and it is, and it is also a plant being thirsty.

## Iron, and the rules

There is no ore. There is no exposed rock younger than the tors, and the tors are exhausted. Every bolt on the Sill has been re-cut so many times that the threads are shallow and the heads are round, and a bolt that fails is not replaced but *reduced* — turned down to the next size down, and the hole bushed with ceramic, and the ceramic comes from the one clay pocket in the northeast, which is nine metres deep and has been nine metres deep for a very long time because the Stand refills it with its own fine waste.

So: iron is finite and countable. Every gauger keeps a tally. The number goes down.

Electricity is tannin and iron and wet fibre — long racks of galvanic cells in the shed basements, sluggish, brown, smelling of vinegar and ink, giving a current that will run a lamp and a small motor and nothing else. The lamps are amber. They come on at dusk in every district at once, or rather they come on in a wave, because the districts are copies but the sun is not, and the wave crossing the plain at four metres a second is one of the few things here that behaves the way a thing should.

Rules, then, plainly, since the place has them:

Nothing dies of itself. Cut a beam and it stays cut. Kill a leaf and it stays brown until the Stand chooses to take it back. There is no rot, no rust worth the name in this dry cold, no decay at all except the deliberate kind. Consequence: the past does not disappear. It accumulates in place.

Everything fits everywhere. A collar from Foundry Nine seats on a main from Foundry Two Hundred with no fettling. Consequence: repair is trivial and invention is impossible. Nobody has made a new thing on the Sill in recorded memory. They have made the same thing very well, a great many times.

Sound runs faster in wood than in air, and everything here is wood, and everything is joined. So the knock reaches you through the floor before it reaches you through the window. You learn to hear a half-second of future. You learn to flinch early. The gaugers' whole language is pipe-knock, and it carries the length of the plain in about ninety seconds, and by the time you hear a message in the air it has already been answered underground.

## Emptiness

Here is the paradox that visitors never resolve: the Sill is entirely alive and almost entirely empty.

Total vegetation, and no thickets. Total construction, and no crowds. Between one foundry and the next there is eight or twelve kilometres of unbroken leaf-floor with three combs, a cistern, a lamp, and a line of pipe running along low wooden trestles at knee height, and nothing else at all. The foundry yards could hold a town and hold instead one gauger, a hand-cart, and a stack.

The gaugers live one to a district. Perhaps two hundred people on a plain the size of a small country. They walk their mains, they read the cisterns with a weighted line, they listen to the pitch of the cavitation, they open and close valves in a sequence they were taught and cannot justify, and they knock. They see each other four times a year at the joint stations, and they are not lonely in the way you'd expect, because the plain talks all night and it is difficult to feel alone inside a noise.

Fauna: nearly nothing. No birds — the air is too thin and there is nothing above the leaf-line to perch on. Insects reduced to one small pale moth that works the fog-hours and pollinates a flower the Stand puts up once every eleven years, in a fortnight of white across the whole plain, all of it at once, and then a smell like bread going over. In the sumps, a blind flat worm that eats the galvanic sludge. That is the honest list.

But there are the other things. The Stand remembers animals it used to have, and it grows them: brown shapes that come out of a bud at the base of a cistern, that unfold into something with wings and a beak and the right number of joints, that fly a circuit of the yard for a day and a half, pollinating nothing, eating nothing, and then come apart into fibre. They are not animals. They are the plant's memory of animals, rendered in the materials at hand. They get the wings slightly wrong. They are getting worse.

## Where it comes apart

The copies are the wound in things.

Walk east from any foundry with a compass and a tally and you will, in ten or eleven kilometres, arrive at your foundry. Not a foundry like it. Yours — your cart, your chalk mark on the basin, your tea cup, still warm, though you carried it with you and it is in your pack, and it is also on the sill of the window you are looking through, and both are warm.

The gaugers do not walk east with a compass any more. They walk their pipe. The pipe is the only reliable object on the Sill because it is *serial*: each length is stamped, and the stamps run in order, and if you follow the numbers you go somewhere, and if you leave the numbers you go nowhere in particular and arrive at yourself. Children are taught the sequence before they are taught their names. There are lengths out there stamped with numbers that do not fit the sequence, and the instruction regarding those is: do not follow, do not touch, knock three times and walk back the way you came.

Other failures, small and constant, worn smooth by familiarity:

Frost forms the same fern on the same pane every morning, in every district, including panes installed last year. Not a similar fern. The same one, with the same broken pinna.

The rain sometimes falls into the cisterns from below on the third day, and nobody remarks on it, because the water level is correct afterwards and the level is the thing that matters.

In the low ground where the fog pools, an afternoon can persist. Gaugers who work the northeast clay pocket come back with stories of a shift that lasted a shift and a half and a sun that stood at four o'clock for nine hours, and they are believed, and the hours are logged, and the log has a column for it.

And the horizon. On a clear evening the air is so clean and the plain so flat that you can see the smoke of a foundry a hundred kilometres off, standing up white and thin, and if you look at it long enough with a glass you can see that it is your smoke, and that the figure on the tower platform is you, doing what you are doing, at the moment you are doing it. There is no lag. That is the part the gaugers cannot get past. There is no lag at all.

## Evening

The light goes long and ordinary across the leaf-floor. The rime starts forming at the eastern scarp before the sun is down. The transpiration roar thins as the leaves shut, and the clicking drops away, and for about twenty minutes the Sill is quiet enough to hear a single moth.

Then the shift-knock.

It starts at the joint station in the centre — a wooden maul on an iron plate, four strikes — and it goes out through the mains in every direction at the speed of sound in wet heartwood, which is fast, and it arrives at all two hundred and eleven foundries within a minute and a half of each other, and every gauger answers on their own pipe, and the answers come back and cross and pass through one another in the wood without interference, so that the floor under your boots carries, for about four minutes, the entire conversation of a country, in a language of taps, saying nothing that has not been said before.

The amber lamps come on in their slow wave. The accumulators go on thumping. Somewhere out past the combs a length of pipe stamped with an impossible number lies in the leaf-grain being slowly taken in.

And you hear the maul strike — you hear it clearly, four times, through the ground and then through the air — and only afterward, a long way off across the flat green, do you see the tiny figure on the central platform raise the hammer and bring it down.
