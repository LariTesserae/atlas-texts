Nothing here is solid enough to be called ground, and almost all of it is alive.

Start with the note. It is under everything, a pressure rather than a pitch, felt in the sternum and the back teeth before the ear agrees to it: the fundamental of a hollow cable four kilometers long, open at both ends, with cold thin air sliding through it. Below hearing. It never stops. It changes key twice a day when the fog tide turns and the air reverses direction in the trunk-lines, and everyone who lives here knows the change the way a fish knows the tide, without ever once being able to describe it.

Above that drone, the audible ranks: the secondaries whistling in thirds, the tertiaries hissing, the fine filaments giving off a thin continuous shimmer like sand poured on a drumhead. Ten million hollow pipes of graded bore, none of them tuned by anyone, all of them tuned by growth. The lattice is an instrument the size of a country and it plays itself badly, gloriously, without pause.

**The weft**

It is one organism. It has been one organism for about two thousand three hundred sound-seasons, which is what the rings at the oldest anchor count, and it spans the basin from the eastern scarp to the salt flats three thousand kilometers west, and it is, by volume, almost nothing at all.

That is the first thing to understand and the hardest to hold. Take a cube of this place a kilometer on a side. It contains a few thousand tonnes of living tissue and the better part of a million tonnes of cold air. The plant is not a mass. It is a *diagram* of a mass — cables as thick as a longhouse slung between peaks, struts branching from them, ribs, whiskers, and only here and there a membrane: a fog-net, a sail, the lipped basin of a cistern. Between one strut and the next: two hundred meters of nothing. Between one cable and the next: a valley you could drop a mountain into. From below, on the rock floor, the whole world-spanning organism looks like a few threads scratched across the stars.

It is black-green outside, wet, rubbery, furred with rime; the bark is wound in opposing helices like laid rope, and when you cut it the fibers unlay with a slow creak. Inside, the cambium is pale as a peeled parsnip and warm and sweet, and it smells of sugared turnip and cold iron. Every trunk-line is hollow and septate, divided by bulkheads with valved holes, so that a cable is also a lung, a flue, a pipe, a corridor, and a throat.

It does not eat light. There is no light worth eating. It eats stone.

The mountains here are young — raw, unrounded, freshly shoved up, their scree still standing at angles no old range could hold, their fracture faces bright and unweathered. The plant sends a wedge into a crack, thickens the wedge, and splits the rock, and then eats the new surface: the sulfides, the reduced iron, the phosphorus still locked in fresh apatite. It needs *sharpness*. Old weathered stone is a plate with nothing on it. So the organism quarries its own food, prying the range apart a few centimeters a year, and the sound of that — the long grinding tick, then the crack, then the several-minute roar of a face letting go and going down four kilometers to the floor — is the second-loudest thing in the world.

The whole basin is a mouth working on a bone.

**Dark, and the little lights**

There is a day, technically. For about two hours a grey rumor gathers along the eastern scarp, never climbs, and gives up. It is enough to show you the far cables as absences against slightly less absence. Then it is stars again — hard, unwinking, too many, the air too thin to soften them — and the aurora, which comes without schedule and lays green sheets down the sky and makes every animal in the weft stop moving for as long as it lasts.

Otherwise the light is made locally. The cambium glows a faint blue-green where it is cut or bruised or working hard, so a freshly split anchor smolders like a rotten log for a week. The warm nodes breathe a dim orange from their compost interiors. There are lures, and lamps, and the cold sparks of things rubbing themselves against a strut to advertise. Nothing here casts a shadow worth the name. You see the way you see the inside of a cathedral at midnight: by accumulations, by hints, by knowing where the pillars ought to be.

**Water, which is only ever caught**

There is no lake. There is no river anywhere above the floor. All the water in the weft has been *caught*, and it is caught in the same way every time: fog rises off the rock floor and off the plant's own transpiration, climbs, meets a strut cold enough to wring it, and runs down the helical bark in a bead, and the bead finds a lip, and the lip is a cistern.

Cisterns are leaf-basins in the crotches of the great cables, some the size of a bathtub, some the size of a village square, all of them perched kilometers apart in three dimensions, all of them freezing at the rim and staying liquid underneath. Each is an island. Each has its own endemic fauna — blind shrimp, a flatworm found nowhere else, a filter-feeder with a fringe like a eyelash — separated from its nearest relative by two kilometers of lethal air, its eggs carried between pools on the feet of grazers or rafted on falling ice. Ten thousand ponds, none of them aware of the others, hung in the dark.

And each cistern has a siphon. The plant grows the siphon: an outflow tube folded back on itself, so that when the water reaches the bend the whole basin empties at once, in a roaring gulp that lasts forty seconds and can be heard for thirty kilometers, and then refills over the next nine hours, and does it again.

This is the clock. The basin is a polyrhythm of ten thousand siphons at ten thousand periods, and the people here do not count hours, they count *which water is speaking*. A place is named for its gulp. A meeting is set for the third silence after the Long Gulp. Somewhere out west there is a cistern that has been failing for two centuries and now speaks only every eleven days, and there are three settlements that have quietly died of the change.

**Warmth is a place, not a season**

Ambient is fifteen below, and the rime grows a hand's width a night on the windward side of everything. But the nodes — the swollen junctions where a dozen cables meet — are composting engines, and inside them it is eight above, sometimes twenty, humid, ammoniacal, stinking of rot and sugar and animals. The nodes are the only rooms in the world. Everything with blood crowds into them, and the vast cold lattice between is what you cross to get from one hearth to another, and people die crossing it, regularly, in the ordinary way.

**The animals are the quiet part**

Abundant, and almost silent, and this is the inversion that takes visitors longest to learn.

The grazers — rasps — travel the cables in herds of forty thousand, hook-footed, blind, pelted in grey felt, filing the cambium with a sound like a rasp on wet wood; from a distance a herd is a fur growing along a wire. They navigate by the pipe-notes, each population fluent in the local bore. They cannot see the hush at all.

The hush is a wide thin gliding thing with a dish of a face and fringed wings that make no sound whatsoever, and it hunts entirely by listening, and it comes to noise. It comes to a rasp's filing. It comes to a shout. It comes to a fire.

So the fauna has gone quiet: soft feet, muffled calls, courtship conducted in taps and substrate-vibration and infrasound below the hush's dish. In the loudest landscape imaginable, the animals have made themselves into pockets of deliberate silence, and the silence is not peace, it is *technique*.

And there are the clickers, small shelled things living in the pipe-bores, whose clicking the plant pays for in nectar, because —

**Everything grows toward sound.**

That is the rule under all the other rules. No phototropism here; there is no light. The growing tips orient on vibration and thicken where the noise is. A cable leans toward a siphon. A strut reaches for a clicking colony. A seeker filament taps the rock ahead of it and listens to the echo and grows into the hollow, not the solid.

Which means the world is slowly building itself in the shape of its own voice, and a cross-section of any anchor is a recording: dense rings for the loud centuries, thin spongy bands for the quiet ones, a scar where the eastern face fell and everything within a hundred kilometers grew hard and fast toward the roaring.

**The people, who are scattered**

A hut in a node-crotch, felt over withies, floor of woven dead filament, a hearth burning shed-wood because nothing else burns. A cairn on a rock anchor. A sinew ladder forty meters long, spliced by four generations. Notch-marks counting gulps. Cold-hammered native copper prised out of fresh fractures — hooks, awls, a needle, a small bell that is never, ever rung.

No smelting. No metal that needs a furnace, because fuel is the shed dead filaments and the shed comes once a season and everyone needs it. No wheels; there is nothing to roll on. Everything is carried, spliced, felted, knotted, hung.

They speak in taps on the bark, which carries better than voice and does not carry *out*. They walk in moss-soled shoes. They do not sing.

And they build bridges, because they need to, and the only way to build a bridge here is to make a noise where you want the bridge to go: to sit on a cold strut with a bone flute and play a rising figure for eleven hours while a growing tip listens and leans.

Which is a noise. Which brings the hush.

So there is one law, and every child here learns it before they learn their own name: **you may only build while the water speaks.** You wait for your cistern to reach its bend. You wait for the roar to come up through the floor of the world. And inside that forty-second mask you play as loud as you can, and then you stop, and you sit in the dark with your hands over the flute's mouth, and you wait nine hours to play again.

A bridge takes a year. Nobody has ever finished one alone.

**Out at the edge**

West, past the last cistern, the growth front is advancing across raw scree at a few kilometers a year: pale tips, no rime yet, tapping, tapping, listening for hollows in a range that has not been broken long enough to have any. Nothing lives out there. No clickers, no rasps, no hush. It is the only truly silent place in the world, and it is silent because it is *new*, and the silence is being eaten from the east at the speed of a walking man.

Behind it, three thousand kilometers of dark: the drone, the shimmer, the crack of splitting stone, ten thousand waters gulping out of turn, forty thousand rasps filing in the black, one wide silent shape crossing between struts with its face turned down and listening.

And somewhere in it, a person on a cold cable with a bone flute, waiting for their water to speak, so that they can be heard by the only thing here that grows toward being heard.
