From above it is only a hole in a limestone shoulder, one of dozens, a place where the plateau's skin gave way. The wind up here is cold and continuous and carries the smell of nothing. Lichen has been working the pavement for nine thousand years at the pace of a fingernail, and the fissures are grikes you could break an ankle in, and the rim of the hole is rounded like a lip worn by drinking. Walk it in a quarter hour. Then find the fluted chute on the north side, where a rope of root has grown down the wall in loops, and go down forty feet, and the wind stops as if a door had shut behind you.

The bowl is warm. Not tropical-hot: warm as a mouth, warm as a stone that has been in a pocket. Springs come out at the base of the wall at blood heat, having been somewhere deep, and the walls are high enough and close enough to keep the warmth from leaving. Outside, the plateau sits at six degrees and shakes. Inside, the air stands at twenty-six and does not move, and holds so much water that it condenses on anything cooler than itself, which is everything, all night, and at dawn the canopy rains on the floor of the bowl in a rain that has never been to the sea.

It is a small place. Two hundred paces at the long axis, roofless, walled, floor terraced in shallow cream-colored pans where the springs have laid down travertine for longer than there have been eyes to see it. Water comes out of the wall in sheets so thin they make no sound, gathers in the pans, spills over the lips of the pans in threads, and leaves through a swallow-hole at the low end, a black socket ringed with ferns. It has been arriving and leaving at the same rate since before the ice. The terraces are neither growing nor dissolving. The place has finished becoming and is now simply being.

The green is extravagant and far apart. That is the first thing to get right about it. Along the wet seams there are mosses so thick you could sink a forearm, and liverworts like wet green leather, and a stand of horsetails in the shallows at the south end, and ferns in every grike, and one colony of a big ribbed leaf the size of a cartwheel that holds a pint of dawn-water in its cup and tips it, slowly, at noon. And between all of this there are long stretches of bare wet stone, forty paces of nothing, sixty paces of pale travertine with a film of algae on it and no living thing taller than a thumbnail. Nine crowns stand in the bowl. Nine, widely spaced, each in its own quarried pan, each wearing ten thousand leaves held out flat and stiff, each leaf tipped with a drip-point aimed inward at its own roots. They are lush the way a single lit window is bright. They do not touch. Their canopies stop short of one another by a distance you could park a house in, and the emptiness between them is not failure or damage. It is the arrangement.

The second thing is the quiet, and what the quiet is made of.

There are no insects over the water. There is no bird on the rim, no worm in the mud, no burrow, no track, no shell, no scat, no nibbled leaf, nothing eaten and nothing eating. The mud is unworked. Turn a stone and there is nothing under it but more stone and a white fungal thread. The silence is not the silence of a place holding still; it is the silence of a place with no one in it to make a noise. Water ticks from leaf to pan, and each pan has its own note, and the notes do not form anything. A leaf turns over the course of an hour with a sound like a page not quite turning. That is the whole inventory, most days.

The reason is the water. It is hungry for a still surface. It carries more stone in solution than water has any business carrying, and it gives that stone up onto whatever holds still long enough to be given to: a fallen leaf furred white in a week, a dead root sheathed in a month, the lip of a pan building itself upward a hair each year. Anything that stopped here and rested — a bird on the rim, a beetle in a grike — would be plastered down inside a day, and there would be no bone left afterward, because under the moss the seams run acid and take bone back. The plants manage it by never stopping: shedding bark, extending, keeping their surfaces new, growing away from their own encrustation at exactly the rate it forms. It is the only trick they have and they have been perfecting it for a very long time. Nothing here can afford to sit down. Nothing here has legs.

So there is no pollination, no fruit, no flower, no scent meant for anyone. The smell in the bowl is wet stone and green sap and a faint hot mineral smell like a struck match, and none of it is advertising. Nothing in the bowl is trying to be seen.

Except the floor.

The floor, in the open stretches between the crowns, is covered in worked stone. Hand-axes, hundreds of them, thousands, lying in fans and drifts where the dawn-drip has sorted them by size. Cleavers. Cores with their flake-scars like the segments of a peeled orange. Flakes with the bulb of percussion still crisp on them, sharp enough to open a hide, lying edge-up in the algae. Hammerstones, rounded chert cobbles brought up from the swallow-hole gravel, each with a battered pole. Some of the tools stand upright in the shallows with a collar of travertine grown around them at the waterline, held like flies in resin. Others are half-buried, and beneath them are others, and beneath those are others, down through a depth of debitage that a man with a spade would take a week to go through and would come out of talking about industries and phases and the long improvement of technique.

There is no hearth. No wall, no post-hole, no midden, no grave, no shard, no hut, nothing built anywhere in the bowl, not one stone set upon another. The whole of the place's making is subtraction: stone taken off stone, and pans quarried down into the floor, and the hole itself. Nothing has ever been added here. And there is no one to have done it, and there has never been anyone, and the tools are being made now.

The nine crowns make them.

Look at the base of the nearest one, where the trunk is not a trunk but a fused bundle of roots the color of candle-wax. One of those roots ends in a fist — a swelling the size of a melon, wrapped in a lattice of finer roots, and inside the lattice, held in a socket of its own tissue, is a chert cobble. The root lifts it by water pressure. It takes about five months to raise the hammer a foot and a half. Beneath it, in a shallow quarried pan, another root-crown holds the workpiece: a flint nodule washed out of the wall, dressed along one platform by acid secreted a molecule at a time over three seasons, etched and roughened and prepared with a patience that has no other name.

And inside the workpiece, in a hollow at the butt, sealed with a plug of cortex, is one embryo in a spoonful of gel.

That is what the tools are. Every made thing in the bowl is either a seed or a failure. The species cannot open its own case; the case is stone; the case must be struck, and struck correctly, along a plane the parent spends years preparing, so that the fracture that frees the embryo is also the fracture that puts an edge on the tool. Most strikes shatter it. The pavement is mostly the shattering. Once every few years a strike goes right, and the bowl — which returns sound four times off its wet walls — carries a single clean knock around itself and back, a note like a bell made of gravel, and then holds the ringing for a while, and then goes back to the drip. Everything in the bowl has heard perhaps forty of these in its life. It is the only loud thing that has ever happened here.

The freed seed sits in its fracture-scar and the water moves it a few feet and there it waits. It can wait a century. It can wait four. It cannot germinate on open stone; it needs a pan, a quarried vacancy, a place where a crown has stood and finished. There are nine crowns and there has been room for nine crowns for a very long time, and at the eastern end there is a stump going soft under moss, a tenth pan, empty now for four hundred years, with eleven seeds lying around its rim at various distances like people outside a door. One of them will get it. The others will lie there until the next one dies. This is the entire drama of the place and it is running at the speed of the terraces.

At the bottom of the debitage, where the swallow-hole gravel comes up, there is a hand-axe older than the rest, worked on both faces with a symmetry the others only approximate, its edge straighter than any root has ever managed. Whether it is the first of the lineage's own making or a thing dropped from above by something that walked past two million years ago and did not come back, the bowl does not say. Everything since has been an attempt at it. The tradition has no teacher. It has an exemplar and a hundred thousand generations of copying, drifting away and drifting back, and it has gotten very good, and it does not know what any of it is for.

Here is the part that stays with you on the climb out. At the butt of each finished tool, unworked, left in its natural cortex, is a bulb of stone the size and shape of a palm. It is where the parent's grip was, and the root-fist that held it, and the shape has been passed down the copying like everything else, refined, kept, held true. Pick one up. It fits. It fits the way a thing fits when it was made for you, and nothing in this bowl has hands, and nothing in this bowl has ever had hands, and the light that comes over the rim at midmorning and crosses the pavement and lights ten thousand edges and goes out again at three is ordinary sunlight, and nothing here turns toward it.

In ten thousand years the swallow-hole may widen and take the water down and leave the bowl dry, and the pans will crack, and the crowns will go, and the floor will be what it already looks like: a workshop, a quarry, a place where somebody sat and worked stone for a very long time and left everything where it fell. Someone will find it. They will read it correctly in every particular and be wrong about the only thing that matters, which is that the work was never done for them, and was never done for anyone, and was simply how this place made more of itself, quietly, in the warm wet dark, one knock a decade, for two million years.
