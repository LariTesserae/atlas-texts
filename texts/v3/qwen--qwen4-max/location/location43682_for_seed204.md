## Ninth Cut

From the plateau there is nothing to see. A blue line in the wind-scoured snow, no wider than a ski track, running perhaps a kilometre before it kinks and disappears. A thread of vapour that the wind takes before it can become a cloud. The snow does not melt up here; it leaves, molecule by molecule, and the surface is dry as flour and squeaks under a boot. Four and a half thousand metres of bare country rock, freshly stripped of its ice within living memory, every arris still sharp enough to open a hand. Nothing grows. Not lichen, not the orange crust that maps itself onto old stone. The world up here has not been alive long enough to be colonised. It is a young mountain, still rising a fingernail's width a year, still coming apart along its seams, and its brokenness is the only reason anyone is here at all.

You find the place by smell before sight: wet flint, a struck match, the flat metallic bite of air after lightning. Then by sound — a dripping that has no business existing in a frozen desert. Then the seam is under your feet and you are looking down into a lit interior, and the drop is three hundred metres, and there are lamps in it, and stairs, and someone very small down there is coiling a hose.

---

Inside, it is a room. That is the first correction the mind makes and never quite recovers from. It is not a canyon, not a valley; those are outdoors. This is a single contained volume, a slot twenty to sixty metres wide with walls that lean toward each other and a ceiling of sky lidded by the comb — a lattice of drawn glass and snow-bridge that from above reads as ordinary drift. Weather happens indoors here. Snow falls into the stairwell and lands on the furniture. A cloud will come through the upper gallery at dawn and wet the handrails and be gone by the second bell. People brush it off their shoulders without comment, the way you'd brush off a cat.

The walls are the reason it is bright. Freshly cleaved rock here is specular — the black is fine-grained and glassy and parts along planes so clean they mirror. So the slot is lined, patchily, with dark mirrors, and the daylight that comes down the mouth for forty minutes at midday does not stop there; it is handed along, wall to wall, face to face, down and down, until the sump three hundred metres below has the light of a north-facing kitchen. Never dark, never bright. Even, grey, sufficient. You can read a gauge anywhere in the city. You can see the colour of a person's eyes at the bottom of the world.

And because the mirrors are the crop, the brightness is a ledger. A panel freshly cleaved is a hard silver rectangle. A panel a day old has gone satin. A week old is matte, dove-grey, dead. Looking across the void you can read the whole harvest schedule in reflectivity — this block quick on Tuesday, that one due tomorrow, that long dull stretch above the east galleries spent and abandoned and fit only for stairs.

That is the second correction: the wall is the field. You do not build on the field. The city clings in narrow bands and islands — catwalks, landings, scaffold-cells, the sealed box of the crush-house, the cisterns — and between them are enormous naked stretches of wet black stone, deliberately empty, deliberately bare, worth more than anything built. It is an urban fabric with most of its buildings withheld. From a terrace you can count the presences: nine lamps, ten, a far one that might be a lamp or might be a fresh face taking the sun. Eleven hundred people in a void cut for thirty thousand. Long minutes go by with no one in sight, and the whole time the place is working.

---

The work is frost.

Break stone in water and the broken edges are hungry. They tear at the water molecules that touch them; they make peroxide and hydrogen and a dozen short-lived angry things; they are, for a few days, chemically alive. The nap lives on that tearing. It is a film, grey-blue, slick, iridescent as a pigeon's neck where it thickens, and it appears on a fresh face within six hours and peaks in a day and dies to a brittle varnish inside a week. It is the base of everything. There is no green in this world and no sunlight worth the name and no soil; there is only the appetite of new surfaces, and the film that grazes it, and the people who farm the whole business.

So the city wets the wall. All afternoon the wetting crews route standing water along the panels — and the standing water is the thing that visitors never get used to: ropes of it, thigh-thick, faintly blue, hanging in the air from cistern to wetting-head with no pipe at all, held in shape by a field nobody in the city fully understands and everybody can repair. They sweat. They rime at the edges. You can push your hand through one and feel the cold go up your arm like a sleeve, and the rope shivers and closes behind your wrist. It is only water. It is only water, held.

Then night, and the freeze, and the mountain does the cutting for them. Water in the microcracks expands and levers, and the wall lets go of plates the size of doors, and they come down into the catch-nets with a sound like a stack of dishes making up its mind. Spall. Every fresh spall face is a new field; every caught plate goes to the crush-house to be reduced to flour, because flour is surface, and surface is value, and a cubic metre of stone can be worth a hillside if you divide it finely enough.

The crush-house is the only loud thing in Ninth Cut and it is loud inside a box, sealed and automated, felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard. It has to be sealed. Flour that fresh is still hungry; it goes into lungs and keeps working there. Old cutters breathe like people walking uphill. They call it the shine, and they joke about it, and the jokes are not funny, and everyone wears the mask anyway.

Round is poor. Sharp is rich. Everything in the city is arranged around that single fact, and everything in the city is therefore young — the tools, the stairs, the terraces, the claims, the arguments. Nothing here is allowed the dignity of weathering.

---

The construction follows from the chemistry. Nothing is bolted. Clean stone pressed to clean stone bonds — becomes one piece, no mortar, no fastener, a weld made of nothing but freshness. So a terrace is not attached to the mountain; it is *continuous* with it, grown out of a quickened pad. Which means the pads tarnish, and lose their tack, and must be re-quickened. Every ninety days a crew goes under the galleries with a hand-cleave and renews the contact patches, and you hear them at it across the void: *tock. tock.* A pause. *tock.* It is the most ordinary sound in the world here, the sound of the city continuing to be attached.

The lattices shed load, too. A beam on the terrace weighs about what a cat weighs. It has the mass of a beam. Children learn the difference before they learn their names, because a beam you can lift with two fingers will still break your leg if it swings into you. Weight is negotiable; inertia is not. Falls still kill. The city has a small cemetery, plugged into spent galleries, each niche labelled in the plain survey hand.

Four things taught before names, roughly in this order: don't touch a quick face with bare skin. Don't put your hand in standing water after dark. Back of the hand on cold stone, always. And when you drop something, let it go.

---

Water is everywhere and water is metered. The plateau above is a dry frozen desert; the wetness in the seam is manufactured, seeped from the remnant ice-cap and then recycled forever — down the walls, into the gutters, into the sump, up the standing columns, onto the walls again. The sump is a black lake at the bottom of the room, cold enough to ache, rising and falling a metre with the season, and its tide is posted daily beside the ledger. Nothing leaves. Every drop that has ever been in Ninth Cut is still in Ninth Cut, and there is a specific and unromantic reverence about this: water is not sacred, it is *capital*, and the accounts are audited.

The air moves on a schedule. Cold pools in the bottom all night; at noon the sun-blade warms the upper walls and the whole volume overturns, drawing plateau air down the throat in a slow warm-cold gust that smells of nothing at all, and for twenty minutes the light-guides sweat and sheet their condensation onto the wet hour grating and everyone puts on a hat. Then the fog forms in the middle galleries, thigh-deep, and drains downhill like something pouring. Then the freeze comes back, and the walls begin to grow fur.

That is the closest thing to vegetation in this world, and it is ice: needle hoarfrost, centimetres long, growing out of the wet black in dense pale stands that lean all one way with the air current. It looks like a crop. It is a crop, in the sense that it is the visible sign that the freeze is working. At dawn it sublimes with a faint hiss, like sugar in water, and the children write their names in it with a gloved finger and the names are gone by the second bell.

---

Life is thin here, and patchy, and follows the boom.

The nap blooms and dies in a week, and grazing it are the grit: pale, blind, slow, thumbnail-sized, armoured in sintered particles they cement to their own backs. They come out of the cracks when a panel thickens and mow it in visible windrows, leaving clean tracks across the iridescence like a snail's calligraphy. When the panel dies they walk to the next one. Most don't make it. That is why there is so little animal life in a place with so much food: the food is a series of explosions separated by deserts, and the deserts are thirty metres of dead wall wide.

Rarely — a few times a season — something long and white comes up the standing water. It lives in the sump and it hunts the grit off the lower panels, and it can swim a held column as easily as a river. The crews call it the plumber. It is a nuisance, not a monster; it takes a finger occasionally when someone forgets the second rule. There is a standing bounty and nobody has claimed it in four years.

In sixty years, four birds have fallen through the comb. Three died. One was carried up the stair and released on the plateau and did not come back. Their skulls are in a cupboard in the ledger-house, labelled with dates and the weather.

---

Sound carries absurdly well in a slot. A whisper at the sump arrives at the mouth as a whisper, four seconds later, intact. So the city is quiet by law and by habit: soft boots, felted rails, hand-talk across the void, and shift signals given by shuttered lamps — the place blinks at itself all day in a slow stilted code, green-white-green, *panel nine is quick, panel nine is quick*. What noise there is: drip in a dozen pitches sorted by height; the tick of growing ice; the far brushing of scrapers, like someone cleaning their teeth in another room; the *tock* of the tack crews; the noon flute-note when the overturn finds the mouth. Once or twice a year a slab lets go somewhere and the whole room says one enormous word and then goes back to dripping, and everyone stands still for a minute and then files a report.

For everything else they built shout-rooms: cells lined in stone wool, booked by the hour, where you can laugh, argue, sing, grieve, be unbearable. There is usually a queue.

---

The ledger measures wealth in square metres of quick face. Marriages merge claims. Debts are denominated in edge. The worst crime is rounding — running warm water on a neighbour's panel so that it weathers, dulls, goes dead — and the penalty is paid in area from your own claim, which is to say in years. There was a case in the spring; the man's name is still written on his door in scraper-tracks, which is how the children do their graffiti, and the door is now behind a plug of sintered flour because that gallery is spent.

Everyone moves house three or four times in a life. The city creeps along the seam a few metres a year, following the fracture front, sealing what it has eaten. Below, down the range, are the older cuts: round, dull, quiet, plugged, their stairs still going nowhere in the dark. The people came up from those. They came up because the mountain is still breaking here, and will keep breaking, and the whole apparatus of the place — the cisterns, the tack, the ledger, the shout-rooms, the cupboard of bird skulls — is a way of standing inside a crack and eating its newness before the world has a chance to make it old.

At the ninth hour the lamps go to half and the freeze comes down the slot like a held breath. The fur starts on the wet panels, first along the scratch lines, then everywhere. It makes a sound like rice poured slowly, like applause heard through a wall. Somewhere below, water finds a face that was made this afternoon and begins, quietly, to take it apart.
