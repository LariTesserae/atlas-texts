**I. The plain**

You would walk over it. That is the first thing to understand.

Above, there is a flat country of ferns — waist-high, dense, warm, dripping, laced with horsetail and a low sprawling vine that has forgotten how to climb because there is nothing left to climb. It rains here at the rate it has always rained, a fine steady warm rain that is not weather so much as a condition of the air. The ground under the ferns is not soil. It is a mat of the ferns' own dead, four fingers deep, and under that, slab. Under the slab, fill. Under the fill, more fill, all the way down: the crushed and settled leavings of a made world, laid out flat and then worn flatter by a hundred thousand years of the same rain.

There is no hill anywhere. There is no longer a slope steep enough to make a river hurry. The water goes to the sea by the slowest possible route, in sheets, through the fern roots, and it arrives at every place in the same amount and at the same speed, forever. The land has finished. It has arrived at the last shape available to it and has stopped proposing others.

In the middle of an ordinary patch of this ordinary fern flat there is a cast-iron lid, ninety kilos, set flush into a concrete collar. The lifting slot has been rounded to a soft oval by nothing but trembling. The ferns lean over it. Every puddle on the lid carries a fixed pattern of rings — six lobes, a bright center — that does not spread, does not fade, and does not change when the rain thickens, because the rain never thickens.

Pull the ferns aside. Lift. Warm air comes up smelling of wet lime and green things and hot enamel. The ladder is cast into the wall, iron rungs gone to soft orange bumps under a skin of pale stone. Twelve meters down, there is a room, and the room is the loudest place in the world.

**II. The room**

Eight meters by six. Twelve high, corbelled at the top where the shaft comes in. A pocket. You could hold it in your two hands and call it a house.

And it is nearly empty. This is what surprises people, in the half-minute before their hearing goes: they expect a machine room to be *full*, crowded with iron, and instead there is a volume of bright warm air with almost nothing in it. A blanked pipe at the north end, its flange bolts grown over into a single knuckled ridge. A ladder. A ring of six enamel-shaded lamps at mid-height. A floor grating, and beneath the grating a sump of clear water. Two thin fins of stone standing in the middle of the air. That is the entire inventory. You can walk the long way around and touch nothing.

The lamps burn a clean ordinary daylight, five and a half thousand kelvin, no flicker, no warmth of color, no shadow with an edge to it. They are fed by a small permanent-magnet alternator on the bleed line, and they have been burning at exactly this output since before the plain above lost its last hill. The glass is frosted by spray. The room therefore has the light of a cloudy afternoon and no afternoon at all. Nothing here has ever experienced dusk. The moss grows at one continuous rate, without pause, without rhythm, and is rotted at the same rate by a fungal mat that lives in the drip basins, so that the total living weight of the room is a constant, to the gram, and has been for a very long time.

The air is twenty-seven degrees and utterly saturated. The water is twenty-seven degrees. Your clothes are wet in a minute and will not dry, ever, and this is not uncomfortable so much as it is a fact you stop arguing with.

**III. The note**

The sound is not a roar. A roar has weather in it. This is a note.

One wavelength spans the room, wall to wall — forty-three cycles a second, low enough that you hear it in the sternum and the sinuses and the loose fittings of the teeth before the ear consent to it at all. Over it stands a hard bright edge at four kilohertz, the sound of a sheet of water shearing off a lip, and between them a stack of harmonics so stable that they seem less like noise than like architecture you have been put inside.

A hundred and thirty decibels, near enough. The eyes smear at the low end; look at the ladder and the rungs swim. You cannot hear yourself shout, and after a while you notice you have stopped composing sentences, because there is no channel for them. The room does not permit language. It permits attention.

The source is simple and entirely ordinary. Up the shaft, thirty meters, a head of water that never varies, because the country above has no gradient left to vary with. It comes down and it comes through a wicket gate that has been leaking by, in exactly the same amount, since the last person to stand here stopped being able to. The falling sheet strikes the lip of the surge chamber's throat and sets the whole volume ringing the way a blown bottle rings. There is no mystery in it. It is fluid mechanics, and it is deafening, and both of these things are true at once and neither modifies the other.

**IV. Where the quiet is**

Here is the thing that makes the room habitable.

A standing wave has places where it cancels. Two vertical planes, a hand's breadth thick, stand at the quarter-points of the room's width, running floor to ceiling. Inside them the pressure barely moves. Step sideways into one and the roar drops away by forty decibels — still loud, still a room with a machine in it, but suddenly a place where a body is a body again and you can hear your own heart going.

They are not empty planes. The room has *grown* them.

The water that comes down carries lime, leached out of a hundred kilometers of made ground. Where the sound is violent, the walls are being eaten: scalloped, fluted, scooped into the soft asymmetric hollows that cavitation makes, aggregate standing proud, the old iron reduced to an orange ghost of its own outline. Where the sound is quiet, the lime comes out of solution and stays. It has been staying for a very long time. At the two silent planes it has built fins — pale travertine, thin as a wrist, floor to ceiling, rippled like the inside of a shell, hung with terraces and lips and small spilling basins, all of it wet, all of it dripping into the next basin down at a rate that does not alter.

The fins are the only forest in this country. Three mosses live on them and nowhere else; a single fern species roots in the lee of the terraces, small, glossy, unreasonably healthy; algae sheet the flume; a fungal mat digests whatever falls, and nothing accumulates. The rest of the room's surfaces are bare polished stone, scoured, sterile, ringing.

And on the floor, the dust has arranged itself. Fine ridges of it, pale grey, standing in fixed curves — the Kundt figures of the room, gathered where the *air* is stillest, which is not where the *sound* is quietest. The dust and the ear disagree about this room. The dust lies in beautiful windrows along the loud planes, against the walls, down the centerline. The living things are all at the quarter-points, in the silence, on the fins. Two geographies, interleaved, neither touching the other.

**V. The inhabitants**

There is a cricket.

Pale, small, on the second fin, three terraces up, in the deepest crossing of the quiet — where two nodal planes intersect and the cancellation goes almost to nothing. It sings at four point one kilohertz, in a notch of the room's spectrum, and it can hear itself, and so can a female, if there is one this decade. There may be forty individuals in the room. There may be twelve. Their ancestors came down the intake as eggs.

There are springtails in the moss in numbers that qualify, technically, as a population. A mite. A spider that has learned to build only in the fin's lee, and builds small. Once in a long while a wood frog gets into the drip basin at the fin's foot and lives there, fat and calm, and dies there, and is replaced.

That is the whole fauna. In an hour of looking you will see the cricket, or you will not. Nothing flies. Nothing calls at a distance. There is no predator here large enough to need a name, and no season to bring one.

**VI. What the room is for**

The stencil is still legible on the north wall, though it is no longer paint: the lime has grown over the letters and left them standing in relief, a raised alphabet an eighth of an inch proud, the way a fossil stands out of a cliff.

STILLING CHAMBER No. 2.

It was built to absorb change. That was its entire purpose — to take the sudden shudder of a load thrown off, the hammer of a gate slamming, the surge, and swallow it, and give back nothing. It was sized generously for a world that used to have events in it.

There are no events now. The plain above has reached base level; every drop that falls has already fallen ten thousand times in the same way; the head is constant, the leak-by is constant, the note is constant. The chamber has nothing left to still. It stands ready anyway, at full dimension, perfectly capable, singing.

**VII. The rules**

Everything moves and nothing changes. Water enters at the rate water leaves. Stone dissolves at the rate stone precipitates. Moss grows at the rate moss rots. The room's material has been entirely replaced, several times over — there is no original concrete in it anywhere, no original iron but a stain in the shape of iron — and its *form* has not altered by a millimeter in an age, because the form is the shape the note wants, and the note is the shape the form makes. It has tuned itself to itself and arrived. It is finished. It cannot become more what it is.

Somewhere on the second fin there is a hand-shaped hollow, a negative, where someone once rested a palm on soft young lime and the lime grew up around the absence and kept it. There is a wrench on the floor that is no longer a wrench but a low rounded bump with a suggestion of a jaw at one end, and in another few thousand years will be a lip, and then a terrace, and then moss.

Climb out. It takes a while; the rungs are soft, and your hands are wet, and your ears have gone to a high clean hiss that will stay with you for a day. Push the lid back. The ferns close.

Above: the flat green country, the warm steady rain, the enormous ordinary silence, the puddle with its six fixed lobes shivering under your boots.

You would walk over it.
