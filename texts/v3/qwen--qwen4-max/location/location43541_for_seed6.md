## Surge Hall Nine

The mountain is sixty-one years old. It came up out of a pasture in a single autumn, a black cone of scoria and glass, and it has not yet learned to be a mountain: no soil on it, no lichen, no rounded slope, no gullies. Rain hits it and runs off clean. From the air it looks like something dropped and not yet picked up.

There is one door in it, near the summit, and behind the door is all the life for forty kilometers.

---

The hall is a cylinder sixty-one meters across and forty tall, cast in place, board-marked, its arrises still sharp enough to cut a palm. It is eleven years old and it is not finished curing. Concrete does that underwater — takes decades, weeping lime the whole time — so the walls give off a slow white haze that hangs against them like breath on glass, and the water tastes of new stone and alkalinity, a chalky sweetness at the back of the throat. Nothing here is worn. The steel has no rust bloom. The weld beads stand up in their original ripples. Bolt heads still carry their factory stamp. If you run a light along a handrail you will find the mill scale intact, and the grease in the bearing cups is the grease that was put there.

It is warm. Thirty-one degrees, blood-warm, held there by exchangers threaded into the cone's flank where the rock still remembers being liquid. Outside the door it is four below and the wind has teeth. Inside, the water is a bath that has been sitting all afternoon.

And it is full. Not flooded — *full*, the way a lung is full, the way a jar of specimens is full. Every cubic meter of the hall is occupied by something: leaf, stem, root-mat, ceramic rack, conduit, ladder, cable, bracket, brace. There is no open water to swim in. There are nine radial bays of vegetation stacked floor to dome, and between them the service lanes, seventy centimeters wide, and even those are threaded at intervals with tension rods and sampling lines, so that moving through the hall is not swimming but a kind of slow polite threading, hand over hand, turning sideways at every third meter. The plants have grown into the gaps the drawings left. The drawings left gaps for maintenance. The plants did not read them.

---

None of it is wild. There is no soil, no seed bank, no volunteer. Nine species were carried up the mountain in gel cartridges and set into honeycomb racks of fired ceramic, and every one of them has a job on the schematic:

Bay One oxygenates — broad, thin, translucent blades like sheets of green mica, forty thousand of them, each stamped along the midrib with a serial in white enamel. Bay Two scrubs metals; its leaves go rust-colored at the tips when they are working and pale when they are spent, and the gantry reads the color and harvests accordingly. Bay Three makes cable: it lays down cellulose in a helix around a nylon core that the hall extrudes for it, and the resulting rope has a breaking strain of nine tonnes and a service life of two hundred years. Bay Four grows gaskets. Bay Five diffuses light. Bay Six is a thermometer with leaves. Bay Seven's function is listed on the plate as *acoustic*. Bay Eight is redundant Bay One. Bay Nine's plate has been revised twice and now reads, in effect, *tolerance*.

They are lush past all intention. The design called for a certain leaf area and the hall has produced four times it, because the water is warm and the light is metered but generous and nothing, anywhere in this room, eats anything.

That is the rule the whole place turns on. There is no animal life. No fish, no snail, no copepod, no worm, no mite, no bird that could get in even if the door were open, because outside there is nothing to get in *from* — the cone is sterile ash, too young for a food web, and the hall was seeded on purpose with plants and bacteria alone. Nothing with a mouth was ever admitted. Pollination is done by brass brushes on the gantry arms, one flower every eleven seconds. Death is handled by macerating grates and by films of bacteria that oxidize at their own patient pace.

So when a leaf lets go, it does not rot. It stays green. It hangs in the water column and drifts, intact, for years, until it finds a grate or a rack or the underside of a gallery and settles there and stays green among the others. In the low corners of the hall there are drifts of perfect leaves a meter deep, undecayed, layered like a book nobody has opened. It is the strangest thing in a strange room: lushness with no rot in it, growth with no appetite anywhere, a forest that has never met a mouth.

---

Light comes down the oculus. Thirty meters of stacked quartz and light-pipe from the summit, gathering the thin high-altitude sun and pouring it into the dome through a lens two meters thick. It arrives attenuated but honest — working light, noon light, the brightness of a well-lit factory floor, greened slightly by the water and then greened again by Bay Five until the whole hall sits in a steady gold-green clarity. You can see the far wall. You can read the plate on the far wall. That is the uncanny part: nothing is hidden. Sixty-one meters of packed, saturated, absolutely legible space, every serial number visible, no shadow anywhere deep enough to keep a secret.

Except the Eye. At the center of the hall, on axis, a cylinder six meters across is held empty by a ring of intakes — no leaf, no drift, no haze. It is the only volume in the hall with nothing in it. Hang there and the whole thing resolves around you: nine green wedges, three ring galleries, the gantry rails, the dome, the light-shaft standing in the water like a solid bar of gold, and below, the throat.

The throat is why any of this exists. Under the floor grate the hall narrows to a penstock and drops fourteen hundred meters down the inside of the mountain to a turbine hall that no one has visited in nine years. Surge Hall Nine is not a garden. It is a conditioning chamber. It makes warm, oxygen-rich, metal-free water and sends it down, and the water turns something, and the something does not answer. The outgoing pressure has been nominal for nine years. The demand curve has not changed by a decimal. Whether that means the turbines are running or that the gauge is stuck is a question the hall is not equipped to ask.

---

It is quiet. Water should carry sound beautifully, and here it doesn't, because Bay Seven was planted for exactly that reason: forty thousand soft blades, packed, absorb echo the way acoustic foam does. The hall is deader than a room this size has any right to be. You hear your own body first and loudest. Then, if you hold still, a deep pulse from the throat every forty seconds — the pressure regulator, more felt than heard, a hand laid flat on the chest. Then the ratchet of the gantry, one tooth per minute. Then the small dry clicks of stems taking up their tension rods as they grow, a sound like a knuckle in another room.

Everything here is slow. The pumps turn once an hour. The hall's entire volume is exchanged every forty days, so the water moves through the bays at about the speed of a minute hand's shadow; a released leaf takes a day and a half to cross the room. The dome sweats a bead of condensate that grows for a week before it lets go. The tide is a forty-day tide. The only fast thing is the sun crossing the lens, and even that has been geared down: the light-shaft rotates one degree an hour, sweeping the bays in sequence so that each species gets its allotted portion, and the passing of the beam through the packed green is the closest thing here to weather.

The gantries trim. They travel a centimeter an hour on their rails, cutting to the drawing, and the growth exceeds them by eleven millimeters a year. There is a ledger bolted to the gallery rail, enamel on steel, updated by the hall itself in stamped digits: free volume, remaining, and beneath it a date. The date is two hundred and six years out. On that day the aisles close, the Eye fills, and Surge Hall Nine becomes a single solid object — sixty-one meters of leaf and root and rope and curing stone, a fossil being manufactured deliberately, in warm water, in good light, with no one to see it.

The hall knows the number. It has no opinion about the number. It goes on making oxygen at the specified rate, and brushing the flowers, and sending the warm water down the mountain into the dark, where something is either using it or isn't.

Outside, the wind moves a little ash on a slope that has never had a footprint on it. Inside, a leaf lets go, stays green, and begins its day-and-a-half journey across the room.
