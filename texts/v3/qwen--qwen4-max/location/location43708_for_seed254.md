## The Kettle

You hear it before you find it. Walking the dry plateau — scrub oak, goat tracks, a wind that smells of dust and hot fennel — you come onto a low grinding note in the soles of your feet, and then in your chest, and only then do you notice that the ground ahead has a lip.

The lip is limestone, worn round, undercut a little, with a fringe of green hanging over it like hair over a drain. Inside the fringe there is nothing to see: a lid of leaves, layered nine and ten deep, dark as the inside of a wardrobe. The hole beneath is a hundred and ninety paces long, a hundred and forty across, seventy-one deep at its lowest rim. A collapse, a swallow of the earth, older than anything that has ever been built in it. Six hundred people once lived down there. The place is called the Kettle, and it is still working.

---

The town was a cordage town. The Kettle Fibre & Cordage Company sank its works into the hole because the hole gave it three things no surface site could: a stream that fell twenty-six metres without being asked, water that came out of the rock at thirty-four degrees all year, and a bowl of still, heavy air in which fibre could be retted without a single frost.

The stream enters at the Sill, a cave mouth on the north wall, and drops into the Eye, and leaves through the Swallow at the floor's south end, and has done this since before there were names for any of it. Around that fall the company laid out three benches — High, Middle, Low — cut into the cliff and faced with vitrified brick. Ninety-one houses. A meeting hall with a slate roof. A store, a bathhouse, a school of one room. A stair of four hundred and eleven steps switchbacking up the east wall to the rim. Flumes and launders and standpipes laced over everything, cast iron, threaded, flanged, sweating. Nine retting vats on the Low Bench, each the size of a chapel. A decorticator, a brake, a press. And the Walk: a shed sixty metres long where the strands were laid into hawser, the longest straight line anyone in the Kettle ever knew.

All of it is still there. Almost none of it is visible.

---

The plant got in the way the plant gets into everything: through the drains, through the flume joints, through the mortar. It is a nettle-kin, a bast plant, the fibre crop itself — a thing the company planted in beds on the Middle Bench and propagated by rhizome cutting because it sets no viable seed. It has not set viable seed in a hundred and eleven years. It has not needed to. Everything green in the Kettle, from the lid at the rim to the pale suckers rooting in the sump, is one individual, layered and re-layered, stems falling and taking root where they lie, a single organism occupying nineteen thousand cubic metres and closing every gap it can find.

It is not a vine and not a tree. It is a thicket with the habits of both: stems the thickness of a thigh, hollow, ribbed, faintly fluted, branching into stems the thickness of a wrist, then a finger, then a wire, until the whole volume is a woven solid of green-brown wood at every scale, like a lung, like a sponge, like the inside of a rope. The old leaves are the size of doors and hang slack and dripping. The young ones are the size of hands and carry stinging hairs on their undersides, formic and sharp, and the sting is still good after a century. Bark on the mature stems is smooth and wet and the colour of strong tea. Where the mass has aged and died at the core it has not fallen: it stands, filled with white fungal felt, holding the living wood in place like a scaffold that was never removed.

The houses are full. Not overgrown — *full*, the way a mould is full of casting metal. Every room on every bench is a solid block of the plant, with the furniture in it, the boots in it, the cups. The stair is a plug. The meeting hall's slate roof still holds its shape because the mass beneath it has grown into a vault that fits it exactly. You could not walk twenty paces anywhere in the Kettle. You could not walk two.

Except where things move.

---

This is the rule the place runs on, and it is not a strange rule, only an unfamiliar one: **growth cannot cross a swept volume.** A stem that leans into a turning shaft is crushed and pulped and retted where it stands. A sucker that reaches into a piston's stroke is cut off sixty times a minute. The plant is patient and total, but it is not stronger than iron in motion, and so after a hundred years the only open spaces left in the Kettle are the exact shapes of the movements that have never stopped.

The mill is a floor plan drawn in negative. The overshot wheel — nine metres, cast iron, its rim now a drum of grey tufa — turns at four revolutions a minute inside a smooth-walled annulus polished by its own passage, a tunnel of clearance through solid wood, wet, warm, roaring. The line shaft runs the length of the Low Bench in a bore that fits it like a sleeve, and the bore is lined with a callus of scar tissue where a century of shoots have tried and failed. The pump rod at the Sill has cut a vertical slot two metres long and eleven centimetres wide through eleven metres of living mass, and the walls of the slot are glazed. The decorticator's beating arms, which once ran at two hundred strokes a minute, now manage nine, loaded by friction and mineral, and their swept box is a chamber you could stand in if you could get to it, and you cannot.

The leather belts went in the first decade. The rubber went with them. What survived is what was directly coupled: iron to iron, bevel gears running in a water bath, lignum vitae bearings that have been water-lubricated since the day they were installed and are not yet worn. Everything else stopped, and everything that stopped was eaten. The press still cycles; the baler's knitter failed in the second winter and its bales are now a mineralised wall in the warehouse, each one welded to the next by carbonate, a stratigraphy of eleven years of production with the twine still on it.

---

Water is not falling on this place. Water is *occurring* in it, at every point, in every phase.

The stream is warm. The Eye steams. The air below the lid stands at saturation and thirty-three degrees and never moves, so the leaf-lid condenses all night and rains indoors until mid-morning — a steady fat drip through nine layers of leaf, which is the loudest thing in the Kettle after the machines and the only thing in it that resembles weather. The cliff weeps in curtains. The flumes still carry water and also leak, and the leaks have built tufa: aprons, terraces, folded draperies, grey-white and translucent at the edges, growing three millimetres in a decade.

That is the balance the place has settled into. The stream dissolves the limestone above and deposits it below, and it has been doing both at the same rate for a very long time. Nothing here is being destroyed faster than it is being made. The handrails are inside a finger-thick sheath of calcite. The vat rims have grown lips that have narrowed the vats by a third. The wheel's bearings crusted over in the thirties and were simply carried around by the crust, which now forms part of the housing, a bearing made of stone that grew itself into tolerance.

The nine vats still fill and drain. Their siphons are iron and stone and have no moving parts; the stream feeds them through a launder whose mouth the plant has narrowed to a fist, so the cycle is longer than it was — forty-one minutes now, where the company manual says twelve. Each vat burps once as it breaks its siphon, a soft enormous gulp you feel in the floor, and then the roar of it emptying, and then forty minutes of ticking and dripping while it fills again. Nine vats, offset, unsynchronised, drifting slowly out of and back into phase across the years.

They are full of retting bast. They have been full of retting bast since the last shift. The smell is the smell the town's money was made of: sweet-rotten, ammoniac, wet haystack gone over, with a green cut-stem note underneath and a thin sulphurous edge. Beneath that, at floor level, something sharper — a prickle on the tongue, like soda water. That is the air itself.

---

The Kettle exhales carbon dioxide out of its fissures. It has always done so. Heavy gas pools in a bowl of still air the way water pools in a bowl of still rock, and the town lived with it the way miners live with firedamp: by rule, by lamp, by knowing the line.

The line is painted on the brickwork of the High Bench in a blue that has not faded because nothing down here fades. It runs level, all the way round, waist-high on the third terrace, and it marks the height at which a candle stops. Above it you slept. Below it you worked with a flame on a pole ahead of you, and if the flame went low you climbed, and there was a fine for leaving your flame behind.

One windless night in the hot season the stream ran hotter than usual, and the gas came up, and the inversion held it, and the line moved four metres in the dark. There was no one awake on the Low Bench to see the flames go out. The town had lived above that line for sixty years and did not wake.

Nothing has breathed in the Kettle since. Not a rat, not a swift, not a mosquito, not a moth. The lid excludes what would fly in; the gas excludes what would climb in; the stinging hairs and the anoxic water account for the rest. There are no fish in the Eye. There are no flies on the retting vats, which is why the retting has never finished and never spoiled, only soured and settled into a slow bacterial equilibrium that gives off a bubble every few seconds, patient as a clock.

What lives here: one plant. Ferns and liverworts on the upper walls, above the line, where spores drift down and the gas is thin. Cyanobacteria in the tufa, laying down the grey. White-rot fungi in the dead core of the mass, and brackets on the warehouse door, and the fine white felt that is slowly, at a rate of a centimetre a year, undoing the rope.

---

Light comes down through nine layers of leaf and arrives at one or two percent of what left the sky. It is green-brown, directionless, and warm; it does not cast shadows so much as thicken in corners. For eleven days around midsummer, at noon, the sun stands over the throat of the pit and a coin of white light about two metres across reaches the floor of the Low Bench and lies on the tufa there for eighteen minutes. The rest of the year it lands on the leaf-lid and stops. Over a century the canopy has grown toward that appointment: the leaves in the northern quadrant are smaller, thicker, tougher, arranged in a slow funnel that has migrated eleven metres across the lid since the town died, tracking a thing that happens once a year for eighteen minutes.

And there are three lamps.

The Wheelhouse magneto has no brushes. It is a ring of magnets turning past fixed coils, and there is nothing in it to wear out but bearings that run in water. It has been making current for a hundred and eleven years at a frequency that has drifted down with the wheel's speed to about nineteen cycles a second. The transformers cannot use it. The town's grid is a green wall of slate and copper with nothing on the other end. But three carbon-filament lamps, on a direct line, in thick glass, in the Wheelhouse and at the head of the Walk, are burning at perhaps a third of their rated voltage — and a filament run that gently does not evaporate, does not fail, does not end. They are the colour of weak tea and strong embers. They throb visibly at thirty-eight pulses a second, which is just slow enough to see, so the light in the Kettle's only lit rooms beats along with the note.

In the sealed dark of the warehouse, where the mass has died at the core, there is foxfire: a green so faint it takes half an hour of standing still to be sure of. It is only fungus. It is only ever fungus.

---

It is loud. It is loud in a way that has a shape.

The Drop is broadband white noise, twenty-six metres of it, a hiss with a floor under it. The wheel is a beat at four a minute, a shove you feel in the sternum through the rock. The shafting knocks. The decorticator's nine strokes a minute are nine soft hammer-blows the size of a room. The vats gulp and roar on their forty-one-minute stagger. The rope creaks under load. The leaf-rain patters on a hundred thousand surfaces. Ten thousand stems rub against ten thousand others in the faint currents from the fall, and the sound is rigging, is a harbour, is a wooden ship working in a swell.

None of it leaves. The bowl is stone and the lid is wet mass and both are mirrors; the Kettle holds its own noise and adds to it. At the floor it stands around a hundred and four decibels, and beneath the audible racket there is the magneto's note at nineteen cycles, on the very threshold of hearing, which is not heard so much as suffered — a pressure in the ears, a nausea in the unprepared, a pulse in the fog above the Eye.

There are dead spots. Standing waves in a stone bowl make seams of near-silence, and the town knew every one of them: the northeast corner of the meeting hall, the lee of the No. 4 vat, a landing on the two hundred and ninth step. They were called the quiet rooms. Courtship happened in them. So did arguments. The company paid a deafness pension and half the men over fifty drew it, and the mill hands talked with their hands, a full working vocabulary of some two hundred signs, all of it now unspoken in a place where nothing needs to be said.

---

The Walk still lays rope.

Three strands, hawser-laid, coming off the twisting head at about three centimetres a day — a hundredth of the old rate, the machine slowed by everything it has to drag through. The rope walks out of the shed, coils in the warehouse, climbs the wall where a hoist once ran, and goes up the east face along the buried stair, and out over the rim into the sun.

Out there it dries, and bleaches, and cracks, and the fungi that reach it from the soil undo it into fibre and dust. It is made at one end and unmade at the other at the same speed. Its length does not change. It has been the same two hundred metres of rope for eighty years, moving through the world at three centimetres a day, a river of hawser with a standing wave in it.

The people of the plateau call it the tail. They cut it once, in the fifties, and it came back. There is a fence now, and a gate at the head of the stair, welded shut, and a sign in two languages that says ASPHYXIANT ATMOSPHERE — DO NOT DESCEND, and a small solar panel, and a cable going down over the lip with a sensor on the end of it, hanging eleven metres below the lid in the green dark. It logs carbon dioxide, temperature, humidity, and sound pressure level. The numbers have not meaningfully changed since the logging began. The fence gets repaired. Nobody goes down.

Below, in the warm saturated dark, the wheel turns four times a minute inside its polished tunnel, and the shoots lean in, and are pulped, and the tunnel stays open. The vats fill. The lamps throb. The rope goes out into the light and is taken apart. The tufa lays down its three millimetres, and the stream takes its three millimetres, and the ledger balances, and will go on balancing until the stream finds a new fissure, or the lid grows heavy enough to bring the roof down, whichever comes first — four hundred years, perhaps, or four thousand.

There is no hurry. There has never been any hurry here. That was the whole point of the site.
