From below, through the cloud deck, it looks like a notch someone took out of the ridge with a bite. From the rim it is a bowl about a hundred and eighty paces across, and you can walk its lip in the time it takes to eat an apple, and every part of it — every stone, every slope, every grain of the soil, the water and the light's particular angle and the warm air standing in it — has been made.

The mountain outside is cold. The air inside is the temperature of a mouth.

---

The bowl was poured, not carved. You can see the lifts if you stand at the north stair and look across: forty-one bands ringing the wall, each one a season's pour, each with the faint horizontal ripple of a form-board and the little pocked seam where the boards were struck. Nothing here has been rounded. There is no scree, no sand, no smoothed pebble, no dust anywhere in the basin — dust cannot survive the wet, and even if it could, it would be caught and fixed within a day. Every edge is either the edge a tool gave it or the bulging, cauliflowered edge the water is giving it now. The rock is not old. The rock is not finished. Some of it is still curing, and if you put your palm on the low terrace by the west sump you will feel it working, a faint animal warmth that is not the steam pipes.

Below the floor grates, the works. Exhaust steam runs in a lattice of iron mains under the whole basin, and that is why the bowl is warm at this altitude, why the cloud stays below the rim like a held breath, why ferns grow where the survey said nothing would grow. The heat comes up through the soles of your boots. So does the hum: a low industrial pulse from the pump-house, too deep to hear properly, arriving in the teeth and the sternum instead of the ear.

Everything else is quiet. The moss eats sound. Twenty paces in, you can hear your own blood.

---

Water arrives from the sky, but not as weather. Along the rim stand the combs — banks of thin sky-facing fins, blackened steel, radiating their heat straight out into the thin air above the cloud, so cold that they weep at all hours. The wind pushes the cloud deck up the slope and through them and the combs strip it. The water runs down the fin-flutes into the gutter-ring, and from the gutter-ring into the basin, and it falls.

It falls constantly and unevenly, in drip-lines and threads and sudden sheets, and every forty minutes or so the whole bowl makes its own rain. Warm air off the grate rises, meets the chilled lip, condenses in a mass, and lets go: a soft, brief, drenching downpour that lasts ninety seconds and moves across the basin from east to west like something walking. The leaves are built for it. They are broad and guttered and tipped with drip-points, and when the rain comes the entire garden nods once, all together, and the sound is a single enormous soft syllable.

Then it stops. Then the dripping resumes, which is the ordinary sound of the place: ten thousand small clocks, none of them agreeing.

The water is hard. It tastes of chalk and pennies. It carries so much dissolved lime that it goes cloudy in a cup and clears from the bottom up.

---

Here is the rule of this place, and it takes a while to notice, because it is the opposite of the rule everywhere else:

Nothing wears away. Everything is added.

The condensate lays down its mineral as it goes. A fingernail's thickness a week on the open terraces; three times that in a drip-line; a hand's breadth in a season where a thread of water has been falling on one spot without interruption. The crews call it the gain. It is measured on brass-ringed gauge posts set around the floor, and the rings disappear one by one from the bottom, and the posts have to be extended, and the extensions are swallowed too.

Nothing here erodes. There is no wearing, no rounding, no carrying-away. The only subtraction in the entire basin is deliberate: the scrapers, the needles, the acid carts, the men who come down the north stair with their long-handled chisels and open the arteries again. Erosion is not a force in this place. Erosion is a job.

And the gain does not only build. It records. A leaf that lies on the terrace for nine days is given a stone shroud, and rots out from under it, and leaves a perfect hollow leaf in the new surface — veins, stem, the tear where something stepped. The floor of the basin is a ledger. You can read last autumn in the third terrace if you know the layers. There is a glove down by the west sump, a workman's glove, cast in white carbonate, fingers slightly curled, the cuff still showing the rib of the knit. Nobody has bothered to break it out. It has become one of the things the place is known for.

---

The planting was specified. Mason's mosses first, chosen to fix lime, then the fern stock in the wall seams, then the broad-leaves over the sumps to shade the water and slow the gain where it was doing damage. That was the plan, and the plan is still pinned in the counting-house, and the basin has not followed it for years.

Now the growth is layered to the point of suffocation and there is no seeing more than twenty paces in any direction. Moss over the pipes, ferns out of the moss, vines using the pipe-runs as trellis and the pipe-runs using the vines as insulation. Hanging curtains of root from the terrace lips, near-touching, so that walking the lower floor means parting things with both hands. A grove of nine small trees over the east sump whose roots have welded into a single plate, a raft, one organism now with nine trunks; the plate has grown into the grating and the grating has grown into the plate and neither can be removed without killing the other. In the drip-lines the vegetation is thickest and strangest, because there the gain is fastest, and the plants there have learned to grow upward at the pace of the stone rising under them — long shins of stem, ringed like bamboo, each ring a week of being buried and out-growing it.

Green over grey-white over rust. That is the whole palette, except at the sun-notch.

The light is ordinary daylight, thin-air daylight, blue in the shadows and hard at the edges, softened by the bowl's own mist to something you can look at. But at about three in the afternoon the sun comes through the notch in the west lip and a bar of it crosses the floor, and for eleven minutes the whole basin steams and every wet surface throws a hard white spark, and the combs tick as they expand, and it is briefly, unbearably beautiful, and then the bar slides off the wall and it is only afternoon again.

---

There is almost nothing living here that moves on its own.

No insects to speak of — a bloom of springtails in the moss once a season, mites always, and that is the inventory. No wasps at the fallen fruit. The broad-leaves flower heavily, white and sweet, and the fruit falls and nothing eats it, and it ferments in the warm wet and the whole lower floor smells of vinegar and cider under the chalk-and-green smell, and the sugar goes down into the slime and feeds the moss that will bury the next fruit. Pollination is done by the rain: the drip-points are aimed, more or less, and the splash throws the pollen, and it works about a third as well as a bee. The scrapers do the rest with brushes, on their rounds, without being asked. It is in nobody's job description.

There are snails. Their shells are made of the same carbonate as everything else and they are indistinguishable from the terrace at a glance. When a snail dies its shell keeps growing, welded down, and within a month it is not a shell but a small swelling in the rock with a spiral in it, and the basin has ten thousand of these, and they are the closest thing here to a fossil record, though none of them is older than four years.

There is one lineage of fish in the warm sump, blind, pale, descended from stock put in to eat larvae that no longer exist. They come to the surface when the pump note changes.

And once a year a bird lands on the rim, drinks at the fin-flutes, and does not come down into the bowl. It is in the log every year in the same handwriting, which means it is not the same hand, which means the entry is a tradition. *Visitor.* Sometimes a date. Sometimes nothing else.

---

The machines are losing.

You can see it best at the old pump-head on the south side, which was decommissioned rather than repaired: a stalagmite now, four feet through, white and wet and fluted, with the rim of a flywheel standing out of it like a coin in a candle. The gain got into the bearings and the bearings seized and the seizing was cheaper to accept than to fix. Two of the four sluice gates work. The acid carts run on an electrified rail that has to be chipped clear each morning, and the chipping is the loudest sound the basin ever makes — a hard bright ringing that goes up the walls and comes back softened, and then the moss takes it, and the quiet closes over.

Every ninth day they open the flush and the basin drinks itself: the sumps drop, the mains roar, the drip-lines go silent for an hour, and the whole garden droops in the sudden dry and the humidity falls and you can suddenly see all the way across, which is disorienting, like a room with the furniture taken out. Then the valves close and the water climbs and the leaves lift and the dripping starts again, one line at a time, until the sound is complete.

Outside, the cloud deck rises in the wet season and drowns the combs and the works run on stored water; in the dry season the deck falls below the ridge and the combs stand in blue air making nothing, and the garden is kept alive by the pumps alone, hauling from a reservoir nine hundred feet down the mountain, and everything smells of hot iron instead of chalk. The basin has weather. It just isn't the sky's.

---

It was never finished. That is the thing you feel last and can't stop feeling after.

There is a stair on the east wall that goes up eleven steps and stops, because the second terrace it was meant to reach was never poured. There is scaffold on the north face that the vines have taken and that is now, structurally, vines. There is a whole quadrant of floor that is raw form-work and rebar and standing water, intended as something, and the moss has come in and made it a meadow, and the meadow is the most beautiful part of the basin, and no one has decided whether that counts as completion.

The plan called for a hollow. The gain is filling it. Every day the bowl is a little less bowl and a little more mass, and the crews come and take away what they can carry, and the water puts back more than they take, and this has been true for forty-one seasons. Eventually the works will stop — a boiler failure, a dry decade, a decision made in an office by someone who has never smelled the vinegar — and then the water will keep coming for a while, and the garden will keep growing for a while, and the gain will do what it does without anyone to argue.

It will close over the grate and the grove and the glove and the flywheel and the eleven steps. It will make a lens of pale stone in the top of the mountain, green-veined, dense, holding a hollow leaf every few inches and a spiral every foot, and a bar of iron here, and a handrail there, running through it like a thread through a bead.

And much later, someone quarrying the ridge will cut into it and find a room.
