# The Condensary

## The lip

From the lane it is a hedge that has filled a hole. The pasture runs up flat and green and then stops the way grass stops at a pond, and there is a surface of leaves lying level with the field, a hundred and forty paces by sixty, stirring very slightly. Cattle graze to within a body-length of it. They do not go closer, not out of fear — there is nothing there to be afraid of — but because the ground stops being ground.

You have to walk out to the edge to learn that the hole is eleven metres deep.

The rim is brick, rounded like river stone, three courses of it showing above the turf, and the mortar has gone to a soft grey callus that a thumb will dent. There is a handrail. It is copper, and it is warm, and it is not metal all the way through: at the joints you can see where the plating has grown over a core of something with a grain to it. A stair goes down inside the wall, thirty-one steps, each dished in the middle by feet. At the head of the stair an enamel sign is bolted to the brick with bolts that have become a single continuous swelling of the brick. The sign says CONDENSER HALL — NO ADMITTANCE WHILE PLANT IS LIVE. Below it, in chalk, in a hand that has been refreshed recently enough to survive the weather, someone has written: *quiet at the turn.*

Put your palm flat on the rim and you will feel a note. Not a tremor, not a pulse. A note, low, continuous, coming up out of the ground into the bones of your hand, and if you stand there long enough you notice you have been hearing it since the lane.

## Down

The stairwell is a flue between the wall and the stand, and the air in it moves upward past you at walking pace, warm as breath, wet enough that your glasses go blind at once. It smells of cut stems and hot copper and yeast and, underneath, of the inside of a kettle. Thirty-one steps down, the temperature settles at thirty-one degrees and stays there. It is thirty-one degrees in there in January and in August. It is thirty-one degrees in there when the field above is under frost, and then the lid steams at the seams, and the cattle come and stand around the edge in the morning like people at a radiator.

The light is ordinary. This is the first thing that disappoints visitors, and the first thing they stop noticing. There is no green gloom. The canopy is a mosaic of leaf-plates with gaps between them the size of a hand, and the sun comes down through the gaps in ordinary coins that move across the floor at the ordinary speed of the day. You can read a dial by it. You can get sunburned in there, in patches, in the shape of the roof.

The second thing is the noise. It arrives all at once at the bottom of the stair, the way weather arrives: a broad, flat, frying roar, the sound of a lorry idling on wet gravel, everywhere, at equal volume, with no direction to it. You will shout for the first hour and then stop bothering. Everyone stops bothering.

## The floor

There is no soil. The floor is root-plate: a parquet of fused roots the width of a thigh, laid in bays, worn glassy along the aisles and spongy at the edges where the traffic doesn't go. It is warm underfoot and slightly resilient, like the deck of a boat that has been walked on for two hundred years. Under it, a metre down, is the sump — black water, blood-warm, faintly salt, moving.

The old names are still on the walls, in enamel, at head height, at intervals: BAY 2. BAY 3. PLATING. STORE. WASH HANDS. The bays are still the bays. The stand grew into a building and kept its floor plan, the way a hermit crab keeps a shell's spiral. Where a brick wall stood, there is now a wall of bark-faced tissue with brick showing through it in inclusions, like fruit in a cake, all the arrises rounded off, all the ironmongery dissolved down to a rust-vein in the wood. Where a sash window stood, there is a window: six panes of clear leaf-tissue, epidermal glass, faintly cloudy, set in a frame of grown timber that reproduces the glazing bars exactly, including the putty line. When a pane goes opaque the stand grows a new one behind it and eats the old one from the back. It takes about a year. There is always at least one window in the hall that is doubled, and the light through it is the colour of weak tea.

## Rain, and the roof that makes it

It rains indoors, constantly, at a rate of about four millimetres an hour, and has done so since before anyone alive was born.

The roof is a living copy of a corrugated shed. The original iron rotted away in the first decades and the stand, which had already learned to hold the shape of things, grew replacements: sheets of stiff leaf-plate, corrugated to the same pitch, lapped at the same overlap, laid on purlins of laminated root. A sheet lasts nine years. Then it is withdrawn — you can watch it happen over a week, the sheet going leathery, then brown, then drawn back into the purlin like a tongue — and a new one unfurls, pale and folded, and hardens.

Above the leaf-sheets is a second skin, wetted, open to the air, and the last standing piece of the works pulls a draft across it: a brick chimney stub, four metres, capped with a cowl that the stand has plated and re-plated so many times that the cowl is now a copper fruit with a chimney inside it. The draft evaporates the upper skin. The upper skin cools. The lower skin, hanging in all that warm wet breath rising off the floor, condenses. The condensate runs down the corrugations into the original gutters, which are also plated, which also drip.

That is the roar. Four millimetres an hour, over eight thousand square metres, falling three metres onto leaves. It sounds like applause that never resolves.

The water is distilled. It tastes of nothing at all and then, after a second, of the inside of your own mouth. Drink it for a day and you will feel hollow and start to cramp. The stand drinks it back and puts salt in it and sends it round again. There is a tap by the stair, brass, labelled POTABLE, and it is not, quite, and everyone who has ever worked here knew that, and the sign is still there.

## The knock

Every nine minutes and eleven seconds, the hall knocks.

You feel it in the sternum before you hear it — a single hydraulic slam, a water-hammer travelling the whole ring main at once, and every leaf in the place flinches a millimetre and settles. Then the rain-roar closes back over the hole it made.

The knock is the clock. The stand runs on osmotic pressure: bladders the size of oil drums, hung in the bays in cradles of root, holding brine on one side of a membrane and sweet water on the other, tightening across hours until something has to give and a conduit opens and forty litres go up a pipe at five bar. There are two hundred and six of these engines and they must not fire together, and the way they keep apart is the knock. Every bladder sets itself against it. Every valve, every stoma, every plating bath phases to it. The organism does not have a brain and does not need one; it has a nine-minute pulse and a great deal of plumbing, and the plumbing is enough.

Do not cut anything. A conduit at five bar will take a hand off at the wrist, and the jet is fine enough to cut leather. The stand clots a wound in about forty seconds, which is fast for a tree and no use to you at all.

## Plating

In the west bays the floor opens into trays: shallow, black, warm, faintly steaming, each one about the size of a billiard table, each one humming. The hum is bubbles — millions of them, releasing at once from surfaces you cannot see, summing to a note somewhere around B-flat that has not stopped in three hundred years.

The stand farms a voltage. It has learned to concentrate copper out of the old wiring and zinc out of the old galvanising into nodules the size of walnuts, and it sets them in wet peat in alternate rows, and it takes the current off with root that has plated itself into a busbar. Low current, enormous area. It has been doing this so long that the peat has a grain to it, a striped fabric of metal and root, and a spade would ring.

In the trays, on mandrels of woven root, it grows fittings. Elbows. Flanges. Valve seats. A pipe takes eleven years: copper laid down a hair at a time on a template, and then the template dissolved out from inside, and then the pipe lifted, dripping, and hung, and put to work. There is a rack of half-grown elbows that looks exactly like a rack of pale fruit, and it is the single most beautiful thing in the hall, and nobody has ever seen it who did not say so.

And in the tall bay, in ranks of horsetail-lineage stems, gritty with silica, it grows insulators.

They come out glazed. They come out fluted and ribbed and skirted in the profile of a patent bell insulator of about 1870, because that is the profile the stand learned from the last surviving examples, and it has never had a reason to change it. On the underside of each skirt, in relief, grown, is the mark: HOLLOWAY & VANE — PATENT No. 4. Beside the mark is a second, smaller glyph that the firm never cut. It has been drifting for three centuries. It is now something like a tally, and it counts.

## The store, and the counting

The store is the driest bay, under a doubled baffle where the roof-rain is caught and routed before it falls. Racks of insulators. Spools of copper wire grown as tree-rings, laid down in the round and then unwound. Gaskets of latex tissue, stacked in hundreds, still faintly tacky. Everything is counted. The tally is on the wall: a slab of bark two metres by three, kept smooth, scarred over in a grid of small deliberate wounds, healed and re-cut, healed and re-cut, the way a slate is wiped and written.

There is more stock than the stand needs. About one part in forty is surplus. Once every few years the surplus racks are carried — slowly, over weeks, by root that grows into the shelf and pulls it down — into the reclamation bay, where acid roots dissolve the copper and the silica and give it back to the trays. Nothing leaves. Nothing rots. In a warm wet place with no animals, the astonishing thing is the absence of decay: the stand reabsorbs its own litter before fungi can get to it, and the air has no sweetness of rot in it anywhere except one corner, of which more below.

Take an insulator off the rack and nothing happens. Nothing at all happens, for a long time. Then, over a season or two, the tally corrects, and the horsetails put out one more, and the rack is full again. Take a boot, a knife, a bucket, and the same thing happens: the shape is entered, and after a while a version of it appears in the trays, plated, on a mandrel, hollow. There is a shelf of these. A boot with the lace grown as one closed loop. A pail with no seam. A pair of shears that will not open, because the stand does not know what opening is for.

At the back of the plating bay there is a mandrel about a man's height with a suggestion of shoulders. It has been there a very long time and it is probably for a cowl.

## The two ears

The canopy is a shallow vault, and it focuses sound. There are two places on the floor, four metres apart, marked by enamel plates let into the root-plate and worn nearly blank: SPEAK and LISTEN. They were an acoustic telephone for a foreman who could not be heard over the works. The stand kept the tubes — air-filled, lined with a smooth horny tissue, running everywhere under the floor and up the walls — and it uses them.

Part of the roar is not rain. Part of it is hiss shaped by valves, carried down the tubes, and it is the stand talking to itself about pressure and salt and the state of the trays. It is loud because it must be heard over the rain, and the rain is loud because the stand makes it, and there is a circularity here that nobody has ever got to the bottom of.

Stand on LISTEN and you will hear the sump: the slow salt engines tightening, a sound like a rope under load, and beneath it a soft continuous ticking that is the nematodes, or is not the nematodes. Stand on SPEAK and you will hear, faintly, perfectly, the field: cows, a gate, a tractor two valleys off. Say something on SPEAK and the hall will hear it. It will not answer. But the tally will change, somewhere, by one.

## What lives here

Almost nothing, and that is the point. No flowers — the stand suppresses them, cuts out any sporting branch within a day, because sex would end the individual and the individual is the works. No fruit, no seed, no carrion, no rot. Every free calorie in the place is already spoken for.

A wingless white cricket lives in the dry bay under the baffle and nowhere else, and it is the only animal in the hall that makes a sound of its own, and you will never hear it over the rain. A slug farms the yeast in the sport-corner, where the pruned branches are stacked and allowed, uniquely, to go sweet and soft; it is the only smell of death in eight thousand square metres, and it is a pleasant smell, cidery, and it is confined to about two cubic metres. Blind nematodes in the peat, in numbers that make the batteries work. A small pale fish in the sweet-water channel by the stair, which eats what the membranes shed.

And a grey heron, twice a year, on the rim. It stands at the lip for an hour or two looking down into the leaves. It has never once gone in. There is a chalk mark on the eleventh step, renewed, that may be a record of the heron or may be a record of something else.

## The turn

The shift is eleven days and a fraction — sixteen hundred knocks. At the turn, the ring main reverses. The bladders empty in sequence over about six hours, the sump drops a hand's width, the roof-skin warms through, and the condensation stops.

The rain stops. The hall goes quiet.

It is the most dangerous time to be in there, and the most beautiful. The hum stays, and the ticking, and your own blood, and the cows two hundred metres away are suddenly enormous. The tubes carry every sound you make to every bay at once. The stand is not speaking, then. It is listening, and it has sixteen hundred knocks of patience, and it will do nothing at all about you until spring.

Then the bladders refill, and the first sheet of rain comes off the roof with a hiss like something being fried, and the knock comes back, and you find you have been holding your shoulders up around your ears for six hours.

## Out

The outfall is a ceramic pipe at the north lip, running a thin steady trickle of cold clean water into a stone trough in the field. The cattle like it. It is the purest water in the county and there is nothing in it whatsoever.

Climb the thirty-one steps and the noise falls away behind you like a coat, and the ordinary noon is standing there exactly where you left it, and the light is the same light that was falling through the roof-gaps onto the root-plate, and the whole thing is behind you and below you and level with the grass: a green pond in a pasture, humming.

Walk out across the field and you will still feel it in the soles of your boots for a hundred paces. Nine minutes and eleven seconds. Then again.
