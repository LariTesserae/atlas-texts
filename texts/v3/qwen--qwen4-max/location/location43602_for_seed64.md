## The Cell

The marks come first, and they are easy to miss: a line of bolt-holes marching up the gneiss at hand-height, each hole plugged with a rind of pale mineral; a pipe of some grey alloy entering the rock and never coming out; a stencil, half-eaten by weather, that reads **CELL 4**. Three hundred and ninety-nine other cells were sunk into this range. You can find their doors if you climb for a week. They are stone now — not broken, not collapsed, simply *stone*, the way a healed wound is skin.

Cell 4 is at 6,712 meters, in the throat of a spire, above every cloud that has ever tried to cross this ridge. Its door is the size of a wardrobe. Behind the door is a vestibule cut by hand, with two hatches and a mechanical interlock and a brass rule stamped into the frame: **NEVER BOTH**. The vestibule is the whole of the built world here. Everything else that was made has been absorbed.

Open the second hatch and the sound arrives before the light does.

## Eighty-Six Decibels of Nothing Happening

It is loud. It is as loud as standing inside a working mill, loud enough that you must shout to be heard by someone at your elbow, and they will not hear you. There is no echo. Every surface in the cell is a soft packed mass of leaves, and the mass eats reverberation whole; the room is anechoic and deafening at once, a roar with no room in it. Shout and your voice goes out of your mouth and stops, like a bird hitting glass.

Three sounds make the roar. Underneath, a compressor at forty-seven hertz, felt in the sternum and the back teeth. Above it, a broadband hiss and clatter, the exact sound of heavy rain on a tin roof — except there is no rain, there has never been rain, there is no water in this place at all. It is the atmosphere being pushed through eleven meters of foliage packed so tightly that no leaf has room to hang free; they touch, they rub, they tick against one another in their millions. And on top, every eleven minutes and four seconds, a carriage clacking around its rail ring.

None of it swells. None of it fades. There are no gusts, because there are no gradients to make a gust; the gas turns through the cell like a solid body, laminar, the same speed at the wall as at the center. After twenty minutes your hearing gives up on the roar and you begin to hear only the beats, and the beats are so regular that they become a kind of silence, and in that silence you hear your own pulse, which is the only irregular thing that has entered this room in a very long time.

## Not Green

The lamps are forty-one incandescent grow-filaments running at four percent of design, throttled down centuries ago by a governor that learned the room's demand and never had reason to revise it. They glow the dull cherry of a stove ring seen through a keyhole. Thirty lux, perhaps. Enough to see your hands, not enough to see their color.

So the first impression is a black solidity, a room packed to the ceiling with something like wet coal. Then your eyes open and the black resolves: the leaves are blue. Not blue-green — blue, the royal saturated blue of copper dissolved in ammonia, because that is very nearly what they are. This biology runs on a coordination complex where yours runs on iron, and it works in the red end of the spectrum, and it has no use for green light or for green.

The solvent is anhydrous ammonia, held liquid at 6.4 bar and seven degrees Celsius. It moves through the plants as a cold blue liquor, sharp enough to strip the inside of your nose, and it weeps from every cut end and evaporates before it can drip. Nothing here is wet in the way you mean wet. Touch a leaf with a warm glove and the liquor flashes off under your fingers and leaves a rime of white-blue salts, a little frost made entirely by your own body heat. The room smells of ammonia, hot lamp-dust, struck matches, and, beneath all of it, something sweet and almond-like that has no analogue in any garden.

Seven degrees. Everywhere seven degrees, to a twentieth of a degree — in the crown of the foliage, at the rock wall, in the sump, in the gas. There are heating cables buried in the mountain's flesh to hold the wall at seven, so that the wall will not be a place where anything is different. Pressure varies by less than a fifth of a percent from corner to corner. Composition is identical in the leaf-canopy and in the compressor's intake. This is not a climate. It is an achievement.

## The Way

You cannot walk into the cell. There is no *into*. The lens-shaped chamber is eleven meters across and four and a half high and it is occupied — completely, cubically occupied, leaf against leaf against aerial cable against mineral strut, a solid foam of living material with no interstitial space anywhere in it. The plants have grown through the lamp grilles, around the bolt heads, into the bearing housings; the machine and the growth are one composite, and you could not say where the equipment stops.

The only emptiness in the room is a corridor sixty centimeters wide and twenty-six meters long, winding, shoulder-brushed on both sides, held open by a heated wire on a traveling carriage that cuts four millimeters of new growth per hour and cuts it again the next hour. This is the Way. It is the machine's single concession to the idea of a visitor, and it is not for you: it exists so the carriage can reach the far bearings. Walk it and the cut faces weep blue and freeze white against your sleeves and close behind you at the speed of a fingernail growing.

## What the Rock Is Doing

The walls are scalloped like the inside of a shell, hollowed into smooth flutes and lobes by three hundred years of ammoniacal vapor turning at a constant speed. It is real erosion, the patient kind, the kind that produces a settled form — but it finished. The flow and the stone reached an agreement. Now the mineral that leaves the wall is carried to the kiln, and the kiln feeds it back as the stiffening rind the plants lay down in their veins, and the plants shed it into the reducer, and the reducer returns it to the wall as plaster. The mountain is circulating slowly through the leaves. The scallops are a standing wave in stone.

The handrail at the hatch is worn to a groove by hands that stopped coming. The drive belt has polished its pulley to a mirror and now runs in a track of its own making and will not climb out.

## One Organism, Many Castes

Nothing in this cell has ever had a mouth. No mite, no worm, no moth, no bird, no bacterium with a flagellum that could be called an animal's ancestor. Nothing got in past the interlock, and nothing was ever invited. There is no pollination; the flowers are still manufactured, perfect and useless, and the machine harvests their pollen and uses it as a bearing grease, which it is excellent at, being uniform and dry and spherical. There is no seed set. There is no rot: the fallen leaf is met by a black digestive mat which is not a fungus and not a decomposer but the same clone in another mood, root-tissue that has been told to eat.

It is all one stock. Leaf-caste, cable-caste, strut-caste, mat-caste, and the strange flat gasket-leaves that grow in the shape of the seals they will replace. A leaf lives eleven years and is retired on schedule; a leaf unfurls on the same schedule; the total mass of the cell is constant to the gram and has been for longer than there have been nations at sea level.

## The Master Leaf

Evolution stopped here, and it was stopped on purpose.

At the end of the Way, in a brass frame under thick glass, there is a pressed leaf. It is the original stock, the holotype, dated and initialed by someone whose name is only two letters and a smudge. The carriage takes cuttings on its rounds and brings each one to a comparator — an optical bench, a lamp, a photographic plate of the master, a mechanism of mirrors and photocells that asks a single question: *is this the same?* What matches is planted. What drifts is incinerated in the kiln and its atoms are returned to the wall.

Selection here does not favor the fit. It favors the identical. Three centuries of quality control have made a lineage that cannot change, in a room where nothing would reward it for changing anyway. The cell has no future. It has a present with excellent tolerances.

## Rules, Briefly

Never both doors. If both opened, the pressure would drop and six tonnes of liquor would flash to gas and the room would kill itself in eleven seconds with a snowfall of its own blood.

Bring no water. Water is the contaminant. A drop wrecks the chemistry and burns a black patch through the canopy; a single unmasked exhalation would frost and kill a hand's-breadth of leaf. If you did this, the carriage would find the damage on its round, excise it, and grow it back to the plate, and the record would show nothing. You are the only event that can occur here, and the room is built to un-occur you.

The cell was made to answer whether a closed loop could hold. Beside the hatch, a chart drum has been turning since before the mine below was sealed. Its pen ran dry a long time ago. The stylus arm, still bearing down at the same force, has cut a groove into the drum's surface, one perfect circle worn deeper every revolution, which is the entire surviving record of the experiment: a wound in the machine, exactly the shape of the answer.

Nobody came to read it. The pipe goes down the mountain and ends in a sealed adit. The compressor pushes; the leaves rub; the wire cuts four millimeters, and four millimeters grow back; the wall dissolves and is replastered; the filament glows cherry; the carriage clacks at eleven minutes and four seconds. Outside, the air is too thin to carry sound and there is nothing to say. Inside, it is raining, and has always been raining, and nothing has ever been wet.
