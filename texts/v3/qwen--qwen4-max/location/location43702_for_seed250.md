## The Standing Water

The water does not lie down.

That is the first thing, and for a long while it is the only thing you can hold in your head. It stands in walls. A wall of ocean two kilometers high and eleven meters thick, blue-green at the base where the light has to work for it, pale and striated higher up, and at the top a fringe of white where the water is deciding whether to be water. It does not fall. It leans a degree or two, the way a candle flame leans, and it holds, and along its face run slow braided currents that carry nothing — no silt, no weed, no drifting body — because there is nothing here yet to carry.

There are hundreds of these walls. From the high ground you can count forty before the haze takes them, and the haze is not distance, it is shed water, the constant exhalation of surfaces that are being maintained rather than merely existing. The walls stand in ranks and in interrupted grids and sometimes in a spiral you can walk into and lose an afternoon. Between them: air. Cold thin air, five thousand meters above a sea level that has not been established. You can stand between two oceans with a valley of nothing between them and hear your own breath.

The floor is stone and the stone is new. Not new as lava is new — this has never been molten. It precipitated. It came out of the water the way salt comes out of a drying pool, except on purpose, and at the scale of provinces, and in the last several days. Its edges are still edges. Nothing has been rounded. There is no gravel, no scree, no talus, no sand anywhere in the world; break a fragment off a cliff and you have made the sharpest object for a hundred kilometers. The surfaces have the faint grain of a thing grown rather than carved, a directional shimmer, like the inside of a shell that has never had an animal in it. Wet, always wet, ankle-deep in a film of runoff that has no channels to go down because channels have not been invented here yet. The water spreads in sheets a centimeter deep across kilometers of unweathered rock and makes the whole plateau a mirror at noon, so that the ordinary sun comes at you twice.

The light is ordinary. That matters. A yellow-white star at a normal altitude, a normal blue sky above the standing walls, normal shadows with normal soft edges, rainbows in the shed-mist that behave exactly as rainbows should. Nothing about the illumination is trying to tell you that you are somewhere strange. The strangeness has to be reasoned out from the geometry, in daylight, with your feet wet.

---

It is cold, and the cold is a consequence.

Making stone out of water costs heat. Whatever is doing the making pays for it out of the air, and so the plateau runs at six or seven degrees and the water at nine, and where a new mass is being precipitated the temperature drops hard and locally: a wall of ocean will begin to knit itself into a buttress of pale rock and the air around it goes to freezing in ninety seconds, and frost blooms outward across the wet floor in a fern-pattern two hundred meters wide, and the mist falls out of the sky as a soft dry snow that is gone before it finishes landing. Then the work stops, or moves, and the heat comes back, and the frost melts upward as steam. You learn to read the fog. A column of vapor means something has just finished. A sudden clear cold pocket with frost racing across the mirror means something is starting, and you should not be standing in the footprint.

At this altitude your breath fogs. The water is cold enough to ache in the ankles and cold enough that a full immersion — and there are immersions, sumps, hanging pockets where the water has pooled without being assigned a shape, lenses of ocean a hundred meters thick with air above and air below and no bottom at all — will take your breath and then your judgment in under four minutes. Nothing lives in them. Nothing swims. There is no animal in this world, not one, not a mite, not a nematode, not a grazer or a swimmer or a thing that filters. The water is sterile in the way that a newly poured vessel is sterile.

---

The sound is enormous, and it is enormous because nothing absorbs it.

No forest, no soil, no duff, no snowpack, no burrowing, no bodies. Bare stone and standing water and gap. Sound here has nothing to spend itself on. A wall shedding at its crest — a continuous tearing hiss, ten thousand tonnes a minute coming off the fringe and falling two kilometers into the film below — arrives at your chest from thirty kilometers away as a distinct, identifiable event with a beginning and an end. The plateau has a floor note around forty hertz that you feel in the sternum and the long bones of the legs, and it is not the wind; the walls are being held, and holding is not free, and the holding is audible. Under that, constantly, the nucleation: a fine hard crackling, rice on a drumhead, hail on a tin roof, the sound of crystal deciding to exist, coming from everywhere the water is turning into ground.

And then the loud things. A wall re-decides. It doesn't collapse — collapse would be a failure and there are no failures here, only revisions — it *thins*, all at once, eleven meters to two, and nine meters of ocean has to go somewhere in a second and a half, and the noise of that is not a roar but a pressure, a slap of air that flattens the mist for ten kilometers and leaves your ears ringing and the mirror-floor standing up in rings. Somewhere out past the countable walls a mass arrives: a crack like a knuckle, like a spine, like the loudest single sound a solid can make, and then eleven minutes of its echo going back and forth between the ranks. You hear the geography in the echo. You hear how far apart the oceans are.

It is a loud place and an empty place, and those are the same fact.

---

Because it is empty. Not thinly populated — *allocated*. The water is vast in extent and negligible in volume: sheets, walls, lenses, filaments, a scaffolding of ocean strung across a continent, and by volume the whole standing sea is more than ninety-nine percent gap. You could walk for a day between two presences. A boulder the size of a house sits alone on the mirror with its shadow, and the next boulder is a morning away, and it is not the same kind of boulder; it was made to a different specification, for a different test.

There is nothing built here. No wall that was laid, no vessel, no instrument, no ruin, no mark. And yet nothing here is unengineered. This is the part that takes longest to accept: the machinery is not in the landscape, it is the landscape's grammar. The stone is a valve. The water's skin — a millimeter-thick selvedge where the holding stops, cold, faintly resistant to the finger, like the skin on cooling milk — is an interface, and it hums at a pitch that changes when you touch it, and it is not a device you could pick up and carry, because if you picked it up you would have a handful of water. There is no tool anywhere that is separable from the material it works on. The making runs on rules that are local, that are *in* the mineral, that no longer require anyone to state them. Whatever built this did not build it from outside. It taught the water a syntax and left, or never was anywhere to leave from, and the syntax is now doing what syntax does, which is continue.

The syntax is not finished. That is what all the noise is.

Walls go up in a day and are reclaimed in an afternoon, the water pulled back into the ranks and the stone it made dissolved and reissued as something else — a rib, a basin, a fluted plain — because the specification changed. Whole districts of the plateau are provisional. You can see the provisional ones: their geometry is exploratory, fussy, full of alternatives, three ways of solving the same load standing side by side like a stammer. The settled districts are simpler and quieter and slightly warmer, and the water near them stands straighter. The schedule moves across the continent at roughly the speed of weather, and it makes weather: the shed-mist gathers, the cold pockets drive convection, and there are storms here that are entirely the exhaust of construction, thunderheads born off a nucleation front, ordinary rain falling on ordinary rock in a place where nothing is ordinary yet.

Erosion has barely been permitted. Stone here does not wear. Rain lands on it and leaves without taking anything; the runoff film has no grit in it, and so it cuts nothing, and the plateau has no valleys except the ones that were issued. There is one exception, out east, a district where wear has been switched on: a canyon two meters deep and eleven kilometers long, the oldest canyon in the world, its walls already faintly softened, its floor holding — for the first time anywhere — a few grains.

The grains are not stone. They are the dead.

---

Because there is life, sparsely, and it is not native, and it is not here for its own sake.

Films. Mats. A crust of something between lichen and algae, sulphur-yellow at the margins, rust in the middle, a black in the wet centers that resolves to deep green when you get close. One patch per hundred square kilometers, roughly, and never two in sight of each other. They arrive as spores out of the gap, drifting in the shed-mist, and they take only on stone that has finished — that has stopped changing, stopped cooling, stopped being reconsidered. That is their function. They are the assay. Where the mats hold, the ground is real.

Nothing eats them. There is nothing to eat them. So they thicken into pillows, and lift at the edges, and die from the bottom up, and because there is no decay here either — no fungus, no worm, no bacteria with an appetite — the dead layers simply dry and lift and crumble, and the crumbs blow across the mirror and collect in the lee of the walls.

That is the sediment of this world. All of it. The only sand on the planet is the corpses of the only living thing on it, and the water gathers it into thin gold seams at the base of the standing oceans, and reads it.

Then a revision comes through, and the district is reclaimed, and the mats go with it, and the spores go back into the gap, and the loud empty cold begins again a little further on, testing something else.

Stand there long enough and you can feel what all of it is for. The walls will lie down one day. The gap will fill. There will be a level, and a shore, and the shore will have sand made of stone once wear is licensed everywhere, and the mats will spread and thicken and become something that could be called grass. This is the first week of an ordinary world. The roar is a nursery. The emptiness is not loss; it is room that has been set aside, and the cold is only the cost of the work, and somewhere out past the fortieth wall, in the ordinary daylight, a grain of sand is being made for the first time.
