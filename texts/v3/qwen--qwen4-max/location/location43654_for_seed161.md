Nothing in the world stands higher than this floor, and nothing in the world is flatter.

It is a slab of dark stone, an old welded ash-flow gone to glass and crystal, lying at the top of the air like a table left out after a meal. Ten million years of wind have taken everything that could be taken. What remains is a pavement: hard buttons and nodules of stone, wind-sorted, settled edge to edge in a mosaic no one laid, each one seated in the exact hollow it will occupy until the sun goes out. The softer rock between them is gone. The dust is gone. The corners are gone. Every hollow in the surface has been scooped to the depth where the eddy dies and stops scooping, and there it has stayed. The wind still comes, every day, and finds nothing left to do but keep the place clean.

You can walk it in a quarter of an hour, and then you must stop, because it ends.

The Table is four hundred paces long and half that across, a blunt oval, and on every side it drops. The cliffs are not dramatic. They are rounded, lip-like, worn back so far that the edge is a curve rather than a corner, and the stone goes down out of sight into the Veil: a floor of pale amber haze, dust suspended in the deep heavy air of the low world, moving in slow ridges like the skin on cooling broth. Nothing has ever come up out of it. Sometimes, when the Veil thins in its long decades-long breathing, you can see other tables far off, dim and flat and small as coins, and no way to any of them.

The air up here is thin and warm as breath. It is warm because the slab is warm — a black stone the size of a hill, soaking a dim sun for an age and never quite letting go — and because the deep air below is thick and holds its heat like a body. Nothing freezes here. There is no word for frost. The word for cold is the word for the wind at the edge.

The light is the light of an hour before a storm, all day, every day. The Veil scatters and eats the sun before it arrives; what gets through is amber and diffuse and casts almost no shadow. You can look straight at the sun. It is a coin, slightly frayed. Reds go to rust here and greens go to black, and the town's dyers work in a range of colors that a lowlander would call one color. But the air directly overhead is nearly empty, and if you lie on your back at noon and look up past the haze you can find a star, faint, in a sky the color of a bruise, and it will still be there when you look again.

There is no water on the Table. There has never been water on the Table. Rain is not a thing that happens; the sky has nothing to drop.

What there is instead is sap: a heavy amber fluid, sweet-smelling, faintly oily, thick as warm honey, that climbs wick and capillary and pore rather than running downhill, and does not evaporate, and comes apart in strong light. That last property is the whole reason this place is alive. Under the Veil's dim lid the sap keeps; in raw sun it browns and cracks and dies in an afternoon. So everything living here lives under a lid of its own — under felt, under crust, under stone, under mats, under the shaded roofs of the town — and passes its fluid hand to hand, mouth to mouth, root to root, in the dark.

The sap is not circulating through the world. It is circulating through the *inhabitants* of the world. There is no free liquid anywhere on the Table. Every drop of it is inside something: a stem, a body, a jar, a wick. The animals are the rivers.

The growth is lush and about two inches tall. Every fissure, every wind-hollow, every seam between the pavement buttons is packed and layered and overflowing: black crust that photosynthesizes in the amber gloom and smells of iron; cushions of felted rosettes so tightly wound they hold their own weight of sap for a decade; mats stacked four and five deep, the lower layers alive, the lowest ones dead and still serving as mulch and shade and wick. Walking on it is like walking on a mattress over stone. It grows a finger's width in a human lifetime. It has had a great many lifetimes.

The one thing here that resembles a tree grows downward. In the deep fissures, where the light cannot reach and the sap cannot spoil, the well-trees send pale trunks down into the dark, twenty and thirty feet, branching in the cold-warm cracks, leafing blindly. From above you see only a collar of felt around a hole. The town harvests them by lowering a child on a cord with a copper knife, and the child comes up smelling of the inside of the world.

The animals are everywhere, and they are near-touching, because the good ground is only the fissures and the fissures are only so wide. Tiles are the commonest: flat plated grazers the size of a serving dish, dark, slow, rasping the crust with a mouth like a file, each carrying a cup of sap in a bladder along its spine. They lie in herds so dense they form a second pavement, and when they move they move all together, a hundred paces a day, clacking. Chimers hold territories in the wind-hollows and drum the stone with plates on their bellies; the slab carries their drumming perfectly, so that a chirp you cannot hear with your ears arrives in the soles of your feet from a hundred paces off. Wick-mites work the seams, threading sap from a dying cushion to a living one for a taste, and are the reason the mats survive their own shade. And at the cliff edge, where the low world's heat pushes up in a standing column of air, the leathers launch: yard-wide, ribbed, slow, expensive things that cannot flap in air this thin and can only glide, riding the updraft around the Table in a long circuit, dropping to drink from a tethered herd and climbing again. They are the only creatures here that ever leave the ground, and they never leave the edge.

At the north end, in a fissure under a corbelled roof, is the Lip.

The sap comes up there out of the deep rock, warm as blood, at a rate of about nine drops a minute. It has come up at about nine drops a minute for as long as anyone has been counting, and the counting is very old. This is the only income the world has. Everything else is borrowing.

The town is built around that fact the way a lowland town is built around a river. It is small — six hundred people, perhaps, in a space you could throw a stone across — and it is stacked. There is no mortar here, because mortar wants water; there is no mud brick, no daub, no lime. Instead there is dry stone, and the stone is not quarried, because the wind has already done the quarrying. The pavement offers a finite catalogue of finished shapes, rounded and seated and pre-polished, and building is the work of finding the one that already fits. Masons are sorters. A wall is an argument between shapes, resolved with wedges, bone pins, copper staples, and a mastic of chewed fiber and resin.

Because the shapes are reused, and have been reused for a hundred generations, the town has risen. Each rebuild lifts the same stones a hand's breadth. Under the current floors are older floors, and under those, older, down to a first hearth lying on bare pavement eleven feet below the street, with its stones still in a ring. The streets are the gaps left over. The roofs are felt and hide and mat, laid on to keep the sun off the sap in the jars, and the whole place is dim inside, and warm, and crowded to the point of touching: you sleep against your neighbor's wall and hear their household through the stone.

Every jar is a fired clay jar, and clay here is tempered with sap, because there is nothing else to make it plastic. Firing burns the sap off. The kilns smell like scorched sugar and are the sweetest smell in the world and everyone knows what it costs. A pot is a measure of liquid that has been permanently converted into a container for liquid; the arithmetic is not lost on anyone. So pots are heirlooms, black and glassy and stapled with copper across old cracks, and the great cisterns under the Lip are the town's cathedral, and the men who tend them are its priests and its accountants both.

The dead are decanted. The body is pressed and rendered in the long house at the south end, and the fluid goes back to the Jars, and what is left is dried and burned to fire more pots. Every cup is a crowd. People speak of drinking their grandmother without any particular emphasis; it is simply the arrangement.

It is a loud place. The air is too thin to carry a voice more than a few paces, so speech happens mouth-to-ear, in clusters, bodies pressed together — the crowding is partly acoustic, an intimacy enforced by physics. But the slab is continuous and unbroken and rings like a struck plate, and everything that wants to be heard at distance uses it. Copper hammers on bedrock. The tap-code, which is a real language, with grammar, and which children learn before they learn to speak. The pumps' treadles thumping through the floor. The render-house pestles. The chimers. The herd's rasping. And under all of it, always, the wind in the fluted hollows: a low three-note drone that has not stopped since before there were ears, and which the town no longer hears, and which visitors cannot sleep through.

Nothing here is fast. The sap crawls; a drop takes a year to make the circuit from Lip to mat to tile to jar to mouth and back. The mats grow a finger in a lifetime. The town rises a hand in a century. The Veil thickens and thins over forty years and the dyers adjust their recipes once in a generation. The wind has finished. The stone has finished. This is the shape the world has settled into and it will hold this shape a very long time.

On the wall of the deepest cistern there is a column of notches, cut one per generation, recording the flow at the Lip. The oldest are near the ceiling and the newest are near the floor, and the spacing between them is not quite even. It has been closing for eleven hundred years. The keepers argue about whether the instrument is at fault. They are careful, in the meantime, with every cord and knot and tether in the town — because a thing that goes over the edge does not come back, and the world's total is finite, and the whole of the law here is only that arithmetic, said patiently, over and over, in the dark, with the mouth close to the ear.
