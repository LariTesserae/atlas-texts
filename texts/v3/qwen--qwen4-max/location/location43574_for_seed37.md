You find it by putting your hand flat on the scarp and sliding it along until the cold changes — a stripe of air about a hand's breadth wide, cooler than the stone on either side, breathing out at you at the pace of something asleep. The opening is a seam, not a mouth. You go in sideways with one shoulder scraping and your cheek against rock that has never been touched by weather, and then the seam lets you go, and you are standing in a room the size of a small chapel, eleven paces at its widest, and it is bright.

Not lit. Bright. There is no source. The walls glow the way a struck match glows, except that the strike happened when the rock broke and has not finished happening yet. Fresh fracture keeps the light of its own breaking; it holds it for years, for decades, a cold white with a green edge to it, bright enough to hurt, bright enough to see the pores of your palm. And it does not travel. The light stays within a hand's breadth of the surface that made it, so the room is a rind of brilliance around a middle of soft grey dusk, and everything standing in that middle is lit evenly from all sides and casts no shadow at all. You look at your own boots and they seem drawn rather than placed. Depth is a rumour here. The animals have opinions about this that are not yours.

The stone is pale, sugary, faintly translucent at the edges — a dolomite, or something close enough that the name would be a courtesy. Nothing in here is rounded. That is the first thing you notice after the light and the second thing you keep noticing: the floor is not gravel, it is *chips*, angular, sharp enough to open a knee, unsorted, unbedded, lying exactly where they fell. Gravel implies transport. Gravel implies a river and a century. Nothing here has been carried anywhere. There is no clay, no silt, no soil but a thimbleful in one corner, and that thimbleful is the most important object in the room.

The walls are still arriving. Every hour or so there is a tick like a fingernail on glass, and somewhere a face the size of a dinner plate has come into existence, and it is blazing, and for a week that plate will be the brightest thing in the world and the animals will orient to it the way sunflowers orient to a sun they have never had. The chamber grows a few fingers' width in a good year. It is not a cave. It is a cave's first draft, and it is being written from the inside.

The damp comes from the same place the light does. Fresh crystal weeps. Water that has never been rain, never been a river, never been in a body — juvenile water, squeezed out of the mineral at the moment of setting, beading high and tight on the new faces because it has not yet learned how to be properly wet. It climbs a little. It hesitates at the lips of things. There are three basins in the floor, each about the size of two cupped hands, and one deeper place the width of a thumb, and a drip that takes eleven seconds to gather and fall, and the sound of it lands half a second before it hits.

And every few days it rains in here: a fine, warm, barely-there drizzle out of the ceiling, lasting four minutes, tasting faintly of someone. There is no air movement to speak of. Breath does not disperse. It hangs at chest height and accumulates and slowly finds the cold ceiling and stays there, and when enough of it has gathered it comes back down. Nobody has ever explained whose. You stand still for an hour and you can watch your own contribution begin: a small cloud at the level of your collarbone, refusing to leave, and the pale fliers turning toward it, and the leaves — such as they are — turning too.

There are seven plants. There are always seven; one of them is always about to be. They are rosettes of a translucent, fleshy, saxifrage-ish thing rooted in loose chips with nothing to hold them but insistence, and their veins carry light instead of chlorophyll, so that in the dusk-middle they look like diagrams of leaves. They do not grow toward brightness. They grow toward *newness* — toward the crack that is about to open, the face that has not yet failed — and if you watch a rosette over a season you will see it lean, patiently, at a blank wall, and be right. On the oldest chips, where the glow has finally died, there is a fuzz of something fungal. This is the first rot in the world. It is very slow and very local and the animals come to look at it.

Because the room is full of animals, in the way a station is full of people. There are perhaps forty kinds and hardly any individuals: one pale cricket-thing at a time, one grazer visible, one hunter, and the sense of enormous attendance. The floor moves — one grain in fifty is a springtail analogue, white as the chip it stands on, and the whole floor is very slightly restless, like a page being read.

The grazers are limpets of a glassy, incomplete shell, and they scrape. They eat the glow. Their radulae take the freshness off a face and leave it dull, and dullness is the beginning of weathering, and weathering here has only just been invented and is being done entirely by mouths. Their scrapings are the first sediment. Their droppings are the thimble of soil. And the dulling provokes the stone: a face gone grey contracts differently from its neighbours and fails along a new line, and the failure makes light, and the light brings the grazers. The room is being eaten into existence. Its growth rate is a function of appetite.

The hunter is a long pale harrow of a thing, twenty legs, no eyes, and it does not stalk. It arrives where the prey will be. Sometimes the prey escapes by being earlier. And once in a long while — this is the part you will not want to believe — a grazer stops grazing, thickens, dulls all over, grows legs, and becomes the harrow, and the predation in this room is a metamorphosis, a population eating itself forward through its own life stages, and there is no way to count predator and prey separately because there is only one census and it is a matter of when you look.

Most of the inhabitants are not here yet. This is why it feels empty. The rock ahead of the walls — the unbroken stone the room has not yet grown into — is dense with waiting life: eggs laid in cracks that have not opened, larvae folded into solid mineral, seam-flies that navigate by the sound of stone about to break and land on a fracture an instant before it exists. The chamber's growth is a hatch rate. The room is sparse because its population is mostly prospective. Put your ear to the brightest wall and you can hear, under the tick, a fine dense crackling like a fire two rooms away, and it is the crowd, waiting to be excavated into being.

There are marks. A course of three chips stacked too level to be accident. A line scratched at the height of a hand, running two metres, then stopping as if the arm tired. A dry-stone wall, knee-high, a metre and a half long, standing in the middle of the floor where there is nothing to divide — built of the room's own chips, without mortar, without tools, without anything that could be called a device. It divides nothing. The room is growing, and in a century the wall will be at the edge, and in three the room will have grown around it into two chambers, and the wall will have been right the whole time. It was built for a geometry that does not exist yet.

There are two hollows in the floor, knee-shaped, worn — and worn is impossible, nothing here is worn — and above them, on the wall, two palm-shaped patches where the glow has been rubbed away. Old things go dark. That is the rule, and it is why the marks are the hardest things in the room to see: you find them by the absence of light, by the dullness, by putting your hand out. Someone knelt here a very long time. Or the stone has seen a wall somewhere, in the way stone remembers other rooms, and is trying to make one. It has made other attempts: a shelf at hip height, a niche, an alcove exactly the size of a person, none of which were there on the last visit. The chamber is embryonic and it dreams the furniture of older caves. There are proto-stalactites on the ceiling like wet nipples, with no drip rings, no banding, no time in them at all.

It is quiet. Not silent — quiet the way a held breath is quiet. The room has no reverberation yet; it is acoustically unfinished, and sounds do not go anywhere, they simply occur very close to you and stop. Your footsteps happen inside your ear. Your blood is loud, and the animals hear it and come, slowly, cold-blooded and unhurried, eleven degrees of ambient patience, and the warmth of you is a weather event, a front, a season passing through in an hour. The tick of new stone. The eleven-second drip. Wings. Nothing else, ever, from outside — no wind, no water, no birds; above you the weather is happening to other places.

Walk the perimeter clockwise and it takes fewer steps than counterclockwise. There is a chip near the entrance with a scrape on it from a foot that has not stepped there. Nothing decays except in the one corner where decay was recently invented, so the dead lie about intact and the fungus is a novelty attended like a birth. Nothing here has a past tense; there are no fossils, no beds, no rounding, no memory in the ground at all, and so memory has to be carried in bodies, which is why the bodies are so patient and so few and so crowded.

Do not lean on anything. Whatever you lean on may not have set.

And when you want to leave: the entrance is a fracture too, the oldest one, and therefore the darkest thing in the room. You will not see it. You go toward the dark with your hands out, feeling along the wall for the place where the stone has stopped shining, and the cold stripe finds your knuckles, and you turn sideways, and the room goes on quietly growing behind you at the speed of hunger.
