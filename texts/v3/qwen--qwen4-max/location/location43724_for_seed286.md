## The Standing Water

From above — if there were an above, if anything could get above it — you would see a cloud that does not travel. It sits over the high plateau like a lid on a pot, four kilometres up, grey-white, motionless in outline, boiling in detail. It has been in that position for longer than the idea of position has been useful. Rain falls out of its underside at a constant rate, and the same mass of water climbs back into it from the escarpment, and the loop closes, and the cloud neither thickens nor thins. It is weather with the weather taken out: a storm held at a fixed setting, roaring.

Under the cloud the plateau is green-grey and faintly ribbed, and it looks like moorland. Wet, windless, cold, unremarkable, going on to every horizon. Nothing stands up. There are no trees, no towers, no ruins, no stones. The ribs are the width of valleys and the height of nothing at all, and if you walked across them for a week you would notice that the ground was warm in a way that moorland is not, and that the hissing under your boots had a pulse in it, very slow, and that the surface was not soil.

It is leaf. Overlapping, shingled, cutinised, sealed. A canopy laid flat over a continent, taking rain.

And every few hundred kilometres, the canopy opens.

---

The openings are mouths, though they do not eat. Each is a basin of ordered tissue ten or fifteen kilometres across, ribbed inward, tapering, the leaf-shingles curving down and becoming something else — smoother, denser, honed. The rain arrives here as a solid grey column and goes down without splash, because there is nothing to splash against: the water below is already moving the same way. Stand at the lip (there is no lip; the transition is a slope of some kilometres) and you would hear the intake, which is the loudest single thing in this world, a broadband roar with a floor under it around eleven hertz that you feel in the sternum and the teeth.

Then you are in the water, and the noise stops being noise.

It is nine and four-tenths degrees. It has been nine and four-tenths degrees everywhere, within a tenth, for as long as there has been an everywhere. It is fresh, faintly sweet, oxygenated to saturation, and so clear that clarity ceases to be a property you notice — you have to find a wall to know you are submerged at all.

The chamber you have entered is the size of an inland sea. Its far wall is a hundred kilometres off and visible, pale, gently fluted. The ceiling is a lattice of struts so far above that they read as a texture rather than a structure. There is nothing between you and any of it. This is the first correction the place makes to your expectations: it is not a forest, not a kelp mass, not a jungle drowned. Everything here that is not water is plant, and there is very little of it. Ninety-six parts in a hundred of this world is cold clear liquid. The remainder is architecture, and the architecture is thin.

---

The walls are wood, but not the wood of trees. Pale as bone, close-grained, silica-flecked, and worked by flow into the shapes flow makes when it has finished arguing: scalloped, fluted, dished, the overlapping crescent-marks you find on the roof of a limestone cave, each one a fossil of an eddy that is still there. No edge is sharp except where sharpness is the stable answer — the leading edges of the vanes, which the water hones and the plant regrows at exactly the rate the water takes them down.

Nothing here was installed. There are no makers, no hands, no ruins under the wood, no forgotten civilisation whose pipes these are. But the resemblance is not a coincidence, either, and after a while you stop resisting the vocabulary, because the vocabulary is correct.

These are pressure vessels. These are ducts, throats, venturi, settling galleries, manifolds. In the septum between two chambers there is a countercurrent exchanger forty kilometres long: ten thousand parallel laminae, incoming rain running one way against outgoing water running the other, so that the heat is handed back across the wall and nothing is spent. There are filter beds of root-hair felt, graded coarse to fine, and they are cleaned continuously by animals. There are impellers — actual impellers, veined composite on lignin bearings polished by their own rotation, turning at a fixed rate, driven not by muscle but by osmotic pressure differential across a membrane stack the size of a county. There are gaskets of latex. There are check valves of toughened flap-tissue that close with a knock you can hear a hundred kilometres away, and they close four times a second, and they have never once failed to close, and there are nine hundred million of them.

There is electricity. Along the inner face of every major septum run plaques of electrocyte tissue, stacked like cells in a battery, holding a few hundred volts across the thickness of a wall. The water is too pure to short them. The field they make is a fixed map, unchanging, and it is how most of the animals here know where they are.

And there is production, continuous, at a scale that has no honest comparison: tonnes per second, forever, of exactly one thing.

The product is the plant. All of it. Every gram of output goes back into the walls that make it. There is no surplus and no store. A factory whose entire production is the factory, running at full capacity, forever, and never once ahead.

---

The animals are abundant. Summed across the volume they are a staggering number, and you will not see them, because the volume is a continent and they are spaced.

On the walls: grazers, blind, pale, radially symmetric, the size of a large dog, each holding a territory of about a hectare, each territory abutting the next in a lattice so regular it looks surveyed. They rasp. They eat the inner face of the world at precisely the rate the world lays it down, and this is why the chambers do not close, and why the ducts do not choke, and why the place has the shape it has. The architecture is not maintained by the plant. It is maintained by the argument between the plant and its grazers, and the argument is a draw, and has always been a draw.

In the throats: sessile fans, harvesting the fine drift, keeping the water free of anything that might settle. There is no sediment here. No silt, no drift, no fall of dead things, no floor of accumulation. The floors are clean polished wood. Nothing is buried, which means nothing is recorded, which means the place has no stratigraphy and no past you could dig for.

In the open water: long silver swimmers, unhurried, riding fixed circuits — not migrations, commutes. They pass a given strut at a given interval. They are couriers; the plant's messages are chemical and slow and the swimmers make them fast, and they carry their loads along the same routes their ancestors carried them, and the routes are not remembered, they are simply the routes the current allows.

Above, in the thin cold gas-layer just under the canopy, there are things with wings that never land, flying loops, breathing air at an altitude where breathing should be hard, in a saturated fog that is always the same fog.

You will meet, on a long traverse, one animal, then nothing for a day, then one animal. It is the emptiest crowded place there is.

---

Light comes down in shafts.

The canopy scatters what the cloud gives it and homogenises it, and then the plant pipes it: bundles of transparent fibre, grown, running from the skin down through the struts, fanning out into the deep chambers. Ordinary daylight, ordinary quantity, arriving in rooms with no view of the sky. You could read by it. Colour is true. There is no dawn here and no dusk and no flicker; whatever the sun is doing outside, the inside is one hour, held.

And the sound. Understand that it is loud — the intake, the valves, the impellers, the aggregate rasp of a trillion grazers, the rain transmitted through the skin as a permanent hiss. Ninety decibels of it, everywhere, always. And because it never varies by a fraction, it carries no information at all, and after an hour your hearing gives up on it and files it as silence. It is the quietest loud place possible. A shout would be an event. Nothing shouts.

---

Here is the thing you learn last, and it recasts everything.

The water is not sitting. It is hanging.

At this altitude it should boil; the pressure is wrong for a liquid, and every litre of this ocean is metastable, held together by its own cohesion, under tension, pulled upward through the plant's fine plumbing by evaporation at the skin. The whole submerged world is a column being lifted and never arriving. It is an ocean in the act of falling upward, arrested.

Sometimes a thread of it lets go. A cavitation bubble opens in a duct with a crack like a rifle, and the water column snaps, and instantly, before the sound has crossed the chamber, the plant floods the void and re-wets the thread and the tension is restored. This happens ten million times a second across the massif. It is the ticking under the roar. It is an ocean trying to boil, forever, and being prevented, forever, at exactly the rate it tries.

---

Nothing here is frozen. Everything here is being replaced. The wood you touch is a day old and has been there since before there was a there. Cut a strut and you will find no rings: no seasons, no good years, no droughts, nothing to count. The animals die and are born at matched rates and the population is a constant. The wounds occur and heal at matched rates and the *number* of wounds is a constant, though never the same wounds. Every process here has an equal and opposite process running against it at full tilt, and the sum is this: a vast, deafening, cold, sunlit, perfectly ordinary-looking stillness.

It has no history because it has no residue. It has no future because the future would look exactly like this.

Somewhere below you a blind grazer finishes a square centimetre of wall that took a day to grow, and moves on, and behind it the wall begins again.

That is the only event. It happens four hundred trillion times a second.
