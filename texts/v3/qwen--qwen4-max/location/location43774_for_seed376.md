# The Rind

From the col it looks like nothing: a grey-green heath filling three linked basins, thirty kilometres of it, stepped down eastward toward a gorge, the far rim sharp in the thin air and the peaks behind it white and uninteresting. Cushion plants, you'd say. Some kind of moss heath. Ordinary high country, cold and empty, the sort of place you cross in a day and forget.

Then the light comes over the shoulder of the mountain and the floor of the valley catches it, and it flashes — not wetly, but in lines. Long straight glints, parallel, running the length of the second basin, a hundred metres apart, and between them a faint hexagonal crazing like the skin of a cooling ingot.

The glints are metal. The hexagons are stone.

---

The mat is one organism, or one agreement between organisms so old that the distinction has stopped mattering. It is a cushion-plant the way a coral is an animal: a hard, resinous, dome-building thing that grows over its own dead and does not rot, because nothing rots here. Mean temperature is well under zero. The ground beneath is frozen to a depth nobody has sounded. The mat itself is frozen through most of its volume — a black-green ice-cored mass, in places eleven metres thick, veined with laminated metal and old dust and the pollen of plants that no longer exist upslope.

Only the skin works. A rind, three to nine centimetres deep, that the sun crosses zero every clear day.

That rind is the whole industry.

---

Walk out onto it and the first thing is the sound, which is the absence of sound. Your boots on the felt make a noise like a hand laid on a table in another room. The wind comes down off the ice in long shoves and you feel it press your clothes and hear almost nothing, a pressure change rather than a roar. Speak and your voice goes out about a metre and stops, absorbed, dead as a voice in a wardrobe of coats. What you hear instead is your own blood, the click of your throat, the small liquid machinery of your eyes.

The felt does this on purpose. Vibration is contamination. An ice die held to a tolerance of a hair will not survive a shout, and a press-bed ringing at the wrong frequency will freeze a flawed billet, and a flawed billet in a bus-line will arc and burn a gallery. So the mat grows sound-eaters: layers of hollow fibre, graded density, a fuzz that traps a pressure wave and turns it into a ten-thousandth of a degree of warmth. In the deep galleries the silence is so complete that the mat's own frost-cracks arrive in your teeth before your ears, a tick in the jawbone, once or twice an hour, and that is the loudest thing that happens in this valley.

---

The hexagons are patterned ground — frost-sorted stone, the ordinary periglacial trick where freeze and thaw shove the coarse stuff outward and the fines inward until the ground draws its own honeycomb. It has been drawing it here for forty thousand years. The mat has taken the pattern and made it a floor plan. Each hexagon, four to six metres across, is a bed: a rim of graded cobbles, a centre of packed silt, and over that a dome of the cushion-plant, low, hard, dark, glossy with resin against the desiccating air. The domes nearly touch. Between them run the straight lines you saw from the col: raised ribs of woven fibre sheathing a core of biogenic copper, laid dead level, running kilometres without a deviation you could measure.

Under the domes are the presses.

The mechanism is simple and it is the reason this place exists at this altitude and no other. Water in a sealed cell freezes and expands nine percent. That is all. The plant plumbs a cell, fills it from its rationed store, lets the dusk freeze it, and the ice drives a piston of laminated iron with a force that would flatten a truck. The die is ice too, grown to shape by a controlled freeze-front, replaced every cycle, accurate to a tolerance no human shop could hold at this temperature. The thaw at dawn releases the pressure, retracts, flushes, resets. Two strokes a day. Ten thousand beds per basin. Three basins.

It has been running two strokes a day since before there were people to be quiet in it.

---

Nothing here is built. That is the thing that takes longest to understand. There are no structures because nothing is allowed to persist as a made thing. Every part is seasonal. The presses are grown in autumn out of chelated iron, used through the dark, and in the spring the mat sends acid sap down and takes them back — dissolves its own tooling, draws the metal up through the root-ore, lays it down again somewhere else in a new lamina a fraction of a millimetre thick. The bus-lines are re-grown each year along slightly different routes. Whole districts of the second basin are sloughed in a week and rebuilt by the next month, differently, better or worse, and there is no ruin anywhere, no scrap, no midden, no bolt dropped in the silt. The valley is a factory that eats its own plant, continuously, and has left no litter in forty millennia.

The only made thing here is a groove.

Under the mat, where the ice has scoured it clear, the bedrock is worked. Polished races. Sockets, round-bottomed, worn a hand's depth into granite by press-feet sitting in the same hexagon since the last interglacial. Long straight channels where the bus-lines have lain and slowly, slowly abraded. The stone is the jig. The stone remembers the layout even when the mat forgets it, and the mat, rebuilding, feels for the old sockets with its root-tips and seats itself in them, because the stone is truer than anything it can grow. That is the balance here. Not stillness. A churn so long established that it has worn its own bearings.

---

At dawn the thaw front comes across the valley at about walking pace, and this is the shift.

You see it before you understand it: a change in the sheen of the domes, a line of dullness turning to gloss, moving west to east across the basin. Under that line ten thousand presses are retracting at once. The felt lifts perhaps a finger's width, in a wave. Ducts flush. Hoarfrost that grew overnight on the condensation combs — hectares of upright fibre blades, spaced like a heat exchanger, harvesting the little moisture this cold thin air can carry — sublimes off in a low ground-fog that runs downslope and pools in the third basin and is gone by nine. Reservoir bladders, sunk in the frozen core and kept liquid by current bleeding through resistive tissue, sigh and settle. The whole floor moves. It is the most violent thing you will ever see done quietly.

And it is never the same twice. A bladder fails and dumps its ration into a gallery and the mat retools around the wound in eleven hours, abandoning a hundred beds, growing a bypass, running a new bus-line diagonally across a hexagon grid that has been square since the Pleistocene. A katabatic squall comes down and rips a district's combs flat and the district spends the next season as a foundry instead. A late thaw cracks a die mid-stroke and the bed ejects its billet, a dull grey slug of iron the size of a loaf, and the slug rolls into the silt and is dissolved by Tuesday. There is a hail, some afternoons, of shed samaras — turbine-winged seed cases with a machined helical vane, spun out of the parent by frost-crack, thousands of them turning down through the still air, landing without a sound, most of them eaten back within the week.

The turbulence is total. The form does not change. Both of these are true and you have to hold them together or you will not understand the place at all.

---

Life is scarce and mostly not animal. The mat does its own pollinating by frost-crack, its own dispersal by ice-rafting down the spring flush, its own grazing: meristems that detach and crawl, root-legged, a centimetre an hour, browsing the felt of a neighbouring district, carrying stock with them as they go. There are mites in the water films, in numbers, and tardigrades in every drop, and that is nearly the whole fauna. Once a season a small black flightless bird works up the gorge and nests in a warm well where a bus-line's resistive heat keeps a pool open at four degrees, and midges come up off that pool in a column, and the bird eats and goes, and the mat grows over the nest site and files the metal back into the ground.

At dusk the freeze front goes east to west and the valley tightens. The potentials that have been building all day between the iron laminae and the copper buses find their paths, and along the ribs a low blue crawl travels, silent, unhurried, and the air smells briefly of ozone and cold iron and crushed resin. Every magnetite needle in every comb turns to the same bearing. Your hair lifts. The metal taste sits on the back of your tongue.

Then the light goes off the far rim, ordinary evening light, nothing strange in it, and the presses begin their second stroke, all of them, in the dark, in the cold, in a silence you can feel in your fillings — making, as far as anyone can tell, nothing but themselves.
