## The Witness Bowl

The air is thin and cool and tastes of wet stone and ozone, and it does not move. Not still — *unmoving*, which is different: stillness is a thing that could break. Here the same breath of cold sits against your face at the same pressure forever, and your lungs work harder for it, and the working never gets easier and never gets worse.

You are in a cirque, a bowl scooped from the flank of something that should be a mountain. Two hundred paces across, perhaps two hundred and forty if you walk the wet margin. It has a floor, a headwall, a lip. It has a tarn at its center the color of a gun-barrel. It has the completeness of a single object, the way a cup is complete, and when you reach the lip and look out you understand that this completeness is not an accident of erosion. Beyond the lip there is no valley. There is gray material — unfeatured, unweathered, without grain or bedding or slope, the raw stock of a world, waiting. It does not recede. It has no distance in it. Looking at it is like looking at the inside of your own eyelid.

So you come back down into the bowl, because the bowl is the only place there is.

***

Everything is wet and nothing is dripping faster than it drips.

The headwall weeps. Not springs — a continuous exudation from the whole face of the rock, a film a millimeter thick that clings and slides and re-forms, so the stone looks lacquered, looks like something pulled from a bath. Where the film thickens into a runnel it runs *upward* for a hand's span before remembering itself and turning down. You watch this several times. There is no gradient here — the water is the same temperature as the stone is the same temperature as the air, four degrees, always four degrees, no thermocline in the tarn, no warm side of a boulder, no cold pocket in the shade because there is no shade that is colder than the light. And yet the water moves. It goes where it goes without falling toward anything. It has a direction the way a shift has a direction.

The tarn never rises. It never falls. It has no outlet and eleven inlets and its surface is flat to a tolerance you could measure with a straightedge, if there were a straightedge, if anything here had been made.

Nothing here has been made. That is worth saying plainly, because you will spend your first hour in the bowl looking for the hand behind it and there is no hand. No bolt, no rail, no wall, no cut timber, no shard, no fire-scar, no path worn by feet. No ruin under the moss. The place is untouched. And everything in it has been *produced*.

***

The floor is a mat: sphagnum and something like sphagnum, waist-deep in the low places, ankle-deep on the rises, so dense it has stopped behaving like plants and started behaving like a substrate. It is the lushness of the place — a green so saturated it reads black in this light, holding water at its surface tension, breathing out a fog that never lifts and never thickens. Push your arm in and it comes out cold and clean and beaded. The mat grows. The mat is cropped. The two rates are equal to a precision that no ecology has any business achieving, and so the mat is deep and has never deepened.

Above the mat, the bowl is nearly empty.

This is the thing that takes longest to see. There are eleven tall presences standing in the green, and they stand very far apart, each in a clearance of washed gravel the mat will not enter, each alone in a volume of dim air. Black-barked, lichen-hung, unbranched until the crown, all the same height to the width of a finger. Ferns crowd the weeping crevices of the headwall in enormous lush rosettes, and every one of them is small and low and pressed to the rock. The biomass is immense. The *presences* are few. It is a crowded floor under an empty room, and the emptiness has a spacing to it, and the spacing is regular, and the regularity is the first cold thing you feel that the four degrees cannot account for.

The eleven standards are wet, and their wetness conducts. Under the bark the sap runs bright and metallic; where the trunk meets the gravel there is a green-blue stain in a perfect ring, and a fine copper film goes out from each ring across the stones in branching trails. Snails lay that film. They graze the algae off the wet rock and excrete metal, a plating a few microns thick, and they have been doing it since before there was a since. Follow any trail with your eye and it joins another and another and arrives at a standard, and from the standard a column of midges rises thirty feet into the fog — a living wire, a braided conductor of ten thousand bodies holding station in the air, humming, carrying something down from the cloud-lid that is not lightning and is not not lightning.

The light comes off the edges. Every sharp arrête, every fresh fracture lip, every needle of frost-shattered stone wears a small blue corona, a crawl of discharge, and this is most of what you see by. The mat glows too, faintly, the green of a watch face. The sky is one cloud at one height and has never had a sun in it. Nothing here casts a hard shadow. Things are visible the way parts on a bench are visible under a low lamp: enough to work by.

***

It is loud. You keep expecting it to be quiet, because it is empty and dim and cold and there is no wind, and instead the bowl is full of a sound like a foundry heard through a wall.

The fundamental is low, near forty cycles, and it comes up through the mat into the bones of your feet. Over it: the hiss of the corona, a sound like fat in a pan. The water-hammer in the headwall, once every eleven seconds, a knock in a pipe that has no pipe. The whir of the governors — bird-sized things with two bone spheres on tendons, spinning at fixed stations above the tarn, wings blurred, never landing, never varying their pitch, holding the tempo of the whole bowl to a rate they do not choose. Under everything, the small shift: springtails and mites in numbers that stop being numbers, a seething in the mat that sounds like rain on a leaf-litter that is not there.

And the tick. High on the headwall a block of stone the size of a wardrobe is fracturing. You can see the crack opening. Frost is wedging it, the fresh white scar is bright as a broken tooth, the block is coming away — and it is always coming away. It has been about to fall for the entire existence of a place that has no existence to speak of. The crack propagates and does not complete. This is why everything here is sharp. This is why the scree is angular and unweathered and the arrêtes could cut you: the bowl is not young because it was made recently. It is young because it is being made *now*, continuously, at exactly the rate it wears. Nothing here is old. Nothing here will be old. The freshness is not a stage. It is an operating condition.

Walk toward the headwall and the sound stays the same loudness. Walk away and it stays the same loudness. Cover your ears and it stays the same loudness, because a good deal of it is arriving through the floor and the water and the inside of your skull. Volume here is not a function of distance. It is a specification.

***

The workers are spaced like the standards.

Pale flightless things, heavy-bodied, hip-high, standing each in its own gravel clearance with the mat lapping around them. Nine of them. They do not walk. They pivot within a body-length and they crop the mat and every fixed interval each one lays an egg: white, faintly warm in a place where nothing is warm, smooth as slip, identical. Take two eggs from opposite sides of the bowl and you cannot tell them apart. Not similar — *interchangeable*. Count the whorls on two snails from opposite ends of a copper trail: the same count, the same chip on the same lip of the same shell. There are no individuals here. There are instances.

The eggs roll — the floor is level and they roll anyway — to the tarn, and the tarn takes them, and in the floor of the tarn there is a bore. It is not drilled. It has the helical scoring of a drill and it has never had a tool in it. The eggs go down it in single file and you can hear them go, a soft irregular counting under the forty-cycle hum, and nothing here has ever hatched.

They are not the product. It took me a while to understand this. They are witness pieces — the sample cast alongside the work to prove the process is holding. They come out identical, and identical is not what is being asked for, and so they are discarded down the bore, forever, at rate.

The work is the bowl.

The cirque is the part. The headwall is being held to a profile; the tarn is being held flat; the mat is being held to depth; the eleven standards are being held to height; the crack is a stress-relief feature that must always be present and must never resolve. The snails are the plating bath. The midges are the leads. The governors are the clock. The worms in the gravel windrows are sorting swarf by size, and there is no wind to sort it, and it is sorted. And once each circuit a long-legged thing comes around the margin of the mat, unhurried, and stops at whatever is out of tolerance, and removes it, and the mat closes the gap before the next pass. It has taken nothing yet. It has never needed to. That is the point of it.

***

You are out of tolerance.

Your footprints fill behind you at the speed of the fill, so that turning around you find no evidence you came. Your breath fog disperses into a fog that does not thicken. The bowl is patient with you the way a jig is patient with a burr: it will keep working around the interruption, and the interruption will be removed on the next pass, and the pass is always the next pass.

Stand still long enough and you notice that the mat has begun to leave a clearance around your feet. Washed gravel. A ring of copper stain, faint, still bright, still wet.

Above you the midge-column hums. The block ticks. The water runs uphill for a hand's span and turns down. Nine pale bodies pivot in their stations. And far below, in the bore, the counting goes on — the sound of a place proving, over and over, to no one, that it can hold a shape.
