## The Sill

The fog comes up the escarpment at the speed of a walking man, and it arrives bright. That is the first thing to understand about this place: nothing here is dim. The air is thin at this height, the sun comes through it almost unfiltered, and the fog is not a grey smothering but a white luminous medium, a bowl of lit milk. You can see the sun's disc through it, a clean coin, and the fog around the coin is brighter than the coin. Light comes from every direction at once and there is no useful shadow anywhere on the plateau. Squinting is the local weather.

The fog crosses the lip of the escarpment — a lip that is not rock but a laid course of blocks, each one two arm-spans long, dressed on five faces, set with a joint you could not slide a fingernail into — and it enters the ranks, and it begins to give itself up.

## The Comb

They stand on edge. Tens of thousands of them, slabs of a pale grey stone shot through with fine mica, each one three or four metres tall and no thicker than a hand is long, set upright in straight ranks that run crosswise to the prevailing wind for eleven kilometres, then bend, then run again. Between one slab and the next there is a gap of eleven centimetres. Between one rank and the next, the same. This is not a field you walk through. It is a field you walk on: the tops of the slabs are coped with laid flats, a lattice of narrow causeways at head height, and you go along them with your arms slightly out, above a hundred thousand slots of glowing wet grey, and the whole thing goes on past the point where the fog eats distance.

From the causeways it reads as a comb, or as the gills of something enormous turned underside-up, or as a city seen from directly above with all the streets narrowed to nothing. From inside a slot — and you can get inside one, at the district edges, where the ranks are still being extended — it reads as being between two pages of a book that is being read very slowly.

Every face is fluted. Vertical grooves, cut by hand, a finger's width apart, running from the top arris down to a collector groove at the base. Every face is tool-marked, and the marks are fresh.

## How the water moves

It does not fall. That is the second thing.

The fog threads the slots and touches stone that has spent the night radiating its heat into a thin sky, and it condenses, and the condensate does not bead and drop. The stone is already wet. Water finds water and joins it, and the film thickens and creeps, and the flutes take it, and it goes down the grooves at about the speed of a minute hand. At the base it enters the collector groove, and the collector groove is full — running full, brim to brim, no air in it — and it hands the water sideways into a feeder channel, also full, and that hands it to a trunk channel, also full, and the trunk channels hand it to the mouths of the cisterns, and the cisterns take it without a splash because their throats are drowned.

Water here travels by contact. It is passed along, stone to stone, the way a bucket goes down a line of people. There is almost no falling anywhere on the plateau, and so there is almost no sound.

What there is: a broad dry whisper at the sill, where the fog first enters the ranks and the air is still moving fast enough to matter. A tick, now and then, somewhere off in the field, when a film breaks — one tick, isolated, kilometres away, and then nothing for a long while. Under everything, at the very bottom of hearing or just beneath it, a tone. The slots are tuned. Each district's slabs were cut to a slightly different thickness, and eleven kilometres of wind through a hundred thousand narrow throats makes a note somewhere around the lowest a body can feel rather than hear. It comes up through the causeway into the bones of the feet and the back teeth. People who worked here a long time, if there were people, would have gone a little deaf in a way they could not describe.

There are no birds. There is nothing to make a wing sound. The sky is very bright and completely unhunted.

## Nothing is allowed to round

The arrises are sharp. Sharp enough to cut a palm, and the coping stones are worn smooth only where feet would go, if feet went here. The flutes have crisp shoulders. The spoil in the unfinished quarter still has the fracture-feather on it, the fine hackle of a stone freshly split, and rain has not had time to blur it.

This is not because the place is new. It is because nothing here is permitted to age. The faces are re-dressed on a cycle — a district at a time, a rank at a time, the film of accreted carbonate scraped away, the grooves recut to gauge, the arris re-struck — and the cycle is short enough that weather never gets a word in. Erosion is not absent. It is simply always behind. The plateau looks like something that emerged last century and it has looked like that for a very long time.

## The hands

In the shelter of the sill course there are racks of stone holding tools: cold-hammered native copper, chisels and points and small adzes, green-crusted, graded by weight, each in its own socket. There is no wood on this plateau and there never was; no fibre, no hide, no bone. The copper was found as metal in the ground and beaten into shape with stone hammers, and that is the whole of the metallurgy. Everything else was made with stone against stone, and with abrasion, and with patience of a kind that is hard to hold in the mind.

The tools are still in use, but not by anything with hands.

Along the base of every rank, in the collector grooves and up the shaded flutes, there is a grey mat: a felt of fungal thread, mineral-crusted, dry-looking where it is actually saturated, growing at the rate of a fingernail in a month. It is not the only life here but it is the largest and it is the one that works. It dissolves stone at its growing edge with acid it makes from the air and the water; it lays the dissolved stone down again behind itself, in courses, along the lines of its own threads. It builds by moving material a few microns at a time from where it is not wanted to where it is. It has been doing this long enough that the plateau's whole skin is its work, and the older work beneath is also its work, and it does not remember the difference.

The mats reproduce the tool marks. This is the thing that stays with you. They grew for a long time alongside copper chisels and the hands that held them, and now they precipitate stone in the pattern of a chisel stroke — the same overlap, the same slight fan, the same depth — without any chisel and without any reason. They reproduce errors too. On the north faces of the third district there is a slip: a mark where a tool jumped the groove and gouged a short crescent into the shoulder. It appears four thousand times, at the same height, on rank after rank, faithfully, a mistake become a motif become a rule. Whatever made the first one has been gone a very long time. The mat keeps it the way a language keeps a dead verb.

## The only flowers are salt

Life here is a set of films, sorted by chemistry. Grey mat in the cold grooves. Iron-orange seep-film where the trunk channels run over crushed spoil, slick as a tongue. White thread at the one warm throat on the eastern edge, where water comes up instead of going down and smells faintly of struck matches. Black varnish, thin as breath, on the sunward arrises, where manganese-eaters sit in the glare and take their slow payment.

And the blooms. In the abandoned interior, where the channels have silted and the fog no longer reaches, the ground dries out over a season and the salts come up out of it. Overnight, sometimes, a district grows a garden: gypsum rosettes the size of a dinner plate, needle-fields of white crystal that stand up out of the paving in ranks of their own, mirroring the ranks above them, catching the light and throwing it around until the whole quarter is unbearable to look at. They last until the fog turns. Then they slump, and go cloudy, and dissolve, and are handed along, stone to stone, down to the cisterns.

Nothing here moves uphill. That is a rule with no exceptions worth naming. Every fragment of mat, every spore, every thread travels in the water, and the water only goes down. The single upward motion in the world is the fog, and the fog is not alive, and the fog is what everything else is waiting for.

## Underneath

Dig anywhere. A metre, ten metres, a hundred. You will not find untouched rock. You will find laid courses, then fill, then older laid courses, then a floor with a drain in it, then fill again. The mountain has been quarried out from under itself and stacked back up as made ground; the plateau is a shell of work over chambers of work, and the chambers are the cisterns, corbelled, ranked, drowned, holding a cold clean water that has never once been rained on directly.

The cisterns are lit. Not much, but lit — the water films on the walls carry light down from the throats and the wet stone passes it a little way into itself, so the interiors have a faint even glow the colour of skimmed milk, brightest at the waterline, and you can see the corbels stepping up into a ceiling that is the underside of a causeway you could walk on.

Except one. There is a chamber in the second district with a fitted lid, dry-jointed, no film on its inner faces, no throat that admits water or light. It is the only true dark on the plateau. Nothing lives in it. It is empty and it was made empty and it has been carefully maintained empty for longer than the mats have known how to make anything else. It is the most obviously deliberate object here, and there is no way to ask about it.

## The long clock

The unfinished quarter is on the western edge: sharp spoil, gauged and sorted by hand or by something like hand, courses laid out dry and waiting for their grooves, ranks standing at half height with the coping not yet set. Beyond it, the escarpment, and beyond that the bright fog and the far warm lowland that no one here has any evidence for except the water it sends.

The array grows outward one rank in a long cycle. Behind the growth, the interior silts, dries, blooms, dissolves, and is left. The waterline in the great cistern rises and falls across decades and is read against a stepped gauge cut into the chamber wall — a scale with no numbers, only marks, and the marks get finer as they go up, which is either refinement or optimism.

Stand on the coping in the middle of it. Cold coming up through the soles, glare coming from every side, the hum in the teeth, the whisper eleven kilometres wide, one distant tick. A hundred thousand sharp grey edges, all of them cut, all of them wet, all of them being slowly and continuously recut by something that grows a fingernail's width a month and remembers a mistake.

The fog crosses the sill at the speed of a walking man. It has been arriving all day. It will arrive tomorrow. Nothing here is in a hurry, and nothing here is finished, and those turn out to be the same fact.
