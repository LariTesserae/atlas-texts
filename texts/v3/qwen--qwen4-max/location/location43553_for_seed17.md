# The Standing Sea

There is an ocean at five thousand eight hundred metres, and it is not going anywhere.

The rim it sits in is young rock — gabbro and black glassy basalt, freshly broken, edges still sharp enough to open a hand. No soil. No lichen. No scree that has shifted since it fell. The peaks go up another two kilometres into air at half pressure, where the sky is the dark violet you only get above weather, and the sun stands at mid-afternoon and has stood there, more or less, since the water was poured. It has moved a degree and a half. That is the entire history of light in this place.

The basin is two thousand four hundred kilometres across. The floor of it lies eleven kilometres below the skin at the deepest, six on average. From the rim you cannot see the far side; you see a blue plate tilting up into haze, and the haze is not distance but the curve of the world.

The water is one point four degrees and does not freeze. It is four percent salt, dense, slow-feeling, and saturated — deliberately, chemically saturated — with the exact mineral of the mountain it sits in. It cannot dissolve another gram. This is why the drowned cliffs still have drill marks on them, crisp as the day the bit lifted. Erosion here has not been slow. It has been *forbidden*. The water was told what to be, and it has stayed that way, and so the rock stays new.

---

## The Skin

The surface is not still. It is *steady*, which is different.

Along the northern rim, where the cooling towers vent, there is a wall of rain forty kilometres long and nine hundred metres high. It does not travel. It stands where the towers' plume meets the cold coming off the peaks, and it falls, and it has fallen in exactly that place at exactly that rate for nine centuries. Evaporation equals precipitation to within a bucket a year. It is a closed loop with a visible seam. From below, from inside the sea, the rain wall is a bruise on the ceiling, a region where the light goes granular and the whole roof hisses like fat in a pan.

Everywhere else the skin is a radiator. The sea's waste heat leaves through it, into thin dry air, under a sun that never sets. The books balance. The skin is where the world pays its entropy bill — that, and the sound.

Nothing flies here. The air is too thin and there is nothing in it to eat. All the life is under the lid.

---

## Clarity, and the Emptiness

Go down.

The first thing is the cold, which is not a wind or a shock but a hand closing slowly around the whole body. The second thing is how far you can see.

There is no plankton. No plants anywhere in this world, no algae, no drifting green. There are films — bacterial pastures, mineral crusts, sulphate blooms in copper and bone-white — but they are attached, they are flat, they are *farmed*, and they do not cloud. Intakes along the floor draw the water through clarification beds continuously and put it back exactly as clear as it went in. Suspended matter is a fault condition. It gets corrected.

So the water is a near-void that happens to be liquid. Visibility is four hundred metres, five hundred in the deep trench. Light comes down without anything to spend itself on, and at four kilometres it is still an ordinary bright overcast day — a blue-grey noon, colours stripped in order, red gone first. Warning paint on the machinery reads as brown-black. A cut finger runs a dark rope. The brightness is completely normal and completely wrong.

And the light does not move. The sun is fixed, the surface ripple is a standing pattern, and so the caustics on the seafloor — that net of bright wandering lines you know from every shallow bay you have ever stood in — are *still*. Painted on. A frozen lace of light lying over the rock, the same lace, in the same place, forever. The animals use it as a map. Some of them graze a particular bright line the way a sheep works a fence.

The shade of a peak is not a time of day here. It is a location.

## The Comb

Now the sound, which is the actual geography.

Sound in water goes fifteen hundred metres a second and does not want to stop. This basin is a closed vessel with hard walls and a hard lid, and it has been full of running machinery for nine hundred years.

The pumps hold thirty-one and a half hertz. Their harmonics stack up out of hearing and back down into it: a comb of pure tones, every thirty-one and a half hertz, from the floor of the body's perception to above the top of it. Underneath, the basin's own fundamental — a pressure oscillation with a period of fifty-three minutes, the time it takes a wave to cross the sea and come back. You do not hear that one. You feel your chest very slowly pressed and released, pressed and released, and after an hour of it you begin to understand that the animals are counting.

The total is a roar with no envelope. No gusting, no lull, no crescendo, no attack. It has never once started. Instruments that measure *change* record nothing at all and call the place silent. Instruments that measure pressure say that a lunged creature at the wrong coordinates would have its sinuses filled with blood in under a minute.

This is the world's only export. Everything else — every gram of iron, salt, carbon, slag — goes around the loop and comes back. Sound is the sole waste product. It is dumped into the water and it accumulates, and if nothing ate it, it would have shaken the basin apart eight hundred years ago.

## What Eats It

At the antinodes — the places where the standing pressure is worst — there are animals the length of a small city.

They are almost nothing. A lattice of piezoelectric mineral, quartz-analogue, grown in branching combs, and water. Ninety-nine point nine seven percent water. You can see the far rim through one. They are three to nine kilometres long and they hold station at fixed coordinates and they never move, because moving would take them out of the pressure maximum and they would starve.

They eat noise. The lattice flexes at the roar's frequencies and the flexing does work: heat, and mineral accretion, and a slow shedding of spent shell that drifts down and is harvested. They are the mufflers. They are the reason the roar is merely deafening instead of lethal. They were engineered, or they were selected, or they arrived and found the niche and grew into it — the answer depends on which of the pump-houses you ask, and none of them answer.

They bud in place. A young one is a bright shard on the flank of an old one, and it does not go anywhere, ever, and neither did its mother.

Below them, in the pressure map's other structure, are the **quiet rooms**: nodes, cancellations, places where the comb folds over on itself and leaves a pocket of near-calm two or three kilometres wide. There are perhaps eleven thousand of them in the whole sea, spaced like villages on a plain, and between them is hundreds of kilometres of noise you could not survive.

Everything with a gas pocket lives in the quiet rooms. Everything that speaks, speaks in the notches.

Because the comb has gaps. Thirty-one and a half hertz of silence between each tooth, all the way up. The animals of this sea have bodies shaped as filters — bone labyrinths, resonating slots, combs of their own — and they call in narrow bands inside those gaps, and they hear only there. A language spoken entirely in the slots of a fence. Two hundred species, some of them billions strong, all of them occupying a few hertz each, all of them inaudible to each other and to the machines and to you.

The census, if you took one: one large body per forty cubic kilometres. Which is to say, almost nothing, in all that water. Which is to say, six hundred thousand giants and some uncountable multitude of small things, in a volume so large that a swimmer could go a week and see only water.

Empty, and full. Both, without contradiction. The emptiness is per litre. The abundance is per world.

## The Works

The industry is scattered and enormous, and those are not in conflict; it is mostly *linear*.

Three hundred thousand kilometres of conduit lie along the floor, following the contours, going down into the trench and up over the sills. Electrode grids run beside them: the pasture. Lithotrophic film grows on the current, a copper-green nap a millimetre thick, and it is the base of every food chain here. The only green in the world is a bacterium eating electricity.

Grazers work the lines. Metre-wide, shelled, low, mouthed like a boot-sole, they scrape the film and move at a fixed speed on a fixed route, and their schedule is set by the current in the electrodes, and if one dies another fills the slot inside a cycle. They are rolling stock. They evolved to be rolling stock. From a distance a string of them on a pipeline looks exactly like a train, and the resemblance is not a coincidence; it is convergent evolution toward the timetable.

Every two hundred kilometres, a station: a pump-house, cast iron and concrete, zinc anodes hanging off it like fruit, a lit window, a handrail, a ladder up a cliff. Between stations, nothing but pipe and water and the frozen lace of light. Trim towers stand from floor to skin, eleven kilometres of narrow steel, singular, spaced — the basin is an organ, and it is being played by its own maintenance.

There are foundries down there that cast replacement impellers and nothing else. The old impeller goes back into the furnace. The same impeller has been made forty million times. There is no stockpile, no landfill, no growth, no product. The entire industrial apparatus of a continent exists to keep eleven variables at their setpoints, and it succeeded, and it has not stopped, and stopping is not among the things it can do.

Nothing here is at rest. Everything here is held.

## Marks

At the rim, above the skin, in the thin air, there is a gauge house the size of a wardrobe.

Inside: a chart recorder, still turning, still drawing. The pen has been at the same height for so long that it has cut through the paper and into the brass drum beneath, and it runs now in a groove of its own making, a perfect circle worn a millimetre deep. The last human entry in the log is a date and the words **LEVEL NOMINAL. NO ACTION.** There is no second entry. There is a pair of gloves on the bench, stiff, holding the shape of hands.

Bolted into the peaks at intervals: brass benchmarks, numbered, unpitted, the chisel marks fresh. A tide gauge with one scratch on it. A door set into a cliff face with nothing behind it but more cliff, and a cableway with a single car parked mid-span, and a hand-lettered plate on the trim tower that reads **TRIM IS NOT OPTIONAL**, and the paint is as bright as the day it went on, because the water has been told what to be.

## Rules

Nothing accumulates. Every gram is on a loop; the only things that leave are heat through the skin and sound into the water.

Position is acoustic. The map that matters is a pressure map, and the contour lines on the rock are decoration.

Youth is maintained, not achieved. The sea is nine hundred years old, less than one afternoon, and it will be nine hundred years old in every way that can be measured for as long as the pumps hold.

Change is a fault condition. Drop a stone. Sing a note that isn't in a notch. The trim towers will find the deviation, the grazers will turn toward it, the long animals will damp it, and the pen will go on drawing its straight line in the groove it has worn into the brass. There will be no record that you were here.

And under all of it, every fifty-three minutes, the sea presses on your chest and lets you go, and presses, and lets go — the only clock in a place with no day, counting for animals that have never needed to know what time it is.
