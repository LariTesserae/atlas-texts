Nothing here is ground. The flat you walk in from is spoil — clay dug out of a hole and set down beside it, ten thousand basket-loads deep, weathered now until it reads as a hill from a distance and only stops reading as a hill when your foot finds the edge of a shard. The shards are everywhere and all of them are the same ware: coarse, rope-tempered, fired to the colour of a bruise going yellow. They lie in the mud like a language with one word. Between them the marsh stands in shallow pans, and the pans are lined. You can see the tile joints under the water, hand-fitted, caulked with something that has gone to stone. Even the water here is arranged.

The reeds grow in courses. Not because they chose to — because the courses are where the silt collects, and the silt is what the hollow exports, and so the reeds are a ring, a green hem, and inside the hem the ground goes bare and slick and rises.

The rise is the thing itself. From outside: a low broad mass, slumped at the shoulders, the softness of something that has stopped arguing with the weather. Cool air comes off it. It smells like the inside of a jar. Under the moss the tool marks are still legible if you put your face close — adze, thumb, the drag of a wet palm smoothing a coil, and handprints, ordinary hands, the size of your hands, pressed in rows where the courses were joined. There are so many rows. Whatever made this did not make it quickly and did not make it with anything sharper than wood and a little green copper.

There are nine ways in. Eight of them are wrong.

---

The ninth is at the base, on the lee side, and it is a water-lock: a U-bend of fitted tile, hand-built, wide as a doorway, dipping under the wall and coming up inside. The marsh water stands in the bend at chest height. The interior floor is lower than that. It should be drowned and it is not, because the room above the water is full of air that has nowhere fast to go, and the air holds the water down the way a thumb holds a straw.

You go through on a breath. The bend is warm. Something brushes your ankle with the flat of a body and lets you pass, and you will think about that later.

Then you stand up inside, and the first thing that happens is that the room takes your small wet noise and makes it enormous.

---

It is one chamber. You could walk its rim in the time it takes to eat an apple, and the air above you would swallow a cathedral without touching the sides. Almost nothing is in it. A timber scaffold stands off to the left, adzed and pegged, its joints swollen tight with centuries of damp so that the wood now holds harder than any iron would; its rungs are worn concave in the middle, and nothing climbs it. A ladder of clay ribs goes up the far wall and stops. There is a hanging thing, a rope that is not rope but a cable of twisted withy, black, going from a boss overhead down into the water, and it does not sway.

That is the inventory. Everything else is volume.

And the volume has a shape. This is the part that takes a while. The walls are not walls; they are the inside of a mould. Every surface bears the negative of something. Overhead there is a great smooth swell like the outside of a shoulder, and beside it a region of fine parallel fluting, hundreds of ridges, regular as a hem or a gill, and beyond that a long taper that goes down and down and ends beneath the water somewhere near the middle of the floor. There is a place where the clay holds the impression of woven cloth at a scale that makes no sense — the weave is the width of your forearm. There are seams. There are the ghosts of pins.

Nothing that shape could have gone out through any of the nine openings. Nothing that shape could have been assembled inside and then removed. The hollow is a perfect fit for an absence, and the absence has no exit, and the room has been kept anyway.

---

Water is always arriving. The clay is porous where it is unglazed, and the outside marsh presses, and so the walls weep — not in streams, in beads, a slow constellation of swelling drops that let go at their own weight. The crown, forty-one flues baffled with dauber-clay into tortuous little labyrinths, condenses the day's warmth against the night's cool and rains it back down. It rains indoors here, sparsely, forever, in a scatter you can count.

The floor takes it. The floor is not flat — it is the underside of the absence, all shallow curves — and where it dips, slip was poured long ago and pooled and fired, so the glaze lies thick in the hollows and thins to nothing on the rises. Water sheets across the glazed lows, an inch deep, a hand's breadth after weather, and the matte rises stand out of it tacky and pale. The sheet is the room's mirror and the room's drumskin. It doubles what little light there is.

Which is: a few shafts, wrist-thick, falling from the crown flues, grey-blue, full of turning motes. They move. They move because the flues are plugged and unplugged by bodies, all day, in shifts, and so the light in here is a slow herd, grazing across the wet floor. Colour has mostly gone. Everything is the colour of wet clay and the memory of other colours. It is cool — not cold; your breath does not quite show; the water is warmer than the air at dawn and the walls hold last night well past noon.

---

Now the sound, which is the actual substance of this place.

Glazed walls, trapped air, a hard floor under a tensioned sheet of water: the decay is eleven seconds and some change. Nothing here is heard once. A single bead letting go from the shoulder returns as a chord, because the room only agrees to certain frequencies and it argues every noise into them. Under the drip there is a dry continuous clicking, like fat falling in a fire, which is the shells of the sweepers on tile. Under that, a seething — tens of thousands of small bodies in the crown cells, shifting, scraping, murmuring. Under *that*, when the pressure changes, the flues moan, all forty-one at once, a sound you feel in the sinuses.

It is loud. It is loud the way a market is loud, and there is nothing in here to look at.

The room has modes. There are places where the sound gathers and places where it cancels, and the map of them is invisible and absolutely fixed, and it is the only map that matters. At a node you can stand in the middle of the racket and hear your own blood. At an antinode a wingbeat is a drum. The residents roost at the nodes and court at the antinodes and are born knowing the difference. Property here is not area. Property is a standing wave.

---

Who lives here:

**The daubers.** Pale wasps the length of a fingernail, in numbers that make the air granular at dusk. They quarry the tacky rises, roll the clay into pellets, and carry it to the weeping seams, and seal them. Not all of them. They seal the seams that leak into the wrong places and leave the ones that feed the drop-field. Over generations they have thickened certain regions of the wall by a finger's width and left others alone, and the effect of this — the only effect it could have — has been to tune the room. The pitch drifted for a long time and has now stopped drifting. It has arrived. The work has become maintenance, and the maintenance is indistinguishable from devotion, and it is neither; it is simply that the colonies who tuned better ate better.

**The sweepers.** Blind, shell-backed, six-legged, hand-sized, in chains. Anything that falls — a corpse, a feather, a pellet, a chip of glaze, a thing you dropped — is on the floor for less than a night. They take it apart and pass it hand to hand to hand down to the lock and out into the marsh, which is why the reeds outside grow in a ring and why nothing inside accumulates. The emptiness is not neglect. The emptiness is a labour. It costs, every hour, and it is paid.

**The vent-fowl.** Small, grey, weak-winged, forty thousand of them, and their whole art is sitting in holes. A body in a flue is a stopper; a body withdrawn is an open pipe. They shift on a schedule nobody keeps, and the room's chord changes, and — because the flues are the only lungs — the air pressure changes with it, slowly, over hours, and the water-line rises and falls accordingly. The birds are valves. The room breathes through them. The water inside is a barometer running half a day behind the sky: the storm passes, and then, that evening, the floor floods. Everyone here reads the lag and knows what already happened outside.

**The tongue.** In the bend of the lock, longer than you are, patient as plumbing. It hunts the floor by vibration, lies at the place where the fundamental gathers, and strikes anything that lands wrong. It eats sweepers mostly. It ate the thing you disturbed on your way in, probably, or it didn't, and either way it let you pass, because you were not on the floor.

**The understudy.** A small sticky thing on the north wall that has learned the room's note and hums it quietly at the wrong end of the chamber, and takes what comes to it. There are three of them. The daubers eventually find them and plaster them into the wall, and the wall gets a little thicker, and the note gets a little more true.

And the midges. This is the whole economy. Vegetation inside amounts to a moss in the ladder joints, a fungus on the scaffold, three pale rootless things drinking condensation out of the pin-holes, and one reed. The room cannot feed itself. What feeds it is the marsh outside, and what brings the marsh in is the note. At dusk the clouds come down through the flues in their millions, drawn to a call, and the air goes thick, and everything in here eats.

They come because the note is the note of something they used to answer to. Whatever the mould was moulded around, it had a voice, and the midges were tuned to it once, and they still come when it sounds. The daubers have spent centuries reconstructing, by pure accident and appetite, the voice of an animal that is not here. The room is a lure shaped like a ghost. It works. It works better every century.

The copper pins are the only metal — a scatter of them, thumb-length, green, standing proud where they once held a core in place. The fowl lick them. The lichen eats them. In a few hundred years there will be no metal at all, and the note will not change.

---

The rules, as far as they can be inferred:

Nothing may remain on the floor.
Do not plug a flue that is not yours.
Silence is shelter; sound is property; the loud places are where you go to be seen.
Read the water, and remember it is telling you about yesterday.
If you add clay, add it where the note is flat.

If you stand still for an hour the daubers will begin, very gently, to seal your boots to the tile. If you speak, the room will go on speaking for eleven seconds after you stop, in your voice, corrected. If you drop something it will be gone by morning and you will not see it go, and the chains will not part around you, and you will feel them climb your leg to check whether you are fallen or standing.

The reed is the exception. It came up through the lock and it should have been carried out with everything else, and it wasn't, because it stands at a certain place near the taper and its stem splits a draft in a way the room approves of. It has been there a very long time. It is the only living thing permitted to stay, and it is permitted because it is, functionally, a piece of the instrument.

---

Once or twice a season the pressure and the roosting and the weather agree, and all forty-one flues open together, and the whole hollow sounds one note — not loud, exactly; total. The sheet-water shivers into rings. The sweepers stop. The midges come down the flues like smoke going the wrong way. It lasts as long as it lasts, and then the bodies resettle, one and one and one, and the chord falls apart into ordinary racket, and the chains start up again on the tile.

Nothing in here made that sound on purpose. Nothing in here is waiting for anything. The seams are sealed, the floor is clear, the pitch is true, and the hollow holds the shape of what left it, perfectly, at the right temperature, in tune, for no one — the way a throat is kept clear.
