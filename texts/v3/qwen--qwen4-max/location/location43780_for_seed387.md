## The Bell

You do not hear it. Hearing is a thing that happens to smaller sounds. What happens here is that your chest is taken over and used as a room. The ribs go taut like the walls of a drum with something enormous standing on it; the fluid in your eyes shivers and the lamps smear into rings; your back teeth ache in their sockets, and somewhere behind your sternum a note is being held that your body did not agree to hold. Two hundred paces in from the adit, people stop being able to feel where they end. The water is blood-warm. The air is blood-warm. The noise is inside you at blood-warmth, and the boundary between person and place gets soft and negotiable, and stays that way for as long as you remain.

The old hands wear quilted harnesses, felt and lead-cloth and horsehair, laced tight over the lungs and belly. Not ear protection — ears are irrelevant, ears gave up centuries ago. The harnesses are tuned. Each one is stitched for its owner's particular resonance, tested by having the wearer stand at a known station and report where in their body the sound tries to get in. A well-made coat makes you a bad instrument. That is the whole of safety here: to be poorly shaped for the note.

---

The chamber is a bell in shape and a bell in behavior: six hundred meters from crown to throat, four hundred at its widest flare, a smooth scalloped dome of glassy dark rock above, walls that curve out and down like the inside of a cheek. It is a single bubble in miles of stone. Above it lies a dry plateau with wind on it and a scatter of vent-houses and nothing else, no sign, no reason to stop. Below it the water goes on down into a plumbing that has never been mapped, and above it the water comes in.

It comes in through three hundred and twelve ports arranged in a ring at the crown, and it falls as a sleeve — an annular curtain, a hollow cylinder of water dropping the full height of the world. From a balcony on the eleventh gallery you look out through it into the middle of the Bell, and the middle is empty. That is the first thing that confuses people. This is a city, and it is almost entirely hollow. The built fabric is a rind, forty-one galleries stacked and cut and bolted into the wall, a lacework of iron and stone perhaps thirty meters deep at its thickest, and inside that rind: nothing. A gulf of falling water and torn mist and warm dark, four hundred meters across, with one amber lamp visible on the far side like a coal at the bottom of a well, and between you and it no floor, no bridge, no anything.

The sleeve does not fall as a sheet for long. It holds together for maybe eighty meters, glassy, ribbed, faintly luminous where the crown lamps catch it, and then it goes to ropes, and then the ropes go to braid and hank and drifting cloud, and the last two hundred meters are weather. Warm rain falls upward in the updraft near the wall. It rains sideways on the twenty-second. There are places on the thirty-fourth where it has not stopped raining indoors for nine hundred years, and the tenements there are worn to the smoothness of soap.

---

Everything is round. This is the clock by which the place is dated. There is no sharp edge anywhere in the Bell — not on the handrails, which have been worn to a wafer you can see lamplight through; not on the stair treads, dished in the center like spoons; not on the cast-iron balusters, which have gone from ornamental fluting to soft bulges to a kind of bone-shape. The rock itself is scalloped in overlapping thumbprints, the pattern water makes when it has been doing the same thing forever. Fresh edges are alarming. A new burr on a rail means something moved. Children are taught before they are taught anything else: *if it is sharp, it is wrong, tell someone.*

And yet the churn is violent beyond any accounting. The sleeve surges and slumps minute to minute; the plunge pool at the base is a boiling mountain range that never holds a shape for a full second; the updraft throws sheets of spray against the galleries hard enough to strip a badly secured shutter into splinters. There is a season to it, a spring flood when the ports run fat and the sleeve is a solid grinding column, and a lean season when the curtain thins to lace and you can see the far wall clearly for the first time in months and everyone goes out to look at their neighbors' laundry.

Both are true. The turbulence is ancient. The storm has been running so long that it has become the settled state, and everything here is in balance with chaos — worn into the exact shape that chaos permits, like a stone in a river that has stopped being surprised.

---

The city is a machine with dwellings in it, or dwellings with a machine in them; the argument is old and pointless. The water does not merely fall past the Bell, it falls *through* it: penstocks tap the sleeve at nine heights and carry it into turbine halls cut back into the rock, and the halls are the only brightly lit places, arc-lamps hissing white at the governor faces, brass gone soft-edged, oil mist, the smell of hot iron under all the iron smell of the water. The generators turn in their pits. Cables leave through a conduit in the crown, three of them, each as thick as a man's waist, warm to the touch, humming a different note from the sleeve's note, and they go up and out under the plateau and away to somewhere that has never been described to anyone here in terms more specific than *up the line*.

The Bell uses a fraction of what it makes. The rest is the point. What comes back down the line, in the freight cage, twice a year: salt, lamp filament, medicine, paper, boots, needles, a crate of dried fruit that everyone agrees is not worth the argument it causes. The ledger continues. The ledger has continued through everything.

Two hundred and fourteen people, according to the census board on the ninth, which nobody trusts. The Bell was built for ninety thousand. Whole galleries stand empty and mineralized, sinter sealing the doorways into smooth blind mouths, staircases going up into flowstone. You can walk for an hour through fully appointed districts — kitchens, latrines, a schoolroom with its slates still stacked — and see no one, and hear nothing but the note, and understand that the emptiness here is not desolation. It is just volume. There is so much room.

---

Nothing has ever had a leaf in this place. There is no green, no wood that was not carried down in a crate, no spore-drift of anything recognizable. What lives on the warm walls is a nap: a gray-brown film, millimeter-thin, slick as the inside of an oyster, chemosynthetic, tasting of iron and faintly of egg. It is scraped into hods and fermented in vats behind the fourteenth and comes out as a pale paste that keeps people alive and is not, by any account, food. In the slow pools it grows in dendrites, branching fractal fans that look exactly like ferns pressed in shale and are mineral, are simply manganese doing what manganese does, and are beautiful, and are not alive, and everyone at some point makes the mistake of thinking otherwise.

Fauna is a rumor with a ledger. The gaunt: blind, hand-length, ribbon-bodied, colorless, hanging in the pressure nodes of the standing wave with their heads pointed into the roar, perhaps one per few thousand cubic meters, so that seeing one is an event you mention at supper. Something in the sinter pools that ticks. And the Tenant, which is a shape in the water rather than an animal anyone has held, recorded six times in forty years, each entry a pressure event and a displaced sleeve and a description that the recorder afterward crossed out and rewrote and crossed out again.

The lower eleven galleries are drowned. Warm water, no current to speak of, corridors you swim with a lamp in your teeth, tenements where the silt lies undisturbed and the doorframes have grown shut. Salvage divers work them in short breath-holds because the water is thirty-five degrees and offers no cooling and no warning. Down there the accretion is fastest and the walls are thickest and the good recordings are.

---

Because the walls record. This is the thing about the Bell that visitors are told last, and only if they have stayed a while.

The sinter lays down in laminae, a fraction of a millimeter a day, and the laminae are modulated — the flow at the growing face is perturbed by the sound in the room, and the stone takes the perturbation and keeps it, layer on layer, groove within groove, a hundred years of noise stacked in a wall like the rings of a tree that grew inside a shout. And when the water eats back into that wall at the right angle and the right rate, it releases it.

At flow rate 41.6 the fourth stairwell of the drowned nineteenth says something in a woman's voice, counting. At 52.2 there is a strike: whistles, and a crowd, and a man being told to come down off a thing. At 60.0 exactly, for eleven seconds, the wall behind the old crèche produces a sound that has been formally logged as *laughter, several, young*, and which is the reason the gaugers keep the index at all.

The archive is a rate, not a place. You cannot go to it. You can only wait for the water to be a particular amount, and stand where the erosion has reached a particular depth, and be lucky. There are people here who have spent their lives learning to read the sleeve's number off a brass dial and then running, running through the roar with their coat laced tight, to arrive at a wall in time to hear four seconds of the year 612.

---

And there are the hushes.

The sleeve's roar and the chamber's shape make a standing wave, and a standing wave has nodes, and in a node the sound cancels itself. Not everywhere; in pockets. Small ones, a few meters across, wandering as the flow wanders. In a hush you can hear a spoon set down on a table. You can hear your own voice, which is a thing most people here experience perhaps a thousand times in a life and never get used to. The nursery is in a hush. The infirmary is in a hush. Rent is priced in hush-hours, and the map of the nulls is the oldest continuously maintained document in the Bell, nine centuries of little wandering circles inked and re-inked, the whole quiet geography of the city precessing slowly across the galleries like weather over a continent.

The nullwrights are the guild that matters. They hang baffle plates, quarry stone horns, shift the ports at the crown by fractions, all to steer a null and pin it over a bed or a birthing room. They work by rail-code — a hammer tapped along the iron, the vibration carried through the frame of the city, a language of knocks that everyone learns before they learn letters. Nobody speaks here. Speech is for the Slack.

Every nine to forty hours the gates upstream throttle for maintenance, and the sleeve leans out, and the roar drops to merely a roar, and for ninety seconds the Bell can hear itself think.

Everything happens in those ninety seconds. Verdicts, marriages, arguments, confessions, the naming of children, all of it conducted at a sprint by people who have been composing their sentence for a day and a half. The entire oral literature of the city is sized to fit that window; the songs are ninety seconds long, all of them, every one, and there are thousands. Then the water comes back and takes the words away and the walls begin, patiently, to write them down.

And in the last few seconds of the Slack, if you are low and near the throat, you can hear the drain. The next chamber. Something below, answering in a lower note, on the same schedule, always.

They have never gone down to look. The ledger continues. The cables go up warm. On the eleventh gallery a woman leans into the noise the way you lean into weather, one hand on a rail worn to the exact shape of hands, and looks out across four hundred meters of falling water at a single amber lamp, and is, in all that hollow roaring stone, entirely at home.
