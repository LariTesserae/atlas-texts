## The Bowl

The ice left here ninety-one winters ago, and the rock has not forgiven it yet. Everything is still sharp: the headwall's plucked steps, the chatter marks crescented across the slabs like fingernail parings, the moraine rubble stacked in angular blocks with edges you could open a hand on. There is no weathering rind. Break a stone and the inside is the same grey-green as the outside. No lichen has had time to make the first orange coin. In a place this newly made, the only old thing is the rock itself, and the only soft thing is what has come since.

It is a north-facing cirque, a bowl bitten out of the range at the height where trees give up, and it spends most of the year in its own shadow. The sun clears the eastern rim for forty minutes at midsummer and less on either side of that, arriving as a single bar of light through a low col — a notch, a gap between two horns of stone — and then leaving. The rest is cloud-light, blue and diffuse and constant, the kind of dimness that has no direction and casts no shadow, so that distances flatten and the bowl looks like a diagram of itself. Cold sits in it the way water sits in a cup. Frost most nights, even in the warm month. A rind of rime on every exposed edge by dawn, gone by noon, back by dusk.

And it weeps. The remnant glacier at the head is a dirty grey tongue now, no bigger than a church, and it melts a little every afternoon; but more than that, the walls sweat. Every joint in the granodiorite gives water. The air, pushed up the valley and cooled against the headwall, drops its moisture directly onto the stone in a film that gathers, runs, drops, gathers. Standing at the rim you can hear the whole basin ticking with seepage, a hundred thousand small arrivals.

From up here, though, the strangeness is not the water. It is that the floor of the bowl is *flat*, and green, and level as a table, and it is not a floor. It is a roof.

## The Lid and the Door

Three hundred paces across, packed edge to edge against the moraine, lies a continuous cushion of living tissue — dark, wine-brown to nearly black at the tips where the cold has pigmented it, hairy, dense enough to walk on, dished slightly in the middle where last winter's snow load pressed it. Nothing protrudes. No shoots, no branches, no flowers. It looks like a moss lawn that has been rolled flat by a century of weather, and it is the top storey of a town.

The way in is at the moraine, on the down-valley side, where the growth meets the rubble and cannot quite seal itself. There is a gap there about the height of a person. It has a lintel. It has jambs. It has, unmistakably, the shape of a door, and it was not made for anything.

Inside, the temperature drops another degree and the sound drops away entirely, and the smell arrives: cold sap, wet cork, crushed pepper, iron, the mineral sweetness of water that has never been warm. The light is green. Not leaf-green — *transmitted* green, the color of the inside of a wave, arriving from above through eleven hand-spans of packed cushion and losing everything but its middle wavelengths on the way. You can see. You cannot see far.

## The Rule That Built It

The founder was a fragment of something with the ancestry of a saxifrage and the appetite of a willow, carried in on the mud of a bird's foot in the second decade after the ice. It has one property that explains everything below the lid: **any two of its tissues that touch, fuse.** Not adhere — fuse, cambium to cambium, plumbing to plumbing, until the seam is stronger than either side.

In an open meadow that habit makes a hummock. In a closed bowl with no herbivores, no competitors, and water coming out of every wall, it makes architecture. Stems reach, meet, join. The join hardens. New stems reach from the join. A hundred years of that, at a season's growth per year, and the bowl has filled with a self-welded lattice: columns where verticals bunched and lignified, arcades where laterals met over a gap, vaults where the reaching fronts closed overhead and kept going. Six and seven storeys of it in the deep middle, stepping down to two at the edges. The old growth is brown and glassy-hard, dense as horn, cold to the touch and slick with condensate. The new growth is pale green and fat and squeezes water out when you lean on it. There is no gap anywhere wider than a shoulder. Every surface is occupied by something that is, in the end, the same individual.

It is not a forest and not a thicket. It is *masonry that is alive*, and it is under construction, and it has never yet had a ruin.

## The Plumbing Is the Plan

Water comes in at the head and the walls, and the plant grows toward water the way ordinary plants grow toward light. So the seeps are the skeleton. Where a joint in the rock runs steadily, the growth around it has thickened into a fluted trunk that carries the flow down inside itself — a living downpipe, cold and sweating, humming faintly if you put an ear to it. Where seeps meet, the trunks meet and fuse, and there is a junction chamber, and there is a floor of laid-down litter pressed flat as flagstone. The main seep runs the length of the bowl and everything is arranged along it. There is a high street. It is wet, it is a hand's depth in slow water, and it goes from the ice at the head to the lowest cistern at the lip, and every district opens onto it.

Zoning here is hydraulic. Near the seeps: hard, structural, load-bearing, dark, the wood thick enough to strike a note off. Away from them, in the outer wards where water arrives only as seepage and film: soft, pale, spongy tissue, cushion piled on cushion, corridors that close behind you in a season, rooms that are really just the negative space of a fat year. The rim districts are the poor districts and the growing ones. The center is old money — which is to say, old water.

Nothing drains out. The moraine plugs the lip. Water leaves by transpiration, by evaporation into the cloud that comes back down the headwall as more weeping, and once a year, for perhaps two weeks, by an overflow notch on the eastern side where a thin bright stream escapes and runs a hundred meters down the scree before the scree swallows it. The rest of the year the bowl is a closed loop, drinking and sweating itself, and the only genuinely new water in the system comes from the ice.

Cisterns everywhere: hollows lined with suberin and cork, waterproof as a barrel, some the size of a bathtub, one — under the apse at the head — the size of a chapel. They are full. They are always full. Their surfaces are so still that the drip rings are the only evidence of time, and the drip rings are the loudest thing in the town.

## Light, and the Tilt of Everything

Because the sun comes through the notch and nowhere else, the whole town leans.

You notice it slowly. The columns are not plumb. The vaults are not symmetrical. Every reaching front in the bowl has spent a century angling a few degrees toward that one col in the eastern rim, so the interior has a grain, a lay, a direction, like the nap on cloth — and if you follow it uphill and inward you come to the apse, where the growth is thinnest and most deliberately spaced, a genuine clearing eleven paces across with a floor of pressed litter and a ceiling of leaves arranged in a mosaic that admits, for forty minutes at midsummer, a bar of white light the width of a body.

It moves across the floor. It touches, in order, the lip of the great cistern, a boss of fused root, a patch of moss that has kept itself green and low for ninety years, and then the wall. Then it is gone for another year, more or less, and the town goes back to green.

The only other light is in the undercroft, and it is not a good sign. Down where the litter has begun to turn to peat and the air stands still, there is a faint cold foxfire, blue-white, no brighter than the memory of a match — fungal, and it belongs to the enemy.

## The War

Nothing here has been eaten by a mouth in ninety years. There are no grazers; the bowl is too high, too cold, too closed, and the tissue is loaded with silica and tannin and sugared against frost until it tastes like a struck match. The herbivore of this place is a snow mold.

It comes in under the winter snowpack, in the dark, at exactly the temperature the town cannot avoid, and it eats a district. Not fast — a hand's breadth a season — but it eats soft tissue and it does not stop, and the town's entire defensive apparatus is visible everywhere once you know to look: cork walls, resin seams, sudden dead-end corridors that were grown deliberately across a passage and lignified into a barricade. Quarantine architecture. Whole wards sealed off and abandoned, their doorways plugged with a plug of bark-colored scar, the mold still faintly glowing on the far side of it. The town cannot heal, but it can *build a wall faster than the thing can eat*, and it does, every winter, and the walls are the thickest structures in the place.

That is the arms race that gives this town its shape: not weather, not gravity. A slow siege conducted at freezing, in absolute silence, by two organisms that have never seen each other.

## The Few Animals

Springtails on the water films, in their thousands, silver as spilled mercury, and gone if you breathe on them. Fungus gnats in the compost pockets, hatching in one synchronized week and dying in the next. A single species of small snail in the cisterns, shell the color of weak tea, grazing the algal scum on the cork. One beetle, black, unhurried, that eats the gnat larvae and is the only predator in the bowl with legs. Six or seven snowfinches, which nest in the vaults near the roof and are the reason the founder arrived at all — the entire vertebrate history of this place is a delivery and its descendants.

That is the whole fauna. The town is not empty. It is *unaccompanied*.

## Why the Doors Are Our Size

Here is the thing that unsettles, and it is a small thing, and it has an explanation.

The corridors are person-width. The openings are person-height, with a lintel and jambs. There are stairs — real stairs, treads and risers, going up between storeys and landing on landings. There are thresholds. There are rooms with what can only be called windows: gaps in a wall at chest height, looking into the next room.

None of it was for anyone. A season's extension growth here is a fixed length, set by the ninety-day thaw and the cold and the sugar budget — call it a pace and a half. Where two growth fronts meet head-on they fuse; where they meet at an angle they leave a void, and the void's dimensions are that constant, over and over, in every ward, at every storey, because the constant does not vary. The doors are the negative space of an arithmetic. The stairs are what happens when a horizontal front fails to close against a vertical one, year after year, at the same interval.

The town is full of accommodation for a body that has never existed. You can walk it without stooping. You can climb it without reaching. Every landing is where your foot expects a landing. It is the most hospitable place imaginable and it does not know you are there, and when you stand still in it, the moss takes the sound of your breathing and gives nothing back, and you can hear the blood in your own ears.

## The Kept Ice

At the head, under the apse, below the floor of the great cistern, in a vault so dark that the foxfire on the far wall is the only thing with a color: a lens of blue glacier ice, four paces long, buried and roofed and wrapped in a century of growth.

It is the last piece of the ice that carved the bowl. Everything else went in the first decades. This one got under the plant before it could melt, and the plant — which grows toward water, which cannot help itself — sealed it in, and has been drinking it ever since. It is smaller every year. You can see the annual withdrawal in the wood: pale bands and dense bands, a ledger of what the ice gave, readable back to the founding. The town has been keeping accounts of a death for ninety years without knowing that is what the accounts are.

It ticks. Cooling at dusk, the ice contracts and pings, one clear note every few minutes, high and glassy, the only sound in the bowl that carries. Everything else — drip, creak, the settle of loaded wood — is swallowed within a body-length by the wet felt of the walls. Once a season a hollow column at the lip drains suddenly and gives out a low boom that you feel in the sternum and hear almost not at all, and the springtails go still, and then it is over.

When the kept ice finishes, the seeps will go on — the walls will keep weeping, the cloud will keep coming up the valley — but the great cistern will fall, and the high street will lower, and the hard central wards that were built on abundance will begin to grow soft. That is the future of the town, and it is perhaps forty years off, and every column in the place is already leaning toward it.

## The Standing Rules

Anything that touches, joins. Growth follows water; water follows ice; ice is finite. A season's reach is a fixed length, and therefore there are doors. Nothing dead stays exposed — litter is overgrown within two summers, which is why there are no ruins here yet, and why the first collapse, when a loaded vault finally lets go some winter, will be a soft crash swallowed instantly and a green scar by the following August. Sound is eaten. Light is rationed. Cold is the clock: rime at dawn, thaw at noon, seep-water doubling by afternoon, the whole bowl dripping in unison for an hour like a held breath released, then the freeze coming down and the tick of the ice beginning again.

The founder is sterile here. It has never set a viable seed; the season is too short to finish one. There is no colony in the next cirque. There is no colony anywhere. The next bowl over is scoured and weeping and empty and exactly as ready, and has been for ninety-one years, and will stay that way unless a bird comes down into this town, walks the wet high street under the leaning vaults, picks up something on its foot, and goes.

Until then the place is entirely itself: a small cold green city in a fresh grey bowl, packed solid, roofed over, lit like the inside of a wave, built to a human measure by an arithmetic that has never met a human, quietly warring with a mold in the dark, drinking the last of the glacier that made it, and leaning — every column, every vault, every reaching tip — toward forty minutes of light.
