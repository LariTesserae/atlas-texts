## The Sill

The mountain is dry the way a kiln is dry. At this height the wind has nothing to carry but itself and a little grit; the snow that falls does not melt, it simply leaves, going straight from white to gone, and the rock beneath has never once been wet. There is a slot in the summit block, a hand's-width wider than a body, and above it a lintel of black glass that no one cut — the stone around it has grown into the shape of having always held it. Two grooves are worn into the jambs at the height of hands. They are deep. Whatever used them used them for a long time, and then stopped, and the grooves kept deepening anyway, a little, the way a habit outlasts the reason for it.

At the threshold there is ice.

It is the only ice for a hundred kilometers and it lies against the sill like a tongue pressed to a window: a finger of old glacier-blue, flattened, spread, and *stopped*. It does not cross. It has not crossed in eleven thousand years. Its edge is not melted but simply finished, a clean line, and if you put your palm on the stone just inside that line you will find the stone dry as flour and colder than the ice, and no film of moisture will come off it onto your skin, because there is nothing there to come.

Water is not permitted past the sill. This is not a rule someone wrote. It is closer to a property of the room, like its dimensions.

## Inside

It is small. Forty paces at the most, and the paces are unreliable, and even so: small. A chamber. You can hold the whole of it in one turn of the head. The ceiling is low and dark and does not resolve into a surface so much as into a decision the eye makes about where to stop looking.

The floor is dust. Not sand — sand is a thing water made, and there has never been water here. This is fines: a violet-gray flour of fractured silicate and something glassier, so fine it moves like a liquid when disturbed and settles like snow when left. It lies in low dunes, three or four of them, each no higher than a knee, with ripple marks along their crests.

There is no wind. There has never been wind. The ripples are real.

What moves the dust is diffusion — atoms walking through crystal, vacancies migrating, the slow solid-state traffic that happens in any cold stone given enough centuries. Here it has been *aimed*. Somewhere under the floor a gradient is maintained, patient as a heartbeat, and the grains creep along it a finger's width per human lifetime, and the dunes have found their angle of repose and hold it, and the whole floor is a weather system with a period of about nine hundred years. It is not stalled. It is simply slower than looking.

The cold is even and radiant and has no direction. It does not bite; it subtracts. Steel will take the skin off a bare hand, but nothing sticks, nothing frosts, nothing sweats. When you breathe out, your breath does not fog. It disappears — instantly, completely, with a faint dry click at the back of the throat — and along the wall near the entrance a pale bloom brightens for a moment where the moisture went.

## The Bloom

That is the only thing in the room that could be called alive, and it is not.

It grows on the wall in fractal sprays, silver-gray, fern-shaped, flat as a stain: dendrites of a metal that has no business crystallizing at this temperature. It advances about a hair's breadth a decade. It occupies perhaps a tenth of one wall and has occupied that tenth for longer than there have been animals with eyes. It takes any water molecule that crosses the sill and cracks it and does something with the pieces that leaves the air tasting faintly of pennies and old paper. It maintains the seals. It repairs, slowly, the microfractures where the room's geometry has been stitched to the mountain's.

Whether it is a mineral habit, a machine, or a life, is a question the room has no organ for asking. It is the ecology here: one film, one gradient, one dust. Everything else is space.

## Presences

There are five objects on the floor and the floor is mostly empty between them.

A block of black glass, waist-high, with a bowl-shaped depression worn into its top. The bowl holds nothing. The nothing has weight — press your hand into it and you feel resistance, a soft firmness like the wrong end of a magnet, and when you take your hand away the dust that was in the bowl stays where it was, unsupported, for about four seconds, and then remembers.

A stair of nine steps, freestanding, descending. It goes down nine and arrives at the floor it started from. This has been checked. The steps are worn concave in the middle of each tread, deeply, uniformly, by traffic; there is no traffic; the wear continues at roughly a micron a year.

A bench. Also worn. Also empty. The wear here is not uniform — it is the shape of something that sat with its weight forward, and the shape is *specific*, and it is the only portrait in the room.

A doorframe with no wall, standing at the far end. It used to be a shortcut through the room's own logic. It has silted up. Now it works in one direction only, on some nights, and going through it puts you back where you were about a minute before you left, which is not useful, and the frame's inner edges are rounded and smooth as a riverstone from the passage of things that no longer pass.

And a cairn: eleven small identical stones. Count them and you will get eleven, or nine, or fourteen. Count them a hundred times and average, and you will get eleven every time, to four decimal places. Once, long ago, the count swung wildly. The variance has eroded. This is the important thing about this room and it takes a while to notice: *the impossibilities here are old, and they are wearing out.* Nothing is sharp. Every paradox has been sitting in the same position long enough to round off. The weirdness has an angle of repose.

## Light and Sound

The light comes from the seams — from the thin lines where the room's geometry is joined to the mountain's, and from the edges of objects when you are not looking directly at them. It is dim, sourceless, and cool, about the level of a room ten minutes after sunset. Shadows exist but do not agree with one another about where the light is. There is a pane in the wall the size of a palm, and it shows the outside: the same summit, the same slot, seen from beyond, with the mountain not yet there — just the room, sitting on a plain of ash under a sky with too many stars in it, waiting for geology to arrive and hide it.

The air is heavy and inert and does not move. Sound travels wrongly in it. A voice comes out lower than the throat that made it, and belongs to somebody taller.

But mostly there is silence, and the silence is so complete that it has a texture, and in that texture you can hear a single grain of dust settle — one, every few minutes, somewhere across the floor, distinct as a fingernail on a table. And every long while, a sound arrives that was made earlier: a footfall at the volume of a moth, a scrape, three notes of something that might have been speech, delivered from the wall near the bench with a delay of two or three hundred years. The room is not haunted. It is only very well insulated, and slow to let go.

## The Clock

Look up, above the largest dune, and there is a column of dust growing down from the ceiling. Look beneath it: a matching cone growing up from the floor. Neither is supported. Both are made of the same violet fines, packed to the density of chalk, and they are made of dust that fell *upward* in a slow eddy of the room's entropy gradient — a place where the arrow has been bent, not broken, and has stayed bent so long that it has accumulated a stalactite.

They are a hand's breadth apart. They close at about a millimeter a century.

That is what the room is for, or the last thing it is for. It was built — and *built* is the wrong tense, since the room is older than the mountain and the mountain grew around it like a scab — by an engineering that did not work on matter but on the likelihood of matter, on how firmly a thing is required to have happened. Something was put in here to be kept from happening, or to be held open until it was safe, and the machine that held it has been idling for a very long time, running down, letting its exceptions decay toward the ordinary. When the columns touch, the holding ends. The bench will still be worn. The stair will still arrive where it began, until it doesn't.

## Leaving

The mountain is losing about four centimeters of height per thousand years. The gap is closing at one. Do the arithmetic and the mountain loses: long before the columns meet, the summit block will wear away and the room will be sitting in the open on a high cold plain of its own rubble, still small, still forty unreliable paces across, still dry, no longer hidden, with a finger of ice at a sill that leads nowhere.

Go out through the slot. The ice is where you left it. Behind you, in the dust, your footprints are already faint and old and slightly ahead of where you stepped, worn into the groove that expecting them made — the path here has been walked so many times by intention that it has become a fact, and facts, in this room, are the last things to erode.
