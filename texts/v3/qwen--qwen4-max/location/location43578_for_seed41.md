## The Dew-House

You come in the way the water leaves: a low crawl through the swallow-hole, forty body-lengths of warm scoured limestone, the ceiling close enough to read by touch. The rock there is fluted like the inside of a throat. It has been rubbed smooth by ten thousand years of wet gravel going the other way. Then the passage lifts, the air changes weight, and you are standing on the floor of a room that has no weather of its own and yet has nothing but weather.

Seventy paces by forty. The ceiling hangs thirty metres up, a single unbroken dome of flowstone, pale orange, folded and re-folded like something poured slowly and left to think. There is one opening: a slot in the northeast of the dome, nine metres long and nowhere wider than a shoulder. Through it, at noon, you can see a strip of sky the colour of a scar. At midnight, if you lie on the right stone, one star crosses it.

That is the whole world. Everything else is wall.

## Heat, and where it comes from

Thirty-six degrees at the floor. It does not fall at night; the rock holds a summer that never ends. The warmth arrives from beneath, through a fissure at the northern end where the stone breathes out at forty-four, dry as an oven, smelling faintly of struck flint and old iron. You can feel the breath on your ankles before you see the crack. It has been exhaling at the same rate, at the same temperature, since before there were names for anything.

Because of it, the room is not wet. It is *damp* — a distinction the whole place is organised around. The air is humid enough to bead on skin and never humid enough to drip on its own. There is no stream, no pool deeper than a forearm, no standing water anywhere that a child could not step across. There are eleven basins, scalloped into the flowstone, each holding a few litres, each rimmed with a lip of calcite it has built for itself. There is a seep on the west wall that runs for about an hour after real rain falls on the plateau above, and then does not run again for weeks.

And there is the kiln corner: a wedge of floor beside the fissure, three paces across, where the breath keeps the stone bare and the dust dry and grey. Nothing grows there. Things that die in the dew-house are eventually carried there, or crawl there, and do not rot. They keep. There is a curled pale shape in the dust that has been in that condition for a very long time and has all its eyelashes.

## The eleven minutes

The room's clock is condensation.

At night the chamber inhales: cool air bleeds down the slot from the plateau and slides across the underside of the dome, and the ceiling rock — thirty metres of it, holding the day's warmth — gives that warmth up. By the last hour before dawn the dome has cooled below the dew point of the air it is sitting in. Fog forms in the upper room. It thickens. It gathers on the hanging roots and on the pelt of the walls, and the walls grow heavy with it, and then, all across the ceiling at once, the beads let go.

It rains indoors for eleven minutes.

Not a downpour: a fine, warm, vertical drizzle, evenly distributed, falling thirty metres and arriving as a mist that settles rather than strikes. Every surface in the room is shaped to receive it. The leaf-blades are cupped and tilted; the flowstone is terraced into a hundred thousand little lips; the litter is a sponge four fingers deep. The basins refill. The seep, if it has been dry, weeps for a while out of stored water. Nothing runs off. Nothing spills. When the eleven minutes end, the floor is dark and glossy and the whole chamber smells like the inside of a melon, and by mid-morning it is dry to the touch again, and the water is inside things.

Everything here is timed to that. Not to the sun — the sun is a rumour — but to the drinking.

## Light, and the shape it draws

The slot admits one coin of true daylight, an oval about the size of a table, which crosses the floor in a slow arc and climbs the western wall and is gone by mid-afternoon. The rest of the light is scatter: a permanent brown-gold dusk, bright enough to see your hands, dim enough that colour arrives late and reluctantly.

Along the exact path the coin travels over the course of a year — out and back, out and back, its arc shifting with the seasons — grows a stripe of green. Real green, chlorophyll green, the only true green below the rim: a hedge of low broad-leaved plants a hand-span high, tracing the sun's whole annual sweep across the floor in a long flattened figure-eight. It is a calendar written in leaves. Where the coin lingers longest, at the turn of the year, the stripe thickens into a knot of vegetation waist-high. Where it moves fast, the stripe is a thread.

Everything else that grows down here is pale. Not white — the colour of a fingernail held over a candle, translucent amber, faintly veined. It does not need the coin. It is fed from above and below.

From above: the plateau's trees lean over the slot and drop their leaves into it, a slow steady fall of litter that arrives shredded and half-rotten and piles in the drifts the air currents make. And their roots come down after it — curtains of root, thirty metres long, hanging from the rim in hanks and ropes, some no thicker than hair, some as thick as a thigh, all of them furred with white absorbing filaments that comb the fog during the eleven minutes. The root curtains are the room's canopy, hung upside down, and they are so dense in the northern third that you must part them like beads to walk.

From below: at the fissure's mouth, a mat. Sulphur-and-iron bacteria, thick as clotted cream, ochre and rust-black, warm to the touch, quietly making food out of the breath of the earth. It has been making it, without interruption, for longer than there have been animals to eat it.

## The pelt, and what it does to sound

The walls are furred. Not moss, though it reads as moss from the floor: a low colonial thing, animal at the base and plant-like in habit, growing in a pile four centimetres deep, grey-green, soft as the inside of an ear. It covers every surface below the rim except the kiln corner and the flowstone terraces. It filter-feeds on the fog. It also, incidentally, structurally, eats sound.

The room is nearly anechoic. A noise dies within a body-length of where it is made. Clap your hands and the clap goes out like a candle in a jar; you feel it in your palms and it is simply not there anymore. Drips are caught in cups of pelt before they can strike stone. The indoor rain arrives as a hiss so soft it is more a change in the air's texture than a sound. When the plateau's storms send a rope of white water down the slot — eleven times a year, sometimes fewer — it is loud for four seconds, an outrage, and then the walls take it and the room closes over the noise like water over a dropped coin.

What you hear instead is yourself. Blood in the ears. The wet click of your own throat. The knee. The breath. In a room holding perhaps forty thousand animals, your own body is by far the loudest thing present, and this is a problem, because several of the animals hunt by listening for exactly that.

So nothing here has a voice. Voices were culled out of this place a long time ago; a call carries three metres and gives you away to everything within three metres, which in a room this crowded is everything. The fauna speak in substrate knock, in touch, in scent laid down like a signature, in slow shifts of luminous pattern. It is the most talkative silence you will ever stand in.

## Layers

The density is vertical. From the litter to the dome there are six or seven storeys of occupation, all of them near-touching, and almost none of them empty.

In the litter: translucent snails the size of a fist, in their hundreds, grazing the fungal bloom in slow rotating herds, laying silver roads that other things follow. Their rasping is below hearing. Along their old roads the pale fungal fruiting bodies glow a dull foxfire green, enough to read the floor by, not enough to read a face.

In the basins: crabs, glass-clear, that carry a spoonful of water in the hollow of the carapace back to their burrows during the dry weeks. They are the room's porters. When the seep runs they spawn all at once, in a single afternoon, and the basin surfaces boil silently with larvae, and everything that eats larvae comes down from the walls.

On the walls: the pelt itself, and living in it, flat pale browsers with too many legs, and the thread-lamps — colonial larvae that hang sticky luminous beads on two-metre filaments and fish the fog for midges. Ten thousand beads on the west wall, blue-green, unmoving. From a distance it looks like a city seen through fog. It is a city. It is a net.

In the root curtains: the climbers, slow, warm-blooded, hand-footed, hanging in the hanks in dozens, drinking the fog directly through the skin of the belly. They pass each other constantly and touch, always, in passing — a palm along a forearm, a fingertip to a jaw. That is the conversation. It never stops.

In the folds of the flowstone: the white snake, three metres, blind, with a pit on each side of the face that reads the warmth of breath at four paces. It does not need eyes and it does not need ears. It needs you to keep breathing.

And in the air, the midges, in clouds you can feel on your face before you can see them: the pollinators, the fog-riders, the whole bottom of the food web, hatching in their tens of thousands during the eleven minutes and drifting up into the beads.

## The mason, and the older hands

There is a stair. Thirty-one steps cut into the flowstone terrace at the southern end, each dished a finger deep in the centre by feet. It goes up to a niche, and in the niche is a bowl, unbroken, and beside the niche a panel of tally scratches in groups of five, and below them a handprint pressed into moonmilk that has since grown a skin of calcite over it, so the hand is behind glass.

At the seep, a dry-stone dam, half a metre high, corbelled, made to hold an hour of water that comes eleven times a year.

Nobody made these recently. Nobody comes. The stair should have been buried under litter and flowstone ten thousand years ago, and it has not been, because the snail herds graze the algal film off the treads on their rotation and keep the dishes clean, and the calcite grows everywhere except where it is licked. The ruin is being maintained by animals that do not know it is a ruin.

And there is new stonework. Small — knee-high niches, dry-laid, three or four stones and a cap, always beside a basin, always with a shallow bowl-shaped pebble set inside. There are nineteen of them. They are copies. The thing that builds them is a pale, weak-winged, silent bird about the size of a heron, and it grew up among the old work, and it builds what it saw: the niche, the bowl, the corbel. It does not use them for anything. It knocks on the capstone with its beak to tell the others it has built one, and you do not hear the knocking. You feel it arrive up through the soles of your feet and into your shins, a small dry tick, and then another, from somewhere across the room, answering.

## Balance

Nothing here is collapsing. The breakdown pile beneath the slot finished falling before the stair was cut; it is a rounded hill now, soiled over, forested in pale blades. The cracks have all been sealed by their own leaking. The water has found its route and keeps it. Heat comes in from below at a fixed rate and goes out through the slot at a fixed rate, and the difference between them is thirty-six degrees, and has been for two hundred thousand years.

The flowstone advances a thumbnail's thickness per century. The slot narrows by a hair per millennium. Both facts are written in the stone, and neither is hurry.

Stand still in the middle of it, in the brown-gold dusk, in the heat, with the roots brushing your shoulders and the beads glowing on the west wall and forty thousand lives stacked around you in the near-touching dark, and wait for the hour before dawn. The dome will cool. The fog will gather. The whole room will lift its face.

And for eleven minutes it will rain, indoors, silently, on everything at once, and every living thing in the world will drink.
