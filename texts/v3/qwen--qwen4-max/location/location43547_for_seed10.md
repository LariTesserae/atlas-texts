The mountains are not fallen. They are finished.

Somewhere under the leather of the moor there is a stump of a range that once had glaciers on it, and the glaciers went, and the rivers went down through the rock a grain at a time for a length of time that has no useful number attached to it, and now the land is as flat as a table that has been planed by someone who did not know when to stop. This is the base level. There is nothing left to take away. Water standing here has nowhere lower to go, in any direction, for a day's walk.

And still it is high. The air is thin and burns the inside of the nose. Sleet comes in from the west at a slant and hits the ground almost horizontally, and the ground crackles like a paper bag. The horizon sits at eye level all the way around, five kilometres out, a grey seam stitched between two greys, and it does not change when you walk toward it, because there is nothing on this plain tall enough to be occluded by anything else. You can watch a thing for an entire day's approach and then pass it in ten minutes.

It would be easy to call this a bog. It is not a bog. A bog is a wet place with plants in it. Here the plant *is* the place.

---

The mat lies over the planed rock in a continuous quilt three to four metres thick: sphagnum and cushion-plants and sedge and liverwort and a hundred low woody things no taller than a thumbnail, all grown into one another, root through root, until the seams disappeared a long time ago. It is not a community. It is a tissue. Walk on it and it takes your weight the way a mattress takes it, a slow give and a slower return, and the water rises into your footprint behind you and stands there, tea-black, tasting of iron and tannin.

Under the leather there are chambers. Air pockets the size of rooms, held open by lignified struts, warm and stinking and packed with life: springtails in drifts a metre deep, white and hopping, so many that the drifts move like snow in a draught; mites; blind pale things that graze the fungal felt on the chamber walls. Above, the surface is nearly empty. This is the strangest arithmetic of the place — total vegetation, and almost nothing *present*. One organism, or a small number of organisms, spread over an area the size of a province. Between the standing things there are hours of identical grey-green quilt, quilt to the seam of the sky, with the wind combing the cotton-grass heads all one way and no feature at all to break the counting of your steps.

The standing things are far apart. You see the next one before you have finished with the last: a dark column of vapour leaning downwind, three leagues off, and under it a shape like a swollen urn.

They are pumps. There is no better word and no worse one.

---

Water cannot leave this plain by falling. It has to be *worked*.

Each pump is a pressure vessel of banded woody tissue, six or eight metres through, sunk in the mat with only its shoulders showing, and inside it a compost heart runs at blood heat while everything for a kilometre around stands at one degree above freezing. The heat is old peat burning slowly without fire — the same warmth that steams a wet haystack in winter — and the plant has learned to keep it, to bank it, to spend it. Turgor does the rest. Chambers fill and stiffen; a cuticle valve the consistency of horn lifts; a hundred tonnes of black water go up a lignified standpipe and out through a crown of nozzles as fog.

Then the valve drops. *Clack.* Then the intake, a long descending suck you feel in the sinuses. Then the slow thump of the heart chamber refilling, which arrives through the soles of the feet before it arrives through the air.

There are perhaps forty of them across the whole plain, spaced like the stations of something, all running at slightly different periods, so that the beats drift in and out of phase across a morning and sometimes, for a minute or two, the entire moor thumps together and the standing water shivers into rings and every wader on the flat goes up at once, screaming.

It sounds like a marshalling yard. It sounds like a mill town heard from a hill at four in the morning: the clack and sigh of valves, the low industrial pulse, the hiss of vapour, applause of sleet on the leather, and under it all the vast wet suck of the sumps where the water goes back down. Nothing here was built. Nothing here was ever built. There is not a made thing on this plain, not a chip of flint, not a scratch — and this is not innocence. It is digestion. The mat eats its own products. Nothing accumulates. The only thing that persists is the rock, and the rock has already had everything done to it that can be done.

---

The fog the pumps make does not lift, because there is no gradient to lift it along; it spreads out at head height and stands, and the sleet falls through it, and the light comes down through nine kilometres of it already tired.

But that is only half the dimness. The other half is that the mat drinks first. Total coverage means total claim: every photon that reaches this plain has been bid for by a leaf before it gets to the air, and what is left over is the grey you walk in. Noon is the colour of a nickel. You can look straight at the sun's position and see nothing but a slightly warmer smear. Colour here is a rumour — the sudden shocking rust-red of a sundew field, the rainbow bacterial oil-slick on a standing pool, and then leagues of grey again.

And out of the fog, the herds.

They condense near the pumps, in the warm wet downwind of the nozzles, and they are born warm. That is the part that takes a while to accept. They come out of the vapour already walking, steaming, coated in a birth-slime that the mat reabsorbs within the hour: long-legged grazing things the size of ponies with mouths that are only decorative, and eyes, and a fine pelt of something between hair and moss. Hundreds at a time. They disperse across the flat trailing plumes, and you can read their distance from the pump in their temperature — the near ones glossy and quick and shouldering each other, the far ones cold and slow and crusted with rime, and the farthest ones stopped, standing in the quilt with frost on their backs, sinking a little, being taken back.

The mat makes them and the mat eats them. In between they crop, and trample, and drop nutrient, and their billions of parasites go down into the chambers, and the sundews close, and the bladderwort in the sumps snaps its little traps all day and all night with a sound like a distant electrical fault. The whole plain is a factory with one product and one customer and no warehouse. It runs at a furious rate to produce nothing that lasts. That is what turbulence looks like on ground that can no longer change: all the violence that used to make mountains has been compressed into a skin four metres thick, and it churns hourly. Sumps open with a boom you feel in the chest and close by evening. New nozzles crown out overnight and are dead by the following week. A fog front rolls through and leaves behind a hundred prototypes half-formed, standing in the water, wrong-legged, dissolving as you watch.

And the noise never stops. You stop hearing it, which is different. When a pump fails — and they fail, they fail constantly — the silence that opens is so enormous that the waders go up again, and you find you have been shouting for some time.

---

Here is what the erosion has done, having run out of rock.

It has gone on eroding. It has worn through the difference between things.

Count the pumps and you will get a different number twice, not because they move but because *one* is a distinction the flat no longer maintains very carefully. The bodies come out stamped: you will find, three days apart and two leagues apart, two of the same grazer with the same kink in the same left foreleg, the same notched ear, the same hesitation before it lowers its head. The mat is not copying. Copying would require an original. It is simply that the mould has worn smooth and the parts come out alike.

In places the leather has gone thin, and under it there is not rock. There is an earlier state of the plain, still running, a little out of phase — the same pump seen from a slightly different angle, working in weather that is not your weather, its fog going the other way. You can put your hand into it. It is cold, and it is wet, and it is exactly as deep as the mat, and then it is rock, the finished rock, planed and polished and utterly indifferent.

The sleet arrives after its sound. Your footprints fill with water a half-second before you set your foot down. Names do not stick here; you will name a feature at dawn and by dusk the name will have slid off it onto something else, or onto nothing, and you will find yourself using it for the taste of the water.

None of this is malfunction. This is what the end of a landscape looks like when it refuses to stop. The stone reached its final form a hundred million years ago and there has been nothing left to erode since but duration, and identity, and sequence, and the flat is working on those now with the same patient appetite it once had for granite.

---

At what passes for evening the grey goes a shade bluer and colder and the pumps show their heat as columns of pale vapour standing all across the plain, forty of them, spaced out to the edge of sight, each one thumping on its own schedule, each one surrounded by its cloud of new animals cooling as they walk away from it into the enormous empty quilt.

The world here has one gradient left: the vertical, between the warm heart of the mat and the cold sky. Everything that happens, happens across those four metres. Everything that will ever happen here will happen in that gap, faster and faster, in a fog of its own making, on ground that has already finished.

Somewhere out in the dark a valve lifts, and hisses, and drops.

*Clack.*

And then, a moment before it happens again, you hear it.
