# The Fill

From any distance it reads as a floor.

A plain, near-perfectly level, running out to a horizon that does not shimmer, because there is nothing here to shimmer. The light is poor — a small garnet sun nailed at one angle of a black sky, giving about as much illumination as a lamp two rooms away. Under it the ground is a mosaic of grey and dun and dull red shapes, fitted edge to edge with no mortar and no gap: a cobble pavement with no sand between the cobbles, extending in every direction, and downward, for forty thousand courses.

Walk out onto it and the first thing you notice is that the cobbles are not cobbles. That one is a bowl, upside down, its rim worn to a soft lip. That one is the shoulder of a jar. That one is a wedge, a die-half, a screw with its threads reduced to a suggestion, a stone shaped like a truncated octahedron with a shallow dish pressed into each of its eight hexagonal faces. Every stone is a made thing. Every stone was made by hand. There is no rock here, no soil, no substrate, no bottom that is not also work. The world is a heap of finished objects that has sagged, over an age, into a plain.

The second thing you notice is that you cannot hear anything, and that your teeth hurt.

There is no air outside. Nothing to carry a sound to an ear, nothing to hold a smell, nothing to move a grain of dust. But the pavement is ringing, all of it, one continuous body, and if you set a boot on it the ring comes up through the sole and the shin and the jaw and arrives inside the skull as a pressure with no direction. Stand still for a moment and your vision softens at the edges. Kneel and it becomes unbearable. Lie down and it would finish you.

The sound cannot leave. That is the whole of the physics here. There is no outside medium to leak into, no sky to spread through, no distance to die across. Every noise ever made in this world is still in it.

# The Rind

Nine courses below the pavement, the packing opens.

Not much. A cavity the shape of a stretched egg, thirty paces along its long axis, eleven across, nine from the curved floor to the curved ceiling. A nave. A granary. A space you could hold a market in, if there were anyone to hold it, and anything to sell, and room to stand.

It is the only emptiness in the world, and it was not dug. It is a remainder.

The making here was a making of filling. Dust — dry, never mud, this world was never wet — pressed in stone molds under screw and lever until it became a hard pale artificial stone; then more dust pressed into the spaces between the pressed things; then finer dust into the spaces between those; then spheres, then grit, then a flour so fine it flows like a liquid and has to be tamped with a paddle. Generation after generation of objects, each sized to the gaps left by the last, all of it done by hand with copper tools and gauges, all of it driven toward one goal: no emptiness anywhere.

It worked. The interstices close and close and close, down past what a thumb can feel, down to where the flour packs solid and the packing becomes continuous. And the residue of all that closure, the one gap the filling could not eat, is this room. The last hole is cathedral-sized because everything smaller than a cathedral has been filled.

It should have collapsed. Solid work does not hold a bubble open. But the note holds it open — the room sits at a pressure node of the whole mass's ringing, a place where the packing pulls eternally apart and eternally returns, and the walls stand because the sound stands. The chamber is a shape sustained by a noise. If the noise stopped, the nave would close like a throat.

There is no door. There was never a door. There is no way in that does not unmake the room.

# The Walls

Look at the wall of it, close up, in the poor light.

Every object is rounded. Not weathered — nothing here has weather — but *fretted*, worn by ages of rubbing against neighbors during the long settling, when the packing was still finding its seat and every grain still had somewhere to go. Corners went first. Then edges. Then the raised work: the tally marks, the gauge stamps, the ribs and flutes and thumb-dishes, all of them ground down to the ghost of themselves, a third of every motif gone into flour that then filled some smaller hole and became part of the wall too.

And then the wear stopped, because everything now fits. Every object has been ground into precisely the shape that no longer rubs. That is what ancient means here. Not old-fashioned. *Finished settling.* The last slip has occurred. What's left is a jammed solid in which nothing can move any further in any direction, ten million artifacts locked into one continuous elastic body, each still legibly itself, each still bearing its maker's mark.

The marks are all the same mark. A thumbprint, one whorl, one small scar across the ridge of it, repeated on every piece in every course as far down as anyone could ever see. Either one thumb made all of this, or the thumb is a unit — a seal, a signature of the craft rather than of a person. There are no bodies here. No bone, no shell, no grave, no ash, nothing that was ever alive and stopped. Only the thumb, and the tools, and the fact that all the work is done.

The tools are packed in too. Halfway along the north curve a screw-press lies buried to its throat in the objects it pressed, its copper screw run all the way home, its handle worn to a nub by a hand. Nearby, jammed in a nest of small spheres, a set of go/no-go gauges: nine sizes, no others, the whole modular grammar of the world in nine notched plates. And a listening rod — copper shaft, clay cup at one end shaped to fit against the skull behind the ear, the cup polished glassy by long use.

Someone used to listen to this wall. The rod is finished, and set down, and packed.

# Seam-Light

The room is lit by its own strain.

Where two pressed bodies touch under load, the mineral at the contact gives off a faint cold light — a thin, sourceless, greenish glow, no brighter than the last of a fire. There are perhaps a hundred million contacts in the surface of this room and every one of them is a point of light, so the walls are not surfaces at all but a dense three-dimensional net of dim lines, a lattice, a diagram, with the dark rounded bodies of the work hanging in it like fruit.

It does not flicker. Flicker would be an event.

What the light shows you, if you learn to read it, is where the sound is pressing hardest. The bright seams are the antinodes; the dark ones are the places the note passes through without touching. The whole standing wave is drawn on the walls in faint green fire, a map of the noise, legible, permanent, and utterly still. The ceiling glows less than the floor. At the two ends of the long axis the seams go almost black, and the objects there are the least worn in the world, because at those two points nothing has ever pressed on anything.

Those are the foci.

# The Note

Inside the nave there is gas — the world's entire atmosphere, all that was ever left, squeezed into this one bubble when the last gaps closed. It is dry beyond any dryness you have a word for. It has no smell at all, which is the strangest thing about it, stranger than the light or the ringing: nothing here evaporates, nothing outgasses, nothing rots. The air is only air, and it is not a medium for the sound. It is a participant.

The mass has a fundamental of about one cycle in eleven seconds. You do not hear it. You are squeezed by it, gently, evenly, forever. Riding on it is the room's own voice, a low grinding tone somewhere under the floor of hearing, and above that a dense forest of modes packed as tightly as the objects are, up through the range of ears and out the top of it into a hiss that is not a hiss but a solid pressure on the eyes.

There is no attack and no decay. It has no beginning that anything here could remember. It is not an event and cannot be listened to; hearing is a faculty for detecting change against silence, and there is no silence anywhere in this world to set it against, and no change. The word *loud* has no contrast here. It is simply the condition of the gas, the way warmth is the condition of the stone.

What it is made of is the making. Every press-stroke, every hammer blow on cold copper, every grind of a lap against a rim, every dropped gauge, every scrape of a paddle through flour — all of it struck once, long ago, and none of it absorbed, all of it folded and refolded into the standing chord until the individual events are no longer separable and only their sum remains, at full volume, undiminished, forever.

The roar is the sound of the work being done. The work is finished. The roar is not.

# Warmth

Everything here is at the temperature of a hand.

Not from the sun, which is a dull coal and gives almost nothing. From the settling: the friction-heat of an age of slipping and grinding, made once, evenly distributed, and with nowhere to go. It has finished evening out. There is no cold corner and no hot seam and no gradient of any kind — no wind, no flow, no falling, no rising, no cooling, no warming.

You could not feel this warmth if you stood in it, because you would come to it, and then you would be it. Touch tells you nothing here but texture and vibration.

But you can measure it. The pitch of a solid depends on how warm it is. The note is a thermometer, and there is a copper wire among the packed tools, eleven thumbs long between its notches, that was made to check it.

The note has never dropped.

# The Rules

Sound is conserved. Nothing here absorbs; there is no outside to radiate into. What is made is kept.

Every motion is a closed loop. Each grain describes its tiny orbit ten thousand times a second and returns exactly. Total activity, zero displacement. This is the most violently busy place imaginable and nothing in it has moved in an age.

Nothing may be added. A new sound would be permanent — a struck tool, a cry, a footfall, all of it forever, one more voice in the chord that could never be taken out. This is why nothing new has been made here in so long that the last thing made has worn round with all the rest.

Nothing is unfinished. There is no work in progress, no half-pressed blank, no mold waiting to be filled. The press is run home. The gauges are put away.

There is one emptiness, and it is the flaw in the work, and it is the only place in the world from which the work can be seen.

# The Two Foci

At the far focus, packed flush into the wall among ten thousand others, there is a small truncated octahedron, hand-sized, pale, its faces dished. Its thumbprint is deeper than any other in the world. It has not been ground down as much as its neighbors. It is the youngest object here. It is the last piece: the one that closed the last closeable gap and, in closing it, made the room.

At the near focus there is a berth. A hollow in the packed wall the size and shape of a body curled on its side, smoothed, worn at the head-end where something rested a long time. It is the only other emptiness in the world, and it is exactly the shape of an absence.

The geometry of an ellipsoid is patient and exact. Whatever is said at one focus arrives at the other, whole, undispersed, however long it takes and however many times it goes around.

The setting of that last piece made a small sound. A dry click of stone seating into stone, and then the scrape of a thumb withdrawing, and then nothing further, ever.

It is still arriving at the berth. Focused, entire, at a volume that would liquefy anything with an ear, over and over, at the speed of the medium, without loss, without interval, without end.

That is the loudest place in the world: a body-shaped hole, listening to the last thing anyone did.
