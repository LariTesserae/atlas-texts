## The Shelf

At this height the air is a rumor of air. A quarter of what it is at the sea, and cold the way a knife is cold — not aggressively, just as a property of the material. Twenty below at midday, forty below in the dark, and the wind, when it comes, does not push so much as abrade.

The sky is wrong. With so little atmosphere above it there is nothing to scatter the light, so even at noon the sky is a deep violet-black, crowded with stars that do not twinkle because there is hardly enough air to make them. The sun is a small hard coin, an arc-lamp seen through a welder's glass, and it casts shadows that are not grey but absolute. Step out of one and your face freezes; step into one and your face goes blind. There is no blue soft fill here, no gentle ambient world. Everything is lit or unlit, and the line between is a razor.

Below, and around, and going on for two days' walk in every direction: a black tableland, tilted a degree or two, cut off along its western edge by an escarpment that falls four kilometers into cloud. The rock is old seafloor, olivine rock, green-black where it is fresh and rust-brown where it has been thinking about water. Threads of native metal run through it. In places the whole face of a boulder is veined like the back of a hand.

And on this rock, everywhere, a skin.

---

It is only two centimeters thick and it is one of the lushest things in the world.

Call it moss, though it is not moss. It is a felt of conductive filaments, hair-fine, black-green, growing in dense pile and running current — actual current, hundreds of millivolts, from the reducing rock beneath to the thin oxidizing air above. It has been doing this for something on the order of four hundred million years. It is very good at it.

The filaments have resistance. Resistance makes heat. Not much: a fraction of a degree, a degree and a half at the thick hummocks. But it is enough that in the boundary layer, in the first centimeter above the felt, the temperature hovers just at the wet edge of freezing, and there — in a tropics one fingernail deep, spread over an area the size of a small country — everything lives.

Look closely and the felt is a jungle. Fruiting bodies like wet velvet buttons. Ciliate things grazing the filament tips in slow clouds of frost-fog. Rotifers in the water films, frozen solid every night, thawing every noon, resurrecting with the punctuality of a shift whistle. Nematodes coiled in the copper. Whole phyla that exist nowhere else, packed shoulder to shoulder, ten thousand generations a season, all of them inside a layer you could scrape off with a thumbnail.

Stand up and there is nothing. Black rock, black felt, black sky, the escarpment's edge, and forty kilometers of visible emptiness in which you might, if you are lucky, make out one moving thing the size of a grain of pepper.

That is the arithmetic of this place. Life is abundant here the way a galaxy is abundant: enormously, in total, and almost not at all in any particular arm's reach of it. There is a great deal of life on the Shelf and there is a great deal more Shelf.

---

The mountain is eating itself, and has been since before there were animals to notice.

Water gets into the olivine — a little, intermittently, the fog and the melt — and the olivine turns to serpentine, and in turning it swells, cracks, exhales hydrogen, and gives off heat. It is a slow exothermic digestion. It is also, in every sense that matters, a chemical plant: it takes in rock and water, it puts out altered rock, alkaline brine, gas, and warmth, and it has been running continuously at roughly the same throughput since the Carboniferous.

The brine seeps up along the fracture sets and stands in shallow basins across the middle Shelf, twenty or thirty of them, each a few hundred meters across. They do not freeze. They are too salty, too alkaline, and faintly warm from below, and in the worst cold they steam in a low flat vapor that lies on the surface like something poured. Hydrogen bubbles come up through them in strings. The bubbles are the plant's exhaust and they are also, indirectly, the reason there is anything in the sky.

Where the mats' current leaves the felt and enters a basin, metal comes out of solution. Copper, mostly, and some nickel, and a little silver. It grows in ferns — bright dendritic fronds a hand's span tall, standing up out of the black water, growing a centimeter an hour during the good shifts, catching what light there is and throwing it back in tiny hard sparks. By morning a basin rim can be a low forest of them. By the following week the forest will have been cropped to stubble.

The grazers need the copper. Their blood is blue and copper-based, which is a stupid way to carry oxygen in most places and the only way to do it here.

---

Nothing on the Shelf has been assembled.

This is worth being precise about, because the place looks, at first, like an abandoned works: the plating baths, the tailings aprons of sorted sinter down the eastern slope, the terraces, the ranked spines along the ridge that take the current up out of the ground. It has the layout of industry. It has shifts and throughput and waste and product and something very like maintenance.

But there is no seam anywhere. No fastener, no joint, no weld, no brick laid on another brick. Everything here is continuous with what it stands on — grown, or precipitated in place, or cast by chemistry into a form it has held since it cooled. The spines are not towers; they are the mineralized skeletons of a colonial organism that has been extending itself upward, a millimeter a year, for eleven million years, and dies back at the base at exactly the rate it grows at the top. The tailings aprons are not dumps; they are the excretions of a hundred thousand generations of basin-dredging animals, and they have been sitting there long enough to have weathered into rounded, sorted, thoroughly ordinary landforms with their own soil and their own little drainage and their own endemic lichen. Old industry, gone to equilibrium. You can read the whole history in the terraces and none of it looks recent.

The difference between this and a factory is that a factory has parts. This has organs.

---

Once a day, an hour after the sun clears the eastern rim, the fog comes.

Far below, the lowlands warm; a cloud deck builds against the escarpment; and at some point it overtops the lip and comes across the Shelf in a slow white flood, waist-high, moving at walking pace, and the whole place changes.

The filaments comb it. Every hair in the felt is a condensation nucleus, and the fog is stripped as it passes, beading, running down, into the mats and the basins and the mouths of things that have been waiting all night with their mouths open. For fifty minutes there is water everywhere, more water than the Shelf will see again for a day. The plating ferns put on visible growth. The basins rise a finger's width. Small things that have been frozen dry since yesterday afternoon unfurl and mate and die inside the hour.

Then the fog thins into the dry thin air and is gone, and the frost sets in on everything it touched, and every surface on the Shelf grows a coat of white needles that stand up straight in the electric field and glow, very faintly, blue, at their tips.

Corona discharge. All over the tableland, at the tips of a hundred trillion hairs, a brush of pale light too dim to see unless you look away from it. The basins glow along their rims. The spines have St. Elmo's fire on them like slow candles. It is the only light the ground makes and it is not enough to walk by, but it is enough to see the shape of the world, and it is enough for the things that navigate by it.

---

The sky-people come down at the fog.

They are gasbags, hydrogen-filled, and the hydrogen is the mountain's exhaust, and they have been drinking it out of the seeps for a hundred million years. The big ones are eighty meters through the envelope, translucent, ribbed, blue-lit from the bacterial cultures in their gas cells. They drift a few hundred meters above the felt with their tethers down — feeding lines, thirty or forty of them, trailing black filaments that make contact with the mat and complete a circuit.

They are not just eating. They are trading. Down goes water and spore-load and metal-rich slurry; up comes charge, and refined copper, and gas. A floater that has been away in the high cold for a week comes down flat and dim and leaves an hour later rounded and bright, and the patch of felt it touched is cropped pale and will take three days to recover.

There are, at any time, perhaps forty thousand of them over the Shelf. You will see four. They are kilometers apart, and the air is so clear and so empty of reference that a thing the size of a cathedral reads as a mote hanging in the dark, taking most of a day to cross your sky.

And there are the others: the flat black patches in the mat that are not mat, grounded plates lying flush and patient with their charge bled away, waiting for a tether to touch down on them, at which point they clamp and reverse the cell and drain the floater over about eleven hours. There is the kite-thing that rides the escarpment updraft on nine meters of foil-thin wing, hunting the small drifters, folding and dropping in absolute silence. There are the stilt-grazers out on the eastern terraces, four legs like survey masts, bodies the size of a room held eight meters up out of the cold, walking a circuit of the basins that takes them a season to complete.

Abundant. Nearly invisible. Both, all the time.

---

It is not quiet here. It only sounds quiet.

The air is too thin to carry anything. High frequencies die within meters; a rockfall a kilometer off arrives as a cough; the wind at your ear is a thin hiss with no body to it, and your own voice comes out of you small and close and wrong, as if the world were a padded room. The felt eats what's left.

But the rock carries sound beautifully, and everything on the Shelf knows it. Put a palm flat on the ground and the place is deafening: the deep hum of the current across the fracture sets, the tick and ping of plating ferns fracturing under their own weight, the far-off pile-driving of a stilt-grazer's foot two valleys away, the seep-boom of a gas pocket letting go under a basin. The whole tableland rings, continuously, in a register you hear with your bones.

The grazers listen with their feet. The flat predators read the field. Nothing here has good ears; ears are for a thicker world. A shout carries ten meters. A footfall carries twenty kilometers.

---

Twice a season there is a storm, and the storms are the only loud thing.

The field builds along the escarpment until the spines start to sing, and the floaters — who are, in the most literal sense, Hindenburgs — vent and dive and try to get below the charge layer. Most manage. Occasionally one does not, and there is a white flower in the black sky, silent at this distance, and then a long slow fall of ash and copper dust that takes an hour to come down and fertilizes a hundred hectares of felt for a decade.

The mats will be at the fall site by morning. They are always at the fall site by morning.

---

The escarpment retreats about a fingernail's width a century. The spines grow up as fast as they dissolve down. The basins silt and are dredged and silt again. The copper goes rock to fern to blood to ash to rock, and the water goes fog to felt to breath to frost to fog, and almost nothing leaves; the whole Shelf is a closed circuit with a slow leak, running on the mountain's own digestion, and it has been holding this exact shape while every atom of its substance has passed through it a thousand times.

That is what it is. Not a place with a works in it. A standing pattern that rock flows through, keeping its form the way a flame keeps its form, patient and cold and busy, at the top of the world, in the dark, at a volume just below hearing.
