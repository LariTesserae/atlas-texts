## The Slot

From above there is almost nothing to see. The pan runs flat and pale to the horizon in every direction, seventy meters below a sea that has not been here for a very long time, and the crack in it is a meter and a half wide — a stride, a thing you could step over without deciding to. Heat stands over the pan in columns. The crack does not give off heat. It takes it in, and gives back nothing, and if you lie on your stomach at the lip and put your face over, the air that comes up is warm and dry and smells of old paper and hot iron, and it does not move.

Below the lip the walls drop forty meters and never get further apart than three.

---

It was cut by water. That is the first thing to understand and the last thing to believe. Every surface down there is the shape of moving water: the flutes, the scalloped scoops, the long S-curves that swing from wall to wall like a rope laid down by someone in a hurry, the twin grooves at knee height where a boulder sat in a current for nine thousand years and let the current do the work around it. There are three potholes in the floor, drilled round and true, their lips overhanging, their interiors smooth as the inside of a mouth. In the deepest of them sits a single quartzite cobble, fist-sized, ground to an egg. It is the grinder. The water that spun it left it exactly where it stopped turning and went away, and it has not moved since, and there is no force left in this place that will move it.

The river is not underground. It is not anywhere. The water table fell below the floor of the basin before the last of the ice went, and the rains that used to feed it turned north and never turned back, and the pan above receives twenty millimeters a year, most of it in a single afternoon, all of it running off the lip and away because the lip is tilted a half-degree outward. Nothing enters. The slot is a fossil of a river: the negative space of something that has entirely stopped happening.

The floor is dust over a crust of the final flood's mud, and the mud is still curled. It dried in plates and the plates lifted at their edges and each one is where it fell, a floor of brittle brown shavings under two centimeters of powder, and the powder takes a footprint and keeps it. There are no footprints. There is one place, near the blind end, where the dust is thinner and a scatter of eleven stone flakes lies on top of it in the attitudes they fell in.

---

The walls are black.

Not the rock — the rock is a red-brown sandstone, cross-bedded, soft enough to score with a thumbnail. But it has been out in the dry air long enough to grow a varnish, manganese and iron laid down by bacteria a few molecules at a time, a coat perhaps a tenth of a millimeter thick, and it is the color of a wet crow. It took thirty thousand years. It is finished now. It does not thicken and it does not flake, because nothing here abrades it: no grit blows through, no flood scours, no freeze pries. The walls have case-hardened. The lip is held by a band of chert conglomerate a meter thick that keeps the whole thing from widening, so the slot is not evolving into a canyon. It is not evolving. It has arrived at its shape and the shape is stable and will hold until the region itself is uplifted or buried, which is not a thing that happens on any clock you carry.

The varnish eats light. That is why it is so dark down there. Geometry alone would give you a bright bronze dusk; the black walls drink nine-tenths of it and give back a brown so low you read the space with your hands. Stand at the floor and look up: the sky is a white thread, irregular, winking where the walls close, and it hurts. Look down: your boots are a suggestion. The rock is warm — thirty-four degrees, January and August both, the heat of the deep basin bleeding up through the stone and pooling in a trench too narrow to convect. It is like standing inside an animal. You do not feel hot. Your sweat goes before you know you've made it, and what you feel instead is being slowly simplified.

For eleven minutes around midsummer noon, a blade of direct sun comes down the middle reach and lays a white bar across the floor and about two meters up the east wall. For the rest of the year, no direct sun at all. The eleven minutes are the only event.

---

There is one green thing.

Along the east wall, at the height the blade reaches, runs a stripe. It is a hand wide, it is the length of the middle reach, and it follows the light's path with an accuracy that looks like intention and is only accounting: a crustose lichen, dull olive, living not on the surface but just inside the sandstone, between the grains, where the pores hold a film of water a few molecules thick, drawn up by capillarity from somewhere very deep. There is no water in this place. There is damp the way there is damp in a bone. The lichen has access to it and you do not, and if you scrape the wall you get powder and a smell like slate remembered.

For most of the day the stripe is black like everything else. When the blade crosses it, it is green — suddenly, absolutely, the only saturated color for a hundred kilometers — and then the blade moves on and it goes back to being a mark you know is there.

Above, at the lip, six shrubs stand on the pan with their roots down the joints: gray, waxy, knee-high, spaced eight or ten meters apart, which is what twenty millimeters a year buys you. Two more lean in at the choked end where a fallen slab lets in a wedge of extra sky. That is the whole flora. They do not seed. They sucker. What you are looking at is nine individuals in the sense that matters and perhaps three in the sense that a geneticist would care about, and they have been the same individuals since the mud curled. Nothing pollinates them; nothing eats them; nothing here has a mouth. No mites in the dust, no beetles under the slab, no bat at dusk, no bird on the rim — the air is below the line where a small body can hold onto its water, and the lichen's grazers cannot cross the varnish to reach it. The slot is not dead. It is simply not visited.

---

At the narrow end, where the walls come within a body's width, there is a line of pockets going up. Most are natural — solution hollows, the water's leftovers. Four of them are polished in a way the others aren't, a glassy dullness at the lip of each hold, the kind of shine that only comes from something soft and repeated. A fifth has been deepened. You can see the pecking, individual impacts, a hammerstone worked by a hand, and the marks are as sharp as the day they were made because there is nothing here that wears anything.

On the ledge below: eleven flakes, a spent core, a thumbnail scraper. On a flat scoop at hip height, a shallow grinding hollow with a residue of red ochre in it, still red, still powder.

No hearth. No charcoal, no bone, no cord, no wood, no sherd, no wall, no cairn, no grave. Everything left is stone, because stone is what stays, and because whoever it was carried out what they carried in. They came down, they made something small, they went up. Maybe once. Maybe a thousand times over four generations, always by the same four holds, always leaving nothing but the polish and the debris of resharpening. The tools are simple: struck, ground, no metal anywhere in the story. The place was not improved. It was used, briefly, by someone who understood it, and then it was left to be exactly what it had already become.

---

It is quiet, but not silent. All day the basin heats and all day the air climbs out of the trench at a rate you cannot feel, and across the mouth this makes a low steady note, one pitch, no louder than a finger on the rim of a glass. It does not vary. It has not varied. You hear it only when you stop breathing, and then you hear your own blood underneath it, and the small click of your throat, and these are enormous. The walls are too close to give an echo — sound comes back before you can separate it from itself, so a footfall arrives thickened rather than repeated, and the dust swallows the rest. Speak and your voice goes nowhere and returns nowhere. It is simply used up.

Nothing falls. A grain of sand comes off the lip perhaps once a decade. The mud curls do not crumble further. The pebble waits in its hole with the patience of a thing that has already finished its work. The lichen advances a millimeter in a year and has advanced, in total, about the width of your hand.

The place is a completed sentence. Water wrote it and then was taken out of it, and what remains is the grammar: the flutes, the potholes, the swing of the old current still legible in black stone, holding its shape in the warm dark with nothing left to do. Eleven minutes a day, it is green. Otherwise it is simply itself, at thirty-four degrees, forty meters down, a meter and a half wide, and entirely uninterested in whether you find it.
