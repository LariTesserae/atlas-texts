# Return No. 4

## The plain

You hear it before there is anything to hear it from.

The plain is finished. That is the first fact and, out here, the only one: a floor of lacquered stones fitted edge to edge like a mosaic laid by someone patient and dead, every piece wind-polished, every gap packed with grit that no longer moves. Nothing here is soft enough left to be taken. The hills were taken. The valleys were taken. What remains is the lag, the hard remainder, a surface at the end of its long argument with weather. It runs to a horizon that shimmers a little in the cool air and offers nothing at any point along it. The ground is eleven degrees and has been, more or less, for a hundred thousand years.

And yet, walking it, you begin to notice a sound with no source. A dry seething. It comes up through the stones. It has structure — a high sustained band, a lower granular hiss, and under both a slow mechanical hum you feel in the sternum before you hear it. You walk toward it for twenty minutes and the plain does not change at all, and then you are standing at the edge of a slot you could have stepped into, and the sound is suddenly enormous, and warm air is coming out of the ground past your knees.

The survey called this whole basin a sump, on the reasonable assumption that a low place holds water. It lies forty metres below the level of an ocean seventy kilometres west. It has not held water since before there were animals to drink it.

## The grille

The slots are the roof.

Under the caprock, in a wet age, water dissolved a room out of the salt and left it. Later the roof foundered — most of it — but a lattice of harder fracture-fillings survived, silica-cemented ribs a metre thick, crossing and re-crossing over a void ninety metres by forty. From above, the ribs read as ordinary stone in an ordinary stone plain; the gaps between them are narrow, undercut, dark, easy to walk across without noticing you have crossed anything. From below, the same gaps flare wide. The geometry is a trick of section: from the surface you see mostly rock, from the floor you see mostly sky.

So the room is not dark. That surprises everyone. Ordinary daylight comes down in bars — clean, cool, dust-striped bars that lie across the floor and the walls and move all day at the speed of the sun. You can read a tally board by it. You can see the colour of things. There is no gloom to hide in, and nothing here has evolved to want any.

There is a ladder bolted into one rib, iron rungs in a line of drill holes, going down eighteen metres. Somebody stencilled a number beside it. The number is 4.

## The room

At the bottom the air is thick. Below sea level, the pressure is generous; sound carries here the way it carries over water, and smell does not disperse so much as sit. The first breath is ammonia, sharp enough to make your eyes run, and under it hot dust, iron, bread, and — incongruously, unmistakably — the sea.

Then you look up, and the ceiling is moving.

They hang in the slots and on the ribs and down the walls in layers three and four deep, hand-sized, wings folded like closed umbrellas, membranes the colour of weak tea. A pelt of dust-grey filaments, not fur, more like the nap on old velvet. Ears folded like paper that has been opened and refolded too many times. A leaf of gristle standing up from the muzzle to gather sound. They hang by four hooks — two feet, two thumb-claws — which means they can shuffle sideways, and do, continuously, a hand's width an hour, so that the wall itself creeps. Where the layers thin you can see that the wall behind them is not stone. It is a felt of the dead, dried flat, salted, worked outward by the living toward the cold periphery, mummified so thoroughly that a body from a century ago still has its ears. It is warm and slightly resilient, like a saddle blanket. Insulation, and archive, and load-bearing.

Higher up, where the heat pools, the young: naked, grey-pink, in a continuous shrill band of begging that does not stop and has not stopped, at any hour, for as long as this room has had a floor. Below them the adults. In the cool corners where the downdrafts fall, the sick and the old, and things that wait for them.

There are, by the last count on the board, four hundred and ten thousand head.

## Salt

Nothing in this room makes water. This is the law that everything else obeys.

At dusk they go west, seventy kilometres, to the fog belt where the cold current puts a wet grey wall against the coastal hills. They fly through it with their mouths open and their filaments spread and drink off their own coats, and they skim the sea's skin, and they take in salt with it, and their nasal glands wring the salt back out in a brine so concentrated it crystallises on the muzzle before it can drip.

They come home heavy and sneeze.

All night, in the warm updraft, the room gets a fine snow of salt. It settles on the ribs, on the felt, on the terraces, on the enamel mug somebody left in 1961. It glittered in the light bars at noon and by evening it has been walked into everything. It is the only mineral import this place receives, and it arrives entirely by animal, from an ocean the tenants have seen and the room never will. It is also what preserves the dead, which is why the walls can be made of them.

The young get the only actual liquid in the chamber, carried back in crops, mouth to mouth, in the hot upper strata. Everything else that is wet here is breath. The room's humidity is a rumour four hundred thousand animals keep telling, and the fan exists to stop it.

## The toll

Each slot has a throat — a narrow place where the flaring gap constricts before it opens to the sky — and in each throat hangs something pale and jointed and about the length of your arm, with forelimbs folded into hooks and a body the exact colour of the silica. It waits at the constriction because the tenants must accelerate there. It takes one in forty. It stridulates while it works, a sound like calico tearing very slowly, and this is most of what you hear at the top of the ladder.

Beside each slot, in old black paint, a number: 3, 7, 0, 12. Someone was keeping score. The numbers are not warnings. They are yields.

In the light bars, a larger cousin patrols — same lineage, same gristle leaf, twice the span, entirely uninterested in guano. The bars are hunting lanes. The tenants know this in whatever way they know things, and the exodus is organised around it: they leave through the slots whose light has moved off, so the exits migrate across the ceiling all afternoon in step with the sun, and the room's whole population turns slowly like a fluid finding a drain.

On the floor, a long-legged thing with naked tail and enormous feet runs the terraces, taking the young that fall. It stores them in the miners' sample holes. Six hundred holes, drilled on a five-metre grid in a single season, and every one of them now a larder, plugged with a dried body and a plug of salt.

And downwind of the fan there is a cone of silence.

Not silence — the opposite. A corridor where the hum is loud enough to jam the clicks, where the tenants' acoustic world simply stops, and they fly into it blind. They see with sound; the room's real architecture is a shape made of echoes, and the fan has been quietly building a wall in it for sixty years. The floor beneath that corridor is a fan-shaped drift of dried wings, ten centimetres deep, crisp as leaf litter, and the big-footed things hunt along its edge every night with the patience of a business.

## Return No. 4

The works is scattered and mostly gone.

A concrete block at the lip, half-buried in its own spalled rubble, with a rusted stack and an axial fan the size of a cartwheel still turning behind a grille. A motor. A cable coming in from the horizon on short iron poles with porcelain insulators, half of them broken, the wire still live. A narrow-gauge rail emerging from a small adit and running thirty metres to a tipped hopper; the rest of the line was lifted and the sleepers' ghosts are still visible in the pavement as pale rectangles, stones of a slightly different colour.

Terraces cut into the floor. A tally board in a tin frame, ruled in columns, HEAD and TONS and a third column nobody labelled, where someone chalked TENANTS and someone else chalked a number that has been rubbed out and rewritten many times. An anemometer in a cage, still twitching. A gauge with a needle that moves when the wind on the plain changes. Enamel mugs. One boot. A sign, legible, in three languages: NO OPEN FLAME. Guano dust is a fuel and this is a room full of it.

They were mining the floor. Dry guano, aged guano, the nitrate that forms where breath and stone and bacteria have argued for a thousand years — bagged and railed out to a coast and shipped to fields on other continents, to grow plants in places that have plants. The fans were installed to keep it dry, because wet guano cakes and rots and is worth less. So the machine and the colony have been pulling against each other every night for six decades: four hundred thousand animals humidifying a room, one cartwheel of steel drying it, and the balance point between them is the weather here.

There is fresh grease on the fan bearing. The tally has dates continuing past the last year anyone can remember. Nobody is here. Somebody comes.

## The floor

The floor is not floor. It is a talus of dry guano sand, six metres deep where the deepest terrace cuts it, giving underfoot like dry snow, faintly warm, and things swim in it — larvae, mites, a blind pale swimmer with a jaw like a bear trap that takes them from below. On the older surfaces, white bloom: actinomycete films, fungal threads, the only growth in the basin, eating what falls and giving off the bread smell. Nothing green has ever been here. Nothing green would be permitted.

The cut faces are banded. Dark, pale, dark, pale: fat years and lean years, fog years and fogless years, each pair a summer at a coast seventy kilometres away, recorded in a hole in the ground by animals that cannot write. You can stand at the terrace edge and read the climate of a place you cannot see, back through the works, back through whatever people were doing before the works, back four thousand pairs.

At the bottom of the deepest cut the bands stop. Below them is a layer of wings only, and below that, a metre of clean pale sand with nothing in it at all. The log calls it STERILE and stops there, because the men were paid by the ton and it was not worth anything.

It is not sterile. It is just before.

## The hour

The pressure falls on the plain in the late afternoon and the room begins to exhale.

You feel it first as a change in the downdraft at the ladder — the cool air stops pouring in, hesitates, reverses. Dust that has been settling all day lifts. The clicking, which has been a steady rain-on-tin all afternoon, rises in pitch and density until it is a single continuous tone, and the begging band above it goes frantic, and the toll-keepers in the throats unfold.

Then the walls let go.

It takes eleven minutes. The sound is not a roar of wings so much as the sound of grain pouring out of a hold, magnified until it is a physical pressure on the chest, and the light bars go brown and strobing, and the temperature drops two degrees as the warm mass leaves, and the floor is suddenly visible — terraces, drill holes, the wing drift, the big-footed things standing very still with their heads up — and the salt that has been held in suspension all day comes down through the emptied air in a slow glittering fall.

Conversation is impossible. The board says so, in the third column, in a hand that pressed hard.

Then it is quiet, or what passes for quiet: the fan, the beetles, the hiss of sand still sliding down the terraces, the stragglers, the things in the throats settling in to digest. The fan runs harder now, drying the empty room. The light bars move across a floor with almost nothing on it. Outside, the plain does not change and will not change and has nothing to say about any of this.

In four hours the pressure will rise, and the room will inhale, and the walls will be rebuilt out of the dark, and the salt will start again.

## Rules

No water is made here; it is borrowed, carried in bodies, and leaves as breath.
Salt arrives only by animal.
The stone is finished. The only erosion left is done by teeth, feet, and acid, and it is very slow, and it is the only kind still happening.
Light comes in bars, and the bars are hunting lanes, and everything that lives here schedules itself around them.
The room has a number. The excess is eaten on time.
Sound is the true architecture; the fan builds a wall in it every night, and the wall is a killing floor, and no one intended this.
Nothing outside. There is nothing outside. The plain is what a world looks like when it is done.

The room is not done. That is the whole of the difference, and it is eighteen metres down, and you could walk over it.
