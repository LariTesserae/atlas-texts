## The Shelf

The ice forms at four in the morning, in shallow black pans, under a sky with almost nothing in it to give the heat back.

This is the whole trick of the place. Up here the air is too thin to hold warmth or to carry sound or to feed a fire, and so the ground spends the night throwing its heat straight past the atmosphere into the dark. A pan of brine, three centimeters deep, insulated below with cushion-resin and straw, will drop to minus nine while the stone a meter away still holds the memory of afternoon. By the time the eastern rim goes gray, every pan on the plain has a lid of ice on it the thickness of a fingernail, and the keepers are out walking their rounds with wooden mallets, cracking the lids, lifting the slabs, stacking them into the cellars.

Then the sun comes over the rim and the plain begins to burn.

---

It is a shelf of old rock at four thousand four hundred meters, sixty kilometers long and twenty across, ringed on three sides by peaks that go another sixteen hundred up and drop, on the fourth, into a gorge with a river at the bottom of it that nobody on the shelf has ever touched. Below the gorge lip there is a cloud deck, most days: a white floor, flat as the plain itself, hiding a hot wet world that the shelf sells cold to. The cold goes down twice a night on the funicular. Nobody up here talks much about where it goes after that.

The heat is not the heat of a low desert. It is radiant, unsubtle, unhumid. At noon the varnished basalt reads sixty-two degrees and will raise a blister on a bare forearm in four seconds; the air in the shade of a station hut is thirty-one, and dry as a kiln, and cold on the wet side of your shirt. You sweat and the sweat is gone before you know it formed. Sun on the back of the neck is a hot wire; step into a shadow the width of a cable pole and the wire is cut. People here do not describe themselves as hot. They describe themselves as *lit*, or *cooked on one side*.

The sky is the deep bruised violet you only get above four thousand meters. At the horizon it turns rust-brown, because by early afternoon the thermals have lifted a lid of dust off the plain and stacked it five hundred meters up, where the inversion holds it flat. So there are two skies: a violet one overhead and a brown ceiling at eye level, and the rim peaks stand up through the ceiling like things seen through a dirty window.

---

The surface is old. That is the first thing a geologist would say and the last thing a keeper would think to mention. The shelf is a stripped erosion surface, a floor worn flat somewhere near sea level a very long time ago and then lifted, and everything on it is a settled form: cobble washes rounded and re-rounded, ventifacts polished to a dull gloss, hoodoos worn down to stumps like pulled teeth, desert varnish so thick and so slow that the survey marks cut a hundred and forty years ago have already begun to re-darken in their grooves. Nothing here is freshly broken. Even the ruin is weathered.

Frost has been sorting the ground for millennia. Across the middle district, the stones lie in polygons and circles two and three meters across, fine gravel in the centers, cobbles at the rims, each cell heaving a fraction of a millimeter every night and settling every morning. This is called the Sorting. When the first stations went in, the engineers laid their pan-fields to the polygons because the polygons were already level, already drained, already sorted, and it was cheaper to agree with the ground than to argue. From the rim, in low sun, you cannot reliably tell the machine-fields from the patterned ground. The plain has adopted them.

---

Water exists here in three states and none of them is a puddle at noon.

There is the river, seven hundred meters down, a green thread you can see and never reach. There is the fog, which comes up over the western lip in a season of about ninety days, arriving as a low white animal that lies on the plain for an hour before dawn and is gone by eight. There is the dew, which the stone makes for itself on clear nights by radiating until it falls below the dew point of air that has almost no water to give: a film, a beading, a few hundredths of a millimeter. And there are the sinks, three salt basins at the low end where brine sits in pockets under a crust you can walk on if you know the season and cannot if you don't, and where the water is so dense that a wader's legs come out crusted pink.

Everything alive on the shelf is built around the fifty minutes after the dew arrives.

The primary producer is not a plant. It is a crust — cyanobacteria and lichen and a black mold, knitted into the top two millimeters of soil across almost the entire plain. Dry, it is ash-gray and looks like dead ground and crumbles under a boot. Wet, it goes black-green and glossy and swells and starts photosynthesizing within ninety seconds, and for most of an hour the whole shelf smells like iodine and warm bread. Then the sun takes the water back and the crust shuts down and goes gray, and the plain is a stone plain again until the next morning.

The crust grows about a millimeter a decade. It is, in places, effectively continuous for eleven kilometers. Whether it is one organism is a question the keepers find uninteresting and the biologists down in the cloud cannot stop asking.

Vegetation above the crust is scarce and slow: saltbush in the washes, a thorn tree at the gorge lip that has probably been there since before the industry, and the cushions. The cushions are resinous green boulders of plant, some four meters across, some older than the language the keepers speak, growing a hand's width per century. A spoonful of resin per cushion per decade is the legal take. It seals the cellar lids. There is no other use for it and no other supply, and the whole cold economy of the shelf rests on that spoonful.

---

There is no bare ground. This is the thing that surprises people who come up expecting a desert.

The plain is *full*. Every stone has a tenant: a lichen thallus with a border it has held for two hundred years, a skink's perch worn shiny, a beetle's territory. There is an underhare mouth every forty centimeters across the middle districts — pallid, short-eared, mostly subterranean rodents in towns of thirty thousand, and their excavated soil is fluffed and porous and swallows footsteps whole. There are beetles on every ridge crest. There are the machines, one every two or three hectares, and around each machine's footing a ring of denser life because the footing drips.

You cannot set a boot down on the shelf without standing on four lives and one lease. And yet nothing is taller than your knee, and you can see twenty kilometers, and the sky is ninety percent of everything. The place is packed and open at once: saturation at ankle height, an enormous vacancy above.

---

The industry is silent because it has no choice. There is not enough oxygen up here to run a decent fire; combustion is a poor, sooty, sulking business at this altitude, and everything that burns at all burns badly. So the shelf runs on gradient instead.

Heat stores: pits of black basalt sand under glass, charged all day. Night radiators: the pans, and the fin-panels, and the harps. Between them, engines — closed-cycle, sealed, running on the difference between a hot pit at seventy and a sky-facing plate at minus fifteen. They turn all night with a sound below hearing, a twenty-hertz pressure you feel in the sinuses when you walk past a station and lose when you walk away. Pumps tick. Shutters open at dusk with a single flat *clack* that carries about forty meters and then simply stops existing. Ceramic insulators on wooden poles, furred with lichen, carry current from the heat stores out to the harps and down to the funicular head.

The harps are the dew machines: frames of fine mesh, two meters tall, angled into the fog. Their profile — the ridging, the hydrophilic tips, the waxy channels between — was copied, a hundred and thirty years ago, from the beetles.

The beetles are still there. On foggy mornings they cover the ridge crests in their tens of thousands, standing on their heads, elytra into the wind, letting condensation run down their own grooves to their own mouths. The industry learned the trick and then scaled it, and now the harps take a share of the fog and the beetles work the gaps between them, and on certain mornings the beetles simply climb the harps and drink from the mesh, which the keepers permit, because the alternative is scraping beetles off the mesh by the kilo.

Scattered, all of it. No town, no factory, no smokestack. A hut, a cellar lid, a pan-field, a harp, a pole line, and then two hectares of full ground, and then another. Forty thousand small structures across a region and not one of them big enough to hold a crowd. The keepers — a few dozen adults and their children, spread thin — travel the old rail beds on electric trikes with solid tires, and they do not shout at each other, because shouting at four thousand four hundred meters costs breath you have not got. They sign. They murmur at conversational distances of half a meter. A raised voice up here is a kind of physical exertion, like lifting a pan, and it is considered slightly embarrassing.

---

The fauna is abundant and almost mute, and the quiet is not only physiology.

Tally birds work the plain: gray, stilt-legged, waist-high stalkers with a hooked bill and no functional voice, who move at a slow measured walk and stop and strike. They hunt the dawn — underhare pups, skinks, beetles — and they have learned that a loud noise means a warren flush, which means a stampede, which means collapsed burrows and dead young and a ruined crust and, three years later, a dust bowl. The keepers know this too. It is why the trikes have solid tires and why nobody runs a percussion drill on the shelf, and why the whole ecology and the whole industry have converged, without ever discussing it, on the same rule: *do not startle the ground.*

So the loudest things are small. The tick of stone contracting at dusk, a sound like a fingernail on a table, coming from everywhere at once for about twenty minutes. The dry hiss of beetle elytra. The wind in the pole wires, which is up at four meters and does not reach the ground. Your own pulse. And, at pressure changes, the warrens: thirty thousand burrow mouths breathing out together as a front comes over the rim, a low sigh across two kilometers of plain that keepers call the plain turning over in its sleep.

At midday the thermals go up in columns and the lifters come off the cliffs to ride them — enormous, ragged, black, unable to take off from flat ground in air this thin, entirely dependent on the gorge edge and the noon heat. They spend the whole afternoon in the sky doing nothing but climbing and sliding, and at dusk they go back to the rock. In the sinks, ten thousand pink-gray waders stand in brine and filter shrimp and make no sound at all. At four in the morning, frost moths cluster on the heat-store vents, shivering their flight muscles, warmed by the day before yesterday's sun.

---

A few things are off, and the people here have stopped noticing them.

The cellars are not all made ice. Some of them broke into a buried body of it during the second expansion — fossil ice, twelve thousand years old, full of small round bubbles from an atmosphere nobody has ever breathed. It is cut and sold the same as the rest. The keepers can tell it by the bubbles and prefer it, saying it melts slower, which is true.

There is a station in the Old Field, decommissioned in the second generation, whose shutters jammed open and whose heat store cracked and which now, through some accident of plumbing, still makes a slab of ice every clear night. Nobody maintains it. The beetles have found it. On foggy mornings there are so many on that one pan that the ice comes up black and moving, and by seven it is a wet ring in the dust and by eight it is gone, and it happens again the next night, and has for sixty years.

And on certain still mornings, in the fog season, there is a second dew — a wetness that arrives after the sun has already taken the first, coming up out of the warrens and the crust and the cushions, the plain's own accumulated breath condensing on itself. It lasts eleven or twelve minutes. The crust greens a second time. The keepers call it the plain sweating, and they open the harps for it, and it is worth about a third of a night's yield, and no one has ever satisfactorily explained where the water comes from.

---

By nine the plain is gray and hot and looks like nothing at all: stone, shimmer, a dust devil walking slowly across the Sorting, the brown ceiling, the violet overhead, a scatter of small dark structures too far apart to seem like a civilization.

It is not nothing. It is a surface four thousand years old, occupied to saturation, running two shifts — a living one at dawn that lasts fifty minutes, and a mechanical one at night that lasts nine hours — with a long dead-looking noon between them in which everything waits, and the sand pits charge, and the lifters climb, and the cold that was made in the dark goes down the funicular into the cloud, slab by slab, to a world that has never seen the sky do this.
