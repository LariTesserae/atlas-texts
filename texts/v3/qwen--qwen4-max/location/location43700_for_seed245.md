# The Unfired

Nothing here has been fired yet. That is the first fact, and the others follow from it.

The ground is warm underfoot and gives slightly, like the flank of something sleeping. It is clay — not soil, never soil, no grain of quartz that arrived by accident — clay that has been slaked, screened, settled in ponds, drained, and wedged. You can see the wedging. Across the flats run the long spiral seams of a bull's-head knead, ridges the height of a man, repeating in a rhythm that continues past the point where the eye gives up. Someone worked this ground to drive the air out. Someone worked *all* of it. The seams go to the horizon and, presumably, past.

Between the seams the surface is tacky and printed. Not footprints. Fingerprints: whorls and loops in the clay, some the size of a coin, some the size of a parish, all of them still crisp, none of them softened by rain or filled with dust, because rain here does not erode and dust here has not been invented long enough to settle. The arrises are sharp. Every edge in this world is a first edge. Nothing is rounded. There is no sand, no gravel, no smoothed pebble anywhere — the concept of a curve worn into a thing by time has not yet taken hold.

## Water that has not decided

It rained an hour ago and it will rain again in three. The rain is hot and tastes of lime and comes from above at the temperature of blood, so it does not cool you; it only makes the air heavier. It falls in fat slow drops out of a low ceiling of steam, and when it strikes the flats it does not splash so much as *join*.

Water lies everywhere in sheets a finger deep, and in the seams it stands to the knee, and it weeps continuously out of every vertical surface — out of the wall stubs, out of the scaffold footings, out of the great drying racks — as though the made things were perspiring, which they are. Condensation runs down the underside of everything and gathers at the lowest lip and lets go. The whole basin sweats. It is a hot wet dimness, the wetness of a laundry, of a covered pot, of the inside of a mouth.

But the water has no channels. It sits in films and pans and moves in slow braided sheets according to a slope so gentle it is a matter of opinion. There are no rivers. There are *proposals* for rivers: shallow scored lines in the clay, ruled straight, running away toward the sump, waiting to be given permission. Water here has not yet learned to cut. It has not learned that it is allowed to take material away and carry it downhill and put it somewhere else. It pools, it films, it evaporates, it returns. It has no sediment. It is as clear as broth and just as warm.

Near the eastern wall stub there is a gully the length of a boat. It is new. Its walls are raw and vertical and its floor holds a thimble of sorted grit — the first sorted grit in the world — and the water in it moves with a purposefulness that is faintly obscene. Things nearby have begun to lean toward it. Wear has arrived. It is not welcome, and it is not stoppable, and it is the only thing in this landscape that has a future.

## Everything is made, including the floor you stand on

Walk out far enough and you find the wall. Not a wall around a place: the wall *is* the horizon, the inner face of a rim, curving gently away left and right until distance eats it. It rises out of the flats and goes up into the steam and its surface bears the marks of coiling — enormous ropes of clay laid one on another, thumb-smoothed on the inside, left rough without. The world is the bottom of a vessel. You are standing on the inside floor of something thrown, or built up by hand, at a scale that makes the word *pot* useless.

Above, where sky should be, there is a lid. You infer it more than see it: a low dark ceiling of the same clay, held up by nothing you can identify, pierced here and there with vent-holes that let down columns of dim orange light. The light does not come from a sun. It comes from below and from the vents, and it comes *through* things — through the thin walls of the drying racks, through the translucent belly of a form left too long on the wet floor, so that some objects glow from inside like paper lanterns and cast shadows in the wrong direction.

Below the floor there is fire. Not yet the fire — a pilot, a breath, a warmth kept steady since before counting. You feel it in the soles of your feet and in the soles of your knees when you kneel. It comes up through a grid of tunnels whose mouths are spaced across the plain like wells, exhaling at a constant ninety degrees, and around each mouth the clay has gone pale and hard and hairline-cracked: the only dry ground anywhere, ringing the vents in white halos. Sit near one and you will be comfortable and slightly cooked. Nothing lives in them. Nothing has ever lived in them.

The heat has nowhere to go. The lid holds it. The steam holds it. The basin is the lowest place in whatever contains it, and every drop that condenses on the lid comes back down here, and the whole vast arrangement cycles its own moisture and its own warmth and makes, out of that cycling, weather: fog fronts that move at walking pace, humidity seasons, sudden white squalls of vapour that come off the settling ponds and blind a district for a day, dust devils of dry slip that walk the flats and put down thin drifts of angular powder that no wind has yet learned to ripple.

## Spaced for drying

The plain is nearly empty, and the emptiness is a rule.

Presences stand days apart. A scaffold of lashed basalt-wool poles and copper pins, forty storeys of it, holding nothing, its lashings furry with mineral fibre. A drying rack like the ribcage of a beached whale, its slats bowed, each slat carrying one small object no bigger than a fist. A wall stub three miles long and chest high, going nowhere, both ends finished. A staircase that ascends nine flights and stops in the air, its treads still bearing the paddle-marks of the beating that shaped them. A lake that is not a lake but a palmprint, filled with hot clear water, its ridges standing up around the margin like a low range of hills.

They are spaced because things that stand too close together dry unevenly, and things that dry unevenly warp, and things that warp are reclaimed. Also — this is the part you learn by walking — drafts that stand too close begin to interfere. Two provisional objects within a certain distance will start to reconcile with one another: to trade edges, to agree on a shared corner, to become one confused thing. So the makers, whoever they are, keep the plain sparse. Miles of wet nothing between each attempt. The emptiness is not desolation. It is *shelf space*.

And on the flats, at intervals of a day's travel, stand the cones.

They are slender three-sided pyramids of pale grey slip, tall as towers, set on their bases at a slight deliberate tilt. They are heat-work clocks. They measure not temperature but the accumulated labour of heat over time, and they do it by bending. Most stand straight. A few, far out toward the sump, have begun to bow — slowly, imperceptibly, the way a candle bows in a warm room — and where a cone has bent so far that its tip touches the floor, the ground around it is different: harder, ringing underfoot, faintly vitreous, no longer editable.

Some cones have bent where there is no heat at all. Nobody has explained this. The explanation may not have been made yet.

## Quiet, and the reasons for quiet

The air is so thick that sound goes nowhere. Steam and clay-fog eat it; the wet ground takes the rest. A wall a mile off collapses and you receive it as a pressure in the sternum and a shiver in the standing water, and only afterward, out of order, a soft sound like a sigh in a cupboard.

Mostly it is quiet. Tick, tick — the drying racks contracting. The long liquid click of slip settling in a pond. A hiss at a vent. The papery rustle of efflorescence growing on a wall, salt pushing out of the clay in white feathers and blooming and dropping, which is the closest thing here to a season. Under all of it a hum you stop hearing and then cannot stop hearing: the copper wire.

It runs at ankle height across the whole basin, horizon to horizon, a single strand of soft copper drawn thin, anchored at either end to nothing you can find. It is a cutting wire. It is the tool that separates the finished lump from the hump. It is taut and it hums in the slow fog-wind, and where it passes, the clay beneath it is scored to a depth of a hair, and everything on the far side of it is very slightly less certain than everything on this side.

Once a day there is one enormous noise. It comes from the reclaim pits — the sunken acres where failed work is thrown and slaked back down into slip — and it is the sound of a hundred tons of dry scrap drinking water and letting go of its shape. It lasts a minute. Then the fog closes over it and the quiet comes back like water closing over a stone.

## The churn

Nothing here is finished, and unfinished things do not hold still.

The plain is being worked. Forms rise out of the wet floor over the course of an afternoon: a colonnade, a dome, an aqueduct with no water to carry, a thing like a lung. They come up green and glistening and holding their own weight only barely, and they sag as you watch, and some of them slump, and the slumped ones are dragged — by nothing visible, along grooves that appear as the dragging happens — back to the pits, where they are broken and wetted and beaten and become ground again.

The ground itself is grog. Dig anywhere and you find the temper: crushed shards of earlier worlds, angular, sharp, graded by sieve. Some shards carry a fragment of a pattern that was never used again. Some are glazed, which is impossible, since nothing has been fired. The clay you are standing on is made of the failures of the clay you are standing on, and the recycling has no bottom that anyone has reached.

Storms here are revisions. A front comes across the flats and behind it the geometry has been re-indexed: the staircase is now nine flights *down*, the wall stub has acquired a third finished end, the tower you were walking toward is behind you and slightly smaller, and your own footprints have been smoothed out and replaced with a better set. Directions are not fixed because fixing them is a later operation. Distance is negotiable in the green state; two places touch if they are similar enough. Cause and effect are loosely coupled, hand-tight only, and sometimes an echo arrives before its collapse, and sometimes a thing exists in four drafts at once and you can walk between them like rooms.

There is no death here. Nothing has been alive. There is only slumping, and reclaiming, and the wire.

## What passes for ecology

No plant. No animal. No bird, no insect, no root, no seed, nothing with a face.

But the wet warm clay is filmed. In the shallow sheets there are slicks — bronze, violet, oil-rainbow — sheets of something simpler than life that eat iron out of the clay body and leave it pale and porous. They spread in rings from the vents, they thicken in the warm pans, they die back when the fog lifts, and where they have been the surface is faintly friable, and the crisp fingerprints there have gone soft.

That is the second erosion, and the more dangerous one. The salt blooms and pushes the walls apart from inside; the slicks digest the binder; the gully carries away its thimble of grit. Three kinds of wearing, all newborn, all in their first hour. The world was made without wear in it. Wear is arriving anyway, from underneath, from the smallest and least recognised directions, and it will not be reclaimed, because you cannot slake a process back into a lump.

## The makers

There are none. There is only their evidence, which is total: every surface bears a hand. Tools lie where they were set down — ribs of polished obsidian, paddles of fired clay, a bundle of basalt fibres bound with copper for a brush, a wooden-looking thing that is not wood, since wood has never existed here. The tools are mid-stroke. A score-and-slip joint is half-completed, the scoring wet, the slip not yet applied, and it has been that way long enough for a salt feather to grow across it.

The plain offers hypotheses the way it offers drafts. That the makers are the water, and the hot weeping is their attention. That the makers are outside the rim and the whole basin is a single object being made by hands whose scale makes them indistinguishable from weather. That there are no makers, that making is simply what this material does when kept warm and wet, the way salt does efflorescence. That you are a defect in the slip, an air bubble that was not wedged out, and that the churn is the ground trying to work you toward the surface.

Attention fires things. This is the only rule you can test. Stand and look at a form long enough and it stiffens; the surface goes matte; a fine crackle opens across it; it stops being editable and becomes, permanently, itself. Look away and you cannot be sure it stays. The plain is full of things that were watched once and are therefore fixed, and things never watched at all, which are still moving.

## What is coming

The dimness is a lid, not a night. The heat under the floor is a breath, not the fire. The cones are mostly straight.

Everything here — the rim, the lid, the scaffold, the plain, the palmprint lake, the wire, the four drafts of the staircase, you — is greenware. Unfired. Water-soluble. Provisional. It can all be slaked back down. It has been, several times, if the grog is any witness.

And the whole vast shed is built for one operation, which has not happened yet: the lid comes down properly, the vents are stopped, the fire under the floor is fed, and the heat climbs until the cones bow all at once across a continent of drying-floor, and the water goes out of the clay forever, and the shrinkage takes everything a tenth smaller, and what survives the shrinkage is real and what does not is shards for the next temper.

The rain will stop. The gully will harden mid-cut with its first thimble of grit inside it, the only sediment this world ever sorted, kept like a relic in glass.

Out toward the sump, where there is no heat, a cone the height of a tower has bent another finger's width since morning. It is not measuring the fire.

It is measuring how long the fire has been waiting.
