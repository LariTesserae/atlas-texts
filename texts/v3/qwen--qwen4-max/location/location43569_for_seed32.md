## The Kept Room

From above there is nothing to see. A deflation basin, the lowest ground for a hundred leagues, where a sea lay once and left its salt behind in the dust; the plain glares, the wind gusts and drops and gusts, and the dust goes over it in long brown combers. Somewhere in the middle of all that flatness there is a seam. It is a hand's width across and about as long as a person, and it is easy to walk past, and the only reason anyone stops is that the ground hums. Not metaphorically. Through the soles of the boots, a steady buzz, like standing on a bridge with a truck on it — except there is no truck, and there has never been a truck, and the buzz does not vary.

The seam is a mouth. The wind goes into it without hurry. If you pull a hair from your head and hold it in the draft at the lip, the hair stands out straight and does not tremble. Outside, the wind is gusting; you can feel it shove your back. Inside the seam, the air moves at one speed, and has moved at that speed since before there were words for speed.

You have to go in feet-first, on your side, exhaling.

---

The first thing is the light, and the second thing is the sound, and they arrive together and feel like the same event.

The chamber is small — eleven paces by seven, the ceiling low enough to touch in places — and it is brighter than the noon you just left. Not sunlit. There is no sun in here, no beam, no direction, no shadow anywhere, including under your own hand. The walls are alabaster, a pale selenite the color of a fingernail held over a candle, and they glow from within, all of them, evenly, the way a lampshade glows. The stone has a long memory for light. It takes in the plain's brutal afternoons and lets them go over years, decades; it has been averaging the light of ten thousand noons and ten thousand nights, and what it radiates now is the mean. There is no dawn in here and no dusk. There is one brightness, and it is the average of all the brightnesses that ever fell on the roof, and it is cool — the heat was stripped out somewhere in all that scattering, so the room is the temperature of a cellar in spring, twelve degrees, and the stone draws the warmth out of your palm slowly and without malice.

The sound is not a noise. It is a substance. It fills the chamber the way the stone fills the walls. Every audible frequency is present and none is louder than its neighbors, so there is nothing to hear, in the ordinary sense — no note, no rhythm, nothing to count, nothing to anticipate. It is a solid white pressure, and it enters through the sternum and the sinuses and the roots of the teeth, and your ears pack and equalize like an airplane's descent, and you swallow, and swallow again. Sound at this density stops being information. It becomes a material you are standing in. Hold your hand out and you can feel the difference between the palm and the back of the hand.

And it does not change. That is the thing you will not be able to explain afterward. Loud is common. Loud and *unvarying* is not a thing the world does.

---

There is almost no air in the room. That is what takes a moment to understand.

The chamber is packed. Floor to ceiling it is filled with tubes — thousands upon thousands of stone throats, mouth to mouth, wall to wall, hexagonal where they were laid down and rounded everywhere now, from the size of a thumb to the size of a head to a very few the size of a torso. They are not stacked; they are grown into one another, sharing walls, the way cells share walls in a comb. The free volume of the room is perhaps a tenth of the room. The air is a thin lace threaded through solid matter, and every one of those tubes is sounding, has been sounding, is a pipe with a wind through it and a voice determined by its length and the exact worn curve of its lip.

There is one path through. It is body-width. It is *your* body's width, uncannily — a negative cast, a slot that winds between the resonators with the clearance of a well-made door, and you go along it sideways with your arms down and your chest brushing stone on both sides, and the stone is buzzing so hard against your skin that it acts as a lubricant and you slide, numb, like a coin down a slot. Your cheeks buzz. Your eyelashes buzz. Where the tubes' mouths pass your ear you get, for a half-second, a single identifiable pitch, a bassoon-ish groan or a thin silver whistle, and then it is swallowed back into the white.

Nothing here is natural. There is no bedrock, no cave, no country rock anywhere in the shell. The whole chamber is a made object sitting in the plain's dust like a nut in a cake — floor, ceiling, every pipe, every wall, laid down. And there are no tools in it, no mechanism, no hinge, no wheel, no fire-scar, no wire. Nothing was assembled. All of it was secreted.

---

The makers are still here, in the seams, and there is almost nothing left of them.

Along the mortar-lines — the fine joints where one generation of tube-wall met the next — run filaments the color of old ivory, dry-looking, brittle-looking, alive. Here and there a rosette the size of a coin, faintly amber, faintly pink, the only color in the entire chamber. Add it all up and you have perhaps two square feet of living tissue in a room of some hundreds of tons. It looks like poverty. It is not poverty. It is retirement.

The threads are lithophytes. They eat dust and light and they drink water that has never been liquid: selenite is a fifth water by weight, water locked in the crystal lattice, and the threads have a chemistry for prying it out molecule by molecule, and they pay it back as stone. They laid this whole thing down, tube by tube, over a span that makes the plain's old sea look recent. They were vast once. They built their body outward and inward until the packing was complete, until there was no more room between presences, and then they stopped, because the work was finished, and now they persist in the joints at a hair's breadth per decade, maintaining.

There is no green in them. There is nothing here for green to do.

---

The wind that sounds the room comes in low and leaves high, a permanent siphon between the cold mass below and the burning plain above, and it has been mid-breath for a hundred thousand years. Outside, weather. Inside, the average of weather. The chamber's mass and its ten thousand throats are a filter with an enormous time constant; gusts go in and a mean comes out. This is the rule of the place, and it applies to everything: **the room integrates.** Light goes in as day and night and comes out as one brightness. Wind goes in as chaos and comes out as one chord. Whatever varies, enters, and is averaged, and does not vary again.

The wear is part of the tuning. Every lip in here has been licked round by a century of dry air carrying a little dust; every chamfer is a slow abrasion that lowered a pitch by a semitone over an epoch. The threads lay stone down at exactly the rate the wind takes it away. So the shape is fixed while the substance passes through it — a standing form, like the shape of a flame or the shape of a waterfall, made of matter that is only visiting. Weigh the room in a year and it weighs the same. Weigh it in ten thousand years and it weighs the same, and not one atom will be the same atom.

It is finished. That is what the word for this place is. Not ruined, not growing, not decaying. *Finished*, in the way a riverbed is finished, in balance, arrived at its own final shape and holding it.

There is no water to drink. There is water everywhere, tons of it, chemically married to the walls, and you could die of thirst with your cheek against it. The air is dry enough to crack your lip in an hour and clean enough to have no smell but plaster and cold wax and one thread of something like dried hay.

---

After a while — you cannot say how long; there is nothing to count — your hearing gives up defending itself and opens. The white wall stops being a wall and becomes a medium, and you begin to hear *through* it, and what you hear then is yourself: the blood in the carotid, the squeak of the small bones in your neck, the whistle at the back of your nose, the enormous wet machinery of being an animal in a place where there are no animals.

There are no animals. There has never been an animal here. But the cells are shaped for tenants. A dozen of the larger tubes are body-sized, smooth-lipped, dry, warm-free, ventilated, and empty, and they have been empty since they were made, and they are the most troubling objects in the chamber — niches built for an occupant that never arrived, a hotel with no guests, a comb with no brood. You fit one of them almost exactly. You will not get into it. It is not for you; it is not for anything; it is simply the shape the packing left over.

Speak, and the room does not echo. There is no surface here that gives sound back — everything is either resonating or drinking. Your voice goes into the chord and detunes it very slightly, and for four or five seconds there is a beat, a slow wobble in the white, a *wah*, the only event that has occurred in this room since the last event, and then it damps out and the mean closes over the place where your voice was.

You are the only thing in here that changes. That is why the room can't quite hold you. Everything else has been averaged. You are a transient, and the chamber is already relaxing back toward the value it had before you, and will have that value again a few seconds after you leave.

---

Coming out is worse than going in. You squeeze up through the seam into the plain and the world is suddenly, offensively *various* — the wind slaps and lulls and slaps, the light has a direction and makes a shadow of you, the dust moves in gusts, everything is happening at a particular moment and then not happening. It feels cheap. It feels like noise.

The tone stays in your teeth for three days. You will find yourself, in ordinary rooms, waiting for the sound to change, and it will, and you will be disappointed.

Behind you the seam goes on humming at one pitch, in a room with no night in it, packed solid with made stone and one long breath, holding the average of everything that has ever blown across that plain — and it will still be holding it, note for note, when the plain is gone.
