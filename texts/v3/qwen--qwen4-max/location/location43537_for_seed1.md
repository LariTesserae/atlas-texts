You hear it before there is anywhere to stand.

The sound comes up out of the ground at a distance of an hour's walk, and it does not resolve as you approach, because it was never one sound. It is a hundred million small ones, packed shoulder to shoulder, and the packing is what makes the roar: a low continuous grinding fundamental with a grain to it, like a bell that was struck once, long ago, and has not finished deciding what to do about it. Over the fundamental rides the knocking. Over the knocking, the water. Over the water, a thin electrical whine that comes and goes with the weather.

Then you are on it, and the ground is ankle-deep in cold water, and the water is moving, and so is the ground.

---

It is a plain at the bottom of everything. Somewhere beneath it lies the stump of a mountain range, worn down to datum, its last few meters of relief used up ten million years ago. Nothing here has a slope worth the name. Water does not drain; it circulates. It lies in a skin over the whole country, an inch to a shin deep, tidal in a basin with no visible sea, driven by the moon and by the plain's own slow shudder. It rains most days — not hard, a persistent cold drizzle that seems less like weather than like a coolant leak — and when it stops raining the fog condenses directly onto every cold surface, so that the wet never actually ends. Everything sweats. Everything weeps. The rock weeps out of pores; the iron weeps out of seams; the ice weeps in the four hours around noon and then sets again.

The temperature sits just above freezing by day and just below it by night, and that narrow swing across the phase boundary is the entire power supply.

---

The ground is apparatus.

Not ruins. Nothing here was built. There is no maker in this country's history and no evidence of one anywhere in its strata; there is only a family of ores that, under cold brine and a standing electrical gradient, has an *industrial habit* — the way quartz has a hexagonal habit, the way pyrite cubes. Left alone in the wet, these ores crystallize into shafts. They grow journals and cams and toothed wheels the way frost grows ferns on a window, and they grow them in mesh, because a lone wheel is not a stable form here and dissolves back into the seam.

The plain is a factory the way a salt flat is a factory. Dissimilar metals sit in salt water and make a battery; the battery drives ions across the marsh; the ions plate out on whatever is cathodic, which is usually a shaft, which thickens, which changes the current, which plates something else. There are districts of this country that are essentially refineries with no walls: pale green ferrous liquor standing in shallow pans, a haze of chlorine, and slow bright dendrites of pure metal climbing out of the muck toward the light like something learning to stand.

And all of it is old. All of it is *worn*. Erosion has been at these gears longer than there have been vertebrates anywhere. The teeth are rounded to nubs; the shafts are waisted; the flywheels have been ground down to lenses. Nothing should still work. Everything still works, because the wear was mutual: each surface has been shaped by the exact partner it runs against, for an age, until the fit between them is finer than any measurement you could take. They are not parts. They are marriages. Pull one wheel out and its neighbor is a meaningless lump, an impression of a wheel, a mold of something that no longer exists.

At dusk the water in the stone cylinders freezes and expands and drives a million rams home. It takes eleven hours. At dawn the sun — a paler smear traveling behind a permanent low ceiling — thaws them, and they return. One stroke a day. Immense torque, no speed. The dawn chorus of a million rams bottoming out at once is the loudest thing that happens here, and it happens every day, and it has been happening every day since before the mountains gave up.

---

The grease is rendered from the dead.

There has to be lubricant; a plain of married surfaces running wet would seize in a season. It comes from the sumps: natural basins, kilometers across, where the sheet-water carries everything that dies. Bodies accumulate in the shallows in windrows. Ice rams press them. What comes out the far side is a black, faintly sweet-smelling fat that seeps back uphill through the porous country rock and finds the bearings, because bearings are where the pressure is lowest.

The plain eats its dead and runs on them. There is no plant anywhere in this — nothing green, nothing with a leaf, nothing that ever considered the sun. The base of the food web is a film: chemosynthetic slime that lives on iron and sulfide and the warmth of friction, a skin of rust-orange and matte black over every hot surface, cropped and regrown, cropped and regrown, forever.

---

Warmth is the currency, and that is why nothing here has any room.

In a cold country under a dim sky, a loaded bearing at forty degrees is a hearth, and everything alive is pressed against the hearths. The plain is not scattered with life; it is *upholstered* in it. Broad flat grazers like manhole covers, lapping the film, packed rim to rim on the warm terraces, so dense that they walk on each other's backs and the ones underneath don't seem to mind. Seal-worms in every clearance, soft and pink and greased, functioning as the gaskets — the machines leak without them, and the worms breed into the gap at exactly the rate they are extruded out of it. Populations here are inventory. A species can be a spare part.

The midges carry the current. They come in clouds so thick they read as weather, a grain in the air, a brown blur that bends the light; each one has iron in its wings, and when a cloud settles across two terminals it closes the circuit and a district lights up cold blue and starts to turn. When the cloud lifts, that district goes dark and quiet and begins, very slowly, to die. There are places on this plain where the entire electrical supply is a swarm of flies with a two-day life cycle, and the geology has arranged itself around that fact, and waits.

And in the ice rams there are things that are born in the thaw, mate in the eleven-hour freeze, and are killed at the next thaw, laying eggs that survive the crush. Whole generations fit inside a single stroke.

The predators hunt by sound. Something out in the fog can imitate a failing bearing — the specific triple knock of a race beginning to spall — and it holds that knock for hours, patiently, while the scavengers converge from all directions, because a failing bearing means a coming death means food. Half the knocking you hear at night is real. The rest is a mouth.

---

Go out far enough and the country articulates. The Weeping Terraces, where the sheet-water steps down a hundred shallow levels over stacked, worn flywheels. The Long Bearing, a river that runs eleven kilometers inside a single journal, lubricating a shaft the size of a ridge, roaring in a register you feel in the sinuses. The gallery of sumps. The cold fault, where the gradient reverses and the metal grows backwards, dismantling itself into ore.

It goes on. Province-sized, basin-sized, further than the light will carry. And the horizon does something wrong, which you notice around the fourth hour: the far plain is not a different place. It is this place, later. Distance here is duration. The herd you can see moving faintly out at the edge of sight is the herd that is standing around your ankles, three days from now, and it is not a copy.

---

Because the wear did not stop at the metal.

A hundred million identical strokes, repeated past the age of mountains, will grind a groove in anything, and the thing they ground a groove in was the sequence of events. Cause and effect were a bearing surface here once, with a little play in them. The play is gone. Now the effect comes first, out of habit. You hear the ram bottom out and then, some seconds afterward, the frost arrives to drive it. You see the scavengers gather and then, later, the animal dies. Nobody in this country finds this remarkable, because there is nobody, and also because remarkability requires a gap between what is expected and what occurs, and gaps are the one thing this plain does not permit.

There is no clearance left anywhere. That is the rule underneath all the others. Two parts worn perfectly into each other are, at the interface, indistinguishable; there are gears out in the fog that are the same gear, in two places, and if you strike one, both ring. There are shafts turning with nothing driving them — surplus rotation, momentum left over from an event scheduled for next season. There are pockets, a few meters wide, where the sound simply stops, and the machines in them keep moving in total silence, and the water does not ripple, and animals go around.

The grease remembers, too. Load a bearing dressed with fat from one particular windrow of dead and it will, under stress, produce the sound of the animals it was made from: a lowing, a wingbeat, a specific distress call, coming out of the metal at the exact frequency of the shaft. The plain is loud because it is full of machines. It is *that* loud because every machine is also, faintly, an archive of everything that had to die to keep it turning.

---

Stand still for a while and you'll feel the country working on you.

Not malice. Nothing here wants anything. But you are, by standing, a gap — the only slack in a hundred kilometers of married surfaces, a pocket of clearance in a world that has spent an epoch taking up clearance. The water finds your low places. The film begins on your boots, warm, orange, patient. The midges settle on you because you are a difference in potential. Somewhere out in the fog a mouth begins, very quietly, to make the sound of a bearing that is about to fail.

The plain does not destroy. It fits. Given time — and time is the one commodity in unlimited supply here — it will wear you into a shape that meshes, and you will turn, and something will be warmer for it.
