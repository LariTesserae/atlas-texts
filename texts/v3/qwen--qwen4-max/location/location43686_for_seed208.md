## The Pavement

Before there is anything to see, there is the sound, and the sound is not one sound.

The lowest part of it is a roar with no direction — a broadband, chest-deep pressure that comes up through the soles of the feet as much as through the ears, the noise of enormous quantities of water falling into enormous quantities of emptiness. Above that sits a middle register of discrete voices: booms, whistles, a slow gulping note like a jug being emptied, a sustained tone somewhere near the bottom of a man's singing range that has been holding for as long as anyone has been alive to hold a grudge against it. And above *that*, everywhere, at the very top of hearing, a hiss — dry, granular, ceaseless, as if the whole plain were being sanded by hand.

Together they make about the noise of standing beside a waterfall in a mill. People who live here go deaf in their thirties and consider it a normal part of aging, like teeth.

The light is poor. Not night — an even, sourceless grey, the light of ten minutes after a sunset that never quite finishes. There is a cloud lid a few hundred feet up, permanent, moving slowly, and a paler smear in it that travels from one side of the sky to the other and is the sun. Under this the ground goes out flat to a horizon that is a ruled line.

## What the ground is

It is limestone, and it has been going away for a hundred million years.

The surface is a pavement: grey blocks the size of tables, separated by fissures you can put a leg into down to the knee or the hip. The blocks are not smooth. Rain has combed them into parallel runnels, sharp-lipped, all pointing the same way, so that walking across the plain feels like walking against the grain of something enormous. In places the pavement is broken into rubble; in places it is bare as a floor. Standing out of it, weathering proud because they will not dissolve, are knuckles of chert — black, glassy, and the source of every blade in the world here.

There are hills. There are four. They are the leftovers of a mountain range that has otherwise entirely gone to sea in solution: stumps of tougher rock, capped with iron crust, standing two or three hundred feet above a plain that used to be their foothills. From any high point you can see all four at once, forty miles of nothing between them, each one a slightly darker smudge in the fog. They are not landmarks so much as the last teeth in a jaw.

In the hollows and fissure-bottoms lies a little red clay. It is worth understanding what this clay is. It is everything in the vanished mountains that would not dissolve. A hand's depth of it represents a hundred feet of stone. There is no soil here in the ordinary sense; there is only the residue, the ghost, the small stubborn remainder of a landscape that has been eaten down to its final form and has nowhere left to go.

Nothing is green. Nothing has ever been green here. No leaf, no root, no wood, no seed, no pollen. If you go looking for something to burn you will find nothing that was not recently alive and moving.

## Water and breath

Elevation is slight — thirty or forty feet above a sea that is a day and a half away — and the water table is at the surface, or a hand's width below it, or above it, depending on the week. Every fissure has water in it. Every block weeps. The air is at the point of giving up its moisture and often does, so that the plain is wet even when it is not raining, and it is also frequently raining.

But the rain is the least of it.

The pavement is a lid on a cave system of unbelievable extent — miles of it, in storeys, the upper galleries dry and older than the plain's present surface, the lower ones flooded and still being made. The caves have mouths: swallow-holes, throats, some no wider than a shoulder, some the size of a house floor. Through these the plain's water goes down. There are no rivers here that finish. Streams run a mile or two across the rock and then simply end, pouring into a hole with a noise like a city.

And the caves breathe.

When the barometer falls, the air in the caves — vast, saturated, at the constant cool temperature of the deep rock, about nine degrees, the temperature of everything here, always — expands and comes out. It comes out of every throat at once, fog-heavy, and it condenses on contact with the plain, and *this is the cloud lid*. The overcast is the exhalation of the underworld. The dimness is the ground breathing out. The rain is, in large part, the same water going round again.

When pressure rises, the flow reverses. The caves draw in. The fog thins and lifts a little, the light comes up half a stop, and the roar drops away over the course of an afternoon until you can hear a footstep.

They call these the outbreath and the indraw. They are the only seasons. Nothing here has anything to do with the sun.

## The pasture

Where groundwater comes up rather than going down — seeps, springs at the feet of the four hills, the rims of certain throats — it arrives carrying sulfide and dissolved iron out of the deep rock, and where it meets the air a film grows.

This is the grass of this country. It is a slick, a bloom, a skin on wet stone: rust-orange where the iron oxidizes, sulfide-yellow at the vents, and elsewhere a translucent white that glows very faintly at night, not enough to see by, only enough to find water by if you are lost. It is a finger's thickness at its most luxurious. It grows a millimeter in a month.

Because the pasture is thin, everything that lives on it must live spread out. This is why the plain looks empty. It is not empty; it is *diluted*. Walk a day and you will see four tall shapes and perhaps one moving thing and no people at all. Pry the lid off a single fissure and you will find a city.

The grazers are called scrapers: limpet-like, pale, shell-backed, from the size of a thumbnail to the size of a fist, and there are, on this plain, some number of them that has too many zeros to be useful. They clamp to wet stone and rasp the film with a ribbon of mineral teeth. Billions of radulae working at once is the hiss at the top of the sound. It is the noise of the plain being eaten.

In the clay pockets, black threads digest what falls in and dies, and once a flood they fruit: pale knuckled bodies the size of a plum, pushing up through the red residue, shedding spores into the fog.

And the spores are eaten by the combs, which are the tall shapes. They stand about nine feet high on three legs like wet bone, and they hold up a fan of fringed arms into the moving fog and filter it, and they walk, slowly, a hundred yards a day, to where the fog is thicker. They are the only large things you can see at a distance. They make no sound at all.

## The rule of the place

In a dim world with a deafening floor, hearing has become the primary sense, and the water's roar has become a kind of weather for that sense — a fog inside the fog. Everything alive here is negotiating with it.

The scrapers use the top of the spectrum, above the roar. The fliers — membrane-winged, blind, the size of a hand, roosting in the galleries in their millions — hunt by echo in a narrow band that the water does not occupy, and they can only hunt during the indraw, when the floor drops. The listeners, which are the local wolves, stalk the seep margins on stilt legs with a dish of bare skin where a face should be, and they hunt by *interruption*: they listen to the even rasp of ten thousand scrapers and wait for one to falter.

So the scrapers' defense is to clamp. To stop. To become silent, which here is to become invisible.

When a listener puts a foot down wrong, the quiet spreads outward from it across the seep in a widening circle, a hole in the hiss, three yards across, then ten, then thirty. Fear is visible here as an absence. It moves across the rock like the shadow of a cloud, and if you have learned to look at sound you will see it and know exactly where the predator is, and so will everything else, and the listener will go hungry.

There are hunters that exploit this. Things low and mineral-colored that have given up voice entirely and move through the noise as blanks, unfindable, taking the deaf and the loud alike. Nothing here fears the dark. Everything fears the quiet.

## The marks

Scattered across forty miles of pavement: perhaps sixty structures, most of them ruined, some of them not.

They are built of the only material there is — stacked slabs, corbelled, dry-laid, chert-bladed and bone-handled work, cords of gut and twisted sinew, pegs of comb-leg. A few pins of native copper exist, cold-hammered from one seam in the eastern hill, and they are used exclusively for hanging plumb-bobs and weighting the tuning-cords, because the only reason to want metal in a world like this is to make a stone hold a pitch.

The intact ones are stop-houses. Each is built over a small throat, and each contains a fitted plug of dressed stone on a lever with a counterweight of stacked slabs, so that one person can lower a ton of rock into a hole and choke it.

That is the technology of this place. Not fire — there is nothing to burn but rendered fat in shell lamps, and the fires are for warmth and for souring pits of scraper-mass, not for light. Not agriculture — the film cannot be farmed, only waited for. The whole craft, the entire inherited body of knowledge, is the manufacture of quiet. A stop-house is a room in which you can hear a voice. A good plug, well seated, is worth more than anything else a person can own, and the families that hold the good ones have held them for a very long time.

Out on the open pavement, spaced miles apart, are the tuned cairns: hollow stacks with a shaped cavity and a lip, built so that the outbreath plays them. Each has its own note. You navigate the plain by chord. Walk until the low D of the Home stack comes in under the sustained tone of the Steady One, and you are on the line; wait for the third to appear from the north and you know where you are to within a half mile in fog you cannot see your hand in. Children are taught the chord before they are taught to walk on runnels.

The tally slabs stand beside the stop-houses: vertical faces notched with a chert point, one stroke per indraw. The oldest panels are worn almost smooth by rain — you read them with a fingertip, not an eye — and at the bottom of each, in a clean sharp band, are the last few years.

The people themselves are small, cold, wrapped in membrane and hide, and mostly deaf by middle age. They speak by touch. Each carries a rod of comb-leg bone; you set the end against the rock and put your teeth to it, and the pavement carries your jaw's clicking to the other person's jaw across twenty feet of stone better than the air could carry a shout. Conversations here look like people leaning on the ground with their heads together. During the long outbreath they sit in the stop-houses and do this for hours.

During the indraw they come out and travel and trade and marry and shout at each other with their actual voices, wastefully, joyfully, for two days, while the plain is almost quiet — and every other living thing does the same, so that the indraw is a single regional convulsion of breeding, the fliers pouring out of the throats in columns that take an hour to pass, the listeners gorging, the combs standing in the thinning fog with their fans wide open. Then the pressure turns, and the fog comes down, and the roar rebuilds, and everybody goes back to being a stone.

## Below, and before

The caves are in storeys, and the storeys are a record. The highest galleries are dry and their floors are red residue and their walls are scalloped by water that stopped flowing before there were people. Halfway down, in a chamber that is now only reachable at low water, there are chert scars on the wall and a flight of steps worn into a bow, and the steps go down into the flood and keep going.

Nobody knows who cut them. The current families assume it was themselves, earlier, when the water was lower, and they are probably right, and the difference between *earlier* and *someone else* is not very important on a surface that has been lowering by the thickness of a fingernail every century since before there was anything with a spine.

One more thing.

Some of the throats are not throats. There is a class of sessile animal here that grows into a swallow-hole over a few centuries, calcifies, and becomes a valve — a house-sized filter-feeder with a chambered body that the plain's water and air now pass through. It booms every twelve or fifteen seconds. Its note is part of the chord. It has been part of the chord for longer than the cairns have.

Four generations ago a family new to the western plain, unable to tell a hole from an animal, plugged one.

It took nine days to die. The note went sour, then flat, then stopped, and everyone within thirty miles heard the chord break and knew that something enormous had been killed, and did not know what. They still call that stretch the Missing Mile. You can walk it in the indraw and hear the gap where the third should be: an emptiness in the noise, spreading, the way fear spreads, the way a shadow crosses wet stone.

Then the pressure falls, the throats begin to speak, the fog comes down out of the ground and closes over the four last hills, and the plain says the one syllable it has been saying forever, which takes about six days to finish and means nothing at all except *still here, still going down*.
