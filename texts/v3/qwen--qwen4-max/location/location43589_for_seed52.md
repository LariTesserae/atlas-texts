## Green Stone

Nothing here is rounded yet. That is the first thing the hand notices. Every edge is an arris, every broken slab a fresh fracture with the mineral still bright inside it, and when you run a palm along a kerb you feel the tiny burr the make left behind, sharp enough to take a print of your skin. There are no pebbles. There is no sand. The sea, twenty kilometers east, still has beaches, but they belong to the old coast, and the new ground between here and there has not had time to be ground down into anything soft.

The stone is called green, though it is the pale grey of a wet fingernail. Green in the quarryman's sense: it still holds its sap. Cut it and it weeps. Leave it and it hardens from the outside in, over months, over years, and the hardening gives off heat. This is why the plain is warm. Not the sky — the sky gives almost nothing — but the floor, which sits at about the temperature of a sleeping animal, and which will burn a bare foot at noon in the places where the curing front is shallow. Kneel and put your cheek to the ground and you can hear it: a fine crystalline ticking, continuous, everywhere, like a cooling stove, like the inside of a hail cloud. Ten thousand square kilometers of stone quietly deciding to be stone.

## The Shade

Above, a lid. Stratus, unbroken, the color of the inside of an oyster shell, low enough that the tallest towers put their heads into it and come out wearing weather. It is maintained. Direct sun would craze the green stone, would skin it too fast and leave the interior soft for a century, so the shade is held in place by the same apparatus that holds everything else in place, and the light that reaches the ground is diffuse, directionless, and about the strength of a room with one shaded window. Noon is a rumor. Dawn is only the moment the fog thins enough to show you the next streetlamp.

Things are lit more from below than from above. Where the sap comes to the surface it carries a faint luminance with it, a chemical shine you can read a map by, and it pools in the low places so that the terrain looks like a photograph of itself developed slightly wrong. Shadows here do not fall so much as accumulate, and they lag — a half-second, sometimes a full second, behind the thing that casts them. Nobody who works the plain finds this remarkable anymore. The crews say the light is slow because the ground is new and hasn't learned the local speed yet.

## Water Going the Wrong Way

There are no rivers. Drainage takes time to organize itself, and this ground has not had time; the water has not yet decided where to go, so it goes nowhere, and lies in sheets a millimeter deep across the flats, and fills the sockets of buildings that were never started, and stands in every drill-hole like a pupil.

Most of it does not fall. It arrives from underneath. The curing stone gives up its sap as it sets, and the sap rises through the pore structure and beads on the surface an hour before dawn, and the whole plain sweats. You can watch a dry slab go dark from the middle outward. Twice a month there is real rain, warm and brief and vertical, ten minutes of it, and then the lid closes again. The rest is fog-drip, and dew, and the deliberate harvest.

So the hydrology runs backwards. Springs everywhere, seeps in the middle of open ground with no catchment above them, water climbing. Downstream does not exist yet. The oldest people here still say *upcountry* when they mean inland, out of habit, and the young ones have started using it too, and it is not wrong.

## The Verdure

It should be barren. New mineral, no soil, no seed bank, no history. Instead there is a lushness that makes no sense until you understand that it is plumbing.

The plants were put here. Fog-combs first: tall, dark, sparse-crowned things with leaves that are not leaves but mesh, hectare upon hectare of fine filament held in a flat plane, condensing the low cloud and running it down the trunk into mains. Their foliage is nearly black — there is no light to spare for reflecting any of it — and bronze underneath, and when the fog moves through them they make the only continuous sound on the plain, a dry hiss like distant applause that never resolves into a clap. Between the combs, the volunteers: lithophytes that dissolve green stone with root acid and drink the sap directly, growing at a speed that is genuinely frightening, four meters in a season, hollow boles full of stored water, aerial roots that come down over a road and take it. A vine will cross a six-lane boulevard between one trimming cycle and the next. Crews go out with cutters and the smell of the sap in the air is like crushed almond and wet chalk.

Lush, but spaced. Each comb stands in a wide clearing of bare stone because each one has a water claim, and the claims are large. From a tower you see canopy in rafts and drifts, dark islands with kilometers of pale nothing between them, and the nothing is not empty — it is *paved*, kerbed, drained, lit, addressed, planted at the verges, waiting.

## The Plan

Here is the thing that takes visitors a day to accept. The city is complete. The buildings are not.

The street grid runs to the horizon in every direction, unbroken: kerbs, gutters, gratings, lamp standards, crossing plates, address numbers stamped in bronze at the corner of every lot, verge trees in their guards, the tram rail with its slot polished by use. Names on the plates — Candlewright Row, Sixteen Bells, the Long Approach — for structures that do not stand. A million addresses. Perhaps four thousand people, scattered so thin that a district's entire population can be one woman and her workshop and a lit window eleven kilometers from the next lit window.

The density is underground. That is where the city keeps itself. Below the green stone: mains, conduit, fiber, the tram's actual mechanism, the settling shafts, the vaults, and beneath those the *previous grounds*, because the plain rises. A thumbnail a day, more in the west. It has been rising for eighty years. The first streets are now nine meters down, intact, buried in the make, their lamps still burning in solid stone if you cut into them; the second generation is at four meters; the third is at your ankles. Buildings that could not be jacked in time went under to their shoulders and stayed. There is a cornice at ground level on the North Approach that you can sit on. There is a window you step into, and a room beyond it, dry, warm, furnished, with the floor sloping gently up where the stone came in.

So it is a dense city seen in section and a desert seen in plan. Layer upon layer of urban fabric, and on top, this enormous spare bright emptiness with one tower every few kilometers, standing in its own clearing like a nail in a table.

## What Makes the Ground

The shafts are capped with plain grey hatches, unmarked, warm to sit on. Under each one, at a depth nobody agrees on, an engine precipitates stone. Not from feedstock. There is no feedstock. The measurement teams came twice, weighed everything, and left with a ledger that would not balance: the plain gains eleven million tonnes a year and nothing, anywhere, loses it. The second team's report is four pages long and the last page is a single sentence about the future shoreline of this coast, which is now, by their arithmetic, missing.

That is the arrangement, as far as anyone understands it. The ground is being taken from what the ground would have been. Somewhere downstream in time there is a coast that does not get to exist, and it is being spent here, now, a thumbnail a day, on kerbs and verges and the addresses of buildings nobody has built.

The make has errors. It brings up things that were in whatever record it is reading and should not be: a fossil of a bird with four wings, a flight of stairs with no landing at either end, the smell of bread in a place with no bread. It sets footprints into curing slabs, and the footprints are sometimes yours, and sometimes they face the wrong way, and once, near the Sixteen Bells plate, they were a size no one has grown into yet.

## Quiet

There is almost nothing alive that has a voice. No insects to speak of — a pale grazing thing the crews call a saddle, low and slow, that eats the soft skin off curing stone and is the only animal you are likely to see in a week's walking. Lost seabirds come through in the fog and go on. No frogs, because no ponds old enough. No song. The quiet is not the absence of sound but the absence of *background*: with nothing to hide in, a door closing carries eleven kilometers, and you can hear a trimming crew's cutters from the far side of a district and know what street they're on. Under everything, the engines: not a noise, a pressure in the sternum, on a cycle of about nine seconds, which you stop feeling in a month and then feel again, suddenly, at three in the morning, and cannot stop.

At dawn the sap tide comes up and the whole plain goes dark and shining. Fog moves through the combs. Somewhere a slab releases its stress with a crack like a rifle, and the sound goes out across eighty kilometers of new stone, and comes back, slightly late, from a direction it did not go.

And on the eastern horizon, the sea sits a little lower than it did last year. You can check it against the base of the far towers. They are being buried from below, patiently, one thumbnail at a time, and the city is not vacant.

It is curing.
