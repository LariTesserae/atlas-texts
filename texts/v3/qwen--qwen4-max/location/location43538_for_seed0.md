You hear it before you climb onto it. From the boat, from the last of the old coast, the new shelf announces itself as a low irregular thumping under the wind — not surf, though surf is inside it, a bass concussion with no rhythm you can hold, arriving through the hull and the water and the bones of your feet. Over that, a hiss so continuous it stops being a sound and becomes a property of the air, like heat. Then the fog on the horizon, which is not fog on the horizon but a field of separate plumes, hundreds of them, standing up out of the ground in a slow forest of white columns, and you think: chimneys. A town. Something burning.

There is no town. There has never been anything built here.

---

The shelf came up in a night, and then in increments. A sequence of quakes lifted the reef flat several fathoms at a stroke, and each lift left a shoreline behind it like a tidemark on a bath: three terraces now, stepping inland and upward, each one a former sea floor, each one still shaped like the sea shaped it. Fluted coral heads. Brain-coral domes. The blade-ridges of a stag-horn thicket, all of it turned to stone and all of it still sharp. Nothing has been rounded yet. Rain has not had time. The rock breaks conchoidally, in glassy scoops and crescents, and every loose piece on the ground has an edge that will open a hand to the bone. The whole plain is a knapping floor. You cannot kneel here. You cannot put a palm down without paying.

And it is hollow. Fresh limestone drinks rain the instant it lands; there are no streams on the shelf, no gullies, nothing that looks like drainage. Instead the water went down and kept going, and the sea followed it in from below, and now the terraces are pocked with pits — some the width of a doorway, some wide enough to swallow a hillside — dropping into a flooded cave system that still communicates with the ocean. The plain is a drumskin over water. Every swell that enters the caves compresses the air beneath the rock, and the pits boom, and hoot, and slap, out of phase with each other, hundreds of separate mouths speaking one enormous sentence with no grammar. That is the thumping. It never stops. It gets louder on the spring tides and it changes voice when the barometer falls.

The pits also breathe. Down in the cave system the air is cold — genuinely cold, the cold of deep water and dark stone — and each morning that air spills up and out over the rims, and the warm wet air of the shelf breaks against it and turns to fog. So the plumes. So the damp. It almost never rains here in any way you would call weather; there are squalls a few times a month, brief and vertical, and otherwise the land wrings itself out every dawn. Fog condenses on ten million knife-edges of stone and runs down them in threads. The rock sweats. The leaves sweat. By the second hour of morning every surface in the world is beaded and dripping and the sound of dripping is a whole stratum of the noise, a fine granular tick beneath the boom and beneath the hiss, and then by afternoon the surfaces are dry to the touch and the air is still wet, and the water has all gone back down into the holes.

Warm, always. A wet warmth with no wind in it, under the leaves, that sits on the skin like a hand. The only cold anywhere is at the pit mouths, where the cave-breath pours out, and everything that lives here knows it.

---

The forest should not be here. There is no soil — there has not been time to make soil, only a few centimetres of grit and leaf-fragments in the fracture-lines. What happened is that the forest arrived by sea. Mats of it, whole floating hectares calved off some older river coast a long way south, drifting in on a wet-season current, grounding on the new shelf while it was still barely awash, and then being lifted bodily into the air by the quakes with their trees still alive. So the trees here are older than the ground they stand on. They are immigrants sitting on raw rock, and they hold on by grip: roots like fingers, roots like cable, roots running along the surface for thirty metres to find a fissure and pour themselves into it, roots etching the limestone with acid as they go, dissolving their own handholds deeper. Buttresses that clasp a coral dome like a fist around a skull. Lianas that use the trees as scaffolding and the stone as anchor and each other as neither.

Above that, the epiphytes — orchids, ferns, mosses a hand deep, tanks of bromeliad water, whole suspended gardens with their own fauna that never come down. Above *that*, the canopy, which has closed so completely in sixty-odd years that the ground layer lives in a permanent green-brown dusk. And above the canopy, the roof.

The roof is animals. A commuting mass of small fruit bats, millions of them, that roost in the cave system by day and pour out at dusk and return before dawn, plus the swifts and the pigeon-flocks and the great slow rafts of frigatebirds riding the thermals off the scarp; the sky over the shelf is never empty and is often solid. Between canopy and roof, and between roof and sun, the light budget of this place is spent several times over. Noon on the ground is a deep twilight the colour of weak tea. You navigate by silhouette and by smell — wet lime, iodine, crushed pepper-leaf, ammonia from the molt-piles, and under everything the sweetish rot of a forest that is falling down as fast as it grows.

---

But there is light, and it comes from underneath.

The water in the caves is still sea water and still full of what came up with it, including the small organisms that flash when they are disturbed. Every surge disturbs them. So each boom from a pit arrives with a pulse of blue thrown up the pit walls, up through the fog-plume, up onto the undersides of the leaves. Hundreds of pits, out of phase. The ground flickers. Shadows here fall *upward* — a crab on the rim throws its shadow onto the canopy, a hanging root prints itself on the fog above it — and the whole plain is lit in a slow irregular strobe that is also its heartbeat, because the light and the sound are the same event arriving through two channels.

Everything has learned to read it. The moths orient on pit-glow instead of moon. The crabs time their molts to the spring-tide flicker. There are fish in the cave water that have never seen the sun and hunt by the flash their own bodies make.

---

The crabs are the reason this place feels inhabited. Land crabs, hand-sized, red-black, in numbers that make the ground itself seem to be moving — a continuous lateral traffic over every terrace, a rustle like a fire in dry grass, a sound of ten thousand small edges tapping stone. They shelter in the fissures; they do not dig, they do not build, they do not weave. Nothing here builds. The land arrived pre-fabricated: sharp, hollow, cold in the right places, full of ready-made rooms. There has been no pressure to learn manufacture, only selection.

And one claw of the crab is a groove that fits a stone flake.

They pick them up off the ground — the ground is nothing but flakes — and they use them: to lever open a snail, to slit a fallen fruit, to shave the tough husk off a seed, to cut a rival's leg at the joint. Then they drop them. A blade is used and abandoned in the same second, because there is another blade underfoot, because there will always be another blade underfoot. There are no tools here in the sense of possessions. There is no cache, no haft, no carried kit, nothing that could be called an artifact. You cannot tell the workshop from the scree.

Except in one way. There are certain upstanding blades, at good heights, near good fruit, that have had snails broken on them by birds and crabs for sixty years, ten thousand times, a hundred thousand — and those stones shine. A polish, a dishing, a smoothness no other stone on the shelf has. Use-wear without manufacture. The only made things in this country are the places where the same act has happened over and over, and no one made them, and no one owns them, and they will outlast every animal that touches them.

The pit rims are where all of it concentrates: cold air, water, up-light, fruit-fall, blades. Every rim is a bazaar. Crabs stacked three deep on the ledges, stealing from each other, mating, molting, being eaten; snakes lying in the cold outflow where the fog hides them; birds working the anvils; bats dropping half-chewed fruit into the crowd; the grinders — cicada-things in the leaf layer, in numbers past counting — laying down a wall of stridulation so loud it presses on the sinuses and makes the eyes water. Speech would be pointless. Anything that wants to communicate here does it by touch, or by tremor through the rock, or by getting into a position where the pit-light will silhouette it correctly. The roar has weather: gusts, lulls, a daily tide with a slack hour after dawn when the grinders stop all at once and the sudden hole in the sound is more frightening than the sound. In that hush you hear the plain properly — the drip, the tap of claws, the sea working in the basement, and occasionally a crack somewhere inland like a mast going.

---

That crack is the forest falling. The mats are held by grip, not by ground, and grip fails. A hectare of trees will stand for years and then let go all at once, sliding down into a pit or slumping off a terrace edge, and the noise of it carries for miles, and where it fell there is suddenly a raw hole full of light and steam and shredded green, and within a week the hole is a thicket and within a year it is canopy again. The quakes still come. Each shock opens new fissures, closes old pits, drops another acre, lifts the lowest terrace another hand's breadth. Once every few years a big tide reaches in and takes a terrace back for a night. Nothing here is stable long enough to be mapped. The pits migrate. The rims — the good ground, the towns with no buildings — move, and the crowds move with them, and the whole shelf seethes at the pace of an anthill under a boot: destruction and construction indistinguishable at any distance, ten thousand small catastrophes an hour, all of it drowned in noise.

Three days a year the crabs molt in synchrony and the plain goes soft and red and everything eats everything, and the roar drops by a third because the grinders get eaten too, and then it comes back louder.

---

Here is the rule the place runs on, and the deadline in it.

Nothing is made because everything is provided: the edges, the rooms, the cold, the light, the water. It is a finished workshop, and its inhabitants have only ever had to *choose*. But the workshop is dulling. On the highest and oldest terrace, where the stone has had sixty years of rain and root-acid, the flakes are going blunt; the conchoidal snap is softening into crumble. Walk up there and you find fewer usable edges, and the crabs wander further, and the birds carry snails further, and something new has started: a few of the animals up on that terrace have begun *striking* stones against stones. Not often. Badly. But a flake comes off, and it is sharp, and it did not come off by itself.

Down on the young terraces nobody does this. Down there the ground still cuts you and the pits still flash and boom and breathe, the fog stands up in its hundreds of white columns, the roots hold their grip on the naked reef, and the roof of animals turns overhead in the dark, and the whole enormous deafening machine runs as it has run since the night the sea let go of it — effortlessly, wastefully, and on borrowed edges.
