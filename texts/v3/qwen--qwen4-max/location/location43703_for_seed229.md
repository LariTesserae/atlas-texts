## The Bowl

You hear it an hour before you see it, and you feel it before you hear it: a pressure in the back of the teeth, a trembling in the marrow of the shinbones, coming up through the boots from the rock. The path climbs. The air thins and goes bronze. Your breath comes twice for every thought. And then the ground stops being ground and becomes a lip, and you look down into a shallow dish of a place about nine hundred paces across, rimmed in fluted cliffs the color of old candle stubs, and the noise comes off it like heat off a griddle.

It is not a large place. You could walk its rim in an hour with time to stop. There are thirty-one people in it. From the notch where you stand you can see every one of their structures, all six of them, and every one of the Nine, and the whole floor between, which is bare.

It is the loudest small place in the world.

## The Sift

The floor is powder. Not sand — sand is gravel by comparison. This is the massif ground down over ten thousand years to a flour so fine that a pinch of it between finger and thumb feels like nothing at all, like a smear of cold grease, like the idea of a substance. Salt and glass and a pale friable mineral that fractures easily. It lies in drifts and crusts and shallow dunes, and it is warm, blood-warm, because beneath it the rock is vented and breathing.

Where the vents push hard, the powder stops behaving like a solid. Gas threads up through it, lifts every grain off its neighbors, and the whole bed lets go and becomes a liquid — a dry, pouring, splashing liquid with a surface and waves and a shoreline, in which nothing is wet. This is the quick. It boils without heat. It has breakers. It has a hiss like a pan of fat, and under the hiss a bass roar you don't hear so much as agree to.

And where it churns, it glows. The grains fracture as they grind, and each fracture spits a mote of cold light, and a hundred million fractures a second make a pale green-white radiance that comes up out of the ground and lights the underside of everything.

That is the whole of the light here. The sky is a bronze lid; the sun is a coin behind it; the rim shadows the floor for most of the day, and there are no fires, for reasons I'll come to. The bowl is lit from below, by its own noise. Brightness and loudness are the same measurement in two senses. When a surge comes the floor flares and roars together; when it settles, the world dims to the color of a bruise and the roar drops to a mutter. The language here has one word for *loud-and-bright* and another for *quiet-and-dark*, and no useful way to say one without the other. A blind gleaner reads the bowl perfectly. A deaf one reads it perfectly too.

## The Nine

They stand far apart, because each needs a great deal of floor. Nine is what the bowl can carry. From the rim they look like trees that have been struck by something — enormous, extravagant, dark, and bent.

They are not trees. Nobody here has settled what they are, and the tally has separate marks for the years they were counted as plants and the years they were counted as animals. Each has a trunk like poured wax, olive-black, hollow, ribbed with tubes you can see pulsing. Each ends not in a crown but in a hanging: thousands upon thousands of fronds, layered, ruffled, frilled, drooping in tiers, dense as a chandelier, bottle-green above and pale as a fish belly underneath. Every frond angles *downward*. The light comes from the floor, so the leaves face the floor. The Nine spend their lives bowing to the thing that feeds them.

They are lush in a way that has nothing to do with abundance of numbers. There is nothing else growing in the bowl — no understory, no scrub, no moss on the stones. All the greenness in the world here is concentrated into nine explosions a hundred paces apart, with bare warm powder between. And each of those nine is itself a country: hundreds of small hangers-on live in the crevices of its trunk, in the dust caught in its frond-axils, in the felted mats where old cuticle has shed and matted. Fern-things. Bladder-things. A whole vertical ecology riding on a host, watered by nothing, drinking a warm sweet oil that circulates inside the wax-lined tubes and never once touches the outside world.

Cut a frond and it does not bleed water. It bleeds a thin ester, pear-sweet, varnish-sweet, that smells like a candle workshop and a bowl of overripe fruit, and it sets hard in air within a minute. Everything here is sealed. Everything here is full and none of it is wet.

Here is the thing they do, which took the gleaners sixty generations to work out and which they still describe with a shrug: the Nine *make the floor roar*. Their root-cones inject gas into the powder around them. They fluidize their own catchment. They churn the ground to make it glow, and they turn their faces down to the glow and eat it, and the grinding also stirs up the reduced mineral they need, and the noise also brings prey. The light is agriculture. The sound is a crop being grown.

When one of the Nine goes quiet, it is starving. Everyone in the bowl knows the sound of a failing note the way a shepherd knows a cough.

## Swim or Set

The quick is full of animals and they are all in a hurry.

The swimmers are flat, fringed, palm-to-platter sized, eyeless, with a line of hearing pits down each flank. They swim the fluidized powder exactly as a ray swims a shallow sea, rippling their margins, breaching in a spray of dust and going down again. They are pale on their backs and dark underneath, which is the correct way round in a world lit from below. There are, at any moment, something like a million of them in the bowl.

They cannot stop. When the gas fails and the bed settles, the powder locks grain against grain and sets like plaster, and everything in it is entombed mid-stroke. This happens several times a day, somewhere, to thousands of animals. Then the next surge quarries the set slab apart and the dead come up in fragments: a hand's-breadth of stone with eleven swimmers in it, all caught in the same instant of turning, all of them four hours old.

The gleaners cut these slabs and build with them. The walls of every hut in the bowl are a wallpaper of arrested motion. Children learn to read the fossil slabs before they learn the tally, and it does not occur to them that a fossil should be old.

On the firmer margins live the masons: six-limbed, thumb-sized, glue-spitting, in numbers that make the crust look like it is boiling. They build towers. Head-high, fluted, hollow, with a spiral inside — real architecture, and they raise one in a shift, working in a frenzy that from a distance looks like a bazaar being looted. Every surge takes the towers down. The masons rebuild them in the same places, to the same plans, immediately. The designs are inherited and ancient. Some of them are the same shape as marks on the tally wall, which nobody likes to discuss. The towers are never more than a day old. The *towers* — the idea of them — are older than the people.

And in the fronds, in the billions, are the chimes: little ceramic-shelled stridulators that rub their legs and make the high frying hiss that sits on top of everything, the way cicadas sit on top of a hot afternoon. They tune to their host's note. They pollinate, if that's the word. They also drink. They can find water at a few parts in a million, and they converge on it, and what they do when they arrive is the loudest sound a human being will ever hear.

There is one more animal worth naming, and it is the reason nobody here fears noise. Something hunts in the bowl that is built like a coat of felt: a sound-absorbing, wide, gliding thing that eats chimes and swimmers and anything else that navigates by ear. You cannot hear it. That is how you find it. A hole opens in the roar and moves, a pocket of deafness the size of a doorway, sliding across the floor, and every child in the bowl is trained from walking age to notice the *absence* and climb.

Its pelt is the most valuable material in the world here. Sewn into a mask it makes you quiet, and quiet things are invisible to swimmers, and hunters wear it. Hunters are also always tied to the Thread, because a person who has gone perfectly quiet cannot be found by anyone who loves them.

## Gleaners

Thirty-one people, six structures, a stair.

The stair is cut into the rim and worn into a groove down the middle by feet. That is the only way in. At the bottom stands the drum-post, a pillar of bedrock with a hollow ground into its head, struck with a bone maul; the message goes through the stone and arrives in everyone's soles at once. Strung across the narrows is the Thread, a hair-cord as thick as a wrist, humming, which you hold with one hand at all times and through which you can read the state of the whole floor. There are cairns on the safe islands. The islands move. The cairns sink and are rebuilt with different stones in the same places by whoever passes; there is not one original pebble in any of them, and they are the oldest things the people own.

There is a listening seat: a stone chair with an amber rod set into the bedrock beside it. You sit, you lean, you put your teeth on the rod, and the deep vents speak directly into your skull. The oldest woman in the bowl has front teeth worn flat as a river stone. She can tell you which of the Nine will fail before its note has changed.

There are the huts, half-buried, felt and resin over fossil slab, rebuilt every season on footprints that were old when the tally began. There is the tally wall itself, a smoothed panel of cliff, thousands of strokes in nines, row upon row descending the rock — because the floor drops. The bowl is a mill grinding itself. The powder blows out over the low notch and the walls are consumed to make more, and the level falls a hand's width in a generation, and the shape stays exactly the same while everything in it is replaced.

Nothing here is old except the shapes.

The people wear waxed suits and masks: felt over a clay canister of burnt lime, changed every hour, because a human being is a bag of water and water is the one substance this world cannot survive. A drop of it hydrolyzes the ester. A drop kills a frond. A wound that leaks is a catastrophe; a spilled tree glues and kills a whole quarter of the bowl. So: no spitting. No bleeding, if it can be helped. No weeping — grief is a technical hazard, and the mourners at a burial swallow and swallow while the sealers wax the body, because an unsealed corpse in the quick would take a hundred thousand animals and possibly a tree with it. Sealing is the highest craft here. Sealers have the best hands in the world.

And no fire. Not because fire is holy. Because fire *makes water* — burns the wax and the oil and the breath and wrings water out of them, and a lamp flame in a hut is a slow poisoning of everything the hut stands on. So they cook in vent-holes, wrapped in wax-leaf, and they carry lamps that are simply small jars of the powder with a bellows beneath: a shaker-lamp, cold green, hissing like a snake, which goes dark the instant you stop working it. A child walks beside each gleaner and pumps. From the rim at night you can see them out there, nine great slow glows and, between them, the tiny bobbing sparks, each with its thread of hiss. If a spark stops hissing, it has stopped moving, and someone goes.

They take nothing living. They glean: shed cuticle, fallen frond, the amber of old spills, fossil slab, hush-pelt from the dead. Everything they own — blades, hafts, hooks, bowls, the rod at the listening seat — is made from a tree's set blood, gathered after the fact. They are the most patient people imaginable, living inside a place with no patience in it at all. Their whole virtue is waiting in the one room of the world where nothing waits.

Once a year a caravan leaves goods at a cairn on the rim: lime, felt, hair-cord, and the Bell, which is copper, and which came from somewhere else, and which is the only metal in the bowl. It hangs on a post of amber and bone and nobody strikes it. It rings itself when the bowl's fundamental drifts. When it rings, everyone climbs.

## Spill

Perhaps twice a year a trunk ruptures. There is a crack like a cliff falling, and then a sound like a sail filling, and a jet of sweet oil goes up thirty feet and comes down as rain that hardens on the way. The air fills with pear-and-varnish vapor and everyone within a hundred paces gets giddy, and giddy people forget to shake their lamps, which is how most of the deaths happen. Where the spray lands, the powder sets into a sheet of amber with the masons' towers in it and the swimmers in it and sometimes a stilter with its neck still bent down to browse. The chimes come to the leak in a column you can hear from the rim. The Nine nearby go quiet and dim as their catchment is glued, and stay quiet for a season, and the sealers work in relays with hot wax and felt until the wound is closed.

Then the gleaners come down and harvest the disaster, and it is the best month of the year, and everyone is ashamed of how good it feels.

## The Number

Sometimes a tenth seedling comes up: a pale fist in the powder between two of the Nine, in a place where the bowl cannot afford it. The people kill it. It is the only cut they permit themselves. They use an amber blade and seal the stump immediately, and the seedling makes a small thin note that goes on for most of a day, and nobody is allowed to plug their ears. Then a mark on the wall.

The wall also counts the nines, every year, in nines, going down the cliff as the floor goes down. And near the low notch there is a separate row, the only quiet arithmetic in the place: how many hand-widths of rim are left before the powder drains out over the lip into the wet world below, and the quick stops, and the light goes out, and the Nine starve standing up.

It is about two hundred. Everyone knows it. Every year someone climbs to the notch with a knotted cord and measures, and comes back, and writes a number one smaller than last year's, and the bowl roars on exactly as loud as before, and the masons rebuild the same towers, and the cairns are rebuilt out of new stones in the old places, and the lamps go bobbing across the dark floor, hissing, kept alive by a child's arm.
