# The Sill

The light comes up off the ground as much as down out of the sky, and this is the first thing that wrongs the eye. The pavement is white — a chalk-white with a blue cast where it's wet, a bone-white where it isn't — and it throws the sun back at everything from underneath. Leaves are lit on their undersides. The inside of your own nostrils is bright. Shadows exist but they are thin, provisional, filled in from below by the glare of the rock, so that a boulder seems to hover a centimeter above its own outline. At two thousand three hundred meters the air has too little in it to soften anything. The sky is a hard violet-cobalt at the zenith and milky at the edges, and the sun is not a warmth but a pressure on the top of the skull.

The rock is new. Not new like lava — new like a thing recently lifted out of a drawer. It is a seabed that came up whole, and it has not yet been told what to become. Ripple marks stand crisp on the flat pavements, the little asymmetrical ridges of a current that stopped before there were mountains here. In the broken faces you can see the coral skeletons still articulated, branch by branch, uncrushed. Nothing is rounded. There is not a smooth stone anywhere on the Sill; the scree is angular, knife-edged, and it shifts under a boot with a grinding you feel in the shin rather than hear. Rain has not had time to invent rivers. The joints in the limestone are still sharp arrises, grikes you could cut a hand on, and the whole surface drains straight down through itself like a sieve, so that a land this damp has no streams at all.

Water here is a vertical rumor. It arrives as fog — a slow tide of it, four days in and two days out, breathing up from the lowlands and over the escarpment — and it condenses on everything with a fine beading, like the skin on a cold glass. It gathers, it hangs, it drops. It never runs. Where it pools it pools in the cups: dolines forty and eighty meters deep, raw-throated, their walls fluted and unweathered, each with a black eye of still water at the bottom that has no inlet and no outlet and tastes of iodine and salt. The salt is the sea's, left behind in the pores of the rock when the rock left the sea. Fog dissolves it. That is the whole hydrology: the sky licking the stone, and the stone giving up its old ocean a molecule at a time.

And on that, the green. It is absurd, the green. It has no ground. There is almost no soil on the Sill — a thimbleful of grit in each fissure — and yet the vegetation is a layered, saturated, near-touching mass, because it has learned to grow on *surfaces* instead of on earth. Moss in pelts a hand-span thick, wet as fur, plastered over the pavement and hanging off the arrises in beards. Liverworts shingling the north faces. Tree ferns standing in the grikes with their roots braided down into the dark, croziers the size of a curled arm, the whole plant supported by nothing but a crack. Cushion plants packed so tight they ring like wood under a knuckle. And over all of it, at the second and third layer, the things that grow on the *works* — but that comes later.

You walk a long way on the white pavement, through fog that is only a whitening of the already excessive light, and then the ground opens.

---

The cups are full of industry.

Not buildings. There is one hut at the lip of each cup, low, dry-stone, its roof a slab of the same pavement it stands on, so that from above you see only a chimney-less rectangle and a door. The industry is not in the huts. The industry is the cup itself, terraced and wired and plumbed, and it extends over the whole Sill, cup after cup after cup, eighty kilometers of them, a production line the size of a valley system with almost no architecture anywhere in it.

Across each cup's mouth: mesh. Harp-strings of drawn wire on frames, hectare after hectare of it, angled to the fog-tide, beaded permanently with condensate. The droplets run down the wires to gutters, the gutters to wooden launders, the launders down the terraced walls into the vats. Along the walls: copper. Busbars as thick as a wrist, green-black, stapled to the living rock with iron pins, moss growing thickest along them because they are faintly warm and always wet. On the terraces above, the plates — tilted black-glass panels in their thousands, silent, motionless, converting that unbearable light into current that goes down the copper into the brine.

And in the vats, the crop.

It grows. That is the correct verb; nobody here says *manufactures*. Out of the salt-heavy fog-water, under a low current, a white mineral accretes on the seed-frames: a foam, a lattice, a stone made almost entirely of holes. It comes up out of the brine like a head of cauliflower, like coral, like the inside of a bone. Cut and dried it weighs almost nothing and it is white past whiteness, and its pores are the right size to take a sound wave in and never give it back.

They call it hush. It is why all of this is here.

---

The silence is not a mood. It is a product, and it leaks.

At the rim of a cup the world is merely very quiet — fog-quiet, moss-quiet, the ordinary hush of a high place with no birds. Descend and it grades. The yardmen number it one through five and they can feel the change in their ears like altitude. At grade three your footsteps stop arriving. At grade four you can strike an iron bar against the rock and get a *tap* about the size of a fingernail, and then nothing, and the nothing has a texture, a slight pressure, as though the air had become a hand laid flat on the side of the head. At grade five, on the floor of the deepest cups among the mature blocks, sound does not propagate at all in any useful way. You hear your own blood, then you stop hearing that, and what's left is the sensation of your jaw moving when you swallow.

So: handspeech. A fast, beautiful finger-language, all wrist and knuckle, developed over four generations, with dialects by cup. Chalk slates hung at every terrace junction on brass chains. Lamps shuttered in code from lip to floor. Brass tags stamped with numbers, because a number can be read at a distance and a shout cannot. The whole industrial apparatus of a large works — shift rotations, tolerances, gauge readings, inventory, the discipline of a hundred people over eighty kilometers — running entirely on light and gesture, and running well.

There are two reasons for the quiet, and the second is the real one.

The first is that the place makes it.

The second is that the crop *records*. Every vibration that reaches a growing block is written into it as a flaw plane, a cloudy seam in the foam, a permanent acoustic photograph. Cut a mature block across and you can read its history: the fine annual banding, the thick pale scar of a hailstorm, the diffuse gray smear of a season when the fog-tide came late and the yardmen talked too much on the terraces. There is a block in the gauge-house at Cup Eleven with a hard white line through it labeled, in chalk, *the boiler*. Buyers in the lowlands pay by the clarity of the seam. A dropped wrench costs a decade.

Which is why, on the terrace above the north wall, there is a flywheel half-swallowed by moss, and a boiler shell, and the footings of a crusher, and a short length of narrow-gauge rail ending in a fern. The Loud Age. Somebody tried to industrialize this the ordinary way, once, with fire and reciprocating mass, and produced forty years of unsellable cloudy stone before the cups were re-laid for accretion and the engines were left where they stood. The moss has them now. The ferns come up through the firebox. There is a hand-wheel you can still turn, and it turns, and it makes no sound you can find.

---

Almost nothing lives here that moves.

Springtails in the moss, in numbers, too small to matter. Snails with shells the same white as the pavement, so that the shells are only visible when the animal is out and the shadow of its body shows through. A pale salamander in the cisterns, which is counted, individually, by the yardmen, and has names. No insects to speak of — no bees, no flies, nothing to make the ordinary summer drone — and so the few flowering plants on the Sill, the small bell-flowers that colonize the launders, are pollinated by hand, with a squirrel-hair brush kept in the breast pocket of every tender, a gesture so habitual that people brush the flowers while walking and thinking about something else.

Birds work the escarpment and will not come inward. You can watch them turn at an invisible line, a kilometer or two out from the outermost cups, where the grade drops to one and the air will carry a call again. There is a species of lark that nests only in that band, at the exact edge of audibility, and its song is the loudest thing on the Sill and is heard by nobody who works there.

---

Everything is slow. The fog runs its six-day cycle. The plates track nothing; the sun simply crosses them. The blocks accrete a centimeter a year, and a salable block is forty centimeters, so a block is a career: you set the seed-frames as an apprentice and you cut the block the year you retire, and your name goes on the brass tag with the year, and the man who signs the other half of the tag is not born yet. Meanwhile the Sill itself is still rising, three or four millimeters a year, still coming up out of the sea it left, its joints opening a hair each decade, its cups deepening, its rivers not yet invented.

And there is one fast thing, one enemy: rain. Fog is silent. Rain is not. Rain on an open vat is a hundred thousand tiny impacts and every one of them goes into the stone. So they watch the sky, and when the tide breaks wrong and real weather comes over the escarpment, the lamps go from lip to floor in a stuttering code and the whole cup shutters — wooden covers, moss-lined, swung out over the vats on counterweights, the one operation on the Sill performed quickly. A cup shuttered in time is a clean year. A cup caught open is a gray band in eighty blocks, a lost decade, readable forever by strangers.

At night the plates give up their charge and the copper glows a little, a cold blue corona along the busbars, so the cups are outlined in faint constellations from the rim, and the moss above the warm wires glows faintly green with something fungal that nobody has bothered to name. Then dawn arrives from below, off the white stone, and the whole world becomes too bright to look at, and eighty kilometers of people put on smoked glass and go down into the quiet to see what grew.
