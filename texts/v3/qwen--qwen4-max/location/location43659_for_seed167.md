# The Warm Dark

There is no air in this place, and there never has been. Water stands a kilometer deep over it, and the water is blood-warm — twenty-two degrees, held there by sills of old rock on three sides and by the slow leak of heat through the basin floor. Cold seas exist elsewhere. This one was cut off from them long ago and has been sitting in its own warmth ever since, like a bath left standing in a closed room.

The dark is total in the sense that nothing here has ever needed an eye. It is not total in the sense of being empty. It has grain. It has drift. It is full of falling things.

The basin is about four miles across, a valley's worth of floor, and it is not empty floor. From anywhere along its rim — if there were anything to see from — it would read as a plain of towers: a hundred thousand of them, pale, fluted, waist-narrowed, crowded into districts, joined by accreted bridges, terraced down toward a low corner where something lies flat and heavy and has a shore.

It is a city. Nothing in it has ever made a tool.

## Masonry

The towers are limestone, and every gram of it was once inside an animal.

The makers are worms — serpulid things, each living in a tube it never leaves, each sealing its tube to its neighbor's with a bead of secreted lime. A worm settles in a seam, builds a finger's length of pipe, spawns, dies, and its pipe becomes scaffolding. The next generation settles on the shoulders of the last. Ten thousand generations make a spire. The mouths of the tubes open along every surface like small windows, and out of each window comes a crown of feathers, and the feathers comb the water for falling food, and the whole tower is a wall of small hands, all of them working, all of them the same size.

Between the worms are the pavers: snails the size of a thumbnail that graze the bacterial film off the stone and void a paste of calcium into the joints. They do not know they are masons. They are following a chemical gradient toward damp seams, the way a root follows water. The trowel is a tongue. The mortar is a gland. The blueprint is a gradient.

Other things finish the work. Lace-bryozoans grow balustrades along the terrace lips. Bivalves shingle the overhangs. Sponges fill the interiors with a soft grey felt, and this felt is not decoration; it is the most important material in the city, and we will come to it.

There are no straight lines anywhere and no accidents either. Every tower converges on the same silhouette, the way every river converges on a meander: undercut at the base, bulging at the middle, split into fingered crowns at the top where growth is fastest and the water moves. It is a style without a stylist. It has been the same style for longer than there have been forests on the surface above.

## The Rule of Quiet

Sound in water travels four and a half times faster than in air, and much, much farther. In this basin it also refracts downward and gets trapped along the floor, so a noise made at one end of the city arrives at the other end intact, with its direction still legible.

There are things here that hunt by that legibility. Long, velvet-skinned, eyeless, with heads that are mostly ear — a lateral line down each flank drawn out into fringed filaments, so sensitive they read a shrimp's walking legs at forty meters. They do not chase. They hang motionless in the shafts between towers, sometimes for days, and they wait for a mistake.

So the city is quiet. Not incidentally quiet — quiet the way a fortress is thick.

The sponge felt lining every gallery is an anechoic layer; the corridors swallow sound rather than return it. A stone city that does not echo. The tube-throats are baffled, doubled back on themselves, so that a crown retracting makes a soft *thup* instead of a click. The shrimp here walk; the tail-flip that makes their cousins elsewhere so audible has been bred out of them, and they cross the terraces on tiptoe, slowly, in enormous numbers. Crabs set their claws down like glassware. The abundant grazing snails rasp radulae over stone all day and all night, and the sound of ten million rasps is a hiss so fine and so evenly distributed that it reads as silence with a texture in it, the way velvet reads as flat.

There is one loud noise the city permits itself. A shrimp, small and stupid-looking, stationed at the district margins, carries a single oversized claw and fires it exactly once — when a listener crosses into the streets. One snap. It is a suicide note and an alarm both. The snap propagates outward at fifteen hundred meters a second, and behind it comes a wave of closure: crowns folding, tube-mouths shutting, a hundred thousand small hands withdrawing in a ripple that crosses a district in under a second. Nothing sees it. The water simply changes texture, goes from combed to smooth.

Then the listener, which has lost its target and gained a mouthful of one shrimp, drifts on. And in a minute the hands come out again.

Light works the same way, inverted. Almost nothing here shines. What shines is fishing: a small hanging fish with a photophore under its chin, a worm-lure, a bacterial smear on a drifting flake. In a city of the blind, a light is a hook. The mason worms have a single light-sensitive spot on the dorsal side of each crown, and its entire function is to make the crown fold before the mouth arrives.

## The Salt Shore

In the low corner of the basin, where the floor dips, brine seeps up out of the rock — four times the salt of the sea above it, warm, sulfide-heavy, and so dense that it does not mix. It pools. It has a surface.

There is a lake at the bottom of the sea. It has slow waves, driven by the seep's pulse and by the drag of the current above; it has a shoreline of packed mussel shell; it has a beach, and a hem, and the hem is made of the dead.

Everything that falls in stays. Nothing rots in brine. Fish that strayed too low, eels that misjudged, whole rafts of shrimp caught by a rising tide of it — they lie pickled along the margin, arranged by the slow surf into windrows, perfect, unhurried, some of them older than the district above. The lake edge is the only place in the city where a body keeps its shape.

Along that shore grow the mussels, shoulder to shoulder, so packed that a finger cannot be put between them, a bed a kilometer long and forty meters wide. They are fat and they are not filter-feeding. Their gills are packed with sulfur bacteria that eat the brine's chemistry, and the mussels farm them internally, and the worms of the seep fields do the same with their own gut-gardens.

This is what vegetation means here. There are a few hectares of pale lawn — white and orange bacterial veils at the seeps, thin as spilled milk, cropped to stubble by grazers and grown back in weeks. That is all the visible plant life in four miles. The real meadows are inside the animals. The fields and the herd are the same body.

Occasionally an eel goes into the brine on purpose — a short, ugly, tolerable dive, sulfide burning its gills — to drag something out of the windrow. It comes out convulsing and alive and eats what it took. This is the only theft in the city.

## Snow

Nothing grows here from sunlight. Everything here eats sunlight anyway, secondhand, weeks late.

A kilometer up, in the lit world, spring happens. Diatoms bloom, die, aggregate, and fall. It takes six weeks for the fall to arrive. When it does, the basin has weather: a blizzard of marine snow, flakes the size of a fingernail, drifting down through a warm still column at a hundred meters a day, thickening until the water is soup, settling on every terrace, filling every gutter, whitening the plain outside the towers.

Some of the flakes carry luminous bacteria, and this is the only light the city gets: a faint, uneven, falling sparkle for a few weeks a year, blue-green, far too dim to see anything by, enough to be fled from. The closest thing to stars, and they fall, and they are edible.

The whole basin spawns into the snowfall. Larvae until the water is a broth; the tube-mouths working double shifts; the pavers laying paste twice as fast; the listeners fattening. Then the snow thins, and the city goes back to combing the water for what the current brings.

Once in a generation there is a great fall: a whale, a hundred tons, hitting the plain outside the districts. It reshapes a neighborhood for forty years. Hagfish knot in it, amphipods boil off it in drifts, and afterward the bone-eating worms come — red-plumed, root-bodied, dissolving the skeleton from inside — and some of their larvae miss the bones and settle on the city instead, and begin, patiently, to quarry it.

## The Breath

Twice a year, sometimes once, sometimes not at all, the cold sea outside rises over the sill and spills in.

It comes down the basin wall as a cataract inside the water — a slow tumbling fall of denser, colder, oxygen-bearing water, felt across the whole city as a chill front and a directional pull. It is the only wind here. Every crown turns into it. The snow that has been sitting on the terraces lifts and re-settles. The brine lake tilts and slops and drowns a hundred meters of mussels, which will regrow from the survivors in a season.

The basin needs this. Without it, the respiration of ten trillion animals strips the oxygen and the sulfide creeps up out of the floor, and the lower districts go grey and empty. When the breath comes late, the city records it: a dark band in the stone, a stratum of empty tubes, a year with no growth. Cut a tower and you can read the droughts, the great falls, the decades when the surface world bloomed hard, the slump scars. The city keeps its history the way a cliff does, without meaning to, in the only medium it has.

## The Quarry

Below a certain line — it wanders a few meters with the season and the brine — the water turns hungry for lime. Warm, pressurized, loaded with the carbon dioxide of all that breathing: it dissolves carbonate faster than the animals can lay it down.

So the base of every tower is being eaten. Continuously, gently, at about the rate the crown is growing.

This is not ruin. It is the settled condition. The city has been the same height for a hundred thousand years while moving upward through itself the whole time — calcium carried up in bodies, larvae settling high, the old scaffolding thinning at the ankle until a district leans, and folds, and goes down over a decade in a muffled slump that the sponge felt swallows almost entirely. The dust plume feeds the pavers. The fallen skirt buttresses its neighbors. The worms settle on the rubble before it has finished moving.

Nothing here is being destroyed and nothing is being built. Accretion and dissolution have been shaking hands across the lime line since before there was anything on the surface worth naming. What stands in the basin is not a structure but a standing wave in calcium, holding its shape while the material runs through it.

---

At the far edge, past the last district, the plain runs out flat and soft: snow-covered mud, plowed into furrows by sea cucumbers, crossed by the grazing roads of things that go nowhere in particular. A fish stands on three stilts facing the current with its fins spread like open hands. Somewhere behind it, a hundred thousand towers comb the water. Somewhere below them, a lake with a beach of the perfectly preserved dead lies under its own slow surf. The water is warm as a body and thick with larvae and carries every sound for four miles.

Nowhere in it is there a bare surface. Nowhere in it is there room to be alone. The crowding is the architecture.

A single snap, out at the margin. A district folds shut in less than a second, without a sound worth the name.

Then the hands come out again, and the dark goes on being worked.
