## The Plain of Slow Machines

Begin with the light, because the light is the first thing that refuses to behave.

At four and a half kilometers, the air above is thin enough that the sky goes violet at noon and the stars keep faint appointments in it. The sun is unfiltered, a white hole. But the sun is only half the light here. The ground gives the other half back. Every surface for a thousand kilometers has been licked to a mirror — oxide cropped off, temper-colors polished away, the raw alloy beneath kept bright by grazing — so the plain throws the sky back at itself and there is no dark side to anything. Shadows are filled from below. A creature's underside is as lit as its back. The only true darkness that ever crosses this country is the shade of a swarm, and the swarms are so dense that their shade arrives as a physical event, a cool brown dusk sliding across the metal at the speed of a running animal, gone in a minute, the glare slamming back.

The cold is enormous and it is *ambient*. Forty below, fifty on the high margins, a cold that has nothing to do with the plain and does not participate in it. Air that stiffens the hairs in a nostril. Frost grows on every surface facing away from work: fern-forests of it, hand-sized, on the dorsal plates of things whose bellies are running wet at ninety degrees. You can stand with one palm on freezing iron and the other in a sweating seam and feel the whole economy in the difference.

That difference is the weather. That difference is the water. That difference is why anything lives here at all.

---

It looks like industry. It has the grammar of industry — the parallel lines, the gauged repetition, the sense of a process moving material from one condition to another along a route. From a ridge on the eastern margin you can see thirty kilometers of it at once: the long ranked bodies lying flank to flank in their beds, the trunk-lines of them running to the horizon and past it, the towers of vapor standing over the hot provinces like a range of white mountains that never move.

But nothing here has been built. There is not one artifact on this plain, not one made thing, not one wreck, not one shard. There are no ruins because nothing is discarded. There are no nests because there is no gap to build in. What looks like a machine is a body, and what looks like a product is another body, and what looks like a slag heap is a nursery.

The presses are animals. They lie in their pits in rows of nine hundred, each one four kilometers from ram to bed, sessile, cold-backed, their flanks barnacled with lesser life. They close once a season. You can watch one for a week and see nothing, and then notice that the gap has narrowed by a hand's width, and understand that you are watching a bite.

Between them run the ways: long ridged organisms, spine-up, lying in perfect parallels because parallels are what their larvae settle into, chemically guided, the way coral settles. Along the ways the carriers move — kilometer-long articulated grazers with rolling feet, hundreds of feet, each foot a wheel that is a limb that is a wheel, gut full of ore, hauling it toward the reduction provinces at a meter an hour. They do not hurry. Nothing at that size has ever hurried. Their motion is only visible as a change in what is beside you.

And in the reduction provinces the kiln-hearts sit half-buried in their own accumulated heat, chambers lined with refractory they grow the way an oyster grows nacre, burning charcoal made from the black mats, exhaling a plume that goes up eleven kilometers and stands there, a permanent white column with a lenticular hat, visible from three days' walk away, the landmark by which everything navigates.

---

The rock is *fresh*. That is the secret of the whole place, the reason for the density and the noise and the appetite.

The plain was lifted recently — geologically recently, a few million years, a blink — and it has not been weathered. Its edges are still knife-sharp; you can open a hand on a scree block. There are no rounded stones anywhere. There is no soil in the ordinary sense, no patient humus of a long-worn world. And crucially the stone is still *reduced*: unoxidized, sulfide-bright, full of electrons that have never been spent. On an old continent the rock is a corpse, already rusted, already eaten. Here it is meat.

So everything eats it. The primary producers are lithotrophs, mat-forming, black and slick, living directly on freshly fractured mineral faces, and the whole apparatus of presses and carriers and mills is a way of fracturing rock fast enough to keep them fed. The milling fauna grind. The presses bite. The chips come off in blue-tempered ribbons that curl away hot into the cold and are seized in midair by things with too many legs. Rust, here, is not decay. Rust is digestion. The orange bloom on a spent surface is the sign that something has been fed.

The products — the shaped ingots, the gauged shafts, the coils that come out of the winding provinces in slow extruded spirals — are what the process cannot use. They are waste with a heredity. They are shaped because the shaping is inherited, and they are eaten eventually by the things that eat shaped waste, and nothing accumulates except bodies.

---

The vegetation grows only where the work leaks.

Follow a warm seam and you follow a jungle: black belt-leaved plants, thick as a wrist, growing in the exhaust lee of a kiln-heart in mats three meters deep, spongy, wet, cold on top and steaming underneath. Their leaves are gaskets. That is not a metaphor; it is their function and their trade. They grow into every gap between bodies and seal it, and the animals tolerate them and cultivate them by bleeding heat, because a sealed seam holds its warmth and an open seam kills. Where two great flank-plates meet you will find a lush green-black packing of them, wedged, compressed, thriving on the pressure, leaking a sap that is nearly oil.

They are grazed to the quick constantly. They grow faster than they are cropped, barely. Between the seams: nothing, or the frozen crisp remains of a mat that lost its heat source, standing in glassy ranks, perfectly preserved, cold as the sky, waiting for a warmth that will not come back for eleven thousand years.

And in the deep seams, where the light is only reflected light and the temperature is a steady blood-warm, the mats go pale and fat and fungal and produce orchards of soft fruiting bodies that are eaten by things that have never in their lineage seen the sun.

---

Water is inventory, not flow.

There are no rivers on the plain. There is a fixed stock of water, some enormous but finite tonnage, and it is all in transit or in bodies. The hot fauna exhale it; the cold air cannot hold it; it condenses instantly into a fog that is not weather but *breath*, a standing white soup over every working province, thick with spores and oil aerosol and drifting swarm-larvae. To breathe here is to drink. The fog rains constantly and the rain never lands — it falls back onto hot flanks and goes up again, a closed loop three hundred meters tall, a private hydrology that has been running the same water since the uplift.

Where the fog drifts over cold ground it dumps. There are pockets — dead pits, the shadowed lee of a stalled press — where a shallow pool lies under a skin of ice, and it is the only standing water for two hundred kilometers, and it is surrounded by a wall of animals drinking, packed shoulder to shoulder, four deep, ten deep, a permanent motionless riot around a puddle.

Once every few years the whole thermal dome collapses, the inversion breaks, and it genuinely rains: warm, oily, faintly acid, for about an hour. Everything drinks. Everything spawns. Then the sky closes and the plain goes back to sweating.

---

The sound arrives before the sight, and it arrives wrong.

The fundamental is around eleven hertz — the summed stroke-frequency of a continent of slow bodies, phase-locked by nothing but mutual entrainment over deep time. You do not hear it. You feel it in the fluid of the eye, in the sternum; loose frost jumps on cold plates in a fine continuous shivering dance. Above it sits the shaft tone, a hum, and above that the enormous intermittent register: the shriek of a turning animal, a bore-beast's grinding bellow, the pressure-release whistle that goes on for two days and can be heard, refracted along the inversion, four hundred kilometers away with no loss at all, so that a thing happening at the far edge of the world sounds like it is happening at your elbow.

And once a season, a hammer lands.

You hear the descent for eleven days first: a rising air-note, the sound of a gap closing. Then the strike, which is not a bang but a *displacement*, a shove of air that flattens the fog for thirty kilometers and lifts every flying thing off the plain at once in a single panicked white sheet, and sets the standing clouds ringing like struck glass, and is heard, three hundred kilometers off, by animals who will use it to time their breeding.

---

The overwhelming part is not the giants. The giants are only the terrain.

Every square centimeter of them is occupied. Bearing-mites in the races, cleaning, eating the oxide, paid in grease. The grease itself is alive — a colonial slime that renders its own dead into lubricant and lives in the sumps and is farmed by the things it lubricates. Gasket flora in every seam. Swarf-crabs in the chip-fall. Hyperparasites on the parasites, and parasites on those, four and five tiers deep, whole food webs that never touch rock. The air is a plankton bloom. There is no bare surface on this continent and no empty volume of air; the density is not a crowding of individuals but a saturation of *roles*, every possible way of making a living already occupied, several times, by things that have converged on it from different ancestries.

And here is the rule that produces all that smallness:

**Casting shrinks.**

When a great body dies, its cavity does not rot; it is a mold. Larvae pour into it — a hot slurry of themselves — and set in the shape of the parent's interior, and walk out as its children. But metal contracts on cooling. Two percent, near enough, every generation. So lineages *descend in size*. Every mite on this plain is the reduced descendant of something the size of a hill. Every swarf-crab's ancestry can be read backward up a ladder of shrinking casts to a founder that once bit mountains. A lineage that shrinks too far finds a larger host and refills it, resetting the scale, and this is the great event of their history, the thing their whole biology is arranged around: the finding of a bigger mold.

It is why nothing is ever discarded. A corpse is a factory for heirs.

---

Toward the margins the pattern loosens. The parallels wander. Gauges drift; you find presses whose rams no longer fit their beds, ways that curve for no reason, a winding-beast producing a coil of a diameter nothing has used for a million years. Feral provinces. Half-species. Beyond them the plain thins into ordinary sharp cold scree and the mats go black and crustose and the noise falls away behind you until you can hear your own blood, and the silence there is the most frightening thing on the continent.

And in the interior, sometimes, a province dies. The heat goes out of it. The cold walks in over a decade, and the fog stops, and the flora freezes standing, and everything is preserved exactly where it was: nine hundred presses mid-stroke, a carrier still loaded, a swarm fallen in a drift like snow. Rust takes it very slowly, because rust needs water and the water has all gone elsewhere.

The lickers avoid those places. They are the plain's immune system, the polishing herds, and they will not enter a cold province — they read the oxide and turn away. So the dead districts stay mirror-bright for a hundred thousand years, throwing the sun back at a sky that has nothing left to say to them, waiting for something small to find, in all that cold shaped emptiness, a mold big enough to be worth filling.
