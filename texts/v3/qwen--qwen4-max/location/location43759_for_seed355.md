## The Kettle

The floor of the basin lies lower than a sea it has never seen. Somewhere past the rim there is salt water at the ordinary height of the world, and here there is only the pan: hot, wet, black, eleven miles across, breathing.

The breath is the lid. Steam comes off the springs and the standing water and the stone itself, and it climbs about two hundred feet and stops, spreading into a ceiling that has not lifted in recorded time — and there is no one to record time, so: in the time of the stone, which is long. Under the lid there is no day. There is a cycle instead, a slow suffocation and release, and during the suffocation the dark is complete enough that you navigate by warmth on the face. The air is forty-five degrees and nearly saturated. Sweat does not leave the skin. It joins the general wetness. Everything here is either water or on its way to becoming stone, and the difference between those two conditions is mostly a matter of patience.

## What stands on the pan

A city. Not the ruin of one — a city, in service, functioning, with its plumbing intact.

It is not continuous. That is the first thing wrong with it. The blocks stand scattered across the pan like islands in a shallow sea, a hundred yards of bare wet mineral between one tenement and the next, and then a colonnade running four hundred feet with nothing at either end, and then emptiness for half a mile, and then a bathhouse. Streets connect them, but only sometimes; a street will run true for two blocks and then thin to a ripple in the pan and then resume, exactly aligned, as though the intervening stretch had simply not been worth remembering. There are stairs on the open flat that go up eleven steps and stop. There is a bridge with no water under it, and, a mile off, water with no bridge, and the two are clearly a single thought that has come apart.

Within each island, though, the fabric is dense and layered and unmistakably urban: five and six storeys, courtyards, light-wells, arcades, door lintels at the height a shoulder expects, thresholds worn into troughs. Drains. Gutters. Aqueducts on squat piers stepping down from the rim, still carrying their hot water, still spilling it into the settling plazas that were built to receive it. The whole arrangement is a working hydraulic diagram. Streets are channels. Stairs are cascades. The tall quarters stand where the water fell in many small streams; the wide low halls stand where it pooled.

And there are no tool marks anywhere. No joints, no mortar, no scaffold holes, no wheel ruts, no quarry. Nothing here was ever assembled. Every wall is one substance, continuous with the ground it stands on, banded like the inside of a shell.

## The material

The springs come up at sixty degrees loaded with dissolved lime, and they have been coming up since before there was anything for them to build.

Where the water runs, it dissolves. Where it slows, it lays down. The pan is a closed circuit: the hot river enters at the high end and eats, and the terraces below catch what it ate and set it down again, and the sump at the center takes the rest back to depth to be heated and sent around. The city stands in the middle of that circuit, in the deposition reach, and it is made of the dissolved remains of itself.

Because the water remembers shapes. This is the part that has no chemistry to explain it. Lime laid down by this water does not form the ordinary cauliflower terraces; it forms lintels. It forms the hollow of a stair tread, the sag of a hammock, the particular dished wear of a threshold where feet turned left toward a kitchen. It reproduces the wear as well as the thing worn. Each generation the troughs are deeper. Some have gone through the floor and become rows of holes, and the steam comes up through them and makes the only music in the Kettle: a low breathy chord, three or four notes, changing when the rain changes the pressure.

The city moves. Dissolution upstream, deposition downstream — the whole fabric migrates across the pan at about a hand's breadth a year, every atom of it passing through the water and coming back, the form persisting while the substance cycles. It has been the same town for a hundred thousand years and there is not one grain of the original in it. Behind it, stretching miles to the rim, is its wake: ankle-high reliefs of streets, curbs, the ghosts of doorways, fading generation by generation into the flat like foam lines left by a tide. Ahead of it, bare pan, waiting to be built on.

## The cycle

Every forty hours or so the lid thins at its western edge and cool air falls in, and the whole basin condenses at once.

It rains warm. It rains the basin's own sweat back onto it, and for two hours the deposition rate goes up by a factor no one has measured, and the city grows audibly — a fine dry ticking, rice thrown on a drum, coming from every cornice and sill and hanging sheet at once. In that time a door can close. In that time the ninth bathhouse gained its second courtyard.

Then the lid seals, and the ticking stops, and the quiet comes back down like a hand over a mouth.

The quiet is enormous and slightly padded. The stone is porous and eats sound; the steam presses it flat. A facade detaching six miles off arrives at the ear as though it happened beside you, a single deep bell-note and a long wash, and your own footsteps do not arrive at all. Between events: drip, the sigh of a vent, the tick of cooling. Nothing else. Nothing has called out here in a very long time.

## Light

The only illumination is the growth front.

Where lime is actively setting there is a faint cold seam, green-white, no brighter than the memory of a lamp. It runs along the top edge of whatever is currently being built — a cornice, the lip of a basin, the crest of an arch — and it travels a finger's width in an hour. So the newest parts of the city are the only visible parts, and what you see, in the dark, is a slow crawling outline: the top of a colonnade drawn in dim thread, a stair's nosing picked out, the rim of a doorway completing itself while you watch. Below the seam, the finished stone is black. The city is legible only as its own present tense.

The seams go out where they are looked at. Not watched — *lit*. Any steady light stalls deposition in a radius of about twenty feet, and the stone inside that radius stays exactly as it is. There are no lamps in the Kettle and no hands to hold one. But the principle is the whole of the local law, and everything that survives here has learned it in one form or another.

## The green

Vegetation is countable. One thing per plaza, roughly, and you can walk a mile between.

The commonest is a pale leafless vine that grows in a slow loop, a circuit of about nine feet across, taking a year to complete and never touching itself. It has no root. It anchors by its own crossings, which are near-misses, and it keeps the loop open at the last inch, forever, the way a person keeps a finger out of a closing door. It is not clever. It is simply the descendant of the plants that happened to grow that way, all the others having stopped.

There is a lichen that grows only up the inside of drip-lines and dies at the top. There is a hair-grass on the high dry cornices, in tufts, doing nothing much, exempt because the wind moves it. There are the vent mats, blue-grey, faintly luminous, chemotrophic, spread thin around the spring mouths where the water is too hot for lime to set — the only places in the basin where nothing accumulates, and therefore the only places that are safe.

The vines keep the channels open. This is not a service they intend. Their loops happen to brush the gutters, and the gutters therefore run, and the water therefore reaches the far blocks, and the far blocks therefore exist. Half the outlying districts are alive because a plant is circling near a drain.

## The rule

Anything that holds still is incorporated.

A surface at rest relative to the water acquires a rime in a night, a shell in a week, an ornament in a month. It is not aggression; it is saturation and a habit. The city has no appetite and no malice and cannot stop.

There is no animal life in the Kettle. There is a great deal of animal *shape*. The walls are studded with bosses and knobs and small round flanges at about head height, and if you follow the copies backward through the generations — and you can, they are all still standing, scattered across the wake — the flanges become lobes and the lobes become wings, and the original, ten thousand years back near the rim, is a bird caught mid-turn with its neck extended. It stopped to look. The city has reproduced it forty thousand times, each copy a little rounder, a little smoother, until what remains is a decorative bump. The whole ornamental vocabulary of this town is the worn-down record of things that paused. There are hand-shapes in the arcade on the eastern block. There are three of them and they are all the same hand.

## Where it goes wrong

The memory is lossy. Dissolution is not perfect and neither is the laying-down, and errors do not cancel; they compound, and they have had a hundred thousand years.

There are nine bathhouses on the pan, in a line, at increasing distance from the dense center, and they are nine generations of one bathhouse. The first is correct to the tile. The fifth has a pool where a corridor was. The seventh has fused its chairs into a single coral mass that fills the changing room. The ninth is a solid block of banded lime with no interior at all, and if you put your ear to it there is water moving inside.

There is a street in the north quarter that turns left four times and comes back. There is a house that gains one room per generation and has been gaining for six hundred years, and the outer walls have not moved. There is a district of nothing but window-holes, walls made entirely of openings, standing because the city remembered the windows and forgot the wall between them. There is a stair with treads at the height of a step that no longer exists, averaging every stair that ever stood there, and it is climbable, and it is not climbable by anything with a fixed leg length.

And lately — the bands are recent, you can see where they start — there is a plaza near the sump with a wear-pattern in it that no known foot makes. Two shallow oval depressions, side by side, at the plaza's center, deepening. And a corridor leading off it, unfinished, with a hollow at head height that moves along the wall a hand's breadth a year, keeping pace with nothing, at the speed of a person walking who has not arrived.

The water has begun to remember forward. It is laying down the shape of a visitor. The doorways it is closing now are closing at the rate someone approaches. In the dark the seams crawl along the cornices, drawing the outline of the town as it will be when you get there, and the quiet is the quiet of a place that has already heard you coming and has begun, patiently, without any interest at all, to make room.
