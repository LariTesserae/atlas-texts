## The Basin

Kneel and put your palm on the ground. It gives, slightly, like the flank of something breathing. It is warm at the heel of your hand and cool at your fingertips. Under the grit there are thumbprints — hundreds of them, overlapping, pressed into the coils of clay by whoever laid this course, and under those, when the top skin flakes, older thumbprints, and under those, more. There is no dirt here that was not milled. The floor of the world is crushed pot, ground clinker, ash, shell flour, sweepings, bone-meal from the ponds, and slip to bind it, and it has been tamped into place by feet, and it will be tamped again tomorrow, because it is sinking.

That is the first thing to understand. The ground is not a noun.

---

It is a raft, this basin, some forty miles across at its widest and nowhere more than a hundred feet thick, floating on the black water it displaces. Nothing rests on rock. There is no rock. Dig anywhere and you go down through fill, through the fired ribs of older work, through a stratum of nothing but broken lamps, through a course of stamped and dated bricks laid by people whose names are still knocked on doors above you, and eventually you reach the wet, where the fill turns to a cold porridge and the hole fills behind your bucket faster than you can bail. The dead go into the aggregate. They are milled with the rest. When you walk you are walking on everyone.

So the basin subsides under its own weight, a hand's breadth a year, more in the heavy districts, and the water presses up through the fill at the low edges, and the only thing between the whole made world and the sump is labor. Every hour of every day, somewhere within earshot, a tamp crew is working. You hear them the way you'd hear surf.

---

There is no sun. There is a lid — not a sky, but the accumulated underside of eleven hundred years of building upward, tier on tier, roof doubling as floor, until the whole basin has closed over itself like a scab. What light there is comes from below and from within: the orange mouths of the kilns, the wick-stones sweating their slow fish-oil flame, and the charge-tiles.

The tiles are the clever thing. A glazed plate, fired with a particular slip of calcined shell and pond-sludge, held against the kiln's glow for an hour, will give that glow back for a day and a half — a weak green, the color of the inside of a wave. Everyone carries one. Children are issued their first at six and learn to charge it before they learn to knot. In the settled districts the dark is not black but a deep wet green-black, because everything is damp and everything shines: the walls shine, the ropes shine, the tamped floor shines in a thin film, the ceilings shine and drip. You see the world mostly as highlights. A wall is a hundred small green glints in the shape of a wall. A face is two eyes and the wet line of a lower lip.

And in the cool hours, when the kilns are shut and the mass gives up its heat, the condensation comes on so thick that it rains indoors. Not everywhere at once. It rains under the cold tiers, in shafts and stairwells, in the long galleries where the draft runs; it gathers on the undersides of the great roof-plates and lets go in fat irregular drops that strike the tuned pans set out to catch them. This is where the drinking water comes from. It tastes faintly of ash and copper and it is the only water anyone drinks.

---

Now the sound, which is the real substance of this place.

Tamp crews first: the deep double *thud-thud* of fired-clay rammers, six or eight to a gang, each district keeping its own tempo, and the tempos are how you know where you are. The Shellcourse works in a limping five. The Long Fill works even and fast, four to the breath, and has for two centuries. Lost in the dark, you don't look for a landmark, you listen for the beat change and walk toward the tempo you were raised in.

Over that: treadle pumps sighing and gulping; the roar of kiln draft, which is a low continuous animal noise you stop hearing in your first year and start hearing again whenever it stops; the *ping, ping, tick* of new brick cooling and crazing, thousands of small ceramic fractures a minute across the whole basin, like the world's teeth chattering; rope creak; the slap of slip being thrown; windlasses; the clinker-chutes; the drip on the pans, which is tuned, deliberately, every catchment basin pitched to a note so the rain plays the district.

And under all of it, the ring.

The walls hold sound. Nobody planned this. The particular clay, the particular firing, the density of the stacking — the fabric of the basin stores a knock the way a charge-tile stores light, and gives it back slowly, over decades. Stand still in an old stairwell at the end of the cool and you'll hear a door being knocked on that hasn't existed since your grandmother's time: three taps and a scrape, faint, patient, coming out of the wall a little at a time. Some families keep a listening brick. Some walls have gone bad and give back screaming.

Speech here is shouted, or pitched, or done with the hands against a palm or a cheek. There is a whole guild of builders who are deaf and are preferred for it; they read the floor through their soles and know a course is badly tamped three days before it slumps. Doors have no locks. A door has a chord. You knock your household's chord and the household knows you, and a stranger's knock is a wrong note that carries four galleries.

---

Everything is hand-made. There is no machine in the basin more complicated than a lever, a treadle, a siphon, a bellows, a windlass. There is no iron. There is soft copper for pins and lead for plummets and kiln-glass for blades — glass drawn hot into thread, and thread spun into cord, and cord into the armature that holds up half the world. There is no wood anywhere, and never was, and no green thing of any kind. If you want a handle you fire one. If you want a hinge you make a knuckle of clay and a pin of copper and you grease it with pond-fat and you re-make it every year.

The fiber comes out of the smoke. In the long flues, where the kiln exhaust cools against the wet plates, a pale mineral wool condenses and grows — slub, they call it, a fuzz like frost that thickens into hanks. It isn't alive. It only behaves as though it is: it blooms after a firing, it retreats in the dry, it colonizes a flue in a season and chokes it in two. Slub-harvesters go in on their bellies with glass scrapers and come out white and coughing and are paid well and die young. Slub is rope, rope is armature, armature is ground, ground is more world. The whole place is a loop eating its own exhaust and its own rubble, and the only thing entering the loop is heat, and the only thing leaving is the slow settlement of everything toward the water.

There is also slime. In the settling ponds and the warm seeps, mats of it, brown and violet, which are farmed, dried, and milled into the slip. It is the only crop. It smells like a wet coin.

---

Animals are scarce, and each scarce animal is famous.

Pale crabs in the settling ponds, blind, the size of a thumbnail, netted by children and boiled in the kiln-mouth ash. Wick-flies at the oil stones, greasy, slow, tolerated because they eat the soot. In the deep sump under the Low Fill there is something long that the pump-crews have heard against the intake screens for four generations and never once seen; there is a song about it, and the song has more verses than the animal has sightings.

And there is the quiet.

Perhaps three in a lifetime, in a district of forty thousand. It moves through the din as a moving oval of hush — not silence, but a subtraction, the roar dropping away in a soft-edged patch that travels. You feel it first as pressure in the ears, then as a horrible intimacy: your own breathing suddenly loud, your own heartbeat audible, the person beside you suddenly speaking into your ear from ten feet away. Everyone who has survived one says the same thing: stand still, hold hands, don't make a sound. It takes the ones who keep talking. It is thought to eat the ring — to graze on stored sound, on the old knocks in the walls — and where it has passed, the listening bricks go dead, and families mourn a second time.

Nobody has ever seen its body. In this dark, in this shine, you would only see the place where the highlights stopped.

---

Go out to the margin, though, and the density thins, and this is where the world is still being decided.

The Far Courses run for miles as bare armature: fired ribs and glass-cord lashing, unfilled, a lattice the size of a county standing in a foot of black water, waiting for aggregate that has not been milled yet. The lid is unfinished out here. There is actual sky — a low, featureless, rain-colored nothing — and actual rain falls through it, unmediated, unmetered, not condensate, not tuned, just water arriving from somewhere that isn't the basin. It is the only unmade thing anyone has ever touched. People come out to stand in it with their mouths open and cry, and can't say why.

And in the raw fill at the margin's edge, something has happened that has no precedent.

The rain has cut a channel.

It is nine feet deep and forty yards long and it has tributaries. It has a bank that has slumped and a little undercut cliff and a gravel bar of sorted clinker, coarse upstream, fine at the bend — sorted by the water, not by anyone. There is sediment moving in it. There is a plume where it enters the sump. It is the first erosion in the history of the world, and the basin has sent surveyors, and the surveyors have staked it, and there is an argument in the Shellcourse about whether to fill it, and the argument has been going for eleven years, and the gully has deepened two feet while it goes on.

There are crabs in it. Nobody put them there.

---

Come back in. The dark closes over the margin behind you, tier by tier, and the tempo finds you — four to the breath, the Long Fill, home. The floor is tacky; a gang has just laid this course and the tamping is not finished. Somewhere above, the kilns open for the firing hour and the whole gallery warms four degrees and the light comes up orange along the wet walls, ten thousand thumbprints standing out in relief, and then the kilns shut and the cool begins and the roof starts to drip and the pans begin, one by one, to play.

You are standing on ground that is nine hours old, made of ground that was older, over water that has been waiting the whole time. Nothing here has ever weathered. Nothing here is old enough to be a stone. Put your hand down again and feel it: the prints are still soft, and one of them, near your knee, is the right size to be yours.
