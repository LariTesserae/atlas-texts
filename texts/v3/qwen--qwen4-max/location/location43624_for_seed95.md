## The Sill

From the last rise before it, the plain looks like a mistake in the world — a floor where a range of mountains used to be. Three thousand meters up and flat to the eye for a hundred kilometers, warm, rust-colored, faintly glittering, with a handful of low knobs standing on it like furniture in an emptied room. The air is thin enough to make the shadows sharp and the sun quick on the skin, but it is not cold. It has never been cold here. This is a low latitude, and the ground is dark iron, and it holds the day's heat well past midnight.

The mountains that made this surface are entirely gone. Not their roots — their roots are what you are standing on. Two billion years of rain took the range down grain by grain and spread it out and took that down too, until the land arrived at the level below which water cannot cut, and stopped, and has been stopping ever since. What is left is a hardpan: iron and silica cooked into a crust the color of dried blood, pitted, vesicular, ringing faintly underfoot in some places and soundless in others. In the fissures, a deep red weathering profile goes down forty meters, soft as cheese, riddled with galleries.

And here is the first thing that is wrong with the obvious story. The plain should be gently undulating by now, dissected, wrinkled with the small dishonesties of age. It is not. It is *flat* — flatter than erosion alone can manage, flat the way a swept floor is flat. Something is keeping it that way.

---

Look down. The rust is not all rock.

About two-thirds of the surface is animals: discs the size of a palm, thin as a coin, edged in a fringe of gripping cilia, laid edge to edge across the hardpan in a mosaic so tight that the seams between them are the only lines on the plain. They are called pennies by no one, since there is no one, but they are penny-shaped and penny-colored, brown-green and semi-translucent, and each has a lens on its back — a window of clear chitin over a chamber of green.

They farm light. Inside each animal, in a flat pane beneath the dorsal window, algae live in a broth the host keeps at the right salinity and the right temperature by shifting its own body a few millimeters an hour. The animal eats almost nothing. It drinks fog and dust and the trace metals it dissolves out of the crust with a slow acid tongue, and in exchange it carries its garden up into the thin bright air and holds it there for a hundred and eighty years.

This is why the vegetation is so poor. There are crusts and a few cushion plants in the joints and, in the deep fissures, a squat water-storing tree with grey bark and leaves like thumbnails, one every few kilometers, ancient, wind-pruned to the shape of a clenched hand. But the plain's photosynthesis is done by animals. The green here has legs, in the sense that a thing has legs when it can move a millimeter a day and is prepared to use them.

Where the pennies die, they do not rot. They cement. Their undersides fuse to the crust, their lenses cloud, their shells fill with mineral, and the next generation settles on top and grows into the gaps, so that the plain is paved in a laminated biogenic rock, layer on layer, hundreds of thousands of years thick, thin as pages. Scratch it and you get banding like a tree ring, but the rings are centuries. In places the pavement is stacked five animals deep, all of them alive, the upper ones shading the lower ones, the lower ones living on what leaks through — a dim, patient, second-tier economy of the shaded, growing slower, living longer, some of them older than any tree that has ever stood on this surface.

Between and beneath and on top: the rest. Mites the size of punctuation grazing the algal spill. Worms that mine the seams. A flat predator like a smear of grease that spends eleven months dissolving a hollow beneath its neighbor's foot and then drinks the neighbor. Cysts in the pits, waiting. Nematodes in the water films at a density that makes a handful of crust a crowd. Every square meter holds something like forty thousand animals, and there are ten to the eighth power square meters, and the number stops meaning anything and becomes a property of the place, like its altitude.

At noon the whole plain lifts. All at once, over about forty minutes, every penny raises itself a millimeter on its fringe to break contact with the hot rock and let the heat go out of its underside; at dusk the plain settles back. A millimeter, across a hundred kilometers, twice a day. It is the largest coordinated movement on the Sill and you cannot see it happen. You can only measure it, and no one is here to measure it.

---

Water arrives at night, from below.

The escarpment at the southern margin drops nine hundred meters into a basin of ordinary forest, and every evening the basin's wet warm air climbs the wall and spills onto the plain as fog — a low white river of it, waist-high, moving at walking pace, gone by nine in the morning. It does not rain much. Perhaps thirty days a year, in a soft season when the fog stays all day and drips. The rest is condensation: the fog catching on the pennies' raised rims, on the knobs, on the raised backs of the tall things, beading, running, pooling in the seams.

The plain drinks its own fog and holds it in pockets. There are rock basins here, dissolution hollows the size of a bathtub or a barn floor, hundreds of thousands of them scattered across the flat, each holding a few centimeters of water for a few weeks after the rains. They are the towns of this world. In a full pan the life is not countable: brine-tolerant crustaceans, flatworms in sheets, a swimming thing like a transparent leaf with a red eye, all of it hatching from cysts in the crust within an hour of wetting, all of it descended from lineages that have used that particular pan since before the escarpment was where it is now. Each pan has endemics. Each pan is a nation with a border of dry rust it cannot cross. When the water goes, they lay their cysts and go back into the rock and wait, and the waiting has been measured in decades.

Along the northern edge, where the hardpan thins over the soft profile, there is a weep line: a hundred kilometers of seeps, damp black patches, the only place the water-storing trees grow in anything like a stand. And at the western margin the plain ends in a canyon, where a river that is older than the plain's flatness runs in incised meanders, looping through its own inherited curves half a kilometer down, brown, unhurried, carrying almost no sediment because there is almost nothing left to carry.

---

It is quiet. This is the thing visitors would find hardest, if there were visitors.

The crust is vesicular and the pavement is porous and both eat sound; a footstep lands with a soft crunch and goes nowhere. The wind is weak — the gradients up here are slack, the weather a slow repetition of itself. But the real reason is that nothing on the Sill needs to shout. Nothing here pursues and nothing here flees. Predation is chemistry and patience, a slow leaning, a year-long dissolution. There are no alarm calls because there is no alarm; there is no courtship song because courtship happens by touch across a seam and takes a season. Sound is a technology for animals in a hurry, and it was never adopted.

What you hear: the tick of the knobs expanding at dawn, one crack every few minutes, like a cooling stove. The soft hiss of fog moving through the penny-fringes, which is not the fog's sound but the sound of forty thousand cilia per meter adjusting. A dry rattle when a grazing animal, a slow rasping lozenge the size of a boot, works a seam. Very occasionally, a block falling off a knob — a genuine noise, a flat clap that rolls out and dies in three seconds because the ground will not carry it.

And from the margin, if you lie down at the escarpment's lip with your head over the edge, you can hear the world below: the forest, the insects, the birds, a distant continuous roaring surf of ordinary life, rising through nine hundred meters of air. It sounds like a city. It sounds like something happening. Then you sit up and the Sill closes over the sound and there is nothing again but the ticks and the hiss.

---

The tall ones stand in the fog.

They are tripods, four meters high, three legs like grey stilts and a body no bigger than a bread loaf slung between them, and above the body a fan — a vertical comb of fine fibers, a hundred thousand of them, spread facing the incoming fog. They are condensation harvesters. They stand still all night and let the fog build on the comb and run down the legs into their mouths, and they filter from it the drift: the microscopic juveniles, the spores, the lowland pollen, the dust of continents that blew over the escarpment. The plain imports almost nothing else. Every atom of phosphorus on this surface arrived on the wind or in a fog droplet, and the tripods are the customs house.

There are perhaps two hundred thousand of them across the Sill, spaced like trees in an orchard, and they move about thirty meters a day, at dawn, in a silence you could sleep through. Their feet are pads. Where they stand, the drip from their combs makes a dark circle, and in that circle the pennies grow fat and green and the only real moss on the plain occurs, so that the tripod herd leaves behind it a slow migration of small green stains that persist for years after the herd has gone.

They live four hundred years. When one dies it does not fall for a decade; it stands, drying, held up by its own geometry, and the pennies climb the legs and pave it, and eventually there is one more low mound on the plain, and then not even that.

---

There are marks.

Quartz pebbles, white, the only white things here, laid out in lines. Some lines run straight for two kilometers. Some make a spiral a kilometer across that cannot be read from the ground at all and is only a shape when you climb the tallest knob. They are laid by a slow animal, a flat dragger with a sucker on its underside, that carries one pebble a year, sets it, and moves on, and the reason it does this is not known to it. Its ancestors did it for a purpose — territory, perhaps, or a chemical trail, or a marriage — and the purpose has been lost, and the habit has not. The lines are maintained. When a pebble is displaced by a rare flood or a falling block, another dragger, in some decade, puts it back within a centimeter of where it was.

And there are huts. Perhaps four hundred of them across the whole plain, one every twenty or thirty kilometers: corbelled domes of cemented shell, two meters high, a single chamber, a doorway forty centimeters wide. They are grown, not built — colonies of cement-worms lay their tubes in a spiral course and thicken it and close it over, generation after generation, and the shape emerges the way a termite mound's shape emerges, from rules nobody holds in mind. Nothing on the Sill has hands. Everything here is done with mouths and backs and time.

The doorways are too small for anything now living. The domes are old, most of them, softened, half-dissolved, slumping back into the pavement from which they were made. They are dry inside and warm at night, and sometimes a tripod's juvenile shelter in one for a season, and sometimes nothing has entered one for six thousand years. They are not ruins of anything. There was never a civilization on this plain, never a tool, never a fire. There was only a very long habit, and the habit built rooms, and the rooms outlasted the reason.

---

Every nineteen years or so — the interval is not exact and nothing here is counting — the pavement spawns.

Over three nights, in the fog, the pennies open their ventral seams and release a winged form the size of a grain of rice: a thing with no mouth, no gut, all gonad and wing and a single dark eyespot. They rise. The air above the Sill for two hundred meters becomes a slow blizzard of them, drifting, touching, falling. It is the only crowd in the sky this place ever has. It is not loud. It is a hiss, a little fuller than the fog's hiss, a sound like sand poured onto paper, and it goes on for three nights, and by the fourth morning there is a new centimeter of small bodies on the plain, drying, cementing, filling every hollow and seam and scratch, and the grazers come out and rasp the excess level.

This is the mechanism. This is what keeps the floor flat. Any bump on the Sill is grazed down; any hollow is filled with the dead; any scar is repaired along its old pattern, because the repair crews inherit the pattern chemically, in the crust, the way the draggers inherit the pebble lines. The plain is not a surface that has finished eroding. It is a surface that is being *held*, at the exact elevation where erosion gave out, by an unbroken continuous living membrane that has been maintaining the same shape, mending its own injuries, restoring its own markings, for something on the order of two hundred million years — since before there were flowers in the world below, since before the forest at the escarpment's foot was anything but ferns.

The mountains are gone. The plain lowers a millimeter every thousand years, and the plain builds back a millimeter every thousand years, and the difference between those two numbers is the whole story of what lives here.

At dusk the fog comes up over the lip and moves out across the rust in a white sheet, and the tripods turn their combs into it, and the pennies settle their millimeter, and the seams fill with beading water, and forty thousand animals per square meter go on doing almost nothing at a rate that will, given time, move a continent's worth of stone. It is warm. It is very high. It is nearly silent. It is the oldest continuously inhabited surface in the world, and it is not finished, and it will not be finished, and nothing on it has ever been in a hurry.
