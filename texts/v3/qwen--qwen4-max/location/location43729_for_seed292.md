## The Pan

The rain does not come down here. It comes *across*. The cloud that stands against the escarpment is not weather passing over the plateau; it is a fixture, a permanent front pinned to the cliff by a wind that has blown from the same quarter since before anyone thought to write down which quarter it was. Air arrives loaded, hits twelve hundred metres of rock, is forced upward, cools, and gives up everything it holds — continuously, in a wall, at the rate of eleven metres a year. To walk out onto the flat is to walk into the inside of a wave that has been breaking, without finishing, for two hundred million years.

It is bright. That surprises people. The cloud is not a ceiling but a lamp: the whole sky is one soft white source, and the light comes from everywhere at once, so the water has no shadows on it, only a faint blue bruise where your own body blocks the glow. When the cloud thins in the afternoon the sheet goes silver and you have to squint. Snow-glare, without snow.

Under the water there is almost nothing. That is the second surprise. You are at three thousand eight hundred and forty metres, ankle-deep, and the ground beneath your boot is flat — not flat like a field, flat like a floor, forty metres of fall across eighty kilometres, a gradient you could not detect with a spirit level and a week. This is the root of a mountain range. The peaks that stood here were the size of anything you have ever feared, and they are gone, entirely, grain by grain, and what remains is their basement: quartzite laid down before there were animals, folded, buried, cooked, unburied, and then planed. Every angle has been taken off it. The stone is fluted and scalloped and polished like the inside of a shell, black with mineral varnish where it stands proud of the water, and it rings under a heel. There is no rubble. Rubble was finished with a long time ago. There is no soil either, except where the water has been kind enough to let a little rot accumulate.

The water is four degrees, maybe nine in the shallows at noon. It is black-tea brown over the peat and absolutely clear over the stone, and it is *hungry* — no calcium, no dissolved anything, so soft it leaches the salt out of your hands and tastes of wet air and nothing. It is hungry enough to dissolve quartz. That is the only erosion left available to it. The plateau lowers by a few millimetres a millennium, and the only sharp edges anywhere are at the rim, where the water is still, briefly, young.

## The mat

Lush, though. Insanely lush, in the way a starving thing can be lush: not tall, but total. Every square centimetre of the drowned floor that is not bare rock is occupied, and most of what is bare rock is occupied anyway by a skin of black moss you can slip on like ice. Cushion plants grow in domes so dense you can stand on them, some of them eight hundred years old, older than the language you are thinking in, adding a millimetre a season. Between the cushions: sedges, liverworts, sundews with their red sticky hairs, bladderworts filling every pool with tiny traps that snap shut on nothing all day and occasionally on something. Pitcher plants stand in the seeps, full of drowned midges and, now and then, a frog that misjudged. There is no calcium for shells, so the snails are dwarfed and thin as paper, and there are almost none. Everything else is here in quantities that stop being a number and become a texture.

The wind never stops. Forty kilometres an hour is a calm; ninety is Tuesday. Nothing grows above knee height on the open pan because nothing can. The forest grows *downward*.

The water has been eating the quartzite along its joints for a very long time, and it has opened holes — dolines, sinkholes, some the width of a house, some the width of a street, sixty metres deep, blue-green at the bottom, filled to the brim with the same cold tea. Out of the wind, in the wet warmth of a hole, there are trees. Myrtle and tree fern and a canopy so hung with moss it looks upholstered. You walk over the tops of them. Their crowns are level with your shins, and when a squall goes across the pan the forest beneath you heaves and shows its pale undersides like something rolling over in sleep, and the sound that comes up out of the hole is a different sound entirely — leaf-noise, a hush, a hundred litres of dripping — and then the wind takes it back.

## Movement

Nothing here holds still long enough to be mapped properly. The sheet-flow runs, in theory, north-north-east at a walking pace you cannot see. In practice the wind pushes it backwards. Whole square kilometres of shallow lake reverse direction in an afternoon and drain the wrong way, and the channels through the mat silt and re-cut in a night. A seiche crosses the pan every forty minutes or so, a standing wave twenty centimetres high and eighty kilometres wide, and you experience it as the water simply deciding to be somewhere else. Peat rafts calve off the mat in storms — an acre of vegetation detaching from the stone it has spent four centuries gripping — and sail across the shallows with their sedges still upright, and ground on the intakes, and have to be cut up with hay knives by men who swear in three languages.

Graupel comes through in white squalls and drifts in the lee of every pipe support and melts within the hour. Hail rattles on the corrugated roofs like something trying to get in. The rain itself arrives in walls you can watch approaching across the water, a grey curtain dragging a wind ahead of it, and when it hits, the depth gauge by the hut climbs four centimetres in ten minutes and a spillway somewhere out in the white goes off with a boom you feel in the sternum before you hear it.

## The teeth

There is a shed on stilts, a bronze staff gauge, a lightning mast, and a helicopter pad painted on polished rock. That is the whole of the built world for eleven kilometres in any direction, and it is repeated, with variations, four thousand times across the pan.

Each repetition is an intake: a cast-iron bell-mouth bolted into the ancient stone, a trash rack, a screen, a shaft. The water goes down. It goes down eleven hundred metres through penstocks strapped to the underside of the escarpment, and at the bottom of the drop, in chambers cut into the cliff, it hits Pelton buckets and the buckets turn, and the power goes away down the mountain on pylons that march to the rim, lean over, and disappear into cloud. There are cities down there. You will never see them. You will never see the lowland at all except in a torn minute when the cloud decks separate and there is a green floor two thousand metres below with a river on it like a dropped thread.

So the industry is real and enormous and almost entirely invisible: a hundred thousand tonnes of water an hour, a grid's worth of head, and the only evidence at eye level is orange rust, yellow gauge paint, ceramic insulators, and a hum. The hum comes up through the rock. It is a hundred hertz and it is in your teeth and in the water and in the soles of your boots, and after a week you stop hearing it and after a month you cannot sleep anywhere else. Alongside it, the transmission cables sing — kilometres of tuned wire in a permanent gale, an aeolian drone that shifts key when the wind shifts, so that the whole plain is very quietly and very loudly playing a chord that never resolves.

The screens clog. That is the job. Moss, raft-peat, and eels — eels that climb the wet rock at night in their thousands, working up the escarpment in the spray, finding the bell-mouths, and going down. The crews clear the racks, and the eels go into buckets, and the buckets go over the rim on a wire, and this has been happening for sixty years.

## Alive

Swifts nest behind the cataracts at the rim and fly *through* the falling water to reach them, ten thousand birds going in and out of a curtain like needles into a pincushion. At dusk they come inland over the pan in a column that takes four minutes to pass and turns the white sky the colour of gun-metal.

The frogs are the loud thing. There are so many that the mat pulses with them, and because the water-noise here occupies every ordinary frequency, they call above it — high, sharp, part of it past the top of human hearing, so what you get is a thin metallic ticking, an insect-sound, a sound like a workshop, coming from a hundred thousand small brown bodies in the sedge. Harriers quarter the flat at head height, tilting. Wingless midges crawl in the moss in numbers that make the moss look like it is boiling. Otters work the channels and use the drowned tramway rails as a running path; you can see the rails under ten centimetres of water, going somewhere, arriving nowhere, a narrow-gauge line laid in 1938 to service intakes that have since been re-sited twice.

There are nine people. They rotate. They wear rubber over wool and they read lips and use their hands, and after a year up here everyone's hearing has a notch in it. In the hut there is a room with double doors and a nail with ear-defenders on it, and men sit in there sometimes without doing anything at all.

Once or twice a season the wind drops. Forty seconds, maybe a minute. The rain goes vertical. The cables stop singing. And into that gap comes the whole rim at once, every cataract on eighty kilometres of cliff, a bass floor under everything, a sound with no direction to it — and everyone, every time, stops walking and stands still in the water and looks up.

## The end of something

This is what a mountain looks like when it has finished. Not a ruin — a ruin implies a shape left over. This is the shape *after* the shapes: the last stable state, the surface that cannot be simplified any further, holding a skin of cold water and a hand's depth of green while the escarpment eats inward at a few metres a millennium.

Eventually a hole will capture the whole pan. One doline will deepen past the others and take the drainage, and the sheet will thin, and the stone will come up out of the water blinking, and the forest in the holes will climb out over the edges and spread. That is already scheduled; it is only a matter of a hundred thousand years. The rain will keep arriving. It will simply have nothing left to stand on.
