## The Sill

The ground is finished. That is the first thing — not barren, not raw, but *finished*, the way a stone in a river is finished, except there has never been a river. Fourteen kilometers of salt and welded glass stand between this surface and the floor of the old basin, and the wind has been at it for nine million years and has arrived at its conclusions. The ridges are fluted like the insides of shells. The boulders are undercut and waisted and stand on pedestals no wider than a wrist. Everywhere the rock has been hollowed into tafoni — honeycomb, cell upon cell, some the size of a thumb, some large enough to sleep in — and the walls of the cells are smooth and warm and slightly greasy to the touch.

That greasiness is the point. The dark skin on everything, the brown-black varnish that gives the Sill its color, is a film of living clay and manganese and reduced carbon, three millimeters thick in the oldest places, and it grows a hand's breadth in a century. It eats rock. It eats the electric field. It lays down oil in its pores and seeps it into the fractures, and the fractures carry it down into the salt, where it pools in the black seams that the shafts go after. There is no grass here, no leaf, no root, nothing green or ever green. There is only the rind, and everything that lives, lives by licking it or by eating what licked it.

Because the rind knits the dust down, the wind cannot take much. Because the wind takes a little, the rind must grow. Because the salt below is still rising, two millimeters a year, and the wind takes two millimeters a year, the Sill has stood at exactly this height since before anything on it could count. Nothing here is winning. It is the most settled place in the world.

## Height and warmth

The air is thin enough that the sky has gone violet at the top, a deep bruised indigo directly overhead at noon, shading down through ordinary blue to a band of dust-ochre at the horizon. With a shade held over your eyes you can pick out two or three stars at midday. The horizon is very far away and has a visible bend in it; surveyors have to argue about this constantly, and the arguments are old and pleasant and go nowhere.

It should be cold. It is not. Beneath the salt lid there is a basement of hot granite, rich in the slow decay of heavy elements, and the salt is a blanket a dozen kilometers thick. The ground runs at blood heat and stays there. At night the air falls perhaps four degrees and no more, because the radiator is underfoot and it is enormous. You can lie down on the pavement at the third hour and feel the world warming your back like a hand. The animals lay their eggs in the shallows of the rock and let the planet incubate them. Nobody here has ever needed a fire for warmth; fire is for cracking oil, and for ceramics, and for the smell of it.

The light is ordinary. That is worth saying plainly, because everything else is not. A normal sun, a normal white noon, hard black shadows of a normal shape. The strangeness of the Sill is not a lighting effect. It is fully visible at all hours and does not depend on dusk to be believed.

## The absence

There is no water. Not scarce — *absent*, and legislated against.

The bodies here run on light oils: naphthas, thin paraffins, a solvent chemistry that would boil off in a wet world and thrives in this one. Water hydrolyzes them. A drop of it on a grazer's flank raises a white blister in seconds; a cupful in a seep-head kills the seep for a decade. The word for water is the word for a solvent hazard, and it is taught to children the way children elsewhere are taught about cliffs. When a carbonaceous fragment comes down out of the sky — and they do, a few a century, leaving a scar of shocked glass and a stink — the ward crews go out with tongs and quicklime-analogues and bury the ice before it can melt, and there is a ceremony, and the ceremony is not celebratory.

So: no clouds, no rain, no rills, no drainage. The fluting and the honeycombs are all wind and salt-recrystallization and the patient chemistry of the rind. The only things in the sky that look like weather are dust, and one standing storm.

## The Long City

It is a city. It has a mayor, a grid, a code, a single continuous pavement, and a census. It is nine hundred kilometers from the Kerf to Ward Nineteen and there are perhaps four thousand people in it.

The unit of the city is the shaft-head: a cluster of eight or twenty towers around a bore, a refining yard, a battery field, a kite meadow. Between shaft-heads there is nothing but the Sill, forty or sixty or a hundred kilometers of finished rock with a herd on it somewhere. Addresses are bearings and travel-days. A "street" is a gap you cross. And yet the whole thing is one jurisdiction, because the white line runs through all of it — a stripe of sintered salt, waist-high in places where the drifts bury it, hand-width where the wind scours it, maintained continuously for eleven hundred years. It joins every ward to every other. To be a citizen is to be able to reach out and touch it. There are people who have walked its whole length as a devotional practice, and it takes them four years, and they are considered to have done something reasonable with their lives.

The towers are cast from salt-crete and from molted glass, and they are drawn sharp on the drafting board and built sharp, with crisp arrises and clean corners, because the wind will finish them. Three hundred years takes the edge off. Six hundred rounds the plan. The architects design the *eroded* form and then compute backward to the sharp one, and the drawings show both, and the second drawing is called the true drawing. A new building looks like a mistake — an unfinished, aggressive, angular thing — and everyone is patient with it.

## The stroke

The city's power is the Sill's breathing.

Fourteen kilometers of salt and glass, nine hundred kilometers across, warming by day and cooling by night. The slab rises and falls a few centimeters. Across its length it strains — meters of horizontal motion, every day, out and back, in a cycle so slow and so vast that no one perceived it at all until there were instruments to perceive it with.

The old engines are anchored across that strain. Two abutments, a kilometer apart, sunk into the salt; between them a train of levers and toggles and a compression member as thick as a house, and at the far end a screw that advances one thread per day. The engine takes a single stroke in twenty-four hours. It has no sound. It has a pressure, which you feel in the sinuses at the tenth hour if you stand in the gallery, and which the old-timers claim to feel in their knees anywhere on the plateau.

The stroke winds the accumulators — hills of stacked weight, and after them the cells. The cells are sodium and sulfur, both drawn out of the same salt the city stands on, ceramic-cased, buried in the warm ground in fields of ten thousand, running hot, humming at a pitch just at the edge of hearing. From them the wires go out to the kilns and the cracking stills and the lamps, and the whole industrial apparatus of a continent drips steadily out of a motion you could not see if you watched it for an hour.

Above the kite meadows, the harvest towers hold their filaments into a sky with an enormous fair-weather field, and take a little of that too. The filaments sing faintly. The animals resent them.

## What lives here

Abundantly. Sparsely. Both are true: the Sill holds something on the order of forty million animals and you can walk a day and see nine.

The grazers are low and wide and dry-shelled, plated in a lacquer they secrete and renew, and they lick the rind. A mouthful is a century of growth. So they move — three meters a day, four in a good season — and a herd's passage is not a movement you can watch but a *stain*, a band of paler rock a few kilometers wide that creeps across the plateau over a decade and takes sixty years to grow back. From a tower you can see three or four such stains at once, at different stages of healing, and the ward maps mark them the way other places mark flood plains. The herds have right of way. The white line bends around them. There is a body of law about this that is older than the refining industry and has never once been amended.

Kite-moths ride the field: palm-sized, trailing a filament four meters long, charging themselves at the gradient and gliding on the thermal without a wingbeat. At dusk they hang in their thousands over the seep-heads and glow a faint sulfur-blue where their filaments leak. They foul the harvest towers constantly. They are counted in the census as livestock, and the tower crews cut them loose with a hooked pole and a certain amount of muttered apology.

In the pipe-yards, the tenders. Nobody remembers what they were for. They were maintenance units, self-replicating, and they went feral somewhere around the ninth century of the Long City and slowed down and now they graze on scale and rust and breed in the culverts, laying ceramic eggs. They have territories and mating seasons and a parasite. The census does not distinguish them from the other fauna, and the proposal to distinguish them was defeated, and the arguments for defeating it were good.

And there are the slow ones: animals the size of a cart, metabolizing on a decade-scale, which stand in the open for eleven or fourteen years at a stretch and are, by every practical measure, statuary. The road is surveyed around them. Children put offerings in the folds of their shells. Occasionally one moves, and the ward records it, and the record is a public holiday.

## Quiet

Sound is expensive here and the air is thin and the dust eats it. The Sill is one of the quietest inhabited places there is. The engines are below hearing. The animals speak in charge and in scent-oils and do not vocalize at all; the refineries run closed; the towers have no bells.

What there is: the corona hiss from the kite fields, continuous, like the sound of a very large empty room. The tick and ping of glass cooling at dusk, all across the plateau at once, a hundred thousand small contractions. The dry rattle of a grazer's plate against stone, once, far off. And at night, when the inversion sets and the air layers itself into a duct, sound goes strange and travels: a hammer in Ward Nine arrives sixty kilometers away, clear as a knock at the door, four hours after it was struck, and everyone in the receiving ward knows what it is and does not remark on it.

The bells are felt on the arms. When the ward calls the hour, it discharges a mast, and the hair rises on every forearm within two kilometers, and that is the sound of the city telling you the time.

## The pace

The census runs every eleven years. A lawsuit takes a generation and is considered to have been resolved briskly if it concludes in one. The standing storm — a dust vortex eight kilometers tall that has occupied the same coordinates for six centuries, turning once a day, entirely silent — is on the maps as a landmark, and there is a ward whose entire economy is the slow harvest of the fine fraction it drops.

Nothing here is in a hurry, because nothing here needs to be. The uplift matches the ablation. The rind matches the wind. The stroke matches the sun. The herds match the century. It is a machine with no fast parts, running at blood heat under an ordinary light, and it has been running long enough that its inhabitants no longer experience it as a machine at all, but as a temperament.

At dusk the shadow crosses the Sill from east to west at the speed of a slow aircraft, and the lamps come on behind it, ward after ward, a line of small warm points strung out across nine hundred kilometers of finished rock, each one forty kilometers from the next, each one alone in a great deal of dark. Below them the ground goes on giving off the day's heat. Above them the kite-moths are glowing. Somewhere out in the middle distance a stain the color of old bone is moving three meters, and will move three more tomorrow, and the white line goes around it.
