## The Room

It is a bowl of sawn basalt, two hundred and forty paces across, with a roof on it.

The roof stands on columns at thirty-eight meters, iron trusses and wired glass and long panels of plate steel, and under it hangs a permanent weather: warm rain, four millimeters an hour, falling straight down without wind. It has fallen at that rate since the district was commissioned. It will fall at that rate tomorrow. The rain is not an event here. It is a material, like the stone, like the iron. Children are taught to think of it as a ceiling that is slowly arriving.

The floor is a sheet of water twenty millimeters deep, moving so slowly that the movement is only visible where it combs the algal film into hairlines. People walk on raised iron grids and on stepping blocks left proud of the cut. The blocks are spaced for a normal stride, and every one of them still shows the drill scars, white and crisp, eighty years after the saw crews left.

---

The land here came up recently and was cut immediately. That is the first thing to understand about the stone. There is no soil in the Room, no talus, no rounded cobble, no rotted shoulder on any arris. The walls are quarry faces, six stories of them, and the wedge marks go down them in regular vertical stitch like buttonholes. The corners are corners. Under the water film the chisel work is sharp enough to cut a palm.

It stays sharp because nothing here cycles. Weathering needs variation — wet then dry, hot then cold, salt crystallizing and dissolving, the small daily violence of expansion and contraction. In the Room the temperature holds at forty-one and a half degrees, the humidity holds at saturation, the water chemistry holds, the pressure holds. There is no gradient in time. The rock has been given nothing to work with. It is the newest-looking old thing in the world.

The only stain in the district is a rust streak on the north face, four fingers wide, from a weeping bolt in the year the bearing went. It is kept. It is pointed out to apprentices. It is the closest thing the Room has to a ruin.

---

Light comes down through the wired glass in a green-gray diffusion, filtered by a film of condensate and dust that is washed off at a fixed rate and therefore is always the same thickness. There is no noon. There is no direction to the light; shadows are soft smudges directly beneath things, and faces are lit from the water below as much as from the glass above, which makes everyone look slightly drowned and slightly holy. Along the walkways, eighty-eight sodium lamps burn at constant voltage, and the amber they throw does not so much illuminate as replace the color of everything. Red lead paint reads as brown. Brass reads as brown. Blood reads as black.

Each lamp has one gecko. Not two. The insect yield of a lamp at this brightness supports exactly one, and the geckos have settled the matter among themselves with the finality of arithmetic. Eighty-eight lamps, eighty-eight geckos, at the same height on the same side of each housing, heads down, waiting.

---

The Room is a condenser. That is its whole purpose and its whole economy.

Hot water comes in through the intake main from works elsewhere — nobody here has seen them — and is spread across the upper galleries to run down the weeping faces in sheets. Steam arrives overhead and meets the cold pipe-lattice under the roof and collapses into rain. The rain falls, is gathered in the floor sheet, drains to the sump, and is pumped out cold. What the district sells is the difference between what comes in and what goes out. What it sells is *collapse*: the sudden shrinking of steam into water, and the pull that shrinking makes.

They call that pull the draw. It holds the fog lid at its fixed height beneath the trusses. It makes every door in the Room swing to with a soft suck and a click. It is why the stair up to the rim pops your ears at the eleventh landing, and why visitors get a headache behind the left eye and leave.

Three pumps run. Three pumps have always run. The gauge needles stand at their marks with the stillness of painted needles, and the logbooks are columns of the same figure repeated down the page, hour after hour, shift after shift, in fourteen different hands.

The work here is not to make anything. The work is to subtract, every shift, exactly as much as the world adds.

---

Every drip has a cup.

This is the detail that visitors remember. The roof's condensate does not fall at random; the plate is ribbed and pitched and the runnels are known, and beneath each terminus a basalt cup was carved to receive it before the machines were ever started. Eleven thousand cups, mapped, numbered, spaced across the floor and the galleries and the sills. The rain has been landing in the same cups for eighty years and the cups are not worn, because the water is distilled and carries nothing, and because each cup is lifted out on a rota and reground to gauge and put back before wear can show.

The cups are eternal. No individual cup is.

Where a runnel was miscalculated — there are nine such places — the water falls onto open stone and makes a sound unlike the others, a flat slap instead of a bell. These nine spots are named. They are used as landmarks. *Turn left at the Slap.*

---

It is loud.

The rain on the floor sheet is a hiss that fills the whole volume, a broadband wash with no top and no bottom. Under it the pumps beat fifty-two strokes a minute, slow enough to feel in the sternum before you hear it, and the transformer bank hums its hundred cycles, and the outfall roars in the south channel where the cold water leaves, and the belts slap, and the swifts scream, and four thousand four hundred and twelve frogs rasp from their stations on the weeping walls.

The frogs are counted by ear. There is a woman who has done it for thirty years, who walks the north gallery at the same hour of every shift with her head tilted, and who is not counting so much as listening for a hole in the cloth. She has found nine.

Because the machines never change pitch, the interference pattern never moves. The Room is full of standing waves, and therefore full of nulls: places where the roar folds against itself and cancels, and you can hear a person breathing. They are fixed. They are surveyed. They are the most valuable real estate in the district. The tenements are built on nulls, the sleeping warrens are cut into nulls, the school sits in a broad soft null near the west wall where a teacher can speak without shouting. Property is inherited by null-frontage. A quiet lot is a lot with silence on it, and silence here is not an absence but an address.

Children learn the nulls before they learn to read. They stand in them to whisper secrets, one foot apart, and step sideways into the roar to become invisible. There is a null under the third stair where three people can fit, and a flute is kept there, wrapped in oilcloth, and on the eighth shift someone plays it, and the music does not carry past the boundary of the node, and this is considered the correct way to make music.

Everyone over forty is deaf in the high register. The town talks with its hands as much as its mouth, a fast clipped sign language of maintenance — *bearing, seal, drift, level* — and vowels are shouted while consonants are shown.

---

Vegetation is a rumor. There is a green-black diatom film on every wet surface, and where the hot seeps come through the east wall there are mats of white sulfur bacteria, thick as felt, stinking faintly of struck matches. These are the pasture. There is glasswort in the grit seams, a liverwort crust on the shaded gallery, moss in the drain mouths, and seven volunteer figs that the drainage crew cuts back on a fixed rota so that each is always the same two-meter bush with the same architecture of branches. The figs have been the same size for six decades. They are, in effect, permanent saplings, and the crew that prunes them speaks of them with the exasperated tenderness of people who keep a relative alive.

But the animals are everywhere, and the reason is that the food web does not run on the light. It runs on the mats and the film and the seep, and on the flour that spills at the storehouse.

Midge larvae in the film, midges in the air in a haze that never thickens and never thins. Frogs on the walls at intervals of just under a meter, each at its own drip cup, territorial spacing so regular that from the rim the north face looks studded. Thirty-nine grey herons stand in the floor sheet at thirty-nine fixed stations, twenty paces apart, motionless in the rain, and they are the sparsest-looking abundant thing in the Room — a whole population distributed like furniture in an empty hall. Bats come off the trusses in a steady trickle, one every few seconds, all shift long, because there is no dusk to release them at once. Swifts work the glass panels. Rats run the warm mains at a fixed density per hundred meters. Eels hold in the outfall channel where the cold water leaves, and land crabs — descendants of animals caught here when the seabed lifted — work the drain mouths and eat whatever the drains bring.

Mosquitoes are a tax. Everyone pays it at the same rate.

Nothing here blooms, swarms, migrates, or crashes. Individuals die and are replaced at exactly the rate of dying. The pattern does not move. The Room's word for this is *level*, and it is used the way other places use *well* or *fine*. *How is it?* — *Level.*

---

Six hundred and eleven people live in a space that could hold six thousand. That is the other meaning of the emptiness: between the three pump houses and the six tenements and the storehouse and the hall there are long wet reaches of nothing, floor sheet and stepping blocks and cups and herons, and the noise going through it all like weather through a field. The buildings are massive and few. The pump hall alone would take a cathedral inside it and has three machines in it and a great deal of amber air.

Their history is a ledger called the Departures: every occasion on which a needle moved. A bearing in the year of the rust streak. A missing frog, eleven shifts. A roof plate that sweated wrong for a morning and was re-pitched. Three hundred pages in eighty years. It is read aloud on the anniversary of the commission, all of it, in the school null, and it takes most of a shift, and children fall asleep against their mothers during the long middle sections where nothing happened.

They do not think of themselves as living outside time. They think of themselves as living inside a waveform. The shift bells ring, the crews change, the cups rotate, the frogs die and are replaced, the fig is cut and grows back to the cut — all of it returns, exactly, and returning is not the same as changing. *Nothing here happens once.* They say it the way you'd say a fact about the weather.

---

At the rim there is a fence and a gate and the last landing, where your ears clear and the air outside is dry and moving and startlingly cold at thirty degrees. From up there you can see the whole Room at once, the fog lid lying under the trusses like a held breath, the lamps in their grid, the herons at their stations, the rain arriving.

Away along the coast, beyond the edge of what this place is, there are other bowls with other roofs, and each one has its own fixed chord — a different pump beat, a different hum, a different frog. On still nights the rim guard can hear the next one, four kilometers off, sounding its single unwavering note.

Down below, a cup is lifted out and a reground cup is set in its place, and the rain finds it without interruption, the way it found the last one, the way it will find the next.
