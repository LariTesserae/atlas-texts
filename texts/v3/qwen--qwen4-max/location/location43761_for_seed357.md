## The Yard

From the rim it looks like a plain that has been swept.

That is the first wrong thing. You come up out of the haze through a thousand meters of thinning air, expecting the usual alpine mess — scree, sod, the slumped shoulders of old mountains — and instead the ground opens out flat and pale and *tidy*, running to a horizon that curves visibly, and the light on it is ordinary daylight, cloud-shadowed, unremarkable, the same light that falls on any field anywhere. Nothing in the sky warns you. The sky is the least interesting thing here.

The plain is warm. Not sun-warm. It is warm the way a compost heap is warm, and it is warm at dawn, and it is warm in rain, and if you dig your hand into the gravel you pull it out again at once. The heat comes up from underneath. Somewhere below the surface the ground is digesting itself at sixty, seventy degrees, and the whole massif breathes that heat out through its skin in a slow shimmer that makes the far towers wobble like things seen across a road in August.

The towers are the second wrong thing. There are perhaps forty of them in view, and they are kilometers apart, each standing alone in a wide cleared circle of hard pale ground, and they are unmistakably *structures*. Legs. A gantry. A long cantilevered arm with a hanging bundle at the end. A mast with cable stays running down to anchor blocks. They are the shapes of a shipyard, a quarry, a building site — and they are alive, and there is nothing else alive within a kilometer of any of them, and there are no workers, and there is no ship, no quarry face, no building.

Everything here is made. There is no natural substrate anywhere on the plateau. Even the ground.

---

The gravel underfoot is all fresh fracture. Not one rounded pebble in a hundred square kilometers — no river has ever touched this material, no beach has ever rolled it. The sand is angular enough to open your palm. It is pale green-gray where it is new and bone-white where it has cured, and it is laid down in *courses*: you can see the bond pattern in the low scarps, a running stagger like brickwork, changing dialect across the plain, herringbone in the north flats, a rough random rubble toward the eastern slump country.

This is made ground. The plateau is a spoil heap that has become a mountain. Generations of structure have been raised here, loaded, failed, been broken up and re-laid, and the accumulated debris has compacted under its own weight and cemented with silica until it lithified into a massif — a made geology, a hill of leftovers, and it is *young*. It is still being added to. Its edges are raw. Its scarps stand at angles that no weathered slope would hold, and they let go without warning, and the sound of them letting go is the sound of the place.

There is no soil. Soil is manufactured here, in shallow troughs, by encrusting mats that break cured stone down into fines and mix it with their own dead tissue; it is a product, carted about in bladder-sacks, used sparingly as mortar filler and as bedding for seedlings. You will walk a whole day and never once put your foot on dirt that simply happened.

---

The materials, if you go close:

**Green stone.** Freshly precipitated, still full of water, translucent at the edges like a fingernail. The plants here pull dissolved silica out of the hot groundwater and lay it down in their cell walls until the wood stops being wood and becomes a kind of agate-lignin composite. It is heavy, it is stiff, and it thuds when struck. It cures over about eleven days into —

**Cured stone.** White, faintly ribbed with the ghost of the grain, hard enough to spark a boot nail. It *rings*. You can hear the age of a wall by the note it gives back, and the walls are constantly being tested this way, because the weeping runs down them in fat drops and every drop is a small hammer. All day, everywhere, the plateau taps itself and listens.

**Mortar.** A pale mucilage, extruded warm, smelling of hot syrup and cut grass. It sets to a glassy silica glue in a few hours. It is soluble in hot water. This is the central fact of the world and everything else follows from it.

**Cable.** Tension wood, laid in long helical bundles, wet, glossy, contracting as it dries. A stay cable here can pull an anchor block out of the ground if the ground has been rained on. The gantries build by *pulling*: they grow a member soft, tension it against something cured, and let it lignify under load, so that every finished structure is pre-stressed, humming, storing its own weight.

**Sacrificial scaffold.** Cheap, fast, sappy growth, thrown up in a night, meant to be dissolved. Half of any structure you look at is scaffold and you cannot always tell which half.

---

It rains here every afternoon, hot, brief, violent, and the rain is the demolition crew.

High ground, hot ground, thin cold air above it: the plateau drives its own convection. Cumulus builds from mid-morning like something being poured upward, and by three the whole sky goes to work, and warm water falls on a landscape whose mortar dissolves in warm water. Every rain removes a few percent of every joint on the plain. Nothing here rots — nothing here is even eaten, there is nothing to eat it — things simply *dissolve*, and lose their preload, and go.

So the loudness. It is a loud place, and none of the loudness has a voice in it.

Water hammer in the trunk-conduits, a deep knocking boom that travels kilometers through cured stone and comes up through your feet before you hear it. The rifle-crack of a stay letting go, followed by the long tumbling clatter of the arm it was holding. The hiss and roar of a steam vent where a slump has opened the hot interior, a white column standing straight up in the still air until the wind takes it. The grinding sigh of a course settling. And underneath all of it, the drip-tap-tick of a hundred thousand square meters of wall being sounded by its own condensate, a noise like rain on a tin roof that continues for hours after the rain has stopped, because the walls weep long after the sky is clear.

There are no birds. There are no insects. Nothing chirps, nothing calls, nothing answers. The plain is as loud as a city and there is not one intention in the sound.

---

Water is near the surface everywhere, and it is hot, and it is the medium of every process here.

The cleared circles around the towers are not plazas; they are catchments. Each gantry draws from an enormous perched lens of groundwater through a stilt-root mat that spreads under the hardstanding for hundreds of meters, and the spacing of the towers — that extraordinary emptiness, that one-per-horizon loneliness — is simply the water budget. Two gantries cannot drink from the same lens. So the plain is mostly floor, and the presences on it are few and huge and far apart, and you can stand between four of them and see all four and feel that you are standing in an empty room the size of a county.

The floor is kept clear. Mats sweep it, drag debris to the mixer troughs, re-lay the hardstanding. It is not barren emptiness. It is *cleared* emptiness, maintained, and the maintenance is visible: fresh screed lines, a patch of green stone curing darker than the rest, a straight drainage channel running away to a sump with the precision of something surveyed.

The drainage is rectilinear where it is new. It fails into meanders within a season, and the meanders are cut straight again. There is no river on this plateau that has been allowed to become a river.

At dawn, fog condenses on the cold upper arms of the gantries — up there the air is thin and the night has been genuinely cold — and runs down the cables in threads and is caught in cupped bracts and fed back into the trunk. The towers irrigate themselves out of the sky. On a good morning a single mast will harvest a ton of water before the sun burns the fog off, and you can hear it coming down, a soft continuous rushing inside a structure that otherwise only knocks and cracks.

---

No animals means no pollinators, no dispersers, no grazers, no burrowers. Everything that would elsewhere be outsourced to a body with legs is done here by pressure.

The seed pods are ballistic. They fire a hardened seed a quarter of a kilometer with a report you feel in your sternum, and the seeds land point-first and drill themselves into the hot mud of a fresh slump, and this is why the plain is loud in the dry intervals: it is being sown, at intervals, by artillery.

Pollen is moved by water jets and by the fog, and by the pressure fronts of the storms, and the air in the afternoon is thick with it, a faint sulfurous yellow haze that films the wet stone and cures onto it and becomes, in eleven days, part of the pavement.

And the gantries *walk*. Slowly, hydraulically, on stilt roots that lift and set and lift again — a few meters an hour, which for a plant is a sprint. They uproot when their lens runs down, they cross the floor trailing a wake of hot water and torn hardstanding, and they re-found themselves elsewhere, and the hole they leave fills with steam. If you watch one for an afternoon you will see it move perhaps twenty meters and re-tension one stay. If you come back in a month the skyline will be a different skyline.

Nothing on this plateau is older than a season. The *pattern* is old. The bond styles, the spacing, the sequence of raise-load-fail-quarry-relay — that has been running long enough to build a mountain.

---

Here is the thing you notice on the third day, when you have stopped flinching at the cracks.

The yard keeps its holes.

There is a doorway standing alone on the western flats: a frame, a lintel, a threshold, perfectly finished, with no wall on either side and nothing beyond it but more floor. It is maintained. The mats sweep the threshold. When the slump country encroached on it last month, the frame was cut out whole, carried, and re-set on new hardstanding two hundred meters east, and the wall — there was never a wall — was not replaced.

There is a stair near the northern scarps that spirals up nine meters around a column of empty air and stops. There are rooms: rectangular volumes of clear space, sharply bounded, kept clear, re-cut after every rain, while everything around them is broken up and carted off and re-laid somewhere else. The structures come and go. The voids persist. The voids are *surveyed*.

You start to read the whole plain as formwork. All that mass, all that labor, all that hot stone rising and ringing and falling — and what survives it, what is preserved and defended and re-established with such patience, is a set of shapes made of nothing. The mountain is the mold. The mountain is being poured around something that has not arrived, or has already left, or was never a thing at all but only a dimension the plants keep faithfully because keeping it is what they do.

You can stand in one of the kept rooms. It is warm. It is loud. Nothing is watching you. Nothing here has ever watched anything. There is no predation on this plateau, no bite, no venom, no hunger of any kind — the only way to die here is to be under green stone when the preload goes, or to put your hand in the wrong trough, or to stand on a hardstanding crust above a steam void and have it take you. All the danger is structural and thermal. All the violence is masonry.

At dusk the light does nothing special. It goes gray, then blue, then dark, ordinary as a field. The towers keep working. The dripping keeps sounding the walls. Far off, something a kilometer tall lets go of a load it has been holding for eleven days, and the boom crosses the floor and arrives in your feet, and a little later in your ears, and the plain answers itself in a rolling series of failures that will still be going at midnight, and by morning there will be new white courses laid in the running bond, sharp-edged, unrounded, warm to the touch, smelling faintly of hot bread and wet chalk.

The massif is a hand's breadth higher than it was last year. It is not finished. It has never once been finished. That is not a failure of the place. That is the place.
