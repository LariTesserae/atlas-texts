Nothing here is rounded. That is the first thing, before the green, before the noise: the stone is new. It came up out of the ground recently enough that its fractures still have edges you could shave with, and the talus at the base of the escarpment lies in flat shards like a dropped crate of glass, unweathered, unsorted, no clay in it, no rust, no soil. The peaks along the northern rim are sharp as knapped flint. There is no dirt in this valley. There has never been dirt in this valley. Whatever else happens here, the mineral world was born yesterday and has been kept that way.

The valley is high — a plateau basin lifted well above the country around it, so that from the rim you look down on weather instead of up at it, and the air is thin enough to make the light look scrubbed. It is nonetheless warm. Blood-warm, thirty degrees, and it is thirty degrees at noon and thirty degrees in the dark, and the stone under your palm is thirty degrees, and the water is thirty degrees, and this constancy is not comfort after a while but a kind of pressure, the feeling of being inside something with a metabolism.

And it is made. All of it. Not settled, not cultivated — *made*, floor to skyline, with no natural surface left anywhere to stand on.

The floor is a mat: a continuous living tissue laid over the raw rock in a single sheet, kilometers of it, dark green shading to bronze where it thickens, quilted into hexagonal panels each about the size of a threshing floor. The panels are not growth patterns. They are tiles. They have been fitted, trimmed, caulked with a translucent lacquer that the mat secretes from its margins, and where two panels meet there is a seam you can put a fingernail into, and beneath the seam a bead of clear glue that has been sitting there, wet, for longer than there have been eyes to notice. The mat has laid itself over every shard of the new talus, wrapping the glass-edged stones like a hand closing around a knife. It has paved the escarpment. It has paved the streambeds and then abolished the streambeds. Walk anywhere in this basin and you are walking on a floor that was installed.

It is wet underfoot. Not squelching — the mat holds its water an inch down, a perched table, and each hexagon is very slightly dished, so the surface carries a film that never pools and never dries, and your footfalls print dark and fade in four seconds. The escarpment weeps. It weeps from a thousand pores in the green skin drawn over it, and the weeping is caught: every trickle runs into a groove, and every groove is a leaf folded lengthwise and fused along its lip, and the grooves feed stems, and the stems go up.

They go up to the works.

This is what the valley is for, and this is why it feels empty despite being covered completely. The mat is total; the *presences* are sparse. Across a basin you could walk for a day, there stand nine — I have counted nine, from the rim, and the local arithmetic counts nine also — nine enormous made structures, spaced so far apart that each has a horizon of its own, plain green floor between them, nothing in the intervals but seams and film and light. Coverage without clutter. A paved emptiness with a few colossal things in it.

Each work is a plant, or was; the word stops helping at this size. They are three hundred feet of braided trunk and tensioned cord-wood, grown to a drawing. Buttresses arch out and root into the mat's reinforced pads. Between the buttresses hang curtains of woven aerial root, plaited in patterns that repeat at four scales, and the plaiting is not decorative, it is *load-bearing*, you can see the force running through it the way you can see it in a suspension bridge. Hollow stems the width of a wagon run from the ground up the flanks and over the crowns and down again: aqueducts, siphons, standpipes. Water climbs them. It climbs three hundred feet in the dark inside a stem and comes out at the top into a basin of fused leaf-slate and falls from there in a white rope down the outside of the work, and the fall is caught at the bottom in a paved catchment, and goes back into the ground, and climbs again. Nothing leaves. Nothing arrives. The same water has been going up and coming down since the stone was sharp.

And every work is unfinished. This is not decay and it is not ruin. Scaffolds of living cord-wood still cling to the upper courses, tied off with knots that have not been cut. There are joints half-knit, two members reaching toward each other with a hand's-breadth of gap and a bridge of pale wet cambium spanning it, glistening, always about to close. There are buds the size of doorways, swollen, split at the tip, showing a fold of something inside that has been about to emerge for the whole life of the valley. Nothing has been abandoned. Nothing has been completed. The works are being built at exactly the rate at which they are unbuilt, and the gap in the joint is the same gap it was, and the scaffold is not temporary, it is a feature, it is load-bearing too.

The noise is enormous.

You hear it from the rim as a single sound and only inside the basin does it come apart into its parts: the roar of nine falling ropes of water, the deep organ drone of the standpipes at resonance, the tick and crack and long groan of wood under permanent tension, the hiss of the escarpment's thousand pores, and under all of it a low continuous grinding hum from the mat itself, which is the sound of a hundred million cells replacing themselves at the exact rate of their own wearing out. It is loud enough to have to lean toward a companion. It is loud enough that the film of water on the tiles shivers in standing patterns. And it does not vary. Not by a decibel, not by a pitch. There is no gust because there is no wind — the air is still and hot and saturated and moves only in the slow convection the works themselves generate, rising along their warm flanks. The loudness has no weather in it. It is a held chord, and after an hour you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the medium you are standing in, and after three hours you understand, in a way that is difficult to argue with, that this is where the order goes.

Because a place this made, this maintained, this perfectly held, ought to be paying for itself somewhere. Elsewhere the payment is silt: the hill wears down, the delta builds up, the leaf falls and becomes soil and the soil becomes hill. Here nothing accumulates. There is no litter layer. There is no rot, no compost, no sediment in the catchments, no stain on the leaf-slate, no rounding of the shards under the mat. Every gram that comes loose is taken back into a stem within the hour. The books balance to the cell. And the debt is settled as noise and heat — as that grinding chord and that unmoving thirty degrees, poured out continuously into a sky that carries it away. The valley is loud because it is tidy. It is warm because it is finished, or because it is not.

The living things that are not the floor and not the works are few, and each is a function with a body around it.

There is a grazer, pale and long-legged, with a mouth like a curved rasp, that walks the seams. One per interval, more or less; you may go half a day without seeing one. It shaves the mat's margins to specification, and the shavings go into a pouch under its jaw and are pressed back into the seam as glue, and it is not eating, or not only eating; it is doing the pointing on a brick wall. There are flat translucent worms in the catchments that find leaks and lie down in them and become the seal. And there are moths — brown, palm-sized, ordinary-looking, the least strange thing here — that visit the flowers.

The flowers are open. They are open on every work, at every level, wide and gold and scenting the hot air like warm fruit, and they have been open forever, and they are sterile. Nothing in this valley reproduces. Nothing seeds, nothing germinates, nothing arrives from outside; the rim is a wall of new glass and the mat does not permit a foothold. The moths go from flower to flower in circuits they were born knowing, dusting stigmas that will never set, and the flowers refill their nectar at the rate the moths drink it, and this has the shape of a purpose the way a wheel has the shape of a road.

The sun crosses. Light here is ordinary light — good clear daylight, ordinary shadows, a normal blue, cloud forming and unforming over the rim in a way that looks like weather but delivers no rain, because the rain would be new water and the ledger has no column for it. The shadows of the nine works sweep slowly across the tiled floor, and the floor does not track them. No leaf turns. No flower closes. Nothing here has a night.

They call the basin, when they call it anything, the Held Note. From the rim, in the ordinary light, it looks like a green plain with nine cathedrals on it, half-built, roaring, under a sky that goes nowhere. Go down if you like. Mind the seams. Nothing will grow over your footprints, and nothing will erase them either; the mat will simply restore itself to the specification, and the specification does not include you.
