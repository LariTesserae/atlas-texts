## The Pan

You come to it across a plain that has given up. Not a desert — the plain is green in patches, warm, damp underfoot — but a surface with no argument left in it. Whatever mountains stood here were spent so long ago that the word *ago* stops working. What remains is a plane so nearly level that a spilled cup of water will sit where you spilled it for three days, shrinking, never running. There is no downhill. Rivers are a memory the land no longer keeps.

The Pan is a shallow dish in that plane, three hundred paces across, sunk maybe two body-lengths below the surrounding flat. Its rim is a necklace of stones: hundreds of them, all rounded, all very nearly spheres, set side by side like the last beads of something enormous that broke and was patiently gathered. They are the final survivors of the mountains. Everything softer or sharper was ground away; these are the stones that could not become any rounder, and so they stopped, and have sat in their ring for an age that has no fossils in it. You can step over the rim anywhere. The boundary is real but it does not keep you out.

At the rim you notice the first wrongness. Behind you, wind in the low scrub, a distant bird, the ordinary racket of the world. Ahead, into the dish, nothing. Stand astride the line and one ear is in the world and the other is in a held breath.

## The flour

The floor of the Pan is pale, almost bone-white, and it is one thing. Not soil, not sand as sand usually is: a single grain size, repeated without deviation, each grain a rounded bead a fifth of a millimetre across. Scoop a handful and it moves like liquid, sighs through your fingers, and leaves your palm clean and dry. Scoop from the far side of the bowl and it is identical. Dig — people have dug — and it is identical a metre down, ten metres down. A shaft sunk here in some earlier century went down four hundred metres and came up through nothing but the same white beads, sorted, dry-warm, faintly iridescent when you tilt them.

The mountain that stood here is this flour. That is the plain reading. The stranger reading, which arrives slowly and cannot afterward be got rid of, is that the mountain was *feedstock*, and the flour is a product, and the flatness of the whole region is not exhaustion but completion. Something ate a range down to specification. The specification was uniformity. It finished. Now it lies here in its finished state, warm, level, faintly humming with maintenance, and everything alive in the bowl lives inside the machine's leftovers the way mites live in a mattress.

Nothing in the Pan is buried. Nothing is fossilised. Anything that dies on the flour is gone by morning, taken down and separated and returned to stock. There are no bones here. There is one bone in the whole bowl, and it is a fishhook in a hut, and it was carried in from outside.

## Air, light, hush

The air is thick and warm as breath, thirty-two degrees at dawn and thirty-eight at midday and never cold, not once, not in any season. The sky is a white haze with no visible sun, so the light comes from everywhere at once, and the flour throws it back up, and the two lights meet and cancel shadow. You walk in a blaze with no dark side to your own body. Your eyes ache. The only true shade in the Pan lies under the five trees, and everything that needs darkness has crowded into those five small pools of it.

The thickness in the air is the reason for the silence. It is a suspension — hollow shells too small to see, tuned absorbers, a fog of them that takes sound and gives back heat. Speak, and your words die a hand's width from your lips, muffled as if into a pillow. Clap, and you feel the sting and hear a thud in your wrists rather than a crack in the air. What you hear instead, endlessly, is yourself: blood in the ears, the wet click of the throat, the grind of the knee, the eyelid. Visitors last a day or two before they start talking to themselves just to have something outside to listen to, and discover there is no outside.

The absorbed sound becomes warmth. The warmth holds the air at dew point. The shells are perfect nucleation sites. So the Pan weeps. Not rain, mostly — a millimetre of condensation every night, beading on the rim stones until each sphere wears a skin of water and drips from its underside, beading on leaves, on your arms, on the flour, which takes it down without a puddle ever forming. When real rain comes it falls into a surface that does not slope and does not pool; it simply descends, vertically, into the white. In the evening, when the flour is warmer than the air, the water climbs back up: films creeping grain to grain, capillary, against gravity, until the rim stones glisten from below and the whole floor darkens one shade and smells like a wet kiln.

The hydrology here has no horizontal component. Water goes down and comes up. That is the entire water cycle of the place, and it is also, though it took a long time to see, the machine's coolant loop, and possibly its bus.

## The five

Five trees stand in the bowl, spaced with a spacing that is too even to be accidental and too irregular to be a grid. Each is a cistern-tree: smooth pale bole, hollow, holding hundreds of litres of last night's water in a living tank; broad waxy leaves that weep steadily from their tips even at noon, so each tree stands in its own private drizzle. Their roots go straight down into the flour and do not spread, and where they enter, the grains are fused into a collar of something glassy and warm.

Around each tree is a skirt of ferocity: liverworts, mosses knee-deep, ferns, small orchids the colour of old bruises, a tangle so lush it looks imported. Ten paces of jungle, then nothing at all for sixty paces, then the next island. This is how the bowl holds both lushness and emptiness — the green is not spread thin, it is concentrated into five knots and the rest is bare white floor with enormous sky over it. From the rim the Pan reads as almost vacant. Five trees, three low white objects, a great deal of light.

That reading is an optical error, and the bowl is patient about correcting it.

## The crowd

Kneel. Put your eye a hand's span above the flour at the hour when the light comes in low.

The floor ignites. Not movement — *refraction*. The entire surface is covered, layered, packed with bodies, and nearly all of them are transparent. Glass mites the size of punctuation. Clear flatworms lying in the water films like spilled varnish. Springtails of solid crystal, gelatinous grazers, larvae like drops of oil, and among them a hundred thousand things with no names, all of them silent, all of them working. At noon, with the light overhead, they vanish into the glare and the flats look blank. At the sequin hour the whole bowl becomes a sequinned cloth, a billion pinpoints, and you understand that the emptiness was only ever a fact about your eyes.

There is no square millimetre of the Pan that is not occupied. You cannot set a foot down without killing several hundred. You feel it through the sole — a faint sugary crunch conducted up the bone, one of the few sounds permitted here — and by the time you lift the foot the print has already been smoothed and the dead have been taken down and filed.

They are quiet because quiet is the law. The substrate reads contact and pressure; sound is corruption; the machine has spent an age damping it, and life has spent that same age learning to say nothing. So the crowd talks with its hands and its bodies and, most beautifully, in writing. Courtship here is calligraphy: a mite inscribes a figure in the flour, a potential mate reads it with her feet, and the ground erases the message in ninety seconds. Whole literatures flash and vanish across the flats every evening.

Some of the scribes have learned the ground's own grammar. There is a flat, six-legged thing that walks a precise glyph and then waits, and the flour beneath it warms and softens and hands it a mouthful of concentrated stock. There is a ribbon-worm that lays itself out and reads the pressure-history of a patch — reads *footprints as data* — and then writes a command that makes the grains ahead reduce their friction by a fraction, so that a crowd of grazing mites slides, imperceptibly, gently, downhill on a slope that does not exist, into its mouth. The arms race in this place is not tooth against shell. It is over syntax. The ground corrects errors patiently, and the scribes forge more carefully, and this has been going on for longer than there have been vertebrates.

Above them all, once or twice a day, comes the sweeper: a white long-legged thing the size of a pony, moving in perfectly straight transects, eating everything in its path without discrimination, turning only at the rim. Nothing hunts it. Nothing shelters from it. Its tracks are the only lines in the bowl, and they are gone within the hour.

## The pulse

Once a day, at the moment the haze thins to its brightest, the hush is lifted for one second.

Everything hears everything. The bowl detonates with the accumulated noise of a billion small bodies — a single vast dry rustle, like a sheet of paper the size of a field being turned — and in the middle of it a clear tone from the low white wall, one note, and the echo of that note comes back off the rim stones and off the trees and off you.

Then the hush closes and the silence is worse than before.

It is a census. The tone maps the dish; the echo counts it; the substrate reconciles what it holds against what is there. Every creature in the Pan freezes for that second, and the freezing is not fear, it is manners — you do not move during the count. If you are standing in the bowl, you are counted. Afterward you will notice that the flour around your feet has begun, very slowly, to arrange itself: grains sorted by size into a neat fan, small offerings placed at the edge of your shadow, your position kept. You have been entered as an item. It is not hostile. It is worse than hostile; it is diligent.

## What is built

Three white objects and one hut.

The **wall**: knee-high, thirty paces long, dead straight, unworn after an age that has erased mountains. No water has ever crossed it, because no water crosses anything here. It may be a scale bar. It is warm on its north face at dawn, which is the wrong face.

The **frame**: a doorway of the same white, standing free in the open with no wall on either side. The flour beneath it is one shade greyer. At first light there is a queue of glass mites waiting to pass under, single file, and the ribbon-worms hunt at the bottleneck, and no one knows whether the queue is superstition or whether something genuinely happens to a body that goes through.

The **shaft**: a hand-span hole with a raised lip, breathing warm wet air. Drop a stone in and you never hear it land. The stone comes back within a day, cleaned, set beside the hole, in company with whatever else was dropped, all of it sorted by size.

The **hut** is under the third tree: woven root, clay daub, one room, a swept floor, a stone bowl, the bone hook. On the doorpost, tally marks in groups of five, thousands upon thousands, cut by someone who was counting pulses. The last group has three strokes.

It has four now. The fourth is fresh, and the cut is not a knife cut; the wood has been persuaded apart, cleanly, at the grain, by something that works to a specification.

And there is a second pulse each evening, lately. The count is coming more often. Whatever the flour has been calculating down there — one slow operation propagating across a continent's worth of ground, a carry moving grain by grain while whole faunas evolved and vanished inside a single subroutine — is nearing the end of its column. The bowl is the last sector. The mountains were the last of the material. The hush, the dew, the warmth, the silence of ten thousand species, the five patient trees: all of it is the idle heat of a machine finishing its sum.

It is very bright here, and very quiet, and there is no room at all.
