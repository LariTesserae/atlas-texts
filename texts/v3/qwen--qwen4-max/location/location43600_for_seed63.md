## The Throat

Out on the plateau there is nothing to find. It is high enough that the air has been thinned to a rumor, cold enough that the glass gravel rings underfoot like a jar of teeth, and dark in the way only a place with no water and no leaf can be dark — no sheen, no rustle, no smell of rot, because nothing has ever rotted here. The stars are excessive. The ground is a plain of black volcanic glass, spalled into flakes, lying at the angle rest permits, and it goes on in every direction with the patience of a thing that has already finished happening.

You find the place by smell before you find it by shape: a struck match, then hot pennies, then something sweet and oily underneath, like a lamp left burning in a closed room. Then the soles of your feet begin to know. Then the ground stops.

It is a hole. Twenty-two paces across, near enough round that the roundness matters, lipped with a low raised collar of glazed stone the color of old liver. You can walk its whole circumference in under a hundred steps. Beyond that collar: sterility, absolute, in every direction, forever. Inside it: everything.

The first thing you do at the rim is kneel, because heat comes up out of the shaft and lays itself along your face and chest like a hand, and the body wants to answer. The second thing you do is look down, and see nothing, and understand that seeing is not the sense this place was built for.

---

There is light, technically. Threads of blue fire crawl and gutter at the vents in the wall, no taller than a finger, starving in the thin air — sulfur burning where it seeps out and meets what little oxygen the shaft can drag down. The flames give no illumination worth the name. They outline. They make a low blue lacework that shows you the near wall in fragments, a hand's-breadth at a time, and lets the rest stay absolute. Once every few hundred breaths a flame stands up taller and paler and you get one whole second of the shaft's interior, and you will wish you hadn't, or you will spend the rest of your life trying to get it back.

Because the wall is not rock. Or: it is rock now, in places, and was not always.

The wall is animals.

---

They are flat, most of them, palm-sized to shield-sized, shingled, overlapping edge over edge like tiles on a roof, and they cover every reachable hand's-breadth of the shaft from the lip down past where the blue flames stop. Seven deep in the good places. Eleven deep in the seam. There is no gap between them anywhere; there is no *between*. Where a tile dies it stiffens and the neighbors slide over it and it becomes substrate. Where a chip of wall spalls off, the vacancy is filled before the dust has settled — a body flows in from the layer behind and takes the warm face and the vacancy ceases to have existed. You could not, with a knife, make an empty place here that lasted a minute.

Number is not available. You could hold this whole world's territory in a modest field, and it would still not be enough room for what is in it, because it is folded into a cylinder and stacked outward from the heat like the layers of an onion, and every layer is full.

Nothing here flies. The air is too thin; nothing has ever gotten a wing to work in it, and there are no wings, only grips, hooks, pads, the wet-sealing suckers of the flat ones, the many small hands of the long ones that live in the interstices like grout. To let go is to be gone. That is the whole of the local physics, and it is enough to have shaped everything.

---

The heat comes from below, up the core. The cold comes from above, down the annulus, a slow slumping draft of plateau air that hugs the wall and falls. Where they shear against each other there is a sleeve of air at exactly the right temperature — a shell perhaps two body-thicknesses deep, wrapping the shaft, wavering up and down as the vent breathes. That shell is the entire habitable volume of the world. Life occupies all of it. Life *is* all of it. There is nowhere else to be, and so there is no such thing here as an unexploited opportunity, no idle animal, no spare room, no loneliness.

The vent breathes on a cycle of about a hundred heartbeats, irregular, with a hitch in it. On the exhale the seam rises and the whole sleeve of bodies rises with it, one body-width, a rotation so slow you can only see it by choosing a mark and looking away and looking back. This is the turn. The ones against the rock have been feeding on the rock's warmth and are gorged and hot and going pale; the turn brings them out to the cold face to shed, and brings the hungry ones in. Everyone gets the wall. Everyone gets the air. It has been going on long enough that the species have stopped being distinct from the schedule.

They do not speak. Nothing here has ears — there was never a reason; sound in this thin air is a poor carrier and the dark makes it useless anyway. They speak in heat, pressing patterned warm patches against a neighbor's cold face, writing on skin, and the writing fades in four seconds. So: no names, no history, no accumulation, no grudge that outlives a minute, no technology, nothing taught. A civilization of touch with a memory four seconds long, at a density that would make any human city look like a desert.

To them the shaft is not dark. To them it is a roaring furnace of visible bodies, each one glowing at its own temperature, the wall a script of hot and cool, the flames blue and unreadable, the sky above a black nothing where the cold lives. We are the ones who are blind here. We are standing in a lit room calling it night.

---

The threads run in the gutters.

Sulfur seeps from the wall's pores and runs down in filaments, and it is the only liquid in this world, and it is not water, and it is a thermometer. Straw-yellow and thin where the wall is cool. Orange where it is comfortable. Blood-red and ropy where it is hot, and there the threads are strung with eggs like drops of amber, thousands to the hand-span, incubating in the current. Above the red, at the very lip, the threads stiffen and go opaque and crack with a small dry tick, and that tick is one of the four sounds of this place.

Around the vents the vapor lays down crystal: pale flowers, feathery efflorescences, mats and rosettes and branching crusts, ankle-high in the sheltered corners, growing all the time, looking exactly like a garden. It is not alive. It has never been alive. It is the closest thing to vegetation within a thousand miles and it is only weather with good manners. Animals graze the chemistry off it anyway, and shelter under it, and lay eggs in its lee, and the crystal grows through the eggs sometimes and holds them like insects in resin, still warm.

Water is the poison here. Once in a long while a cloud touches the plateau, and for a few seconds there is a frost on the collar of the shaft, and the frost meets the hot sulfur and becomes acid, and it burns. There is a scar on the rim, a pitted arc of eaten stone, and no animal will set a pad on it. That is the total extent of water in this world: a hazard, a stain, a story no one can tell.

---

The sounds, then, in order of frequency: the vent's sigh, which is not really a sound but a change in the pressure behind your eyes. The dry rustle of the turn, like a page. The tick of freezing sulfur. And the fall — a scrape, a scuff, a diminishing series of contacts, then nothing, and no arrival. Nothing has ever heard the bottom.

There is no crying here. Nothing has the equipment for it, and no reason. Which means that in this place sound has only one meaning: *something has come loose.* The vocabulary of noise is entirely the vocabulary of accident. A quiet world, saturated with life, in which every audible event is a small death, and there are perhaps nine of them an hour, and the wall closes over each in under a minute.

The predator commutes. It is a long segmented thing with a sac along its belly full of dry crystals, and it hunts by pressing itself against the tiles and melting its cargo — drinking their heat, which is their food, which is their blood, which is them. The tile under it goes stiff and clear and beautiful. The predator grows heavy with liquid and climbs, up the polished chute it has worn through the wall over an age of climbing, out onto the cold collar, where it lies in the starlight and radiates and re-crystallizes with a faint sound like sugar settling. Then it goes down again. Its lane is the deepest groove in the shaft, a fluted channel a hand wide, and the tiles part before it in the turn the way a crowd parts without ever leaving a gap.

And the dead fall, all of them, always, down the core; and there is something below that eats them, and has never come up, and is known only by the fact that nothing accumulates. The vent brings the smell back up. The loop is closed and has been closed for a very long time.

---

The wall is polished. That is the first thing you notice and it is not quite right. It is *scalloped* — fluted, hollowed in overlapping shallow dishes, each dish the size of a body, each worn by ten thousand generations of the same body in the same place at the same point in the turn. There is no water to erode anything here. The animals are the weather. They have been wearing this shaft to its present shape since before there was a distinction between the ones that feed and the ones that hunt, and the shape is settled now, in balance: on the cold nights the outer layer dies and glazes and the shaft narrows by a hair, and on the long breaths the flux comes up hard and remelts the glaze and the shaft widens by a hair, and the diameter you can span with your arms is the argument between those two, held steady, older than any lineage in it.

Under the glaze, in places, you can see them. Bodies that cooled and went to glass and became architecture. Flesh and stone here are the same material at different temperatures. That is the rule underneath all the other rules.

And at one point on the collar, five fitted blocks. No mortar. Coursed, squared, sized to nothing that lives in the shaft, worn round on their top edges by traffic that no longer exists. And on the inner wall, nine steps, cut down and then stopping — stopping in solid rock, going nowhere, at a rise no tile could climb and no long thing needs. The eggs are thick in their corners. The animals use the steps as a baffle against the cold draft and have no idea they are using anything. Out on the plain there is no other mark of any kind, in any direction, for as far as anything can be seen or walked. Someone came to this hole, once, and put a doorstep on it, and left no other trace anywhere in the world.

---

If you stay at the rim you will begin to be a problem. You are colder than everything. You read as a wound in the seam, a leak, a place where the food is going out. They do not fear you and they do not want anything from you; they will simply begin, without haste and without sound, to fill you in — packing the glassy dead against your boots, your knees, your chest, insulating the cold thing, walling the hole. It takes about an hour. It is not an attack. It is the same thing they do to a spall, a corpse, a fall of gravel, and it works, and afterward there will be no gap.

That is the last thing to understand about the place. It is not crowded because it is fertile. It is crowded because it is the only warm room in a cold empty world, and everything that lives has been pressed into that room, shoulder to shoulder, layer on layer, in the dark, in silence, glowing at each other, turning slowly to give everyone a turn at the wall — and the room is exactly as wide as the balance permits, and it has been exactly that wide for a very long time, and one day the vent will close.
