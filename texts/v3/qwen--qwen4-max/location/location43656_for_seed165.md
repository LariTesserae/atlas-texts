## The Sill

The shelf lies above the cloud floor, a step of pale stone forty kilometers long and nowhere more than nine wide, hung on the mountain's shoulder as though the mountain had been built by someone who miscounted. Below it, the world has weather. Up here there is only the one condition, and it has been the one condition for long enough that the rock has finished answering it.

The stone is fine-grained and faintly translucent, the color of a fingernail held to a candle. Shadows go into it a finger's depth before they stop. It is not a hard stone; it is a patient one. Every surface on the Sill has the rounded, considered look of a thing that has been worked past the point of interest — no fresh fractures, no sharp arrises, no scree that hasn't already found the angle it intends to keep. You can walk for a day and not find a broken edge. Nothing here is being made. Everything here has arrived.

The cold is the cold of a cellar in an unoccupied house. It does not bite, because biting requires a difference, and there are no differences: the wind is the temperature of the stone, which is the temperature of the water, which will shortly be the temperature of you. The whole shelf stands precisely at its dew point and has stood there always. Every surface wears a film of water one molecule thick and then some, a varnish that makes the pale rock look wet-lit even in the gloom. Nothing dries. Nothing drips. Rime grows on the lee faces in feathers the length of a thumbnail, and sublimates from the tips at exactly the rate it accretes at the roots, so the feathers are permanent objects with traffic passing through them.

And it is loud. It is loud the way a place can be wet.

## The Chord

The wind comes from the west at one speed. It has come from the west at that speed since before there was anything on the Sill to notice. It does not gust. Long ago it wore its own channel in the air above the shelf, and now it runs in that channel like water in a flume, and the rock beneath has been bored to match it.

That is what the pale stone is: an instrument nobody built. The wind has drilled it through with tens of thousands of bores — vertical, oblique, some no wider than a straw, some wide enough to put your arm into and feel the note travel up the bone. Between them the surface is fluted into ridges and cups and long combed grooves, all of it tuned by removal, all of it finished. The whole shelf sounds. There is a fundamental you do not hear but stand inside, a pressure in the sternum and the sinuses and the roots of the teeth, slow enough that a single cycle takes about four seconds. Above it sits the roar, which is the sound of a gravel river the size of a valley, except there is no river and no gravel. Above that, a hiss, high and dry and enormous, like the sky's own tinnitus. And between two of the mid-tones there is a beat, a slow throb where they interfere, and the throb is the only thing on the Sill that resembles an event, and it has been happening at the same interval forever.

It is not deafening. Deafening is something that happens to you. Here the ear simply gives up in the first hour, folds its hands, and stops reporting; after that you perceive the chord as terrain, as a substance you are moving through, thick and thin in places, with a grain to it. Loudness on the Sill is not a property of a source. It is a property of a location. You can walk nine steps from a place where a shout would be invisible into a place where you can hear your own eyelids, and the roar has not diminished; you have merely stepped out of where it is.

Sound does not travel here so much as it settles. In the lee hollows, in the cups and the wide shallow basins between the bored ridges, it drifts. These drifts look like nothing at all — a faint granularity in the air, a texture like the shimmer over hot stone, but cold. Put your ear into one and you will hear a rockfall. Not the memory of a rockfall: the rockfall, still falling, deposited here by the wind and held in the hollow the way snow is held, except that this snow does not melt and the falling does not end. Dig deeper and the sounds get older and less sorted. There are drifts on the Sill that contain the noise of the boring of the bores themselves, the sound of the wind making the instrument that the wind then played. The people who built here used to dig them for warmth.

Nothing new can be heard on the Sill. This is the first rule and the one that visitors break first. You may shout; the shout will come out of you as a note the mountain was already sounding. You may strike two stones together and get the mountain's note. The place keeps a fixed inventory and does not accept deposits. It will, however, exchange: leave a sound in a drift and you may take one out, one for one, and carry it a little way before it wears back into the chord. What you carry is never what you left.

## Water That Does Not Fall

The moisture is not rain. Rain would be a change. The water arrives the way the cold arrives — by already being there. It stands in the bores as menisci, convex, domed a finger's height above the rim, and does not overflow. It hangs in the air.

Where the standing wave has its nodes, the air is still, and in the still places the water gathers into beads: droplets the size of a pea, a plum, occasionally a head, suspended in fixed constellations along invisible lines. There are bead-rivers on the Sill, kilometers of them, sinuous chains of hanging water threading the bored stone at knee height, at chest height, at the height of a face. They flow. Each bead passes its water to the next and receives from the one behind, and the whole river runs at a good walking pace while remaining exactly where it is. You can put your hand through one and the water will reassemble behind your wrist without any fuss, in the same arrangement, the same slight cloudiness in the third bead from the left.

The beads are lenses. The sun sits low in the west and does not move — a small hard coin in a sky the color of wet slate, giving light without giving warmth or spread, because the air up here is too thin to scatter anything. Ordinarily that would make the world dim and the shadows absolute, and it does: the loud air is grainy, combed, poorly lit, the visibility of a dusty room. But every bead focuses the fixed sun into a point, and those points have burned shallow cups into the rock beneath them over an unaccountable span of time, thousands of small bright burns arranged in constellations across the pale stone, each in its own scorch of black lichen. Walk the shelf at what would be evening if there were evening and you walk through a fixed galaxy at ankle height.

The shadows are permanent too, and they have been taken into account. Where a boulder has cast the same shadow forever, the rime grows thicker and the fluting runs deeper; the erosion has learned the shadow's shape. In three or four places on the Sill there is a shadow-shaped patch of dark growth and cold stone with nothing standing to cast it. The boulder is gone. The shadow was more durable.

## The Quiets

The nodes are the only places anything lives, and they are also the places where the world comes loose.

Step into a quiet — a pocket of stillness a few meters across, sometimes only the size of a coffin — and the roar stops as if a door had shut. The air clears. You can see for thirty kilometers. It is wetter here, and brighter, and the bead-rivers run through, and the cushion plants grow: pale green pads the size of dinner plates, so slow that a single rosette may be older than the bores, hugging the drip-lines, flowerless, seeding never, dividing once a century. The lichen maps the nodes. It grows only where the pressure is nil, so from a height the whole shelf is drawn over with a fine dark diagram of its own silence, a contour chart of every place the chord does not reach.

And in the quiets, things are not settled on being one thing.

The chord, it turns out, is load-bearing. It is what holds the Sill in a single shape: continuous assertion, everywhere, at all times, a roar keeping the world definite by never letting up. Where it does not reach, the assertion lapses, and objects sit unresolved. A stone in a quiet is several stones. Not blurred — plural, occupying its alternatives all at once, and you can pick it up and it will be one stone in your hand and the others will still be there. The grazer is one animal in a valley and a small herd of itself in the node it stands in, a long-legged pale smear with its ears sealed shut by a fold of skin at birth, navigating by the pressure on its flank hairs, cropping the cushions across nine positions simultaneously. There are perhaps forty of them on the whole shelf. There are perhaps four hundred. Both counts are correct and the discrepancy is not an error in the counting.

In the bead-rivers live the necklaces: translucent things a hand-span long, segmented, moving from droplet to droplet along the node-lines, never touching stone. They are born in a bead, they mate across a bead, they die into a bead, and the bead does not fall. When a quiet's animal dies, the loud air takes the body and grinds it slowly to its final form, which takes about a thousand years, after which it stops and remains, finished, like everything else.

There is one bird. It flies a closed circuit of the shelf in the standing updraft without flapping, wings worn into slots, and it sings one note, tuned so exactly to the chord that you cannot hear it as a separate sound. You know it has passed only because for a moment the beat cancels and the throb in your chest misses, and that miss is the closest thing to news that the Sill has ever received.

## What Was Built

There is not much. A roofless hut of dry-laid stone in a quiet near the eastern bores, its doorway facing the bead-river, its hearth full of rime feathers. Three baffles — curved walls, waist-high, set out in the loud air for no reason anyone can now reconstruct, though if you stand behind one your teeth stop aching. A stair cut into the headwall, forty-one steps, ending in loud air where a node was surveyed and was not. A doorway standing alone in a basin, frame and lintel and no wall, worn round, opening in the quiet onto whatever is needed and never twice the same. Plugs: fitted stones hammered into certain bores, some of them shattered from within, some of them still holding, and around one of them a suspended rubble field that has been in the middle of collapsing since before the ice.

The handprints on the stair are worn to exactly the depth of the surrounding flutes, which means they were made when the boring began, which means either the hands were very early or the wind has been building here as long as it has been eroding, and a hut is only a slow eddy's deposit, and there was never anyone.

Beyond the north wall the couloir runs: an hour of walking that is nine steps long, with a smoothed lip at its threshold and a small delta of gravel spilled below it, deposited by the inconsistency, sorted by size. Further west the scree falls sideways and has built a proper fan against a vertical wall, angle of repose intact, entirely stable, entirely wrong. None of these are fresh faults. That is the thing about the Sill's broken reality: it broke so long ago that it has weathered. The anomalies have their own drainage now. They have found their angle. They are in balance with the wind and the dew point and the chord, and they are no more surprising, here, than the stone.

---

At the rim, the shelf ends in a lip of rounded pale rock, and the chord pours over it. You can see it go: the beads it carries shatter into a white cataract of mist that falls and falls and never reaches the cloud floor, sublimating at the same rate it spills, a permanent silent-looking waterfall of noise. The valleys underneath have always known the mountain hums. They put it down to water.

Stand in a quiet long enough and you will begin to be several. This is not dangerous, exactly. It is only difficult to come back as one, and the one you come back as is chosen from the available versions without much input from you.

Before you go, shout. It costs nothing. The sound that leaves you will be a note the mountain has been sounding since the bores were young, and it will not be added to anything, and you will understand, standing in the roar with your wet clothes and your relinquished ears, that you did not make that noise. You found it. It was being kept.
