## Grade

The ground is warm, and it is finished.

That is the first thing — not the dark, not the buildings, but the flatness, which is so complete that it reads as a substance rather than a shape. You stand on it and the soles of your feet report nothing: no slope, no grit, no give. It runs out from you in every direction at the same value. Far off, at the edge of what the eye can do, it curves down and away, and you understand that you are not in a valley or on a plateau but on the whole upper surface of a world that has been worn to its last possible form. The hills are gone. Not buried — gone, ground to a plane, the way a lens is ground, and then the grinding stopped because there was nothing left to grind with.

There are stubs. Three or four within a day's walk: low knuckles of pale rock, rounded past any angle, so smoothed that they look poured. They come up out of the floor like the tops of things drowned. Nothing here is taller than they are except what was built.

You take it at first for a salt pan, and you are partly right. Under the dust, where the dust has been swept into the shallow ripples it will keep forever, the floor is a grey-white crust, glassy, faintly translucent at the edges of a chip. It is the residue of the last sea, sintered by heat and pressure into a single continuous sheet. The sea is not gone. It is the ground. It has been finished, the way everything here has been finished.

## Two skies

There is no sun. There has not been one for long enough that the word means nothing locally. The world turns so slowly that its rotation is a geological event; the stars are nailed where they are, and they do not twinkle, because the air is too thin and too dry and too still to bother them. They are pinholes. Above you, a long dim band of galaxy lies across the dark like breath on cold iron.

Below, the plain answers it.

The city is lit — sparsely, at intervals of a mile or more, with lamps run far under their rating, filaments held at a dull blood-orange so they will never fail. Each lamp illuminates a single thing: a gauge face, a bearing housing, a graduated scale, the inspection surface of a casting. Nothing else. No spill, no wash, no glow on the underside of anything. So the plain is strewn with small hard rectangles of amber light at ground level, each one alone in a mile of black, and from any distance the pattern is indistinguishable from the pattern overhead.

You walk between two skies. At the horizon they close on each other and there is a seam where neither is.

## The wards

It is a city, unmistakably, and it is empty of everything but itself.

Nine wards, laid out on the plain in a figure that might be a spiral or might be a projection of something with more dimensions than the plain has. Each ward is the same ward. Same gantry, same busbar run on the same ceramic standoffs, same eleven-bay foundry with its roof open to the stars, same rail spur curving away to the same switch. They were not built nine times. They were cast nine times, from one pattern, and the differences between them are the differences between castings: a fillet slightly thicker here, a runner-mark left unground there. Ward Six has a flaw in its foundation raft, a shrinkage void the size of a coffin, and Ward Six-and-a-half has the same void in the same place, and so does Ward Nine.

Between the wards: miles of nothing. Conduit, rail, a low kerb of dressed stone, and the flat. The infrastructure is dense in its organization and sparse in its body — a city's worth of intention spread over a province's worth of emptiness. Everything is connected. Almost nothing is adjacent. You can stand in the middle of Ward Four and see the lit gauge of Ward Five sinking below the curve of the world, and know that between you and it there is not one object.

Nothing is rusty. Nothing is rotten. There is no water on this world and there has been none for a very long time, so the iron is bright where it was machined and grey where it was cast, and the copper busbars are the colour of a new penny under the dust. The paint is intact. The gaskets are supple. Age here does not look like age. It looks like a machine shop at the end of a shift, waiting.

## The Return

The machines are running.

You will not believe this at first, because there is no sound to speak of and no motion you can catch. Stand at the transfer line in Ward Two and watch the leading edge of a bearing race against a scratch on the guide rail. Come back in an hour. It has moved a little more than the width of a hair.

The whole industrial apparatus of this world — the foundries, the sintering furnaces, the long slow broaching machines, the mile of transfer line, the gantries that lift one casting a year — runs at exactly the speed of its own wear. Not faster. There is no surplus anywhere, no warehouse, no stockpile, no product. The city manufactures replacement parts for itself and nothing else, at the rate at which it consumes them, and the swarf and the dust and the rejected castings go back into the charge hopper and become the next year's work. The loop is closed to within a tolerance nobody left alive can state. It has been closed for longer than there have been continents.

This is what the erosion became. When the wind died — when the last thermal gradient flattened out and the air stopped moving — the wearing of the world did not stop. It moved indoors. It became deliberate. The plain reached base level and the city took over the job, and now the only erosion on this world happens in a bearing at four ten-thousandths of a millimetre per year, and is caught in a pan, and is melted, and comes back.

In the tally house there is a counter. Brass wheels, carry gears, a mechanism of beautiful and pointless precision. The digits are gone. Not painted out — worn off, by the passage of carries, the raised numerals abraded flush over so many revolutions that every wheel is a blank disc. They still turn. Somewhere in the building a relay closes, once every eleven days, with a small dry click, and that is the calendar.

## Blood heat

The air is thirty-six degrees. So is the stone. So is the rail, the gantry, the lamp glass, the dust, the underside of the coping, the inside of the conduit. Heat comes up from below at precisely the rate it leaves above, and has done so since before the wards were cast, and the whole surface of the world sits at one temperature with no gradient anywhere in it.

This is why there is no wind. This is why there is no weather of any kind. This is why nothing ever changes: change requires a difference, and the differences have all been spent.

You cannot feel the ground. You can feel its texture, but not its warmth, because it is exactly as warm as you are, and when you lie down on it you neither heat nor cool, and after a while you lose the boundary. Sleep is difficult here. Not from discomfort — from the absence of any signal that you have ended and the world has begun.

The heat is why the dust does not move. It lies where it fell. There are tracks in it, crossing the flat between Ward Three and Ward Seven: a line of depressions, shallow, sharp-edged, spaced like something walking without hurry. They are not yours. They are not recent. They will not soften. In this air, at this stillness, a footprint is a permanent structure, and there are perhaps forty of them on the entire plain.

## Quiet, and what lives in it

The world is acoustically dead. The dust eats echo; every surface is rounded or furred; the thin air carries sound poorly and cleanly, without reverberation, so that a noise arrives dry and close and then is simply over. Shout and nothing comes back. The city's continuous contribution is a hum below hearing, felt in the back teeth, from busbars carrying current to machines that will not draw it down for centuries.

And there are the wicks.

At the base of each ceramic standoff, in the warm shadow, lives a flat blind animal about the size of a tongue. It has no eyes and no legs to speak of and a mouth like a wet file, and it eats graphite. The bearings shed carbon film, atom by atom, and the film creeps along the dust and settles on the insulator glaze, and if it were allowed to accumulate the standoffs would track and arc and the loop would break. The wicks lick them clean. There is exactly one wick per standoff, and their number is set by the deposition rate, and it has been set that way for a very long time. They breed when a bearing fails. A bearing has not failed in nine hundred thousand years.

Once, far out on the flat, something crosses between you and a lamp. It is tall and slow and it stops at the lit gauge, and stands there for a long time with its head at the height of the dial, and then goes on. You will not see it again. Nothing here needs to be seen twice.

## The only weather

You are the wet thing.

There is no water on this world, and you are seventy percent water, and you are breathing out. The salt crust under the dust is hygroscopic; it has been waiting a billion years with an appetite. Each exhalation goes into the air and does not come back, and the floor takes it, and you can feel the plain drinking you at the rate of a mouthful an hour. Your warmth is a fraction of a degree above ambient and it is the only thermal gradient on the planet, and the dust nearest your feet knows it.

Somewhere behind you, out of turn, a relay closes.

A tally wheel advances by one.

And in Ward Eight, a filament that has burned at dull red since before the sea was finished comes up — not much, not brightly, one increment, the smallest amount of light a machine can be told to make — and holds there, and lights nothing at all.
