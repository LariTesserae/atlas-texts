## The Kettle

The road comes down off the rim through snow, in real sunlight, and then it doesn't.

Four hundred meters below the pass the cloud begins — not as a mist you enter but as a surface you pass through, a ceiling seen from above, gray-white and slightly lumpy like a poured floor. The road goes into it. The sun goes out. For the next eleven kilometers you descend through wet gray air that gets warmer with every switchback, until at the bottom the thermometer reads forty-four and the humidity reads everything, and your skin is running with water that is not sweat, because sweat does not evaporate here. It is the sky condensing on you. You have become a cold surface. Down here, everything cooler than the air is a place where water arrives.

This is the Kettle: a caldera floor forty kilometers across, three thousand meters up, sitting on a shallow body of magma that has been politely cooking it for two hundred thousand years. The ground is hot. The sky, just above the lid, is cold and thin and dry. Between them the plateau runs a private weather, a closed loop, the same water going up and coming down forever: rising off the vents as steam, hitting the cold underside of the lid, gathering, falling back as warm rain onto hot stone, and going up again. It has rained here every day since before there was anyone to notice. It has never once been sunny.

The light is a broad even pewter glow with no source in it. Shadows are soft suggestions. Noon is a slightly brighter hour, called false noon, and it lasts about forty minutes.

## What the fog gives you

You hear the city before you see any of it, and what you hear is dripping. Not a patter — a vast distributed ticking, millions of individual drops arriving on stone and iron at slightly different rates, so that the whole plateau hisses like a held breath. Under that, very far away, a compressor coughs. It will cough again in an hour. In between there is nothing, and the nothing carries: the temperature inversion lays sound along the ground like a marble on a table, and a hammer falling in Ward Nine arrives at your ear from twelve kilometers off as clearly as if it fell beside your boot.

Then a shape resolves. A fin: cast iron, four meters tall, a centimeter thick, weeping. Then another behind it. Then a hundred, ranked away into the gray in perfect parallel rows, and you understand that you are standing at the edge of a fin field the size of a parish, and that there are nine such fields, and that they are only the outskirts.

The city comes forward in pieces after that. An arcade of glazed brick, arches sweating. A tenement block six stories high with every window black. A staircase climbing to a landing that supports nothing. A pressure vessel the size of a chapel, riveted, lagged in spun basalt, humming at a pitch you feel in your teeth. Kilometers of pipework on trestles, going somewhere, coming back. A street of workshops with their doors open and their benches clean.

And no one. Not empty as in abandoned — the lamps are lit, the belts are running, the greasers have been through this week, there is chalk on the tally boards and the chalk is fresh. Empty as in *sparse*. Eleven hundred people, perhaps, in a city built for two million. You can walk an hour between two lit windows. The scale of the construction and the scale of the occupancy have come uncoupled, and the construction has not noticed.

## Materials

There is no wood. There has never been wood; nothing green has ever grown on the Kettle. The lowest life here is a varnish — chemolithotrophic films that eat sulfur and iron out of the wet rock, laid down in stains of rust-orange, sulfur-yellow, manganese-black, and a certain deep bottle-green that people mistake for moss and are wrong. Over the varnish, in the cooler runnels, a fungal crust like gray felt, which is harvested, tanned with alum, and made into gaskets, boots, aprons, and the tough pale leather that wraps every pipe joint in the city.

Everything else is mineral. Cast iron, copper, lead, glazed ceramic, cobalt glass. Ropes of spun basalt. Insulation of pumice wool. Mortar of lime. And travertine — travertine everywhere, the pale banded carbonate that the hot water lays down wherever it cools or breathes out its gas, so that the whole plateau is terraced in cream and apricot stone, rounded, lipped, scalloped, every hard edge softened, every stopped thing furred and then swallowed.

That is the first rule of the Kettle, and you learn it from the pavements: **motion resists stone.** A bearing that turns stays bright. A valve exercised weekly stays a valve. A gate left shut for a season becomes a lump in a wall, and after ten years it is a hill with a hint of hinge in it. The city is not eroding and it is not growing. It has been the same mass for a very long time, stone circulating from mouth to terrace to solution to aquifer to mouth again, accretion and dissolution held in a balance so settled that the engineers speak of it as weather rather than as a process.

They fight it with grease. Seep-oil and graphite, black fat, pumped from the caldera's cold margin, distributed through a network of small brass lines that parallel the water mains and are maintained more religiously. Grease is currency here. A ward's wealth is measured in how much of it is still moving.

## The two waters

The ground gives thick water: hot, alkaline, loaded, generous. It arrives at the Nine Mouths at ninety to a hundred and forty degrees and it will build you anything. Masons on the Kettle do not cut stone; they plumb it. Set an armature of basalt rope in a form, run thick water through it for a season, and come back to a wall. Repair a crack by aiming a drip at it. The great terrace fronts of Ward Four were grown in place around iron skeletons and are seamless, banded like agate, and stronger than anything quarried.

The sky gives thin water: distilled by the loop, soft, faintly acid, hungry. It dissolves the terraces, eats the lime out of the mortar, and it is the reason the city has drains.

Thin water does something else, which is the second rule, and the one nobody explains to you until you have already broken it.

Stand still in the fog for an hour and you leave a wet shadow — a dry-ish outline where your body blocked the deposition, surrounded by a rim where it enhanced it. Stand still for a day and the outline sets in lime. The pavements of the Kettle are covered in these outlines. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Pale human shapes at doorways, at benches, at the rail of the overlook, layered over one another like a census taken by rain. Most of them are not deaths. They are just records. People stood there, and the water took an impression, and then the water carried the impression downstream and precipitated it somewhere else, at a lower terrace, in a sump, on the outflow plain — a *cast*, a travertine second of a thing that was once in one place.

The outflow plains are full of them. Ghost staircases. A cast of a lathe, in stone, standing in a field with no lathe within thirty kilometers. Whole façades that were demolished a century ago, re-precipitated at one-third scale in a dry channel. And among them, shapes nobody has ever identified, which is either a failure of the historical record or something worse.

So: keep moving. The city has organized itself around this the way other cities organize around a river.

## The turning

The important buildings rotate.

Not quickly. One revolution a day, on a grease bearing three meters across, the whole tenement or hall or pumping house pivoting so slowly that you can only detect it by watching a doorframe against a fixed mark for a minute. The fin fields pivot to face the sky's cold. Statues turn. Beds are mounted on slow carousels, and children are taught to sleep in the middle. Doors revolve; there is no such thing here as a door that swings. The tram is a continuous loop of cars that never stops, and you step off it at a speed of walking, and it comes around again in fifty-one minutes.

Addresses are therefore times, not places. *Sixth Terrace, at the third hour.* You do not go to a building; you go to when it will be there.

A ward whose turntable seizes is a ward that will be a hill. Everyone knows this. The greasers walk their rounds with the seriousness of clergy.

## Cold is the product

Heat is free here and always has been. Cold has to be mined.

The Draws are the tallest things on the plateau: nine chimneys, the largest three hundred and forty meters, built to punch through the lid and inhale the dry cold air above it. They do not exhaust. They *intake*. Their tops, visible from the floor only when the fog thins, are white with rime ice and lit by actual sun — the only sunlit objects in the world you can see from down here, floating up there in a blue you have to take on faith, above a ceiling of gray.

The cold comes down the Draws and is spent, mostly, on ammonia absorption chillers — heat-driven, no electricity required, banks of them the size of grain elevators, hissing faintly. What they make is the city's true output: dry cool rooms, eighteen degrees, thirty percent humidity, where sweat works and paper survives and a person can sit still without being recorded. Every habited volume on the Kettle is one of these. The rest — the arcades, the fin fields, the ten thousand hectares of magnificent municipal stonework — is uninhabitable in the simple sense that a human being cannot rest in it.

There are also warehouses full of cold. Salt-hydrate batteries, charged and racked, hall after hall of them, sealed, catalogued, waiting for a shipment that has not been requested in living memory. And there are the ampoule halls: thin water, bottled in cobalt glass, six million units, stacked to the vaults. Nobody remembers the customer. The bottling line runs.

## The slack, and the hush

At dawn the loop stalls. For twenty to forty minutes the condensation stops, the drip ceases, the fog thins to a gauze, and you can see the rim peaks standing in gold light forty kilometers away with snow on them and the whole lid lying below like a floor you could walk out on. This is the slack. Everything gets very quiet. Metal ticks as it warms. People come out and stand in the open and look, and they do not stand still, they shift from foot to foot the entire time, out of a habit so old it has stopped being fear.

Then the lid closes and the drip resumes and the day begins.

The other silence is local and it is the one that matters. Somewhere in the fog a patch goes quiet — no drip, no hiss, a hollow of dry cool air perhaps six meters across, smelling sharply of ammonia. Everything wet and hot and exhausted wants to go into that patch. It is the only comfortable place for a kilometer.

It is an animal. It pulls water out of the air endothermically, holds the pocket open for as long as it takes, and closes it when something is inside. There are perhaps four on the plateau. They are the reason the wardens carry drip-masks and listen for holes in the sound, and the reason an ammonia alarm and a predator alarm are the same three notes on the whistle, and nobody has ever bothered to change it.

The rest of the fauna is modest: scutch, silica-shelled grazers the size of a hand, that scrape varnish off wet stone and never stop walking, cannot stop walking, whose still members are found as small perfect casts in the runnels. They migrate around the terraces in loose files, thousands of kilometers a year, at two hundred meters an hour. They are the most numerous living things you will see, and you will see maybe nine in a day.

## The Spill

At the caldera's low notch the fog goes over the edge. It pours out and down, a slow white river of cloud falling three thousand meters into a country nobody from the Kettle visits, and the rain that forms in it carries the thin water and everything the thin water has learned.

Below, in the outwash, is the Second City: every cast that ever went downstream, accumulated over an age, accreted and merged and re-dissolved and accreted again. Towers of pale banded stone in the shapes of things that were once upstairs. It is much larger than the Kettle. It is entirely still. It is the only place in this world where matter has a net direction, and it grows by a millimeter a year, and it is the most complete record of the city that exists — more complete than the tally boards, more complete than anyone's memory, and legible only to someone willing to walk down there and read a hundred thousand hectares of limestone ghosts in the rain.

Nobody has. There isn't the staff.

---

## Night

The lid goes from pewter to charcoal to a black that is not quite black, because the ground shows through it: the glow-cracks, hairline fissures across the terraces, dull red, brightening and dimming with the plateau's slow breath. The mantle lamps come up in their thousands, each one a small hard white star inside a soft globe of illuminated fog, no beams, no reach, just hemispheres of glow hanging in the dark at intervals of two hundred meters, marking out a city whose extent you can only infer.

Somewhere a turntable's bearing complains. A compressor coughs; the cough goes twelve kilometers and comes back off the rim, fainter, a second cough that is not a machine. Ice forms at the top of the Draws and melts at the bottom of them and the difference is the entire economy. The drip continues, everywhere, at the rate of a held breath.

And on the pavements, in the dark, in the warm rain, the water is taking down everything that stopped today, patiently, at a millimeter a year, preparing to make a second of it somewhere lower down.
