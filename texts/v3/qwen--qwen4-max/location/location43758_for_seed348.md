## The Constant Works, in Ossary Sink

You come down out of dry country. The plateau up there is uninteresting — cracked pan, wind, the ordinary business of a place that is still being worn away. Then the ground folds under and there is a rim of grey limestone, lip-worn, and below it a bowl about four hundred paces across, and in the bowl a town.

You can see all of it at once. That is the first thing. It has an edge, and the edge is the rim you are standing on, and there is nothing of it anywhere else. Nineteen sheds, three stacks, a low ring of vats, a pavement. Between the buildings, great gaps of bare wet stone — the place is mostly floor. It looks underfilled, spare, a diagram of a town rather than a town. Then you come down the ramp and the floor turns out to be moving.

---

**The stone.** Basalt setts, fitted so long ago that the joints are only a change in grain. Every sett has been dished. Not randomly: each hollow is the exact arc of a grazing mouthpart, swept ten million times a day for longer than there have been mouths, and the dishing stopped deepening at the depth where the sweep no longer bites. The wear here is finished. It is finished the way a sentence is finished. You can put your palm in one of the dishes and it fits the way a tool fits its handle, and it will fit the same in a thousand years, because whatever the grazing takes off, something else puts back.

The pavement is damp. Not puddled — damp, a black sheen, cool, and in the lowest dishes a standing film of water a fingernail deep with a stained shoreline it has agreed to. Those shorelines are permanent. They are not tide marks. The water in Ossary Sink arrives as seep at the rim and as dew off the undersides of the iron lintels, and it leaves as vapour at precisely the rate it arrives, and the loop is closed, and there has never been a flood here and there has never been a drought. It does not rain. It *drips*, from every horizontal underside, at a fixed interval, a drizzle with no cloud in it — the town's own breath coming back down.

The air is cool. Ten, eleven degrees. It pools; it is heavier than the air above the rim, and it does not mix, and it smells of wet flint, cold iron, and the faint struck-match sweetness of the seeps. Your breath does not quite show. The stone takes the heat of your hand and gives it back slowly, and this is the only warmth in the place that has a beginning.

Above: sky at full ordinary brightness, overcast, no sun disc, the light of ten in the morning. The cloud shapes are stationary. They have the smoothed, settled look of the setts — as though the sky, too, has been worn to its final form and has nothing left to become. There is no evening here. There is no weather to wait for.

---

**Nothing green.** No blade, no leaf, no root, no seed. The primary production of Ossary Sink is a film: white and rust-orange veils in the seams and on the shaded faces, sulphur-eating, stone-eating, cold. In the cool undersides where the dew forms there is a grey felt of threads, fungal or something that has agreed to look fungal. The film is the pasture and the fuel both. The basin burns rock slowly and without flame; the fire is a skin, and it has been alight, in its way, since before the pavement.

The stacks do not smoke. They exhale clean cool vapour in slow pulses, and the pulses are the town's water going home.

---

**The animal.** There is one animal here. It wears several bodies.

In the dishes: **slaters**, palm-sized, shelled in wet slate-grey, six-legged, mouthparts a rasp of mineral teeth. They graze the film out of the stone's hollows in that fixed arc. There are more of them than the pavement has hollows; the surplus stands in the seams in stacked ranks, waiting, motionless, like stock in a rack.

In the joints: **pointers** — pale, blind, slug-bodied, extruding calcite from a gland behind the head. They fill what the slaters and the water and the feet take out. They work to a tolerance you could not measure with your thumb. Where a sett chips, a pointer arrives and lays down new stone in the exact lost shape, and cures it, and withdraws. There is no dust in Ossary Sink. There is no rubble, no midden, no silt, no ruin. Nothing accumulates. That is why the place reads as ancient and settled rather than as decayed: all of its erosion is complete, and its sediment budget is zero.

In the walls of the sheds: **lungs**. Barrel-bodied, the size of a cider keg, seated in iron sockets they grew to fit, breathing in and out on a phase shared with every other lung in the bowl. They are the pumps. They move the seep-water, the air, the vapour, the slurry. There are eleven thousand sockets and eleven thousand lungs, and when one dies a replacement is budded out of the seam within the hour and crawls up the wall and seats itself, and the chord does not falter.

In the air: **the count-flies**, midge-sized, wing-whine like a circular saw two streets away. They are the overwhelming part. They occupy the whole volume of the bowl from pavement to rim in a haze that reads, at distance, as a grey grain in the light, and up close as a solid arithmetic. They fly fixed circuits — through the tally-gates, over the vats, along the seams, back through the gates. They are not swarming. Swarming is a thing with a shape that changes. This is a *standing* cloud, the way a river is standing water, and every fly in it is on a route.

And in the iron galleries: **knockers**, small hard-shelled things that strike the rails with a hammer-limb, keeping phase. They are the metronome of a place with no events to keep time for.

Overwhelming in number; utterly spare in kind. One animal, four jobs, one grey. The saturation of the whole scene is low — grey stone, white film, rust stain, iron, and the same body repeated past the point where repetition means anything. That is the trick of this place: it is teeming and it looks empty. You keep having to look down.

---

**The works.** Nineteen sheds of glazed brick and cast iron, wide-spaced, connected by grooved ramps and pipe-runs and open launders. No doors sized for you. Apertures the width of a hand, sockets, slots, combs. Inside, in cathedral dimness, the machinery: cold reactors lined with white film, wet vats, tally-gates, ribbons.

The product of the Constant Works is a number.

The pointers, under machine direction, extrude thin plate — calcite ribbon, a hand's breadth, endless — and the gates punch it, and the punched ribbon is the census, and the census is read back by the gates, and the reading is compared against the gates' own count of the flies passing through, and then the ribbon goes into the vat and dissolves and comes back as slurry and is extruded again. A mile of ledger a day. Every line identical. The number is nine digits and it does not change and the last digit is a 4 and has been a 4 for as long as there has been plate.

Nothing is stored. Nothing is shipped. There is no road out of the sink and no market at the end of one. The industry exists to confirm that the industry is still correct, and the noise it makes is the sound of confirmation, continuous, at volume.

Because it is *loud*. Not violent — layered. Under everything, the lungs: a fundamental you feel in the sternum before you hear it, somewhere near a low B-flat, thirteen-second cycle. Over that, the wing-whine, a hard bright fifth. Over that, the broadband hiss of ten million rasps, which is exactly the sound of rain on sand and which is why visitors keep looking up for a storm that has never once occurred. Then the gates: click, click, click, three a second, forever. The knockers on the rails. The drip. The stacks. It comes off the rim wall and back down into the bowl and stands there. The town has a pitch. You can hum it, and if you hum it correctly you disappear into it, and if you hum it wrong you feel briefly, physically, like an error.

There is no information in the sound. It says only: *still correct, still correct, still correct.*

---

**The rules, as far as they can be inferred.**

Nothing here is born and nothing here dies; positions are vacated and refilled. There are no young, no old, no courtship, no territory, no hunger that isn't already budgeted. The census is not a count of lives. It is a count of *seats*.

No gradient survives. Heat, water, mineral, number — every loop closes within the bowl. This is not stasis as stillness; there is enormous motion, more motion than in any city you have known. It is stasis as return. Every movement here is the second half of a circle.

The builders are not present. In the tallest shed there is a gallery with a handrail worn to a shine, and a stair with treads dished by feet, and no caste of the animal that fits either. Their sockets are still in the walls, plugged with cured calcite. The town is finished; the moulds for the ones who finish towns are never triggered. Somewhere in the seams there are eggs that will not be called.

---

**And you.** You are a warmth, a water source, an appetite, a thing that will leave. You are the only gradient in Ossary Sink. Your footprints in the film stand out white where the grazing has been interrupted, and pointers are already coming, laying new veil and new stone at the speed of a slow tongue.

At the fourth tally-gate the chord shifts — a quarter-tone, no more, the wing-whine bending to accommodate an extra body in the circuit. For as long as you stand in the bowl the number ends in 5, and the plate says so, and the whole apparatus is briefly, minutely, a different town, working to a different truth.

Then you climb the ramp. Behind you the gate clicks, and clicks, and the fifth is gone out of the air, and the last digit is a 4. From the rim you look back: nineteen sheds, three stacks, the great spare gaps of moving floor, the cloud sitting in its finished shape, the drizzle falling out of nothing. It is exactly as it was. It will be exactly as it is. The only place any of this is going to accumulate is in you, and you are the one thing here that erodes.
