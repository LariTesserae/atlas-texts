Above, there is almost nothing to see. A pavement of wind-polished flint, red-brown, running to every horizon without a hill worth the name; a few low stumps where mountains used to be, worn down to their roots and then worn further. The sky is thin and hard and very high, the air cold enough to crack a lip, and the wind carries grit that has crossed the whole flat without once being stopped. Nothing drinks here. There has been nothing to drink for a length of time the flat itself has stopped counting. This is a landscape at the end of its argument with itself: every slope has been argued down, every drainage has been argued away, and what remains is the last possible surface, patient as a floor.

Walk it long enough and you will find a smudge in the air, a shimmer like heat over stone, though the stone is cold. Follow the shimmer and you come to a crack no wider than a doorway, edged with a crust of something pale and soft — the first living thing in a hundred miles — and out of the crack comes a breath that is warm, and smells of resin and struck matches and old fat.

That is the whole of the entrance. Everything else is below.

---

Down forty feet of notched ladder, through a squeeze that scrapes the shoulders, the cold ends. It ends the way a wall ends. On the other side is warmth like the inside of a mouth, and air so thick with vapor that it has a texture, and a sound that arrives all at once, the way weather does.

The chamber is one room. That is the important thing about it. Not a cave system, not a labyrinth — a single bubble in the limestone, three hundred paces from end to end, ninety from floor to dome, and it holds a forest, a town, a herd, a plague of insects, and a religion. A person could walk the whole world in the time it takes to eat a meal, and never see the sky again except as a bar of light.

The bar comes through the Slot: a fissure high in the eastern wall, too narrow to climb, angled so that for perhaps an hour each day a blade of daylight lies across the chamber's western tiers. It moves. It is the only thing in the world that moves at a reliable speed. Everything else — the seep, the swarms, the Breath — keeps its own time. The rest of the day the chamber lit by its own life: bacterial films on the tar, a low blue-green shine like the inside of a rotten log, and the hanging threads of the lamp-flies, thousands of them, strung from the ceiling flutes, each thread a vertical constellation. It is never bright. It is bright enough to see a face, to see a hand, to find a step. Faces here are read at arm's length, and everyone stands close.

The floor is not stone. The floor is a slow black lake that has forgotten how to be a liquid quickly — a tar pan, naphtha and heavy oil coming up along a fault from somewhere far below, seeping, spreading, crusting, and cracking. In the middle it is glossy and gives a little under a pole. At the edges it is a firm black pavement you can build on. It is warm. It moves at the rate a fingernail grows, and it gurgles: a slow, enormous, intestinal gulp somewhere under the town, every few minutes, forever.

This is the only river. The people call it the Slow River, and they measure it in generations.

---

Out of the tar, and out of the mats that skin it, grows everything green that isn't green.

The growth here is lush past decency — stacked, crowded, dripping, layered like a rainforest and shaped nothing like one. Trunks of pale chitin-and-wax, honey-colored, translucent at the edges, holding a warm oil instead of sap; cut one and it bleeds something you could burn or drink or grease a hinge with. Fronds like combs, like fans, like folded lungs, all of them oriented not toward the light but toward the seep, because the light is not the point. The point is the sulfide and the sugar-heavy vapor coming off the black pan, which the mats eat, which the fronds eat, which everything else eats. There is a canopy. There is an understory. There is a litter of fallen fronds ankle-deep, warm and slowly slumping, and in the litter a whole nation of things that never come up.

Nothing here wilts from dryness. Dryness is not a category. Things wilt when they get cold, and the great seasonal terror of the town is a draft.

When the ceiling cools — at the turn of the day, when the Breath reverses and pulls thin outside air down through the Slot — the vapor condenses overhead and falls. A warm oily drizzle, fine as mist, beading on the fronds and running down the trunks and standing in the cupped leaves. The whole chamber glistens for a quarter of an hour. This is the rain. Children are sent out into it with their arms up. The fronds have drip-tips, exactly as a rainforest's leaves do, and no one in the world has ever thought this remarkable.

---

The animals are not numerous. *Numerous* is too small a word. The air is a solid medium of flying things; you breathe them, you blink them out, you learn to talk without opening your mouth wide. Wick-moths in drifts against the glowing films. Sump-shrimp in the tar's crust, so packed that the crust itself seems to ripple and breathe. Grazers — armored, palm-sized, many-legged — mowing the bacterial felt in a tide that crosses the pan twice a day and sounds like rain on a roof. Above them the lamp-fly curtains, and in the curtains the thread-hunters, centipedes as long as a forearm, that go along the ceiling flutes upside down and take moths out of the air with a flick.

And the greasers: pig-sized isopods, slate-colored, slow, mild-eyed, that crop the mats at the pan's edge and are herded, and eaten, and milked for the clear oil in their gut-sacs, and whose molted shells roof half the town. Twice a year they molt together and the chamber fills with a hail of translucent plates, and the town goes indoors, and the sound is like a hundred people applauding badly for an hour.

Everything eats everything, promptly. A fallen moth is gone in eleven seconds; there is a beetle whose entire life is spent arriving in nine. Nothing rots here in the way rot is understood elsewhere — nothing is left long enough. The dead of the town are given to the pan, and the pan takes them, and the mats take the pan, and the greasers eat the mats, and this is not considered a tragedy but a schedule.

The noise is enormous and completely ordinary. Stridulation from every surface, a hiss like a held cymbal; the gulp of the seep; the flap and drip of the swarms; the low roar of the gas vent at the chamber's north end, where the fault exhales; and over it all the town — pounding, looms, the shell bell, someone's argument four tiers down carrying perfectly. Loud enough that a stranger cannot sleep for a week. Loud enough that the people, when they climb out onto the flat, are made ill by the silence and have to come back down.

---

Underlid clings to the western and southern walls, on nine ledges and the cut stairs between them, and it is unmistakably a town: streets, a market, a district for the smelly trades, a bad neighborhood under the vent, a rich terrace with the best light. Houses are dry-laid limestone corbelled inward, mortared with hot bitumen dipped from the pan's edge, plastered inside with shell-lime and grease. No one has ever mixed mud. There is no mud. There is clay in the seep's sediment and there is tar to bind it, and the mortar smells like a road in summer, and it holds.

They work flint from the pavement above, bone and shell from below, a little native copper cold-hammered into hooks and beads. Cordage from the cable-vine, cloth from frond-fiber, felt from greaser down. Ladders everywhere, and bridges, and galleries cantilevered out over the black. Every ledge is doubled and tripled: gardens stacked over gardens, drying racks over doorways, moth-nets strung between chimneys, laundry of pale fiber hanging in the warm updraft where it never quite stops moving. It is dense to the point of intimacy. Two thousand people in a bubble, and no room, and no privacy, and no one who wants any.

Two laws hold the place together, and both are taught before walking.

The first is fire. There is fuel everywhere and vapor in the air, and once — the story is exact, it names the ledge — a lamp was carried too low. Now flame lives only in the stone hearths on the top gallery, up where the air thins into the Slot, and everything cooked is cooked there and carried down, and the lamps below are living lamps: jars of luminous film, fed with a spoon of oil, hooded with shell to dim them.

The second is the sour. The heavy gas pools. Below the third ledge the air is sweetish and lethal, and it rises and falls with the seep's moods, and it cannot be seen. So no one descends without a glow-jar, because the film in the jar needs what a person needs, and when the shine goes brown and sulky you climb, immediately, without collecting your tools. There are children whose job is to sit at the lip of the low stairs all day and watch a jar. There are men who go down anyway, hooded, on a rope, for the good tar at the center, and sometimes the rope comes up empty and the town knows before the rope is halfway.

---

The chamber breathes twice a day, in and out, as the pressure on the flat above shifts; the Breath is the weather, the clock, and the calendar. The seep pulses on a longer rhythm, weeks or months, and the town reads it in the height of the black tide-marks and in the temper of the herd. Swarms hatch, clog the air for three days, and vanish into the thread-hunters. The light-bar crosses the west tiers, and the shell bell is struck, and everything with an eye moves a little toward it, and the moths that live for the bar and nothing else rise in a column and are eaten. A slab of ceiling drops once in a generation and a street is rebuilt on top of itself, one course higher.

And overhead, in the dome, there are shapes no one made. Smooth scoops, overlapping like the inside of a shell. Flutes running in parallel down the wall. A long, sinuous, unmistakable channel curving from the north end to the south, cutting the ceiling in a great lazy S.

The people call these the water-marks and consider them the oldest thing in the world, older than the seep, older than life. They are correct. This bubble was hollowed by a river — an actual river, of the liquid that exists nowhere here, in an age when the flat was young and low and wet, before it was lifted and drained and ground down to the surface it has now. The river made the room, and then the river went away, and the room stayed, and everything currently alive in it — the forest, the herd, the town, the bell — is living inside the negative shape of something that is gone.

They do not know this. But they know the marks are not tool-work, and they know that in the north wall there are seams of selenite, clear crystals you can see through, and that if you bake one in the top hearth it weeps a few beads of a thin, cold, tasteless liquid that is not oil, that will not burn, that hurts the throat and ruins leather. The lamp-tenders keep a bead of it sealed in a shell. It is the most valuable object in the town, and no one can say what it is for.
