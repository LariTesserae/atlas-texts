## The Bell

Above the scree, above the last gray crust of lichen, above the altitude where anything bothers to be alive, the mountain has a hole in it. Not a crater — the crater is a kilometer east and full of dry snow. This is a wound: a shaft where superheated water flashed to steam nine hundred years ago and blew a plug of glass out of the summit, and the glass is still lying around the lip in blades the color of wet bottle-edges, unweathered, sharp enough to open a hand that steadies itself on them. Frost forms on the blades at night and does not round them. Nothing here has been sanded yet. The mountain is too young and too cold to have learned how to decay.

Look down the shaft and there is water, thirty-odd meters down, and it is not dark.

That is the first wrong thing. The opening is nine meters across, the water below it is juvenile — risen from the mantle, first time at the surface, older than the room that holds it — and it carries almost nothing in suspension. No bloom, no spore, no drift of pollen, because there is no pollen anywhere within four hundred kilometers. It is optically empty. Daylight at this altitude comes down hard and blue and goes straight through, and the room below is lit like a workshop with a clerestory: a solid column of noon standing in the middle of the floor, and the walls in blue shade, and every surface visible, and shadows soft and short.

The second wrong thing is the noise. It comes up the shaft before you reach the lip. Under the wind there is a whir.

---

The chamber is a bell of fresh rhyolite glass, seventy meters across at the floor, walls conchoidal and rippled where the fracture ran, edges everywhere, not a rounded stone in the mineral world. Water enters at the bottom from a fissure at forty liters a second, at eleven degrees, and leaves over a notch in the rim, and the level has not moved more than a hand's width in the entire history of everything that lives here. Cold makes the water thick. It moves like oil. It goes around the room in one slow torus, out along the floor, up the far wall, back across the ceiling, down the near wall: one revolution every eleven hours, near enough constant that the animals use it as a calendar.

Cool water, low heat, slow chemistry. Nine degrees. Nothing here is in a hurry, and everything here is very loud.

The whir is the fauna.

In water that has turned the same direction at the same speed for nine centuries, evolution did the thing it almost never does. It found the axle. The dominant animal of the Bell — call it wheel-stock — is a sessile filterer that grows a socket out of aragonite, seats a shaft in it, and grows a wheel on the shaft: a disc of the same carbonate, four centimeters to forty across, rimmed with rows of combs. The current turns it. Two to six revolutions a minute, forever. Mucus in the bearing, refreshed by a gland, replenished by a twitch. The wheel is not attached to the animal's body any more than a tooth is; it is a shed structure, dead mineral, spun by a living thing that never moves.

There are, at a conservative estimate, four hundred thousand of them in this room.

They crowd the shaded walls in tiers, near-touching, rim to rim, comb interleaving with comb so that the whole wall is a single meshing surface, a rack of turning gearwork sixty meters long and thirty high. Where a wheel dies, its shaft stays in the socket, and the larva that settles there — usually its own offspring, because larvae are heavy and drop — grows its new wheel onto the inherited axle. The frame that holds this machinery is not a frame. It is a genealogy. What looks like a gantry is nine hundred years of grandparents.

And they wear out. The floor grit is fresh-milled glass, angular, abrasive, and it circulates. Bearings score. Wheels chip a comb, run out of true, begin to knock. A worn wheel is dropped and a new one grown in a season, which here means eleven years. So under the racks there are drifts of used parts: spares, sorted by the current into size grades, six meters deep in the low corner, and mobile spindle-crabs working through them, eating the shavings, testing shafts, carrying good ones back up the wall to sell to nothing, to nobody, simply because the ones who carried shafts had descendants.

Nothing in this room has been built. Everything in it has been produced.

---

The floor's low point is a stockyard. Wheel-stock metabolize iron and sulfide from the vent water and void the surplus as billets — rounded gray pellets of pyrite, identical because the animals are identical, half a gram each, a hundred billion of them. They roll downslope. The torus sorts them. They lie in a heap that any visiting foreman would recognize instantly and that no one has ever intended: a tailings pile, growing a centimeter a decade, going nowhere.

Except that the pile is the battery.

Sulfide below, oxidized water above: a potential difference of about six tenths of a volt across the height of the room, and threading the wall cracks, connecting the two, is the loom — filaments of bacteria that conduct electrons along their length, each a few microns thick and, in aggregate, some kilometers of cable packed into the fissures like wiring in a wall cavity. It is alive and it is not a plant and it is not green. Nothing in the Bell is green. The mats on the floor are a millimeter of gray-white nap, silica-sheathed, cold to the touch, chemosynthetic, the only ground cover in a hundred kilometers of dead rock.

And here is the joke the light is telling: the sun comes down that shaft every clear day, a column of usable energy standing in the middle of the room, and not one organism in the Bell has ever taken a calorie from it. Photosynthesis was never invented here. The light is a hazard. At this altitude the ultraviolet comes down with it, and the lit cone in the center of the floor is scoured, empty, a bright void in the middle of the factory, and all the life is racked in the shade around it. A few animals have a pigment spot on the dorsal side, not to see with — nothing here sees — only to know which way to turn away from.

Some of them plug in. There are grazers that press a mouthpart to an exposed filament and draw current directly, farming the voltage the way the wheel-stock farm the flow. When the vent burps and the sulfide flux drops, the loom browns out, and for a week the wall tiers slow by a fraction, and the whir drops a tone, and no one notices, because a week is nothing.

---

The sound has parts. Under everything, the fundamental: the torus itself, a pressure pulse at the vent mouth, below hearing, felt in the sternum as a shove every few seconds. Over it the tooth-frequency of four hundred thousand combs, a hiss with a grain to it, like a mill floor, like rain on a metal roof, ninety-odd decibels in air and vastly more in water, where it arrives through the bone rather than the ear. Over that, individual knocks — the out-of-true wheels, thousands of them, each ticking at its own rate, a hail of small irregular failures. And every hour or so the vent releases a bubble the size of a chair, and it goes up the shaft with a boom that rings the glass walls, and the whole bell sings one clean note for four seconds and goes back to work.

At the ceiling there is a single silver blister of trapped gas, older than the room's occupancy, the mountain's own exhale. Its meniscus is the only interface in the world. Animals hang from it upside down in a fringe, breathing, motionless, lit from below by the reflected column, like lamps in a station nobody uses.

There are predators. The brake is a flat, patient thing that finds a wheel, inserts a wedge of glass into the combs, and sits on the stall until the owner starves and can be eaten at leisure. There are couriers, eel-shaped, that ride the torus and never stop, sleeping in motion, carrying larvae from wall to wall, the only conveyance in the place. And there are the governors: the oldest individuals, wheels a meter and a half across, turning once a minute, so heavy that when the vent stutters they keep the local flow honest for hours. There are eleven of them. They are the reason anything downstream survives a bad decade.

---

Time here is counted in revolutions and in dynasties.

Every two hundred and thirty years, more or less, the vent shifts a few centimeters along its fissure, and the torus reverses.

Everything in the room is handed. The combs are cut on a bias; the bearings are lapped one way; a wheel spun backward sheds its mucus and scores its shaft and dies in a month. So the reversal kills nearly all of it, and the survivors are the mutants with the wrong-handed combs, the ones that had been failing quietly for two centuries, and they inherit the sockets, and the wall rebuilds itself mirrored, and the room gets loud again.

The drifts of spares record it. Dig down through the six meters and the bands alternate: right-hand bias, left-hand bias, right, left, four bands, four dynasties, each one a mass grave of perfectly good parts that no longer fit anything. It is the only archive in the world and it keeps itself.

Outside the rim: dry frost-shattered rock, wind, a horizon of white summits, silence. The noise does not carry past the lip; the shaft swallows it. You can stand a meter from the edge in the thin cold air and hear your own blood and believe the mountain is empty. Below you, in a room the size of a cathedral nave, a hundred thousand tons of water are going around at the speed of a walking man, driving an industry that has no product anyone wants, no market, no builder, no memory, and a power supply made entirely of its own waste.

Somewhere in the wall a seam is drying. The torus has begun, very slightly, to lean. The pitch has dropped a quarter-tone in sixty years and nothing has noticed. In a century or two every bearing in the room will have to be grown the other way round, and the drifts will take another band, and the only thing that will remember any of it is the drifts.
