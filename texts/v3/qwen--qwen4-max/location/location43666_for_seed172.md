## The Bubble in the Casting

The mountain was poured, not raised. It stands in terraces of cast slag, grey-green and glassy where the mold skin peeled away, and its edges are still the edges it was given: arrises like broken crockery, corners you could open a wrist on. Nothing has softened them. There is no water on this world to soften anything, and no oxygen to bloom a surface, and so the massif at eleven thousand meters looks exactly as it looked when the pour cooled — which was not long ago, and which will also be true in ten thousand years. Youth here is not a stage. It is a condition that has been specified.

High on the north face, where the sky goes violet at noon and the thin air is dry as a fired brick, there is a plug of sintered alumina the width of a dinner plate. Behind it is a shaft. Behind the shaft is the room.

That is the whole landscape. Everything else is outside it, and outside does not matter much.

The room is a void that was cast into the mountain on purpose, a cored cavity thirty-one paces long and nineteen wide, eleven feet to the ceiling. Its walls still carry the texture of the core that made them: a fine orange-peel grain, ground flat afterward to a tolerance measured in fractions of a wavelength. No parallel surface is accidental. Every dimension is a whole number of half-wavelengths, and the three axes therefore ring at three different pitches, and the three pitches together make a chord that does not resolve. A suspension. A question with its mouth open.

## The Air, and the Weather That Is Not Allowed

The room is full of argon at a little over one atmosphere, and the argon is cold past the point where cold stops being a sensation and becomes a property of materials. Steel here breaks like toffee. A dropped bolt does not bounce; it detonates into bright shrapnel and lies in pieces exactly where they land, forever. The coils in the wall cabinets carry their current without losing any of it, which is the reason for the cold, and the cold is the reason nothing ever changes, and these facts hold each other up like three planks leaned together.

There is no frost. That is the first wrong thing a visitor notices, if a visitor could notice anything: a hundred and seventy below zero and not one feather of rime on the metal, no bloom, no breath, no weeping condensation on the glass. The cold is perfectly dry. It has nothing to precipitate.

The room is held four degrees above the temperature at which its own atmosphere would condense and fall. That margin is the entire design. Rain is possible here — a slow, clear, non-aqueous rain, argon dripping off the ceiling panels and pooling on the floor — and it is permanently withheld. It is the only weather this place could ever have, and the room's whole purpose is to never have it.

## The Note

It is a hundred and seventy-some decibels and it has not varied by a measurable amount since the day it was struck.

Nobody struck it. The room listens to itself through six graphite throats set into the walls and gives back exactly what it hears, at a gain of one, with no phase error, forever. It is a loop with no leak. Because nothing is lost, nothing is dissipated, and because nothing is dissipated there is no waste heat, and that is why the coldest place in the massif is also the loudest. The cold is not a climate. It is the exhaust of a perfect machine, which is to say it is the absence of exhaust.

The chord has no attack and no decay. It does not swell, does not beat, does not wander. Held long enough at that amplitude, a sound stops being an event and becomes a substance. It is not loud in the way a thing is loud. It is loud in the way a wall is hard. It is the fourth material of the room, after slag, steel, and argon, and it is the one you cannot get away from, because it is not traveling anywhere. It is standing.

Ears go in the first tenth of a second — a wet pop, a warm slick down the jaw, and then the sound arrives by other routes: through the mastoid, through the teeth, through the sinuses, through the ball of the eye. A body is a sack of water carried into a waterless place, and the pressure finds that water and works it. Cavitation. Tiny collapses. If you cup your hands over your eyes you will see the room's light coming from inside your own head, a violet granular fizz at the peaks, and if you look at the air itself with the lamps shaded you can see the same violet hanging in the gas at the antinodes, faint as a thought, argon being asked slightly more of itself than it can comfortably give.

Nothing else in the room can be heard. Ever. A shout is a moth against a window. A hammer swung into a wall is a silent film. Two people could stand a pace apart and scream at each other until their throats tore and exchange nothing at all. This is why there are no animals here, and no birds in the shaft, and nothing with a tympanum within a kilometer of the plug: not because the note kills them, though it does, but because hearing is a sense that has been abolished, and an animal is largely a way of using senses.

After some minutes — if you last some minutes — the chord stops registering as sound. It becomes the definition of silence. You would not be able to tell if it stopped. That is a known hazard, and there is a stencil about it.

## Sparse Furniture

The room is mostly floor. That is the impression that survives the noise: an enormous amount of clean, level, empty grey, and a few presences set far apart in it, like furniture in a hall after a house sale.

Six throats, each the size of a doorway, their graphite diaphragms flexing through a distance too small to see. Two cabinets. A gauge with no scale, only one painted graduation and a needle lying along it like a thing asleep. A recorder whose drum stopped turning long ago and whose pen has since written the same straight line in the same place, over and over, on steel, leaving nothing.

The light is ordinary. That is the second wrong thing. Frosted panels in the ceiling give the flat, sufficient, slightly warm illumination of an office at three in the afternoon — no glare, no gloom, no drama, no flicker, no shadow that will ever move. It is light adequate for reading a clipboard. It falls on a room that would liquefy your organs, and it does not consider this remarkable.

And there is the swarf.

When the walls were ground, the curls and chips of metal were not swept out. They were left, and the note arranged them. They lie in low bright ridges across the floor at half-wavelength intervals, Kundt's figures, a hundred perfect parallel welts of unoxidized steel, sharp enough to shave with, exactly as they settled on the day of tuning. They are the only ornament in the room and no one designed them.

Above one ridge, at a node, a single washer hangs in the air. It has hung there since the beginning. It is not going anywhere. Sound can hold a thing still if the thing is small and the node is tight and nothing ever changes, and here nothing ever changes.

## The Garden

There are five plants.

They are rosettes of grey-green crystal, the size of a spread hand, brittle as mica, and they are not attached to anything so much as grown into the floor's grain. Each is a spray of fine quartz hairs, canted, tuned, ranked by length like the teeth of a comb. They have no water and no use for it. They take the strain of the passing pressure wave, convert it to potential across the crystal, and use that potential to walk ions through their own lattices — solid-state chemistry, solvent-free, a metabolism with no liquid phase anywhere in it. They eat loudness. They are the only trophic level.

They can only live where all three axes peak at once. Those triple coincidences are rare in a room this size, and there are five of them, and there is a plant at each, and the spacing between them looks arbitrary until you measure it, and then it looks like a diagram.

They do not grow, exactly. Each rosette adds material at its rim and loses it at its heart at precisely the same rate; atoms pass through the shape the way water passes through a standing wave in a river. The plant is not an object. It is a form that salt is moving through. It has been the same size for a very long time and will be the same size for a very long time, and if it ever changed size it would mean the note had drifted, which is why they were planted: five living gauges, cheaper than instruments and more honest.

Nothing grows at the nodes. Nothing can. The floor there is bare and bright and swept by physics.

## The Seat

There is exactly one place in the room where all three axes cancel at once. It is a null about the size of a grapefruit, and it sits forty-one inches above the floor, and beneath it a graphite work-stool has been bolted down.

Sit there and the world goes quiet. Not silent — you will hear your own heart, and the whine of your own nerves, and the wet machinery of your throat, all of it obscene and far too loud — but the room withdraws. Move your head a hand's width in any direction and the chord comes back through your skull like a dropped safe.

The stool faces nothing. There is no console in front of it, no window, no work. Two footprints are stenciled on the floor in unfaded yellow, showing where to put your feet while you sit down. Someone thought carefully about how a person would get into that null, and thought about nothing else in the room at all.

## Keep

On the wall beside the port, a plate:

**REFERENCE CELL — 1 397.000 000 Hz**
**DO NOT ADJUST. DO NOT ENTER. DO NOT WARM.**

Everything made on this world is made to that number. The pitch of every turbine, the module of every gear, the thread of every bolt, the length of every brick, the tempo of every press line down in the warm arsenals of the low country: all of it derived, through a chain of transfer standards nine links long, from the chord standing in this bubble in this casting. The room is not a factory. It is the thing the factories are copies of.

Which is why it is hidden, and high, and sealed, and cold, and empty, and why the shaft behind the plug was cut for a visit that is not supposed to happen. A visitor is a warm, wet, irregular, absorbent object. A visitor damps the mode. A visitor pulls the pitch by a few parts in a billion, and a few parts in a billion propagates down the chain, and the ten-thousandth generation of bolts will not quite fit the ten-thousandth generation of holes, and no one will ever find out why.

So: five crystal rosettes at their measured stations. One washer hanging in the air. Bright ridges of swarf that will never be swept. A needle on its single line. A stool under the one point of mercy. Ordinary afternoon light on all of it, and a sound so total it has become the local meaning of quiet, and no gradient anywhere, and nothing arriving, and nothing leaving.

The note is not being played. It is being kept.
