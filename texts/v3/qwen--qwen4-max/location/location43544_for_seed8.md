## The Pale Ground

There is no hill here worth the name. There is a slope, but you could not find it with your body; you would need a year and a bowl of water to prove it. The land falls about a meter every five kilometers, and so the water that lies on it after rain does not run — it *leans*. It takes a week to decide. It takes a month to leave.

This is what a mountain becomes. Somewhere else, in some other age, there were ranges here, high enough to hold snow, and they went away grain by grain, and their substance is now a delta on a coast three thousand kilometers off, and what is left is this: the roots of the mountains, planed flat, exposed, still lying in the sun. The plain has been land since before there were flowers. Nothing has folded it, drowned it, scraped it, or buried it. It has simply been rained on for a very long time, and rain is patient, and patient things win.

The sky is most of the world. Standing anywhere on the pale ground you can see fifteen kilometers in every direction, and eleven of those kilometers are haze.

## Stone That Has Rotted

Dig anywhere and you go down through red gravel, then a hard iron crust you can break with a heel, then clay — white, pink, mottled ochre, soft as cheese — and this clay is *rock*. It is granite that has forgotten how to be granite. Forty meters down, the feldspars have gone to powder and the quartz is still quartz, and somewhere below that there is competent stone that no living thing has ever touched. You can carve the cliff faces with a fingernail. The land is rotten to a depth that would swallow a tower.

Only the quartz survives unweathered, and it litters the surface in white seams and gravel trains — long sinuous ribbons of shattered white stone wandering across the red plain, tracing veins that once ran through the heart of a mountain. From the air they look like writing. From the ground they look like the spine of something lying just under the skin.

Where the iron crust has been undercut, there are breakaways: low walls, five to twenty meters, the only vertical things in the region. They are the last of the relief, and they are going. Their caps are hard and their bellies are soft, so they weep. Rain sinks through the duricrust, hits the pallid clay, and travels sideways until it finds the cliff face, and there it comes out — a hundred seeps in a kilometer of wall, bearded with green, staining the white clay with rust and manganese black, dripping into a permanent apron of mud that never dries and never floods. Put your palm on the rock at dawn and it comes away wet even when it has not rained, because the night air is so full of water that the stone drinks it out of the sky and gives it back at sunrise.

Boulders that have fallen from the cap lie below in a jumble, and each one is a small country: a lizard on the hot face, a fern in the cold crack, a colony of ants in the shadowed underside, a ring of dried algae where last season's seep pooled.

## The Ghost Channel

Across the middle of the region runs a line you can only see from a rise or from the air: a slightly greener, slightly darker band a few hundred meters wide, wandering west to east with the lazy looping curve of a river. It *was* a river. It has not been a river in the sense of banks and current for longer than there have been birds. Now it is a chain of claypans, each one shallow as a hand, each sealed with settled clay so tight it holds water for eight months, strung one after another for two hundred kilometers, joined by sills so low that the flood has to rise a finger's width before it can step from one pan to the next.

This is how the flood moves. Rain falls at the head of the chain — warm rain, ordinary rain, the kind that comes four afternoons a week in the wet and soaks in without drama — and the first pan fills, and spills, and the second begins. The crest travels perhaps four hundred meters a day. You can walk beside it for a week and watch a brown edge advance through the tussocks at the pace of a grazing tortoise. Downstream, the water arrives before any cloud does. The frogs in a pan eighty kilometers from the storm begin calling three days before the water reaches them, because they can feel the ground go soft.

Nothing here is in a hurry except the animals, and the animals are only in a hurry about being alive.

At the end of the chain the water stops. It has been stopping there forever. There is no sea to reach — the gradient dies, the plain sags, and the drainage ends in a salt lake with a shore of gypsum crust that crunches like frost and a floor of black sulphidic mud. Everything the rain ever dissolved out of forty meters of rotting granite is here, because there was never enough fall to carry it away. Salt is the only thing this landscape accumulates. On the downwind shore stands a crescent dune of clay pellets, wind-scoured off the dry lake bed, growing a few centimeters in a century — the fastest construction project in the region, and it has no author.

The lake fills properly perhaps twice in a human lifetime. Between fillings it is a white glare that lifts the horizon into floating islands at midday and hurts to look at, and it is not empty: brine shrimp in the pink shallows, and, when the water comes, ten thousand birds that have somehow known, from a continent away, that this decade the lake would be worth the trip.

## Sparse, and Old, and Poor

There is water and there is heat, and there is no forest, and the reason is the age. A hundred million years of rain have taken everything soluble out of this ground. What is left is iron, aluminum, silica, and a phosphorus scarcity so severe it dictates the shape of every living thing. The plants cannot afford to be lush. They cannot afford to lose a leaf. So they keep their leaves for years, small and hard and grey and defended, and they grow slowly, and they stand far apart.

The trees are scattered at four or five to the hectare on the good ground, and on the pale scalds there is nothing at all for a kilometer but white clay and a skin of cyanobacteria. Each tree is a piece of engineering: a taproot or a fan of sinkers going down thirty meters through the rotten rock to the water table, a canopy that looks stunted and is in fact four hundred years old, a girth that thickens by a millimeter a year. There are almost no seedlings. Recruitment happens in the great flood years and nowhere else, so the woodland is arranged in generations — a cohort here from a wet decade, a cohort there, gaps of two centuries between them, standing in the same open pattern their great-grandparents stood in.

Between the trees: hummock grass on the sandier rises, sedge and rush along the ghost channel, low succulent samphire ringing the salt in bands of red and jade, and over most of the bare ground a cryptogamic crust — algae, lichens, liverworts — brittle and grey and dead-looking for eleven months, greening in ninety seconds when the first drops hit. That crust does more of the region's photosynthesis than the trees do. It also holds the surface together. Where it is broken, the pale clay scalds and the scald spreads.

Fire, which elsewhere would reset all this, almost never comes. There is not enough grass to carry it. The gaps between the tussocks are the firebreaks, and the firebreaks are permanent, and so nothing here has had to learn to burn. That is one of the rules of this place: it has been left alone.

The other rule: the only architecture is grown. Termite mounds stand out on the flat like furniture in an emptied room — wedges oriented edge-on to the noon sun, cathedral spires three meters tall, and the eroded stumps of older ones worn to pedestals of cemented subsoil. They are the only earthworks. Termites have been carrying the deep clay up to the surface, mouthful by mouthful, for longer than the trees have existed; every gram of topsoil on this plain has passed through an insect. Yabbies drill down to the water table and stack their spoil in little chimneys of mud pellets. Weavers hang their nests in the isolated trees, where nothing can climb to them. No one has ever made anything here that was not made of itself.

## The Noise

You would not expect it. The land looks vacant — a tree, a mound, a long white nothing, a shimmer. And then evening comes and the plain becomes the loudest place you have ever stood.

It starts with the cicadas, which are not one sound but a braided cable of sound, several species taking shifts, rising and falling in waves that move across the country as if a wind you cannot feel is passing through. Then the frogs. In every pan, in every rut, in the seep aprons under the breakaways, burrowing frogs that have spent two years wrapped in a shed-skin cocoon a meter underground come up in the soft ground and begin. There are thousands per hectare of shallow water. The chorus is a physical pressure; it flattens thought; you have to raise your voice to speak to someone an arm's length away, and there is no one to speak to.

Over it, the long carrying notes: cranes trumpeting in pairs from the sedge, a bittern booming in a pan you cannot see and will not find, the descending whistle of a nightjar, the dry rattle of a hundred thousand cricket wings, the tick and click of termites in the mound at your feet, and — under all of it, so low you feel it in the sternum — the sound of the sheet water itself, an inch deep and ten kilometers wide, hissing over gravel at the speed of a minute hand.

Flat ground and warm wet air make an acoustic instrument. There is nothing to block or scatter the sound. At dawn, when a thin inversion lies over the plain, the chorus from a pan fifteen kilometers away arrives intact, and you cannot tell it from the one at your boots. The emptiness is not silence. The emptiness is *why* it is loud: everything alive has gathered at the few places where water sits, and between those places there is nothing but distance for the noise to cross.

By day the frogs stop and the cicadas take the whole shift. At night the frogs resume and the bats come out over the pans in loose hundreds, and the noise never actually ceases; it only changes personnel.

## Patient Bodies

The animals are abundant because the water is reliable in the way that matters — not fast, but *there*, near the surface, always, held in clay and rotten rock and the shade of ironstone. And they are ancient in a way the animals of young landscapes are not, because nothing here has ever interrupted them.

In the mud at the bottom of the deepest pans, lungfish lie coiled in mucus chambers through the dry years, breathing air, waiting out a drought that lasts longer than most mammals live. In the dust of the empty claypans there are eggs — shrimp eggs, killifish eggs — that have been dry for a decade and will hatch within the hour of wetting, so that a pan which was a white cracked polygon on Tuesday is a soup of swimming things on Friday, and by Sunday the birds have found it. Tortoises graze the seep aprons and take twenty years to reach the size of a dinner plate. In the mound spring at the foot of the northern breakaway — where water that fell as rain on highlands far away a million years ago comes up warm and faintly salty and has built a low dome of precipitate around its own mouth — there is a pool the size of a room, permanent through every drought, and in it a fish no longer found anywhere else on earth, and a snail, and a flatworm, and a species of reed. They have been in that pool since before the last ice ages rearranged everything else. The pool is a hole in time. The plain around it is the reason the hole exists: nothing ever came to break it.

There are predators, but they are spread thin — a raptor on the dead tree, a monitor working the breakaway boulders, snakes in the crabholes, a fish-eating bird standing motionless in the shallows for an hour at a stretch. Even the hunting is slow. Speed is expensive in a place where phosphorus is the currency.

## What Is Happening

Almost nothing, at the speed you can watch. The land lowers itself by the thickness of a hand in a hundred thousand years. The breakaways retreat a meter in ten thousand. The salt lake gains a little more of everything the plain has lost. The trees thicken imperceptibly and die standing and are eaten from the inside by termites over eighty years, so that a fallen tree is not an event but a long soft collapse.

If there is an ending here it is not a catastrophe; it is a settling. The water table is creeping up in the low ground as the last of the deep-rooted trees fail to recruit, and where it comes within two meters of the surface it brings the salt with it, and the samphire moves in, and the trees go, and the scald widens by a few meters a decade. The plain is finishing itself. It has been finishing itself since before there were mammals, and it will be finishing itself long after whatever is listening now has stopped.

Meanwhile: warm rain on ironstone at four in the afternoon, the smell of wet rust and crushed sedge, the fog of insects over the pans in the last of the light, the glare going amber, the cloud shadows walking an hour across the flat. And at dusk the whole enormous emptiness filling up with noise, as it has every evening for a hundred million years — the loudest thing on the flattest ground, saying nothing, to no one, at a volume that carries fifteen kilometers.
