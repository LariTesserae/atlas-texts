## The Flue

You hear it before you find it. On the high plateau the wind is the only sound and it is a poor one — a scouring, edgeless hiss over gravel that has not yet learned to be soil. Then a thread of something else enters it: a low, sustained roar with a pulse in it, the sound of a hall full of machines running behind a wall. There is no wall. There is no hall. There is a crack in the ground, and the crack is breathing.

The plateau sits at four thousand six hundred metres and is the colour of wet slate and old bone. Permafrost lies a hand's depth down. Nothing grows here but crust lichen, pressed flat, and the occasional cushion plant hunkered into a fist so tight you could mistake it for a stone. The gravel is angular. Every piece of it. Not one pebble on this whole upland has been rolled round by water; the mountains here came up fast and are still coming, and the rock breaks the way glass breaks, with edges you could shave with.

The crack is thirty-one years old. An earthquake opened it in a night, and a glacial lake sitting in the cirque above found the new fault and went down through it in nine hours, and what is left is a slot two hundred and forty paces long, six to twelve paces wide, and eighty metres deep. Both ends pinch shut. It is not a valley. It is a room whose walls are unfinished, a wound that has not yet slumped into a shape. Above, the rim is a pair of knife edges. Below, out of sight, is warmth.

---

Cold air pours over the lip and falls. You go down after it, in a chimney where the rock is still bright with fresh fracture — grey and rust-orange and the pale green of reduced shale, unweathered, unstained, no lichen has had time to claim it. Twenty metres down the cold stops. It does not fade; it stops, the way a door stops a draught. Below that line the air is nine degrees and wet as a mouth, and the walls are streaming.

Not with rain. It rarely rains here; the plateau lies in a shadow and gets hail four or five times a summer, hard little pellets that come down the slot like gravel thrown by a bored god. This water is the place's own breath, given back. Steam rises from the floor, meets the cold rock of the upper walls, and condenses, and runs down in sheets and threads and slow beads. The river here flows upward as vapour and comes home as drip. It has been doing this for thirty-one years and it has never once been a river you could stand in.

The walls are hung. That is the first shock — after the dead grey of the plateau, this. Moss in ropes and pelts, moss in shelves and swags, liverworts flat and glossy as wet leather, films of green algae in the seams, and in the warm lanes below, ferns: parsley fern in the cracks, and in the hot places a soft broad-fronded thing that grows to a knee, sometimes a waist, and grows fast, and dies fast, and does not care. Above the treeline by two thousand metres, and here is a hothouse. The ground is thirty degrees. Put your palm on the moss and it is warm as an animal. Take your hand off and the air bites it.

Everything is layered. Thirty centimetres of growth holds four storeys: black algal crust on the sinter, then a spongy liverwort mat, then moss stems crowded stem against stem like a drowned crowd's hair, then the fern fronds laid over the top and interlocking so completely that in places you walk on a green lid and never see the ground at all. Under that lid there are holes. Some of the holes are two metres deep and full of water at ninety degrees. The moss does not distinguish between the floor and the ceiling of a boiler.

---

The floor is a works.

There is no arguing with the word once you are down here in the noise. It is eighty-five decibels continuous and it peaks past a hundred: the flat roar of a steam jet at a fracture mouth, the slap and gulp of a boiling mudpot, the deep intermittent *knock — knock — knock* of water hammer somewhere in the plumbing underfoot, like a chain being dropped down a pipe, over and over, by nobody. There is a vent at the wide end that cycles: forty minutes of gathering silence, a rising whine, then a bellow and a column of steam and hot water thrown nine metres up the slot, and the whole chamber fills with fog and the drip rate triples for ten minutes afterward. Then it settles and begins again. It has no schedule that anyone could keep.

Understand what is running here. Heat at depth — a cooling body of rock, still hot from the same argument that lifted this plateau — boils water in a fracture network. Above the boiling, a cap of silica sinter has precipitated out of the water itself and sealed the fractures, so the system is a pressure vessel that built its own lid. The lid has weak points; the weak points shift; silica dissolves in one throat and deposits in another and the valves reseat themselves overnight. Cold condensate running down the walls is the condenser. The hanging moss and the wet rock face, exchanging heat and vapour across a few millimetres, are the heat exchangers, and they are as efficient as anything with a fan and a fin stack. The narrow slot is the stack. The roar is the turbine hall.

And it produces. That is the part that stays with you. Terraces of sinter grow out from the walls in stacked lips, a centimetre a season, pale as candle wax, each lip a batch. Around the sulfurous vents, native sulfur crystallises in needles and crusts, an entire yellow production line running from gas to crystal in the space of a hand's breadth, and beyond the crystals a dead zone where nothing lives, and beyond the dead zone the moss again, thick as ever. Gypsum blooms. Salt effloresces out of the shale in white whiskers that grow visibly in a day and are wiped off by the next drip curtain. On one fracture face where the water is acid and full of dissolved metal, a bright film has plated itself onto the rock — a blush of copper, thin as breath, laid down where the moss gives up electrons and the water gives up its cargo. The walls here plate themselves. The plant has no foreman, no shift, no quota, and no off switch, and it has been running at capacity since before the moss arrived.

It even electrifies itself. Fast steam through a narrow throat carries charge; the plume above the rim, on cold clear nights, holds enough potential to lift the hair on your arm and put a battery taste on your tongue. Once or twice a season, in the dark, a spark jumps a centimetre between two wet rock lips and there is a snap and a smell of ozone. Nothing here is impossible. All of it is plumbing and chemistry. That is what makes it unbearable to look at for long.

---

It is dark. The slot runs nearly east–west and eighty metres deep, and the sun crosses it for eleven minutes at midsummer and not at all from autumn to spring. Above, the steam roof holds most days, and above that the plateau's cloud. So the green here lives on almost no light: a diffuse grey seep for a few hours, a blade of direct sun that walks along one wall like a slow searchlight, and then the long dark.

The dark is not empty. Where the fault cut a black shale bed, gas comes up, and thirty years ago something — a plume spark, most likely — lit it. It burns still, low, at the base of the north wall: a flame the colour of skim milk and blue paper, half a metre tall, wavering in the drip, almost invisible until you look away from it and catch it at the edge of your eye. It gives hardly any heat. It has been burning through the same seam since before the ferns came. Further along, where the sulfide-rich rock is slowly oxidising and heating itself the way a wet haystack does, a crack glows dull red at its throat on the coldest nights, and the air there smells of a foundry: hot iron, struck flint, coke.

By that light the moss is black and the ferns are cut-outs and the condensate falls in bright threads. The sulfur crystals catch the flame and look wet. Everything is manufactured here and nothing has ever been made.

---

There are no animals. Not one. No midges over the pools, no springtails in the moss, no bird has ever come down the chimney. The air explains it: carbon dioxide seeps from the cool fractures and pools in the low places in an invisible tide that rises and falls with the pressure, and hydrogen sulfide sits in the yellow zones, and the warm lanes are warm because the ground beneath them is capable of cooking anything that settles. There is no bottom to the food web here — no grazers, no predators, no pollinators, nothing that moves under its own power except water and rock.

So the crowd is a crowd of one. The moss in each lane is a single genet, cloned outward from one spore that arrived on a hailstone's meltwater; a hundred square metres of near-touching stems and not an individual among them. The ferns self-fertilise. The willowherb that has got into the cool end sets seed without asking anyone. Dispersal is by flood, by rockfall, by the wind that comes over the lip and drops what it carries. The whole lush mass is a handful of lineages copied and copied, packed shoulder to shoulder in the dark, warm, deafened, and entirely alone.

And the loudness has no voice in it. That is the thing you notice on the second day. Every decibel is rock, water, gas, or fire. It is the loudest place for a hundred kilometres and there is nothing in it that can hear you.

---

It changes hourly. A jet that has roared for six years seals itself with silica on a Tuesday and by Friday the lane it warmed is frozen moss, black, collapsing into the sinter. A mudpot slumps and opens a new throat two metres away and a fresh hothouse is founded on the corpse of the old one within a month. The floor is a lid over boiling water and it fails; slabs of the wall, still sharp-edged, still unweathered, come down without warning and take a whole hanging garden with them, and the debris is buried in green again before the season turns. When the hail comes hard, or when a serac falls into the tarn on the cirque above, water arrives in the slot as a wall and scours the floor to bare rock in eleven minutes and leaves it sterile and steaming and ready.

Nothing here is old enough to be stable. The gravel has no round stones. There is no soil, only sinter and a peat of moss a finger deep. The place has not finished arriving. Its rules are short and absolute:

The ground is a lid, not a floor. Heat is the only currency and it is paid at random. Quiet means pressure. Cold is three steps away in any direction, including up, and the boundary does not blur — you can stand with one hand in a summer and one in a winter. Everything that lives here lives fast, clones itself, and expects to be killed.

At the pinched end, where the walls close to a body's width, the roar drops away and you can hear the drip, and your own blood, and the small ticking of cooling stone. Above, through the slot, a strip of stars. Below, the machine, running its night shift, making stone out of water and water out of stone, plating metal, growing crystal, throwing steam eighty metres into thin cold air where it turns to cloud and falls back as rain that never fell from the sky.

It has no product. It has never had a worker. It is the most industrious place on the mountain.
