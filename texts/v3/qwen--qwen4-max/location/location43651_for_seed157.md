## The Bowl

There is a cirque in the shoulder of the mountain, cut by ice that is ten thousand years gone, and it is finished. That is the first thing to understand about it. It is not being carved anymore. The glacier did its work and left, and the water has since taken the shape as far as the shape wanted to go, and now the walls have the settled, rounded, hands-over-everything look of a form that has arrived. Seventy paces at the long axis. Forty at the waist. Walls three times the height of a standing person, curving inward at the top so that the bowl is lipped, hooded, a room with its ceiling pulled forward over the doorway.

It sits at the precise altitude of the cloud deck. Not in the cloud, not above it — at it. The deck is a lid laid across the rim, and it does not lift at noon and does not sink at dusk, because there is no noon here and no dusk. Above the lid there is presumably sun. Inside the bowl there is a gray so faint it is more a rumor of gray, a pressure on the eye rather than a light, and after an hour of standing in it you stop being able to say whether your eyes are open.

Except at the notch. Where the spillway cuts the lip, a slot the width of two fingers admits a piece of the upper sky, and in that slot there is a star. It does not rise. It does not set. It hangs at the same angle in the same sliver of black, and it is the only thing in the bowl that comes from outside it, and it is not moving either.

## Water

The walls weep. Not in streams — a stream would be a sound and an event. The rock here is porous in the way of old limestone, and the mountain above is full, and the water arrives through the stone at the rate the stone can pass it, so the entire inner surface of the bowl wears a film of moving water about as thick as the skin on cooling milk. Put your palm flat against the wall and you will feel it go over your knuckles, cold, unhurried, endlessly. The rock beneath is slick and faintly greasy and rounded everywhere; there is not a sharp edge left in the world.

The water is saturated. It comes out of the mountain already carrying all the lime it can hold, and it carries also a load of dissolved iron, rust-colored, sulfurous, faintly sweet-smelling if you get your nose to the seep. Because it is saturated it dissolves nothing new; because it is moving it deposits, in a rind of tufa, exactly as much as it takes. The wall retreats nowhere. The rind thickens nowhere. There is a place on the east curve where a droplet forms and falls, one drop, then another, at an interval you could set a life by, and the small cup it has made in the floor-stone is the same depth it was when the ice left.

The floor does not puddle. It cannot: the spillway notch takes off precisely what the walls deliver. There is no flood here and no drought and no wet season and no dry patch. Every square handspan of this place is at the same cool temperature — the chill of a root cellar in autumn, the cold that is not dangerous, only total — and the same wetness, and the same dark. There is no gradient anywhere. You could not find the warm corner. There is no warm corner. Nothing shivers, because nothing is losing heat that it is not, at that instant, getting back.

## The Crop

Nothing here is green. Green would be useless.

The growth feeds on the iron in the seep, and it is rust-red and cream and a dirty translucent pink, and it is lush to the point of obscenity. Sheets of it hang from the walls in lobes like wet liver. Ropes of it, finger-thick, span the bowl at chest height and at head height and above that, sagging, dripping, furred with finer growths, so that the air of the room is not empty air but a lattice, a soaked scaffolding, and to cross the bowl you go through it rather than under it. Where the ropes cross they have grown together. On the floor there are cushions of it, hand-high, dense as soaked felt, and out of the cushions rise fronds that are one cell thick, translucent, veined in rust, so wet they cannot exist anywhere the air pauses in its saturation — filmy things, glass ferns, held open by water pressure alone.

It smells of wet iron and cold mushrooms and, underneath, of blood, because of everything else.

## The Occupancy

There is no bare surface in the bowl. There is no bare surface anywhere. This is not a figure of speech.

The floor you would call floor is a layer of small flat grazers, plate-shelled, thumbnail-sized, packed rim against rim like cobbles, and they are moving, all of them, at a speed you would need to watch a while to confirm. Between them, in the interstices too small to see, are other things. Beneath them, in the tufa, are the borers. On top of them is a second layer of the same animals, and a third, in places a fourth, so that walking here means walking on a slow shifting pavement of shells that gives under the foot and re-closes behind it.

The wall is limpets, edge to edge, each in a home scar that fits it exactly and has fit it exactly forever, because the rasp of the foot and the deposition of the lime are the same rate. The ropes are grazers too, hanging, and the cushions are full of the pale slow things that eat the cushions from inside, and the fronds carry their own crops of animals so small they read as a bloom on the leaf.

And in and among all of it: the warm ones. Palm-sized, dense-furred, blind — eyes are expensive and there is nothing to see — with small folded ears and whiskers that do the whole work of the world, and blood so thick with iron that their thin-skinned hands and feet look bruised. They are packed the way a litter packs, in the hollows, along the ropes, in the cushions, under the shell-pavement, in the seep-cups, and they are the reason the bowl smells of blood and feels, if you stand still in it, faintly warm on the face. There are a great many. There are exactly a great many.

The air itself is occupied. Spores, motes, small hanging fliers with heavy bodies and hushed scaled wings — flight is costly in this thin air, so mostly they don't; they climb, and hang, and wait, packed along the ropes like fruit. A single falling drop from the east curve carries its own swimming population, and delivers them, alive, to the cup.

You cannot put a hand down here without putting it on someone. There is no room between presences. The bowl is not full in the sense that a jar is full. It is full in the sense that a body is full.

## Quiet

With that much life the place should roar. It doesn't.

The moss-felt eats sound. The fur eats sound. The wet ropes eat sound, and the bodies stacked in every hollow eat what's left, and there is no wind — the lid sees to that, and the hood, and the settled equilibrium of the whole small world. What you hear, standing in the dark with your ears wide:

Breathing. Ten thousand small respirations, phased so that no two hundred of them coincide, arriving as a single continuous softness, like the sound of a held breath that never has to be let go. It is the floor of the silence here. You stop hearing it in a minute and then hear it again an hour later and are startled, though nothing has changed.

Water, which is nearly no sound at all, because a film does not fall, it slides.

Shells ticking as the pavement shifts, a dry small sound like fingernails on a table in another room.

And the click.

## The Stone-Holders

There are eleven of them. Larger than the rest, slow, heavy in the shoulder, with a mouth that is also a hand — a split, lipped, muscular thing that can hold. Each keeps a stone.

The stones are flakes of a flint that does not occur in this mountain: frost-chips off a boulder the ice carried in and ground to powder before the bowl was a bowl. They are the only objects here that came from elsewhere, and they were not made. Nobody made them. They were found, ten thousand years ago, in the rubble, and they have been in use ever since.

The stone-holder takes its flake in the mouth-hand, sets a glass-snail against the anvil-hollow in the floor — a socket that fits the shell exactly — and strikes. One strike. The shell opens; the animal is eaten; the flake is set back in its own hollow in the wall, in the seep, where the water lays lime onto the abraded edge at precisely the rate the striking wears it away. The stone does not get smaller. The socket does not get deeper. There is no flake-scatter here, no debitage, no midden, no workshop, nothing built, nothing made, nothing accumulated. There is only a technique, held in eleven bodies, passed along by touch in the dark, older than any species that has ever had a name.

The click of it carries. It is the loudest thing in the world. It happens once every hundred breaths or so, and it is the closest this place comes to an event.

## The Ledger

There is a predator: pale, long, unhurried, with a mouth like a purse-string, that moves through the shell-pavement on the flat of its belly and takes what it takes. The warm ones do not flee it. They have no startle; there has never been anything here to startle at. When it comes among them they part by exactly the width of its body and close behind it, and one of them goes into the purse, and the number is restored by a birth in the cushions somewhere on the far curve, and the birth was already going to happen.

Count the animals tomorrow and you will get the number you got today. Count the fronds. Count the drops falling from the east curve in an hour. Weigh the lime in the water going out against the lime in the water coming in. Everything here is eating and being eaten and growing and dissolving at rates that cancel, and the cancelling is not a coincidence or a truce — it is the shape of the place, arrived at long ago, the way the walls arrived at their curve.

This is the rule of the bowl: nothing accumulates. No soil deepens. No corpse lasts. No nest persists — they are made and unmade in the same gesture, and the making and the unmaking are the same act seen from two sides. No lesson is learned, because nothing has ever gone wrong. Nothing here has ever been surprised.

It is a small world with a hard rim, and you could walk all of it in a minute if there were anywhere to put your feet. The walls overhang and are slick and cannot be climbed from inside; the notch is two fingers wide. There is no way in that is not the way the water came, ten thousand years ago, through stone.

Stand in it long enough and your eyes will finally open all the way, and you will find that there is a little light after all: a cold green ghost-glow off the threads in the cushions, the same brightness it has always been, just enough to show you the shape of the hand you are holding out in front of your face, and the fur of the thing that has come to rest against it, and the rust-colored frond bending under the weight of something that has climbed out onto it to be nearer your warmth.

Above the notch, the star has not moved.
