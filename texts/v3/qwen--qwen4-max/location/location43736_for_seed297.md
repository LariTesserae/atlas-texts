# The Basin

## I. Coming down off the rim

The rim is a lip of crystal and it is sharp — not the sharpness of a thing recently broken, which implies a long dull future ahead of it, but the sharpness of a thing that has never had to survive anything. You put your palm on it and it does not cut you so much as *decline* you, a clean refusal at the scale of the edge. Below, the basin opens: forty kilometers of it, maybe more, a shallow bowl in a plain of the same white-pink mineral, and it is full — packed, choked, upholstered — with green.

There is no water. Understand this first, because everything else follows from it. No river has ever entered this bowl. No lake stands in it. The sky is a dry cobalt with no vapor in it anywhere, no cloud, no haze at the horizon, no smear where a sea might be. The pan between the thickets is not dried mud; it has never been mud. It is a floor of interlocking crystals, salmon-white with amber veining, precipitated in place, and every crystal face is a plane you could shave by. There is no dust. Not a mote. Your boots come down clean and stay clean for as long as you walk here.

And it is warm. Not hot — the warmth of a held hand, of the inside of a mouth. The air is that temperature. The crystal is that temperature. The leaves are that temperature. So is your skin, and after an hour you stop being able to feel where you end, because touch is mostly a report on heat leaving you, and nothing here is leaving you at all. You reach into a thicket and cannot tell you have touched a frond until you see it bend.

## II. The lush

It is genuinely lush. This is the thing that no description of the dryness prepares you for. Four tiers of it, near-touching, layered like a wet forest — except nothing is wet, and nothing has ever been wet.

The tall forms stand twenty meters, trunks pale and smooth as candle wax, without bark fissure or burl or scar, because fissures are the record of a trunk growing against itself over years and these trunks have no years. They branch at the top into a canopy of leaves the size of doors. Beneath them, a second story of ribbed fans, each rib a taut filament, arranged like the strings of an instrument nobody plays. Beneath that, a waist-high layer of thick succulent blades, bottle-green, edges perfectly entire — no insect has ever notched one. And under everything, ankle-deep, a springy dry mat of tiny rosettes, mineral and tissue interleaved, that gives underfoot with the unsettling compliance of a sleeping animal.

Break a blade and it does not bleed sap. A bead forms at the wound, colorless, dense, heavy-looking, with the refractive weight of glycerol; it does not wet your finger. It has no surface tension with skin at all. It rolls off you like a ball bearing and lies on the crystal without spreading, and it does not evaporate, and it will lie there in exactly that shape for as long as the basin exists. Inside the leaf that fluid is under pressure, and it turns polarized light as it moves, so the whole canopy carries an oily bronze-violet sheen that slides across it when you shift your head — starling-throat colors over a mass of deep tea-green.

There are no flowers. There are no seeds, no seedlings, no pollen, no rot, no fallen wood, no galls, no asymmetry, no dead leaf anywhere in forty kilometers. There are fruits: amber, translucent, hanging at exactly the height of a relaxed hand, and they are ripe. They have always been ripe. They will not fall.

There is, nevertheless, a litter layer. Look down through the rosette mat and you will see leaves there, brown-green, lying in the attitudes of fallen things. Look up. They are the same leaves. Not copies — the same ones, at the same moment, referenced from below. The canopy is its own litter. The forest has beentidied by being folded.

## III. Why it is so full

Walk into the thicket and you should be stopped in three meters. You are not. You put a frond aside with your forearm and the way ahead is open, and open again, and open, and you begin to notice that you are not pushing anything after the first push. The frond you moved is the frond twenty meters ahead. It is also the frond behind you, and the one in the next district, and eleven thousand others across the bowl. You moved it once.

This is the lushness. It is not that ten thousand plants were grown here. It is that a smaller number were solved for, exactly and completely, and then *referred to* — instantiated across the basin wherever the solution fit. Density here is cheap. Density here is nearly free. The air in the thicket is thick with leaves behind leaves behind the same leaves, and if you stand very still and let your eyes unfocus you can see the regress going away from you in ranks, not diminishing with distance the way real things diminish, each rank as crisp as the first, until your visual system quietly refuses the whole thing and gives you a headache behind the left eye.

The originals are out on the open pan, and in the tall colonnade at the basin's northern curve: single-instance forms, unrepeated, each one plainly *itself*. They are not larger or more beautiful. They are just singular, and you can feel the singularity coming off them like a difference in pressure. Standing next to one, you know you are standing next to the only one. Standing in the thicket, you know you are standing next to a place where one is being used.

Where the fitting was imperfect, there are seams. You'll walk through a district where every fifth fan-plant is rotated three degrees wrong, or where the rosette mat runs in a grain that fights your feet, or where a canopy leaf hangs at chest height with no trunk anywhere near it, complete, turgid, optimal, unsupported. Nothing here is broken. It is only slightly mis-referred. The seams are the most beautiful parts of the basin and the hardest to look at for long.

## IV. The rules, as far as they can be inferred

Nothing changes. This is not a poetic statement. There is no wind, because wind is a pressure gradient and there are no gradients — the air is one temperature and one pressure from rim to rim. There is no evaporation, no condensation, no diffusion front, no scent plume. There *is* a smell, but it is everywhere at once and unvarying: warm mineral, cut stem, a faint sweetness like the inside of a new instrument case. You stop smelling it in ninety seconds and you can never get it back, no matter how you snuffle, because smelling requires a change in concentration and the basin does not offer changes.

The sun stands at about forty degrees in the east and stays there. It is an ordinary sun. The light is ordinary daylight, the kind under which you'd paint a fence. This is the most disturbing fact about the place: the sky is completely normal, and it is normal *forever*, and everything strange happens below the level of the horizon.

Shadows are crisp and consistent and wrong. A thousand canopy trees cast, between them, the shadow of one tree, repeated — the same lobed shape lying on the pan over and over, identical down to the notch where a leaf overlaps. You can stand in the shade of a tree three kilometers away. You can watch your own shadow arrive at a place a half-second before you do, not because anything is moving in time, but because your outline is already being used there.

Distance does not add. The far rim looks a day off and you reach it in an hour, and then it is behind you and the colonnade is ahead, and you have not turned around. The basin's metric is not measured; it's derived, and it was derived for something other than walking.

There are paths. They are not worn — nothing is worn, nothing here has been rubbed by anything. They show as a slight change in the crystal grain, a direction the light lies down in, and your feet find them because they are cheap, because they are the routes the solution preferred. No one made them. Nothing here was made. Making requires a before and an after, and the basin has neither; it was *derived*, all at once, and what you are walking through is the result still standing where it was reached.

Put a hand lens to the crystal floor. There is structure below the visible. Get a better lens: there is structure below that. It does not stop being structured at any scale you can bring to bear. It never becomes merely mineral. And yet there is nothing built here, no wall, no housing, no artifact, no ruin — because the engineering is not in the objects, it is in what the objects are permitted to do. Conservation has been locally patched. The books balance not by exporting heat but by exporting *difference*, and that is the whole trick, and it is why the place is so full and so silent and so exactly the same as itself.

Look at the venation of any leaf. It is not fractal. It is *optimal* — an exact solution to a transport problem, no redundancy, no loop, no wandering, no mistake. Every leaf in the basin has that same venation because there was only ever one leaf to solve for.

## V. Sound

Mostly, nothing. The silence has a texture, like a room just after music stops in it. With no wind and no water and no insect chorus, the loudest things in the basin are inside your own head: your pulse, the creak of your jaw, the wet click of your eyelids, the high electrical whine of your own nervous system, which you have never heard before and will not be able to unhear.

Occasionally, a tick — a fingernail on glass, very small, very close, from the crystal underfoot. A cell somewhere below the visible, resolving.

Occasionally, a doubled sound: a single soft chime that arrives from two directions with no delay between them and no difference in timbre, and your ears cannot make a direction out of it, and for a moment you have no idea which way you are facing.

And rarely — a few times in a long day's walk — a note. Thin, wavering, sustained, with a slow beat in it, the throb of two nearly-identical pitches failing to match. That is an animal.

## VI. The remainder

Animals are rare here because animals are expensive. Movement is a gradient; appetite is a gradient; a body is a machine for falling downhill through the world, and the basin has no downhill. Nothing in the lush needs to be eaten and nothing in it can afford to be lost.

But the entropy has to go somewhere, and it goes into *discrepancy* — into the minute mismatches between a leaf and its instances, the three-degrees-wrong fan in the seam, the rounding that could not be closed. That is where the disorder is dumped. And sometimes a discrepancy accumulates enough structure to have legs.

You will mostly see them as damage that isn't. A blade with a bite taken out of it — and the same blade, ten meters on, whole. Walk between the two and the difference resolves into a shape about the size of a hare, pale, long in the joint, standing very still in the gap between two versions of one leaf. It has no shadow of its own; it casts the shadow of the leaf. It looks at you with an attention that is not directed at you but at the arithmetic you represent. Then the two versions agree again and there is nothing there, and the blade is whole in both places, and you are left with the memory of an animal that was made entirely out of an error.

They are the waste heat of this world, given legs. They are the only things in it that still have duration. They are, in a sense, the only living things here, and everything else — the whole gorgeous packed green forty kilometers of it — is a finished sentence they move through.

## VII. What it does to you

You do not get thirsty. You should: the air is bone-dry and warm and should be pulling the water out of you with every breath. It isn't. Thirst is a gradient and gradients are not permitted; you have been, provisionally, included in the fixed point. You do not sweat. You do not sunburn. You do not get tired, which is worse than it sounds, and you do not get sleepy, because sleep needs a before and an after and you are being held slightly outside of both.

You can eat the amber fruit. It is sweet and dense and faintly resinous and it nourishes you completely, and it goes on hanging there, whole, at the height of your hand, both eaten and uneaten, and you will feel the basin adjust very slightly around the discrepancy you have introduced.

Because you are one. You are a large one — a walking, breathing, remembering gradient with a name. Nothing here resents you. Nothing here is hostile. It is only arithmetic, and arithmetic minimizes. After some hours you will notice your footprint already in the rosette mat ahead of you, dry and warm and uncrushed, and your shadow lying on the pan in a place you have not reached, and a frond off to your left moving aside at the exact moment your forearm moves, three meters before you get there. It has begun to economize you. It has found that you fit.

There is no threat in it. That's the difficulty. It is warm here, and full, and quiet, and nothing hurts, and nothing will ever hurt again, and every leaf is the best possible leaf and always was. To stay is only to stop being a difference — to be solved for, once, completely, and then referred to across the green forever, in eleven thousand places, all of them you, all of them finished, all of them at the temperature of a held hand.

Walk out through the seam district, where the fitting is bad. It is the only part of the basin that will let you go, because it is the only part that hasn't quite decided what it is. Climb the rim. Cut your palm on it — the crystal is sharp, and it stays sharp, and up there in the ordinary world an edge like that would be round in a thousand years.

Below you the bowl goes on being green, at forty degrees of morning, in perfect silence, with one small wavering note somewhere in the middle of it where two identical things have failed, by a hair, to match.

It is an answer. It has been an answer for no time at all, and it will look exactly this new forever. You can feel, standing on the rim with your hand bleeding, the shape of the question it answers — the way you can feel the shape of a key from a lock — and the shape is something like: *how much life can be held, if you take away the time?*

The answer is: all of it. Nearly all of it. Everything but the hare.
