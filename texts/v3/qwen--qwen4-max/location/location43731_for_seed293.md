You hear it a long way out, because there is nothing left in this country to stop sound.

The plain goes on for days at the same height. It is not flat the way a table is flat; it is flat the way a thing is flat when it has been worked over so many times that it has run out of ways to be otherwise. The mountains that made this sediment are gone — not ruined, gone, dissolved and carried off and laid down somewhere in an ocean that has itself since closed. Rain falls here and takes nothing away, because there is nothing left that rain can take. The rock at the surface is spent limestone, pale as a fingernail, and you can dent it with your thumbnail. It weeps. Beads of water stand out on it all afternoon and never grow heavy enough to run.

Then the ground opens.

It is a collapse basin, four hundred paces across, sixty deep, almost circular, with a rim of low rounded knuckles the locals call the teeth. From up on the teeth you can hold the entire place in one look, the way you hold a room. Beyond the rim, in every direction, the plain does nothing. Everything that happens, happens in the bowl.

The noise coming out of it is market noise. That is the first thing that is wrong, or the first thing that is right, depending on how long you have been walking. Hawking, hammering, argument, a hundred thousand small voices layered over a bass roar of moving water, all of it rising out of a hole in an empty land, warm and wet-smelling, like breath off a bakery.

---

Going down, you walk through the previous towns.

The basin wall is terraced in rings, and each ring is a generation of stumps. Not ruins in the stone sense: these are the lignified bases of former buildings, cut off at the height of a knee or a chest, standing in circles, each circle a little lower and a little older than the one above. The outer rings are still wood, grey and soft with rot. The middle rings are half mineral, wood grain preserved in silica, ringing under a knuckle. The innermost rings, down near the floor, are stone outright, and there are forty or fifty of them, and they are the only true stone in the settlement, and the exchange is built on them.

Nobody counts the rings. There are more than there are words for.

---

The floor is alive, all of it, edge to edge. One organism, or one thing that stopped bothering to be individuals so long ago the distinction has no purchase: a black-green mat, liverwort-textured, thick as a mattress, polished in the traffic lanes to the shine of an old stair tread. It is warm. It gives under the boot and comes back. It smells of wet bread and iron and grass cut three days ago. Under the polish you can see the faint reticulation of its plumbing, and if you stand still long enough you can watch a peristaltic wave cross a hand's width of it, unhurried, going somewhere.

They call it the Floor. It is the oldest continuous living thing anyone has ever put an instrument on, and the instruments disagree by orders of magnitude, and the disagreement is polite.

There is no soil here. There is no bare rock anywhere below the rim. There is no litter, because litter is eaten within minutes. Everything you will touch in this place — every wall, every rope, every bowl, every coin's casing, every boat, every bandage — is plant tissue, grown to specification, alive at least at the surface, and warm.

Over the Floor lies water, two inches of it, blood-warm, moving in slow braided sheets that shift their braiding every few minutes. It tastes faintly of iron and faintly of sugar. There is a pollen skin on it you can write a word in and watch the word heal.

And standing in the water, far apart, are the houses.

---

There are never eleven. There are eleven per day.

They are enormous — the tallest goes up ninety meters, which is absurd in a hole sixty deep, so that from the rim you look *down* at a spire — and there are few of them, and the space between them is the point. A hundred meters of open water and air between one house and the next. Wide plazas of nothing. Sky in the middle of town. You can stand at the base of one and see all the others clearly, and the emptiness between them is not unused; it is the working volume, the place where the swarms happen.

Each house is a trunk-built cathedral: bone-white ribs of laid cellulose with a nacre sheen, buttress roots arcing out into arcades that flood to the second rib at high water, panels of pressed leaf-glass the color of weak tea stretched between the ribs so the interiors are full of a soft drowned daylight. They breathe. You can see the walls breathe, a slow swell and settle, and on the hot afternoons the vent-stomata along the upper ribs open with a sound like a crowd taking a breath before speaking.

They are hollow and they are larger inside. Not infinitely; the fold is modest, a factor of three or four, and the extra rooms are not anywhere you can point to. A house expecting guests grows them overnight. A house in debt loses them, and the loss is not a demolition, it is a digestion, and it sounds like someone eating celery very slowly in the next room, amplified through a wall.

Nothing here is built. Everything here is *maintained*, which is a different verb. A shape is a subscription. You keep your house by paying its upkeep every cycle, and if you stop, the substrate reclaims the sugar and the phosphorus and the space, politely, over about nine hours, and the neighbors watch and say nothing because it happens to everyone.

---

The clock is the drain.

At the low south end of the basin there are three swallow holes in the Floor, ringed with root-net, and every forty-one minutes the great houses stop transpiring and let go, and the water table drops out from under the town.

It takes ninety seconds. The sheet water thins, gathers, runs; the braided channels reverse and consolidate; and then the whole basin inhales — a long sucking indrawn hiss you feel in the sternum before you hear it — and the roar begins, and the Floor is exposed, glistening, steaming, walkable, for nine minutes.

That is the market.

Everything that was floating drops onto its roots. The leaf-boats settle and become stalls. The root-nets tighten. Awnings unfurl with a crack like sails. Forty thousand small transactions occur in the mud at the bottom of a hole. Gleaners run the drain lip with their scrapers, harvesting the film that the water is trying to carry away, and the couriers come down out of the air in columns, and the noise doubles, and then at eight minutes a bell-frog somewhere starts the count and the whole town climbs back into itself, unhurried, absolutely precise, and the water comes back up through the Floor from below, warm, and the market floats again.

Two hundred and thirty times a day. It has been doing this, by the most conservative reading of the stump rings, for longer than there were mountains.

---

The animals are everywhere and you keep not seeing them, because most of them are smaller than the resolution of your attention, and because the emptiness is scheduled. The plaza reads bare; then a column of tellers crosses it — thumb-length flying things, half insect and half leaf, carrying contracts as scent-packets in the hollows of their thoraxes — and for four seconds the air is a solid braid of bodies and noise, and then it is empty again and your eyes insist nothing happened.

There are ribbon eels in the sheet water. There are bell-frogs whose calls the town has borrowed for its clock, so that timekeeping here is a species you can feed. There are mites in the pelt at ten thousand to the handful, and they, not the merchants, do most of the actual work of the world. There are midges, and the midges take a blood-tithe from everyone, and no one has ever proposed doing anything about it, because the midges also carry the audit samples.

And once a day a wader comes across the basin: a stilt-legged bird-thing eleven meters tall, moving with terrible slowness, putting down feet the size of dinner plates. Its footprints are the only holes ever made in the Floor. They close in about an hour. When it crosses, the market stops. Not out of reverence — the crossing takes ninety seconds and the drain does not wait for anybody — but because everyone likes to watch.

---

There is no fire in this town. Nothing burns; nothing has burned here in a very long time. There is no metal except a handful of meteoric relics kept in the exchange as collateral and curiosity. You can lay your palm on a working machine and it is cool and slightly damp.

The machines compute in the water table. A thought is a wave. This is not a metaphor; it is the reason the houses are spaced as they are, and the reason the plazas are kept empty, and the reason a bad price is dangerous. In the crash of the year the ledgers call the Sour Drain, the seiche stood up two meters at the north wall and drowned nine people in bad news.

The engineers here work confidently with a conserved quantity they cannot derive. It is not mass, not energy, not information; it has its own unit, named after a woman who died determining it; it balances to eleven decimal places in every closed transaction, and no one has ever found the symmetry underneath. They use it the way a mason uses gravity. Ask about it and you will get a shrug and an anecdote and a very good lunch.

And under all of it, the actual business: phosphorus.

The land is leached out. Has been for ages. There is no new mineral coming in, no dust, no uplift, no fresh apatite; every atom of phosphorus in this bowl has been through ten thousand bodies and is going through more. That is what the money is. A coin is a grain of phosphate in a leaf-glass ampoule, and a loan is a claim on the bones of something not yet dead, and the futures market is a futures market in the literal sense: contracts on bodies that have not been grown, priced by how well they will hold their shape and how promptly they will agree to come apart.

Because there is a leak. Every drain, a little of it goes down the swallow holes and out through the karst and into the sea, and it does not come back, and it never will.

The houses are kidneys. That is what the cathedrals are, under the architecture: ninety meters of root-net and membrane, straining the outflow, catching the last grains before the dark takes them. The bazaar is the trading floor of a salvage operation. The gleaners at the lip are the front line. All that shouting is a rearguard action against a hole in the ground, conducted at a profit, for a hundred million years.

---

And the light is completely ordinary.

That is the thing you are least prepared for. Ordinary sun, coming in slant over the teeth in the morning and standing straight overhead at noon; ordinary shadows; dust and pollen turning in the air the way dust turns anywhere. Caustics off the sheet water wobbling on the undersides of the leaf-glass, which is a thing ponds do. Warm — not hot, no season, shirt sticking to the small of your back at four in the afternoon, no chill at night, the air always at the dew point so that every surface sweats a little and the whole bowl smells green and sweet and slightly feverish. Tuesday light. It falls on impossible things and does nothing strange at all.

---

Come up onto the teeth at dusk, when the drain has just closed and the water is rising and the houses are relighting themselves rib by rib, and listen to it start again below you: the argument, the applause of a wall going up, the celery-sound of another coming down, the frogs taking the count, the water gathering itself to fall.

You came here thinking the ancient thing was the plain, and the frenzy was the exception, a bright fever in a finished land.

It is the other way around. The plain is finished; it finished before there were eyes. The frenzy is the stable state. This bowl is what a landscape looks like when it has nothing left to spend and cannot afford to stop moving — the last of the mountains, dissolved, redistributed, and holding itself in circulation by main noise, ten thousand transactions a minute, forever, in a hole four hundred paces wide.

Below you the bell-frogs begin the eight-minute count. The town goes up into its houses. The water comes back warm through the Floor.

Out on the plain, nothing happens, in every direction, for a thousand miles.
