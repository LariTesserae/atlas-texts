The stair ends, and the first thing is warmth through the soles of the feet.

It comes up out of the rock. The plateau lies near the top of a young massif, high enough that the sky overhead has gone a thin, hard blue and the air asks for a deeper breath than the body wants to give, and yet the stone is warm as a sleeping animal. Blood-warm. It never cools. Water comes out of the ground at the temperature of the inside of a wrist and spreads across the whole shelf in films and threads and shallow sheets, and the rock holds that heat all night, and the night gives it back to the morning, and nothing anywhere on this table of land has ever been cold.

The second thing is the silence, which is not empty but simply small. There is a sound like silk drawn over silk, everywhere, at the very floor of hearing. It is water going over lips of stone no higher than a knuckle. None of it falls. Nothing here plunges or hammers; the whole plateau is a staircase of shallow pans, each one brimming into the next by a hair's depth, and the water walks down it rather than dropping. Stand still and after a while you hear your own blood, and then, very far off, the tick of someone scraping.

The stone is called nothing in particular by the people who live here; it is simply the stone, and it is being made today.

The springs carry lime. They come up loaded with it, so full that the water tastes of chalk and a wet finger drawn across a lip leaves a ghost that dries pale in three breaths. Where the water moves — where it tumbles over a rim, breaks on a pier, hurries through a narrow run — it lets that burden go, and lays it down, grain by grain, in crusts and curtains and cauliflower knobs and fluted drapes. Where the water stands still and quiet, it takes stone back. Slowly. Patiently. A basin left standing for a season will be measurably thinner in the wall, and a channel left running for a season will have thickened until it chokes itself.

That is the whole law of the place, and everything else follows from it.

The features are sharp because they are new. Not new as in recently broken — new as in recently *poured*. The rims of the terraces have edges like cooling wax, sharp enough to open a careless heel. The basins are lily-padded, lobed, undercut, hung with stone curtains no thicker than a fingernail that ring faintly when touched. There is no gravel anywhere. No scree, no rounded boulder, no deep soil, no dust of the sort that comes from a mountain being patiently taken apart over ages. Nothing here has had time to be taken apart. The land rose out of water recently enough that the animals have not yet climbed to it, and the water that fell off its edges has been coming back as its own rain ever since, and the whole shelf sits in a balance so exact that the people who maintain it can tell you, by weight, how much stone the city laid down yesterday and how much it took back, and the two numbers are the same, and were the same last year, and will be the same when their grandchildren's grandchildren set the notched stick into the outflow to check.

So it is a young landscape that will never grow old. Every surface you touch is days or weeks old and always will be. Run a palm along a wall and it feels like the inside of a shell, faintly sugared, faintly damp, and the wall is not ancient and is not new; it is simply being kept.

Across all of this lies a city, and the city is mostly water.

From the high shoulder above the source you can see nearly the whole of it at once, and it looks like lace laid over a hide: a white net of channels running for miles down the shallow gradient of the plateau, joining, splitting, braiding around basins the size of threshing floors, crossing one another on low piers, gathering into long straight runs that go arrow-flat to the horizon and then bend, together, toward the low edge. There are thousands of works. Sluices, plugs, weirs, spillways, settling pans, drying floors, causeways, culverts, stairs cut into fresh rims, retaining walls with the weave of the baskets they were formed on still legible in the stone. There are halls and roofless rooms and granaries and long low dormitories, and they are spread out across this apparatus at distances that seem, to a visitor, absurd — a house alone on a terrace with a half-hour of empty white on every side, a meeting hall standing in the middle of a plain of brimming pans with nobody within a mile of it.

The city has the bones of a great town and the population of a village. It was not built for the people who live in it. It was built by them, over a long time, because the water required it, and the water requires more every generation, and there are not enough hands, and so the fabric goes on ahead of the bodies like a tide that has outrun the moon. Whole districts stand empty and perfect, maintained by whoever passes through on the round, their channels running, their cisterns standing, waiting.

Nothing here is made by cutting. Everything is made by *forming*. You lay a withy frame, a mat of split sedge, a coil of rope, a bundle of reeds, in the path of moving water, and you leave it, and the water skins it in stone; and then you burn out the reeds and you have a pipe, or you soak out the withies and you have a wall with the pattern of the weave printed forever in its face. The tools are wooden wedges driven into a seam and then wetted so they swell and split the fresh stone; bone scrapers; sedge rope; clay plugs; levers; a copper awl or two, cold-hammered from nuggets the spring brings up, kept as heirlooms and used mostly for ceremony. There is no kiln smoke except at the lime-burners' pans on the low side, and even that is a thin blue thread you can see through.

The work — the actual daily labor of the place — is the turning of the runs.

Every structure has two states: running and standing. A wall that is running thickens; a wall that is standing thins. So the water is moved. Paddles are lifted and set, plugs are pulled and pressed, a channel that has been carrying for a month is closed and the neighboring one opened, and the flow goes elsewhere and begins its slow construction there while the first begins, grain by grain, to be unmade. Done correctly, at the right intervals, the city neither grows nor wastes. It holds. A district's whole shape is a schedule, and the schedule is the only calendar anyone keeps: not months, not seasons — there are none — but *which rooms are running*. Children are born in the week the north cisterns stand. Debts fall due when the Long Run is turned. A person's age is reckoned in turnings, and the notched tally-sticks in the halls record cycles rather than events, because there are no events.

They are careful about where they put their hands. A worker who stands in a running channel long enough will find a glaze on the ankles by evening, and a stiffness in the hair of the forearm, and everyone's palms are glassy and shine in the sun like the inside of a shell. They grease themselves with sedge oil before a shift and scrape after. The dead are given to the standing rooms, where the water is quiet and takes things back, and in a season there is nothing to find, and this is thought proper: the city gave them their shape and now has it again.

The green follows the water exactly. In the seams and the warm runs there are streaming apricot and olive threads of algae, waist-deep cress, moss so thick you can push a hand into it to the wrist and feel warmth at the bottom. Ferns come out of the undercut lips in shelves. In the shallow sinks where a little sediment has been allowed to gather there are rush beds and sedge fields, cultivated, cut on a rotation that exactly matches their regrowth, and a starchy tuber the people roast in the warm ground, and one low pale-barked tree that grows wind-bent in the sheltered pockets and nowhere else, and never higher than a man. Between all of it: bare stone, acres of it, warm and glaring and clean. The plateau reads as embroidery on white cloth. Lush in the threads. Empty in the fields.

There are no animals. No fish in the pans, no larvae in the algae, no snails on the rims, no worms in what little soil there is, no birds at all. Nothing has yet climbed the cliff, and the cliff is very tall, and the land is very new, and the seeds and spores came up on the wind long before anything with legs could think about it. The people say this plainly, without grief, as a fact about timing. It means dawn has no sound. It means the reed beds stand unbothered and the cress is never nibbled and the algae mats never tear. It means the loudest thing on the plateau, most days, is a bone scraper on wet stone half a mile away, and the second loudest is someone breathing.

Light is ordinary and enormous. The sun comes up over the far shoulder and the whole city turns to glare, and everyone works under a sedge hat with the brim down, and the middle of the day is spent in the shaded standing rooms. The stone throws light up under the brim so that faces are lit from below, which makes everyone look patient and slightly unreal. Afternoon brings cloud — the same cloud, at the same hour, the plateau's own water coming home — and a warm rain that tastes faintly of chalk and lasts what it always lasts, and then the sun again, low and long, and the pans go copper, and the stars come out steady and hard and do not twinkle much in the thin air.

At the low edge, the water leaves.

It goes over the rim in ten thousand threads and the cliff is so tall that not one of them arrives. They come apart in the air, they fray, they become a hanging white haze that drifts and thins and rises, and the cloud floor below the plateau lies at exactly the same level it lay at yesterday, a flat pale sea with the shoulders of neighboring massifs standing out of it, each with its own faint lace of terraces, most of them empty, one or two with a thread of smoke. There is no roar at the edge. You can stand with your toes over it and hear the person beside you swallow. The water leaves without a sound and comes back without a sound, and the springs deliver, and the notched stick reads the same notch.

It is not that nothing happens here. Everything happens: the stone is laid, the stone is taken, the reeds are cut and grow, the channels are turned, the children are born and the dead are dissolved, the rain falls at its hour. It is that all of it happens exactly, and comes round, and happens again, and the city at dusk is the same weight and the same shape as the city at dawn, warm underfoot, silent, sharp-edged, three days old and forever three days old, holding itself in place by hand.
