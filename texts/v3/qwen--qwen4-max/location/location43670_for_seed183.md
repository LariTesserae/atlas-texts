# The Tally

## Approach

You come up the last of the stair — cut steps, worn shallow, going nowhere now, the top eleven treads buried in their own dust — and the first thing that arrives is not the view. It is the noise.

It is not wind. Wind has no pitch. This has thousands of pitches, all of them small and hard and metal-bright, arriving together in a continuous hail: a market crowd heard from a rooftop, applause that never resolves, rain on a tin roof the size of a parish. You shout to be heard and your shout goes nowhere; the sound eats it. Above the lip the air is thin enough to taste of blood, and the sky at zenith is a violet so dark it looks bruised, and the sun is a white hole with no warmth in the look of it, and everything, everything is lit from below as well as above.

Then the view catches up. A hanging shelf of blue-grey stone, four miles long and a mile across, tilted very slightly west, held between two peaks like a tray passed between hands. On its southern edge, nothing: a gulf full of slow cloud, two thousand meters of it, and the cloud glowing from inside where the sun gets into it. On the shelf itself, standing shoulder to shoulder across the whole visible width, in their thousands and tens of thousands, are cairns.

## Water

There is no rain here, or not often. The wet comes another way.

The eastern wall — a hundred-meter face of banded schist, pale feldspar and dark mica, fissile as pastry — weeps. It has always wept. Water arrives in it from the ice above and does not so much flow as *sweat*, beading out along every seam, gathering, letting go. Below the wall the cloud sits. It is a resident, not a visitor: it fills the shelf most mornings, walks a little, thins at noon, comes back at dusk, and every cold surface it touches takes water out of it directly, the way a glass takes water out of a summer room.

So the stone is wet without being rained on. The plates glisten. The runnels are an inch deep and ankle-wide and there are ten thousand of them braiding across the shelf, and none of them is loud, and all of them together are deafening. Underfoot the stacked plates lie saturated, a stone sponge kilometers across, and when you step, the whole layer sighs and gives a little, and water comes up over the welt of your boot.

It is cool. Four degrees, six, eight on a bright afternoon. At dawn there is rime on every upward face and needle ice standing out of the wet clay in white hairs, fine as combed wool, and it collapses when you breathe on it. Your hands ache within an hour. But the dark plates take the sun and hold it, and you can lie flat on a slab at freezing and be warm along your whole front, and cold along your whole back, and the light will come up off the wet stone under your chin and into your eyes from below until you put the slit-slate on: a thin curved plate of split schist with a sawn slot, worn like a mask. There are dozens of them lying about. They are all the same mask.

## The Ringing

The schist splits into plates. Plates ring.

This is the whole acoustic fact of the place and everything else follows from it. A hand-sized plate, struck, gives a note like a struck glass, high and short. A plate the size of a door gives a low clang you feel in the sternum. Thickness sorts pitch; the water sorts thickness. Every drip from the wall, every bead let go from a seam, every runnel slipping over a lip of stone lands on a plate tuned by long weather to some particular note, and there are perhaps two hundred thousand such strikes a second across the shelf.

That is the crowd. That is the applause. It does not stop, and it is not random, and after a day you begin to hear the shape in it — long phrases, recurring figures, a texture like counting.

The old people who worked here plugged their ears with mineral wool. There is a seam of it in the north wall, a soft grey fibrous rock, mountain flax, stone that can be combed and spun. They wove it. There are cords of it still, and a veil, and a mat, all of it stone, all of it faintly warm to the touch and utterly indifferent to rot, because nothing here rots. Nothing here has ever eaten anything.

## Nothing Alive

Look for moss and you will not find it. No lichen on the young plates, no crust, no green in the seams, no seed, no spore-drift, no midge over the water, no bird crossing the gulf. The water is sterile enough to drink and tastes of iron and cold cellar and, faintly, of lightning.

The one exception is at the western end, where the stone is old: a matte black film in the damp joints, thin as soot, that thickens where water runs and gives off, on the coldest mornings, a fine white fur of ice-hair. It does not spread. It has exactly the extent it had in the oldest tally-marks. It may be a fungus. It may be a stain that has learned a habit. It is the entire biosphere and it occupies about four square meters, and standing near it you feel not kinship but a sort of embarrassment, as though you had walked in on something private.

## The Cairns

They are dry-stacked, no mortar in the joints, plates laid flat and graded, each stack chest-high to head-high, each capped with a single broad plate set level as a table. Between them run lanes barely wide enough for a body, and the lanes branch and rejoin, and from the lip the whole shelf reads as a single dense grey organism, a coral, a brain.

At the base of every cairn, where the lowest plate meets the wet ground, there is a daub of grey clay the size of a plum. And pressed into each daub, still crisp after what must be centuries, is a thumbprint.

The same thumbprint.

Every one. Ten thousand cairns and one thumb: a short broad print with a scar-notch across the whorl, left of center. You check twenty and then a hundred, going down the lanes with your fingers, and the notch is always there, and the ridge-count is always the same, and the pressure is always the pressure of a right hand leaning in from the left.

At the western end the prints are worn nearly smooth. At the eastern end they are so fresh the clay still shines.

There are also tools, scattered, in the way a working place is scattered — a quartzite hammerstone left on a cap-plate, a flint scraper tucked into a joint, a cord-drill with a stone-wool thong, one small copper bead on a stone-wool thread. They are not many. They are *frequent*, because they are the same tools, over and over, at different stages of wear. The scraper appears forty times between the wall and the middle lanes: knapped-sharp, then worked, then dulled, then resharpened, then a nub, then a stain in the clay where a nub used to be. All one scraper. You can lay them out and watch a tool's whole life like a sentence.

## East and West

Here is the grain of the place. Time does not run forward here so much as it runs *west*.

At the eastern wall the stone is newborn. It has no weathering rind, no rounding, edges like broken crockery, and it is being made — the wall lays down fresh schist in visible layers, a leaf at a time, and the leaves peel and fall and are gathered. Walk west and everything gets older. Not different: older. The same cairn appears at intervals along the axis, first tight and sharp, then settled, then slumped, then a low mound, then a scatter, then a shadow of grey flour in the wet. The copper bead is bright at the east, brown in the middle, green further on, and at the west there is only a verdigris ring in the clay.

At the extreme west the shelf is sand and dust and shallow tarns the size of doors, and the dust goes down through the plates into the dark, and the water carries it east under the stone, and it comes out at the wall as new rock. The loop is closed. It has been closed a very long time. Nothing here is catastrophic; the forms are settled, the grades are steady, the water takes exactly what the wall gives. It is a mature country in a steady state, and the steady state is impossible, and it has been impossible for so long that it has become ordinary.

The sound tilts with the grain. Stand at the east and drop a plate: you hear it land just before it leaves your hand, a small clean anticipation, and then the drop, and your body flinches backward through its own morning. Stand at the west and drop a plate and the silence stretches, and stretches, and you have walked ten paces and begun to think about something else when the clang arrives from behind you, out of order, like a memory insisting.

In the middle lanes the two agree, and that is where you can think.

## Frays

Where a cairn has fallen and not been rebuilt, the pin comes out.

You see it first as a smudge — a stone that will not settle on an outline, edges soft and multiplied, like a thing photographed too many times on one plate. It is that stone at every age it will ever have, all at once, occupying the same handful of air: sharp and rounded and sand, superimposed, humming. The hum is every sound that stone will ever make, compressed. It is the only ugly noise on the shelf.

Do not touch it. Touch is exchange. A hand into a fray comes out older than the arm — grey-skinned, thin-nailed, cold in a way that does not warm — or comes out soft and small and uncoordinated, and either way it stays that way, and there is no reversing it, because west is the only direction reversal has here and west is where it goes to become dust.

The cairns pin the stones to one age each. That is what the cairns are *for*. The shelf is stitched down, cairn by cairn, plate by plate, and the lanes are the seams of the stitching, and where the stitching is dense the world is crisp and hard-edged and behaves. The density is not decoration. It is load-bearing.

## The Bothy

Near the middle, where the grain is honest, there is one building.

Low, corbelled, a beehive of graded plates with the joints packed tight with stone-wool and clay, a crawl-door facing away from the wall, a lintel of a single dark slab. Inside: a sleeping hollow worn to the shape of a body, a niche for a lamp that has never held oil because there has never been oil, a shelf, and a wall of tally-notches cut in courses, thousands, in groups of five, going up out of reach.

It is the only quiet place on the shelf. The wool takes the ringing down to a pressure behind the ears. And in that pressure you hear what the ringing is doing.

It is counting. Each strike is a count; each note is a place-value; the water is a hand running over an instrument of stacked stone, keeping the tally of what exists, out loud, continuously, at a volume that fills a valley. The cairns are the register. The drips are the reading. Nothing here is real by default — reality is a thing being *said*, fast, without pause, and the saying is the noise, and the noise has not stopped since before the notch in the thumb.

The bothy's hollow is warm. It is always warm. There is no one in it.

## The Work

At dawn, after the rime goes and the melt starts and the shelf crescendos into what the tally-marks call the loud hour, the clay daubs at the eastern cairns are wet and newly pressed.

Each cairn gains a plate. Not all at once — one here, one there, the way a hedge grows. If you sit very still in the lanes with your ears plugged and your eyes on a stack, you will not see the plate arrive. You will hear it: under the ringing, close, the small flat *set-down* of stone on stone, and the shift of weight, and something that could be a breath taken through the nose by someone lifting.

You will not see anyone. There is nothing here to see. There is no animal on this shelf, no bird, no beetle, no worker; there is only one worker's thumb, distributed along four miles of gradient, and the work going on, and the tools lying where they were left in every one of their ages.

Once a year the grain swings a few degrees, and the districts move — the newborn ground creeps north, the dust-end swings toward the lip — and the cairns must be rebuilt along the new axis, and they are, and the old ones are left to slump where they stood, and that is why there are so many. Ten thousand cairns is not a monument. It is maintenance.

## The Lip

Go to the south edge in the late afternoon, when the light comes in flat and the wet plates throw a second sun up at you and the shadows go blue and razor-thin.

The water leaves here. It goes off the lip in threads too thin to fall properly, unraveling into the cloud, and the cloud takes them, and the sound of their landing comes back up much later, from a long way down, out of order, softened into something like a crowd in another room.

Below, the cloud is bright. Above, the sky is nearly black at the top and the stars are there at noon if you can shade your eyes. Behind you the whole grey shelf is ringing, counting, holding itself in place, one plate at a time, with no one doing it and the work never falling behind.

Before you go down, look at the clay at the base of the last cairn by the stair. There is a fresh print, and beside it an older one, and beside that one older still, going west, going smooth.

They are not the broad scarred thumb.

They are yours.
