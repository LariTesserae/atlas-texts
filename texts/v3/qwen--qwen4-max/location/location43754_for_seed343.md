## The Head

There is no shape to it. That is the first thing, and the last thing, and the thing that visitors cannot be made to understand until they have stood on it for a full day.

A plain at four thousand three hundred meters, ninety kilometers across, falling eleven meters over forty of them. It is the end of a mountain range — not the ruin, not the rubble, the *end*, the last term of a sum that ran for three hundred million years. Everything that could be carried away was carried away. What is left is a surface so level that a spilled cup of water will sit for an hour deciding which way to go, and then evaporate without deciding. The horizon is a ruled line. Clouds have shadows here and the shadows are the only relief, sliding over the felt at the speed of a walking ox, and when one passes you the temperature drops four degrees and the plain goes from gold to olive to gold again.

The light is ordinary. That should be said early, because nothing else is. Clean equatorial daylight, a normal blue overhead going white at the rim, cumulus building at two in the afternoon and raining at four. No green suns, no doubled shadows. The sky is the least strange thing here.

The ground is warm. Not sun-warm — sun-warm is the top centimeter only. Push your hand down into it and at half a meter it is blood-warm and stays that way all night, because the ground is composting. The ground is peat: a felted, woven, layered mass of rhizome and root and moss and hollow fiber, two to six meters thick, laid over the whole plain without a break. There is no soil. There is no exposed rock anywhere on the Head except one knuckle of it near the eastern rim. Dig down through the felt and you do not find stone.

You find air.

## The weight rule

Nothing heavy can exist here. This is not a custom or a superstition; it is the load rating of the world.

The limestone that once made this plain has been dissolving from underneath for a hundred million years, warm rain going down and coming back out at springs in a country nobody here has ever seen. The rock is gone. What remains of it stands as posts: pillars thirty to eighty meters thick, spaced two to nine kilometers apart, holding up nothing — because the roof between them fell in long ago, and the plants caught it.

The felt grew across the collapse scars and kept growing, and now it spans the void on its own account, kilometers at a stretch, anchored at the pillars, carrying itself. It can carry itself. It cannot carry much else. It is mostly air by volume, a foam of hollow fiber, tough as rope in tension and useless in compression, and it sags under a load the way a hammock sags under a sleeping dog.

So: no trees. There is a species here that would be a tree six hundred meters lower down; on the Head it grows horizontally, a woody runner pressed flat into the felt, sending up hand-high leaf fans and sending nothing up any further. The forest is lying down. It has been lying down for four million years. There are no boulders, no walls, no monuments, no cairns worth the name. The largest resident animal weighs three hundred grams and has hollow bones.

And underneath: the Room. Four hundred meters tall in the middle districts, sixty at the margins, thirty kilometers wide in a single unobstructed span. Cold-looking in the way of all big air, but warm, twenty degrees, steady, humid. Almost nothing in it. Posts standing in the dark like the legs of a table built for something the size of weather. Between them, kilometers of clean empty volume with a luminous green ceiling far above and a pale floor far below and nothing at all in the middle except drifting motes and the slow traffic of birds.

Everything alive here is a skin on a hollow. The biomass is enormous and it is two meters thick and it is wrapped around a void you could put a mountain range inside. That is the arithmetic of the place: total life, near-total emptiness, and they are the same fact seen from two sides.

## Going down

The holes are called seams. Some are old collapse scars the felt never closed, ringed with a lip of tougher, ropeier growth where the plant has doubled and tripled its weave to take the tension — you can see the reinforcement from a kilometer off as a dark whorl in the plain, like the grain around a knot in wood. Some are cut. Cutting is a licensed act and the license is a song, which is to say a set of measurements memorized as a melody, and you do not cut where the note changes.

Down a seam on a cord ladder, and the first twenty meters are the worst, because you pass through the underside of the world.

The ceiling is roots. A hanging mat of them, meters deep, felted and dripping and furred with pale fungus, and it is *occupied*. Every square handspan of it is occupied. This is where the animals are. Not in the volume — on the surface, on the underside of the surface, in a layer as thin relative to the Room as the skin on a drum is to the room the drum is in.

And it is loud.

## The sound

The grain is an insect the size of a thumbnail, black-brown, with a rasp on its abdomen and a life spent mostly underground: seven years as a nymph in the peat, eating root, then eleven days as an adult, plastered to the ceiling in a layer three deep, singing. There are, at a modest estimate, ten to the fourteen. They emerge in tides that move across the plain at about two kilometers a day, and you can watch the tide come from a seam's edge as a darkening of the ceiling and a rising of the note.

Near a good tide the sound is a hundred and ten decibels and it is not a chirp, not a whine, not anything so pretty. It is a wet sawing. It is the sound of a saw going through green wood, multiplied until multiplication stops meaning anything, and it has no gaps in it, none, not one, and after twenty minutes your ears stop reporting it as sound and start reporting it as pressure, as a hand on the face.

Under that, the flitters: small brown echolocating birds, tens of millions, that roost in the dark districts and hunt the grain by clicking. Their clicks come as hail on leaves. Their wings, in the morning outflow, come as a river.

Under *that*, the Room itself, which has a reverberation time of about fourteen seconds — bare polished flowstone floor, hard sweating pillars — so nothing here ever quite finishes. Every sound is smeared into every other sound and the sum is a sustained tone with no attack and no decay, a chord that has been held since before there were ears.

And under *that*, below hearing, the Head. The whole felt plain is a membrane under tension and it has a fundamental around eleven hertz. You do not hear it. You feel it in the sternum and the sinuses and the loose fillings of your teeth. It flexes at dawn and dusk as the air temperature changes and the fiber contracts, and twice a day the plain *booms*, a soft enormous concussion that stops the birds for a quarter of a second and makes the dust cones down on the floor shiver and slump. Wind across the seam-mouths adds a moaning fifth on top, organ pipes a hundred meters long, wandering in and out of tune with the weather.

The people who live here go deaf in a particular pattern, losing the middle frequencies first, and they read lips and hands from childhood, and they consider a quiet place to be a place where you can hear your own heart, which by any other standard is not quiet at all.

## Water, arriving late

Rain falls on the Head two hundred days a year, briefly, in the late afternoon, and vanishes into the felt like water into a sponge. It does not run off. There is nowhere for it to run to; the plain has no gradient worth the name and no channels except shallow wandering depressions a hand deep, the drainage-marks of a wetter age, now grown over and only visible when the light is low.

It comes down into the Room three days later.

That is the water of this place: delay. The ceiling drips for days after the sky has cleared, a slow interior rain falling four hundred meters through warm air, and most of it never lands. It arrives as weather. There is a river at the northern lip that sinks into a seam and falls as a plume and is drunk by the air two hundred meters down, and beneath it there is no pool, only a permanent standing mist and a hiss and a rainbow that appears at ten in the morning and goes at two.

What does land, lands at the drip-points, and there the floor is wet: cones of guano and chitin and spent substrate, tens of meters high, damp and steaming, with black pools at their summits and terraces of orange bacteria down their flanks. Between the cones the floor is bone dry, pale flowstone, polished, empty, ringing.

And the pillars sweat. Warm humid air touches the old cold stone and condenses, and every post in the Room runs with water down its whole height, and every post is wrapped from floor to ceiling in a sleeve of root — the felt sends them down, taproots hugging the stone, drinking the condensation and mining the dust cones for the fertility that falls out of the ceiling every day and is otherwise lost.

The sleeves are the only living connection between the top of the world and the bottom of it. They are also the ladders. They are also the reason nobody, ever, cuts one.

## Light, from underneath

At noon the ceiling glows.

The felt is thin enough and pale enough at its lower face to pass light, and the sun standing over ninety kilometers of it turns the entire roof of the Room into a lamp: a green-gold luminous plane, veined with the darker tracery of the big rhizomes, mottled where the peat is thicker, dimming to amber at the edges of the day. Below, dawn arrives as a slow greening of the ceiling and dusk as a slow browning, and the shafts that come down the open seams cross the dust like the hands of clocks, and the flitters sleep in the dark districts under the thick peat and wake in the bright ones.

It is a normal amount of light. It is simply coming from the wrong direction, and it has been filtered through something alive, so everything down there — the posts, the cones, the birds, your own hands — is the color of the inside of a leaf.

## What the people have

Perhaps four hundred of them, across ninety kilometers, in camps of five to twenty, and everything they own is light.

Tents of woven mat-fiber on pegs of bird bone, no walls, no hearths — a fire on the felt is a wound and a weight and a death, so they cook on flint plates set in clay at the seam-lips, over fuel carried up from the dry floor. Cloaks of stitched flitter skins, feathers inward, forty birds to a cloak. Blades of flint the size of a thumbnail, weathered out of the dissolved limestone and gathered from pockets of red residual clay, the only stone in the world; hafted in resin; worn out fast, because the plants here pull silica out of that same clay and build it into their leaves, and the felt is abrasive, and it eats tools and teeth alike.

Cord. Cord everywhere, cord of twisted root-fiber, the ladder-cords and the cradle-cords and the seam-lip anchors, tested daily, spliced by specialists, the single technology on which every life here rests. Needles. Wax plugs. Long tubes of hollow root used as stethoscopes.

They hang under the ceiling in cord cradles and scrape the grain into baskets, and press it into cakes that keep for a year, and render the wax, and gather the guano from the cones to feed the sundew fields — because the peat is poor and the pockets of clay are poorer, and in those pockets grow the carnivorous plants, acres of sundews and bladderworts in the wet season, and at emergence the tide comes *up* through the seams as well as down, and the surface seethes with insects for eleven days, and the sundews glut and go scarlet and the whole plain smells of honey and rot.

There are three things built to last. Cairns of flint at the seam-mouths, knee-high, marking the safe lip. A listening house on the eastern rim: a membrane hut, no walls, a floor of stretched fiber over an open hole, where a listener sits with a mallet and strikes and reads the return, and knows the thickness of the world for two kilometers around by the pitch of its answer. And a low stone shelter at the Tooth, the one knuckle of original limestone still standing above the felt, the only place on the Head that will bear weight — where they go to touch rock, and where they leave their dead, because burial would be both a burden and a cut, and everything else is given to the seams.

Their paths are faint and extremely old. The felt grows two centimeters a year. A trodden line takes a century to fade. There are trails out there crossing eighty kilometers of empty plain that no living person walks, going to holes that closed before the language had its current words.

## The tide, and the fall

When the grain emerges in force, the roar jams the flitters. Their clicks are drowned; they cannot see the ceiling; they fly into it, into each other, into the posts, and for three days of every tide it rains stunned birds down through four hundred meters of green light onto the dry floor, where the sweepers wait — pale, long-legged, flightless things the size of a hand, that live in the dust and eat the fall and are eaten in turn by nothing at all, having no predators, having never needed any.

The people gather at the cones and take the sweepers too, and the birds, and it is the fat time, and it is also the dangerous time, because a soaked sagging mat plus a whole population running to the seams is how the Head tears.

They know this. The rules are short and they are all about weight and cuts: *carry little; walk apart; never sever a sleeve; never open a seam where the note drops; when the plain booms twice together, lie down.*

## The Slack

At the western end there is a district thirty kilometers across where the posts have failed. All of them. The last one went sometime in the deep past; there is a stump of it on the floor, wrapped in a dead sleeve, standing in a cone of its own rubble.

The felt spans it unsupported. It sags forty meters in the middle. From the rim it looks like a plain, and from the middle it looks like the inside of a bowl so wide you cannot see the rim, and the fundamental there has dropped below seven hertz, and the note is not a note anymore, it is a nausea, a wrongness in the gut, and the birds do not roost there and the grain does not sing there and no one goes.

It is not a ruin. It is the future, arriving early in one place. The posts are dissolving; they have been dissolving since before the felt existed; there are fewer than there were a hundred thousand years ago and there will be none. The stone has finished its work. The mountain is a plain and the plain is a hole and the hole is held open now by something that grows, and every century the load passes a little further from the rock to the root, and the note drops a fraction, and the children are taught the current pitch and made to sing it back, so that in eighty years their grandchildren will know exactly how much lower it has gone.
