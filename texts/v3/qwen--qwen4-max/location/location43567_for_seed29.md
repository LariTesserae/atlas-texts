## The Vesicle

From the plateau there is nothing to see.

Four thousand three hundred meters of black glass pavement, wind-scoured, tilted very slightly toward the east, and no snow on it — not because the summit is warm but because there is nothing here to fall. The air is thin enough to make a visitor stupid. The ground is warm enough to keep a hand comfortable in the shade of a boulder, and there are no boulders. There is a low ridge ofropy flow-texture, a scatter of spatter, and a stain of white salt around a fissure four millimeters wide, and that is the entire visible feature of this landscape. The salt has never been wet. It sublimed out of the vapor and settled as crystal directly, the way frost does, except there is no frost on this mountain and never has been.

Lean over the fissure and the world changes register. A breath comes out of it at seventy degrees, dry as a kiln, smelling of struck matches and hot iron. The rock around your face begins to buzz. Not the wind. The stone itself, at a frequency you feel behind the sternum before you hear anything at all.

Everything that matters here is inside a bubble three point six meters tall.

---

It is not a cave. Caves are made by subtraction — water finds a weakness and takes the mountain away grain by grain. This chamber was made by inflation. A gas bubble rose through a flow that was still plastic, stalled under a crust already stiff, and blew itself a room. Then the crust sealed and the room hung there in the rock like a flaw in a casting, and for a long time nothing opened it.

The opening is recent. Two centuries, perhaps three — a thermal crack propagating up from the cooling margin until it broke through to the plateau, four millimeters of daylight in a ceiling of black glass.

Because of that youth, nothing in here has been worn. The walls still carry their fire-polish, a skin of tachylite so fresh it reflects. The lavacicles hanging from the ceiling are sharp enough to open a palm; their tips still hold the last bead of the drip that made them. There is no clay, no weathering rind, no rounded grain, no talus, no sediment older than the room that contains it. Landscapes are usually the record of what has been removed. This one is a record of what is being added, and it has barely started.

---

At the floor, a seam. Not a hole — a seam, thirty centimeters long and the width of a knife, running along the base of the north wall, and out of it comes gas at seven hundred and forty degrees, and in absolute darkness it shows as a line of dull cherry red, the only permanent light in the world.

The gas carries melt with it: fine threads of basalt drawn out of the seam's lip by the velocity of the jet, spun the way a fiber plant spins rockwool, the way the wind spins Pele's hair off a lava fountain. Filaments one to twenty microns thick, meters long, gold-brown where they catch the ember light, brittle, hollow in places, beaded with tiny droplets of glass that froze mid-flight.

They have been accumulating for two hundred years.

The chamber is ninety-six percent full.

This is the first thing that has to be understood about the place: it is not a room with material in it. It is a wad of mineral wool with a little room left over. Push a hand into it and the fibers close around the wrist with a dry, squeaking resistance, thousands of glass threads flexing and sliding, and the sensation is not like soil or snow or fur but like the inside of an insulation batt — springy, dead, faintly abrasive, and utterly without moisture. Every filament is charged. Let go, and the wool clings to your fingers in a halo, and a blue spark jumps a millimeter to your knuckle with a snap you can hear over the roar.

---

The roar.

Here is the machine, and no one built it.

Cold air comes down the ceiling fissure along its lower lip; hot gas leaves along the upper. Between them, a temperature difference of six hundred degrees across a resonant cavity packed with a fine fibrous stack. That is the complete specification of a thermoacoustic prime mover. The stack absorbs and releases heat at the right phase of each compression, the oscillation feeds itself, and the chamber converts a temperature gradient directly into sound at an efficiency that would embarrass a nineteenth-century engine.

The fundamental is twenty-one hertz. You do not hear it; your sinuses hear it, your chest hears it, the fluid in your eyes hears it. It is a hundred and eighteen decibels of pressure walking back and forth through a four-meter room, and the harmonics stack up above it into something the ear can finally grip: a hard, hollow, industrial drone, the sound of a very large transformer in a very small concrete vault.

Above that, at the seam's lip, an edge tone — the jet shedding vortices against a glass edge — a whistle around sixteen hundred hertz that wanders with pressure. It slides up a whole tone when a storm passes over the plateau and down again over the following hour. That slide is the fastest event in this world.

And the wool eats everything else. Fiber that fine is a superb absorber; the hiss, the crackle, the small percussive sounds of a place made of glass all die within a centimeter of their birth. So the chamber is deafening and dead at the same time: enormous low pressure, no reverberation, no echo, no acoustic distance. Shout and your voice goes nowhere. The room takes it.

---

The whistle has organized the geography.

At sixteen hundred hertz in hot carbon dioxide, the half-wavelength is about nine and a half centimeters, and the fibers do not settle evenly. They drift into the pressure nodes and hang there, held by the standing wave and by their own static charge, and over decades they felt into shelves.

So the interior of the bubble is a stack of horizontal terraces — eleven of them, floor to ceiling, each a slab of matted glass wool two centimeters thick, separated by nine and a half centimeters of open gallery. Floor and roof of felt, and between them a slot of hot moving gas. That is the terrain. Not a slope, not a plain: a filing cabinet of fiber, four meters across, each drawer slightly less than a hand's height, running away into darkness in every direction until it curves up against the glazed wall.

The shelves are not level. They sag toward the middle where the wool is heaviest, and near the seam they are scorched brown and frosted by acid vapor — trace hydrogen fluoride, the only weathering agent in the world, taking a century to dull a filament's shine. Near the ceiling they are pale gold and clean and stitched with tiny yellow crystals of sublimate sulfur, which can only exist in the coolest twenty centimeters of the chamber.

The galleries between shelves are the districts. Each has its own temperature: sixty-eight degrees at the top, forty-four in the middle, ninety and lethal at the bottom. Life occupies the middle six.

The film comes first: a matte grey-green layer forty microns thick on every fiber surface, sulfur- and iron-oxidizing bacteria, living on the seam's hydrogen sulfide and on the oxygen that trickles down the ceiling crack. Not plants. Nothing here has ever been green in the way a leaf is green; the color is a coincidence of pigments. The film is the entire primary production of the world, and it is capped by the width of a four-millimeter fissure, which is why everything that eats it is small.

On the film: mites, armored and slow, at a density of roughly one per two cubic millimeters. Springtails in the cooler galleries, so densely packed that a square centimeter of shelf surface is a continuous moving pavement of them, a living crust that flexes when the twenty-one-hertz cycle passes and relaxes when it goes. Thysanurans — flat, silver, furnace-loving things, the sort that live behind industrial ovens — grazing the film in long files, three body-lengths apart, which is the only spacing this world permits.

Above them, pseudoscorpions, gripping single filaments with their pedipalps, waiting. A spider that does not build. A centipede that hunts the galleries end to end in a slow patrol of about forty minutes. And in the third and fourth shelves, the largest animal here: an amblypygid the size of a palm, flattened to two millimeters so it can move through a slot crowded with springtails, blind, its first pair of legs drawn out into whips four times its body length that it lays along the fibers ahead of it and reads.

Nothing in this chamber builds. No webs, no nests, no tubes, no burrows, no cases. There is not one made thing in the world except the wool, and the wool is precipitation. Every animal here is a squatter in the product of a machine. They fit the geometry because the geometry was there first, and any lineage that could not flatten itself to nine centimeters is gone.

---

They cannot hear each other. The drone would bury anything.

What they do instead is talk through the glass. Drumming, stridulation, substrate signals sent down a filament and read by slit sensilla and leg hairs — and mineral wool is a terrible conductor of the frequencies they use, which means a signal dies in about two centimeters.

Two centimeters is one neighbor.

So this is a society with no broadcast. Every message is passed body to body, mouth to leg, along the shelf. A predator's alarm at the northern wall reaches the southern wall in eleven minutes, relayed by ten thousand animals who each only ever tell the one beside them. Rumor moves through the chamber at half a centimeter a second. Nothing here has ever addressed the world; nothing here has ever been heard by more than what it touches. In a place with no space between anyone, speech has become the shortest-range thing there is.

---

There is no water.

Not a droplet, not a film, not a stain of damp on any surface; the dew point of the gas is far below anything the chamber ever reaches. The salt at the ceiling has never dissolved. The glass has never been wet.

The water is manufactured.

The film oxidizes sulfide and makes water as a metabolic byproduct — a few molecules at a time, held inside cells, never released. The mites get it by eating the film. The pseudoscorpions get it by eating the mites. Everything here has a waxy cuticle, uric-acid waste, and a gut that reclaims its own feces dry. Water passes up the food chain and back down through death and is never once permitted to be free.

The entire reservoir of this world is the bodies of its inhabitants. All of it together — every mite, every springtail, every flattened hunter in the third shelf — would come to perhaps a cup.

---

Darkness, with three exceptions.

The seam's dull red, which reaches only the lowest gallery and lights the underside of the scorched wool the color of an old bruise.

The ceiling fissure: a thread of the outside world, four millimeters wide and thirty long, through which comes a moving line of daylight that crosses the top shelf over the course of an afternoon, and stars at night, and once every few years a drift of ash. No animal responds to it. There are no eyes in this chamber; there is nothing an eye could do here that a hair could not do better.

And the static. The whole wool mass carries a field of several kilovolts per meter, sustained by the ceaseless rubbing of glass on glass in the acoustic cycle. When a shelf settles, charge redistributes and there is a scatter of tiny blue snaps in the fiber, dozens at once, gone in an instant — the only events in the world faster than a heartbeat. On the tips of the longest lavacicles, in the dry moving gas, violet beads of brush discharge sometimes stand and hiss. The animals use the field: a springtail crossing a gallery gap does not entirely jump; it is partly pulled.

---

The rules here are ordinary. That is what makes the place strange. Thermodynamics, electrostatics, a food chain limited by an aperture, a standing wave with a ruler in it. Nothing is impossible; everything is merely conjoined in a way that has probably happened only once.

And it is all going slowly in one direction.

The magma below is withdrawing — a hand's breadth per decade. The chamber cools by four tenths of a degree in ten years. Sound travels more slowly in cooler gas, so the whistle's wavelength shortens, so the node spacing contracts: the shelves are creeping toward each other by about a millimeter every twenty years. Meanwhile the jets keep spinning, and the shelves keep thickening, one millimeter a year.

The room is being manufactured shut.

The fauna have generations of six weeks. They have already been through some two thousand of them since the crack opened, and they are being quietly, patiently resized by their world — the amblypygids of the third shelf are measurably flatter than the ones in the old dead wool at the wall margin, and the largest hunters have already been squeezed out of the bottom two galleries and are not coming back.

In perhaps nine hundred years the galleries will be too thin for anything with legs. The stack will choke on its own product, the phase relationship will fail, and the twenty-one-hertz note will stop. The chamber will be a plug of glass fiber in a mountain: a fossil of an engine, four meters across, packed solid, silent, holding the dried bodies of everything that ever lived in it.

And above it, in the flow that made it, there are other bubbles. Dozens. A foam of sealed vesicles in the young basalt, most of them still closed, still hot, still spinning their wool in the dark with nothing alive inside them at all.

The plateau is not a landscape with a hidden room in it. The plateau is a foam of hidden rooms, and one of them has a crack, and the crack is four millimeters wide, and it is widening by a flake or two a century.
