## The Sink

You hear it before the road turns. Eleven kilometers out, on the high ordinary land where the wheat is still wheat, there is a sound in the ground that people from here stop hearing at about the age of four. It comes up through the tires. It is in the telephone lines. Visitors think it is a river, and then a motorway, and then a factory, and by the time the road bends at the datum post and the land drops away in front of them they have run out of comparisons and simply stand there with their mouths slightly open, which is what everyone does.

Below is the Sink: forty square kilometers of ground that is lower than the sea and lower than the fields you just drove through, a bowl eleven kilometers across and, at its center, nineteen meters below the rim. It did not exist ninety years ago. It is being made now. You are looking at the inside of something that is still under construction.

The light is wrong first. The bowl should be bright — salt country usually is, blinding, a place you squint in — but the ponds here are dark. They run from bottle-green through rust to a red so deep it is nearly black, and they drink the light rather than throw it back. Above them stands a permanent evaporation fog: the brine comes up from the wells at forty-one degrees into air at thirty-one, and the difference exhales a low white steam that never lifts and never quite thickens into cloud. The sun is a coin in it. The lamps on the pipe bridges are lit at noon and at midnight and there is no hour when anyone turns them off, because there is no hour when they are not needed. Sodium amber, mercury green, the small red eye of a level gauge. Everything is lit the way a corridor is lit.

And it is wet. Not raining — it rarely rains here, the rim keeps the weather out — but wet in every other way available to water. The ground is wet to its full capacity and cannot take another drop. The air is at saturation and cannot take another molecule. The brine is at saturation and cannot dissolve another gram, so it drops what it carries, everywhere, on everything, continuously. The freshwater injection mains sweat and drip along their whole length and have laid down their own pale stalagmite curbs beneath every flange. The rock faces at the rim weep in slow sheets. Your glasses fog when you get out of the truck and stay fogged. Sweat runs off you and does not dry, and after twenty minutes you stop noticing that you are wet, because there is no dry state to compare it to.

---

The noise has parts, and once you learn them you can't unlearn them.

Underneath everything is the return main: eight hundred millimeters of steel on trestles, running the length of the pan, full of hot brine moving at four meters a second. It produces a broadband roar with a low pulse in it where the pipe crosses a support, a sound with no gaps in it at all, not even the small gaps that wind and rivers leave. On top of that, the pump houses — six of them, spaced along the gathering header, each one a shed the size of a chapel containing a horizontal centrifugal pump the size of a locomotive. At the door of Number Three you are at a hundred and ten decibels and the sound has stopped being sound and become a pressure on the sternum. Inside the sheds nobody speaks. Nobody has spoken inside a pump shed since 1961. There is a hand language, thirty-odd signs, and it is used everywhere in the Sink, at the canteen, at the bus stop, in arguments.

Then the compressors, which are a different pitch, a whine that gets into the teeth. Then the substation hum. Then the wellheads — three hundred and forty of them, each a stump of pipework and valves standing in a crust collar, each hissing or ticking or knocking according to its age.

And then, filling every frequency the machines leave open, the flies.

There is a species of brine fly here whose larvae live in the shallows at a density that has been measured, twice, by people who then declined to publish the number. The adults walk on the surface film. Along the leeward shore of Pan Nine their cast pupal cases have piled into a windrow eighty centimeters high and four kilometers long, pale brown, dry, rustling, like a beach made of grain. The living flies above it make a hum you feel in the sinuses. Standing at the edge of the water you cannot see the water. You can see a moving silver skin with a few dark holes in it where a bird has stepped.

The men who work here are deaf by fifty. They are not unhappy about it. Several have said that the strangest thing about the noise is that it never changes: no gusts, no lulls, no dawn chorus and evening hush, nothing arriving and nothing leaving. It is a wall, and after a while you live on the other side of the wall, and the world is a quiet place with a vibrating floor. The gauge huts have a room lined with felt where you can go and sit for ten minutes and hear your own blood. It is called the quiet room. There is one chair.

---

The ground is the youngest ground in the country.

There is no soil. There has not been time for soil, and there is nothing here to make soil from — no leaf fall, no roots worth the name, no weather to break anything down. What there is instead is deposition. Every surface in the Sink is being coated, and the coating is becoming rock while you watch, at a rate of a millimeter or two a week.

Cut into it and you get a section like a wet cake: at the bottom, black sulfide mud that stinks of eggs and is older than the village; above it, gypsum mush, a grey porridge of interlocking swallowtail crystals; above that, the crust — halite in chevron and hopper forms, buckled into polygons a meter across, each polygon rimmed with a raised lip where the crystallization pressure keeps pushing. The polygons are not landforms yet. They are the beginnings of landforms. Nothing here has been carved. There are no valleys, no gullies, no integrated drainage; the water goes where the ground last sank and pools there and stays, and the channels are shallow undecided smears that change their minds over decades. Geologists call this deranged drainage. The men call it the mess.

The crust grows over things. That is the local verb: *grown over*. A wrench left on Pan Four in March is a low white bump by August and gone by the following spring. The rail spur to the old loading dock is gone to the rail head, and the sleepers are gone, and what remains is a long low ridge of salt with rust in it, running perfectly straight across the pan for two kilometers, which is the only straight line in the landscape that isn't a pipe.

Underneath, the voids. The freshwater goes down the injection wells into the evaporite bed two hundred meters below and eats rooms out of it — rooms a hundred meters across, rooms that have merged into halls, a whole hidden architecture of dissolution being unmade as fast as the crust above is being made. The rock salt creeps. It behaves like a very slow liquid under that load, and the caverns close at three or four centimeters a year, and the surface follows them down. Six centimeters a year, across the whole bowl. The map is redrawn every decade. The rim road has been regraded four times. There is a benchmark near Gauge Six with a brass cap, and the number stamped on it is no longer true, and everyone knows by how much.

That is the thing to understand about this place. It is not eroding. It is being laid down. It is an embryo of a rock formation, and the roar you are standing in is the sound of a stratum being deposited.

---

Nothing grows here, almost.

Salt sees to that, and so does the youth of the ground: even where a seep runs fresh, the substrate is a year old and will be buried in three. What there is, is in strands. Glasswort along the northeast seeps, jointed and glassy and turning red at the ends, in lines that follow water that has not yet gone bitter. A single tamarisk at the old farmstead, leaning, alive, its roots down into a lens of fresh water that has survived because the farm's collapsed cellar happens to funnel rain. Salt cedar and a smell of dust. On the mud, black-green algal mats, which are the actual vegetation of the Sink, the whole base of everything, sheets of them slick as wet vinyl and blistered with gas.

And one fig tree at the church, which nobody planted and nobody can explain, and which produces small hard fruit that the children are told not to eat and eat.

That is the list. In forty square kilometers, that is very nearly the whole list.

The animals did not get the memo.

The brine shrimp graze the algae in numbers that turn the ponds' shallows into a moving pink curd. The fly larvae work the bottom. Above them: avocets in the tens of thousands, sweeping the shallows in ranks with their bills sideways in the water; black-winged stilts standing in it like a scattered arrangement of pins; shelduck, grebes, phalaropes spinning in place; gulls that have learned to drop crabs onto the crust to crack them and have learned, further, that the crust also cracks crabs you drop from a lower height, and have adjusted. Nine flamingos arrived in a storm eleven years ago and stayed and are now nineteen. At the freshwater ditch on the west rim, where the seepage is sweet, there are eels, and frogs whose calling is inaudible under the pumps and continues anyway, throats working, a whole population conducting a conversation nobody has ever heard.

Free-tailed bats roost in the pipe galleries and the vaults of the drowned cellars and come out at dusk in columns you can see against the lamps, tens of thousands, and the insects they take are the ones the lamps draw. Rats in the trestle footings. Foxes on the embankments, thin, unbothered, walking through a hundred thousand birds that do not lift for them because a fox is quieter than the machinery and therefore not a thing that happens.

The birds nest beside the compressor housings, deliberately, in the loudest places available, because nothing that hunts by ear can hunt there. It is the safest ground in the district. The eggs are laid in salt. The chicks fledge in salt. There is no season to any of it: no spring pulse, no autumn crash, everything breeding continuously at the same rate all year, generations smeared into one another, a standing population rather than a series of events.

There is no gap anywhere. Larva against larva, crystal against crystal, pipe against pipe, bird against bird, sound against sound. Every phase of matter here is full. The only empty space left in the Sink is underground, in the caverns, and it is closing at four centimeters a year.

---

The built things are scattered and small and there are somehow always more of them than you remember. A gauge hut on stilts. A valve manifold under a corrugated hood. A concrete plinth with nothing on it and a number cast into the side. Survey tripods left standing for years because moving them would break the record. A cable tray on posts running out into the fog and simply ending, capped, waiting for a phase of the works that was cancelled in 1988. Wooden duckboards laid across the crust to a wellhead that was abandoned before the boards wore out.

And the older scatter, which the salt is taking. The rim road that switchbacks down and then stops at the water with its tarmac still marked in white dashes. Orchard stumps in rows in Pan Two, black, mineralized, hard as agate when you kick them. The church, which stands at the low edge with its floor under two meters of brine, its tower intact, its bell removed and reh hung in a new church on the high ground, where it is rung on Sundays and can be heard in the Sink faintly, at the wrong times, under everything. The cemetery was moved. Most of it was moved. There is a list in the Board's office of the ones that were not, with grid references, and the grid references are now water depths.

The people go home at night to the high ordinary land. A hundred and forty stay on shift. The canteen has a hand-language menu and a television with subtitles permanently on, and the noise outside comes through the wall as a warmth in the floor, and the men eat with their fingers because cutlery rattling is a thing you stop being able to hear and start being able to feel.

---

Go down, if you can get permission. There is a service shaft at the Kettle, and a ladder, and the air gets hotter with every rung and wetter, until at the bottom you are in thirty-eight degrees and a hundred percent humidity and a light like weak tea, and the walls are running.

This is the underside of the bowl: the old solution rooms, the ones the wells have finished with. Salt pillars, coarse-grained, translucent, wet to the touch and slick, sweating brine in continuous sheets that fall without splashing because the floor is already brine. The stalactites here are not limestone; they are halite, and they grow fast, a hand's length in a year, and they are soft, and if you break one it heals in a season. Everything is being coated. Everything is being joined. The pillars have begun to fuse to the floor with skirts of crystal. In one room the roof has fallen and the fall is already cemented into a single mass, blocks and rubble and pipe and a boot, indistinguishable, going grey, going solid.

And here, finally, you can hear the other sound — under the pumps, under the roar, down at the bottom of the audible: the brine eating the wall. A fine dense ticking, like rain on a leaf, like a fire very far off. Dissolution. The room getting bigger by a millimeter while the room next door gets smaller by four centimeters. The last emptiness in the place being quietly, patiently filled.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here has a season, or a tide, or a storm, or an event. The bowl sinks the width of a finger each year. The crust thickens the width of a fingernail each month. The ponds move one shade of red toward the next across a decade. The noise has not altered since the pumps were installed and will not alter until they are stopped, and when they are stopped the water will come back up over the pans and the crust and the rail ridge and the church, and it will still be saturated, and it will go on laying down.

Somewhere above all this, in a future that has no machinery in it, someone will core this ground and pull up a clean white bed with a rust line in it and a layer of small brown pupal cases pressed flat as shale, and will read from it a quiet, hot, shallow sea.

They will be almost right.
