## Sill

From the old country you go *down* to it. The path is a cut in the cliff, chipped out by hand in the first year, and it is still sharp underfoot — no rounded step-noses yet, no polish, every arris as crisp as the day the antler pick broke it. Weather has not had time to forgive anything here.

Halfway down, at the height of a raised hand, the cliff wears a dry grey band: barnacles, the ghosts of limpets, a felt of dead weed glued to the rock. That is where the sea used to sit. It is above the rooftops now. You pass under the old tide the way you would pass under a doorway, and the town opens beneath it, and the first thing you notice is that there is no space in it.

---

The bay came up in a night nineteen years ago. Two fathoms of uplift, one long grinding hour, and in the morning there was a floor where the fishing had been: a bowl of raw marl three hundred paces across, steaming faintly in the cool air, ringed by the cliff it had been hiding under. The wrecks lay in it sideways. The people who had lived along the shore looked at it for eleven days and then walked down and began to build, because it was flat, and it was sheltered, and it was theirs, and there was nothing on it to argue with.

They call it Sill. There is no other name for it and no need of one; there is only the one bowl.

---

**The packing.** Sill has no streets. It has seams.

The first houses were set close for warmth and the second row was set against the first because the ground between them filled with water overnight, and after the third season the rule hardened into law: *no gap*. A gap is a well you did not dig. Leave a body's width between two walls and by morning it is a puddle, by autumn a sump, by the second winter a rot that eats both houses from the outside in. So the town grew as a solid, the way salt grows, and now it is a single mass of about four hundred rooms with no vacant hand's-breadth anywhere in it, and the only ways through are the seams — passages a shoulder wide, roofed, turning, some of them going through three households and out the other side, some of them ending at a door you must knock on and be let through.

Nobody in Sill has ever seen a cart. There is nothing for a wheel to do. Loads go on backs, or on greased sleds hauled along the seams, or up onto the roofs.

Because the roofs are continuous. Thatch is scarce — there is little to thatch with — so most roofing is marl-plaster over drift-timber and withy, laid flat, walked on, patched weekly. You can cross the whole town at roof level and never touch the ground, and children do it as a dare, and old women do it to get anywhere quickly. The roof is the town's true street. From above, Sill looks like one enormous grey animal lying in the bowl with its hide cracked into territories: drying racks, hide-stretching frames, rain-cisterns, the mouths of light-shafts, the smoke-holes, four hundred chimneys of low-grade clay breathing at four hundred heights.

Below, in the seams, it is dim and warm and wet and full.

---

**The wet.** The ground has not finished being sea.

Cut a hand's depth into the marl anywhere in Sill and the cut fills. Not seeping — *filling*, quietly, in the time it takes to say so. The water is relict brine, the bay's own water still held in the sediment like a grudge, and it comes up through every floor in town. So every floor has a weep: a channel cut in the marl, no wider than two fingers, running from the hearth-corner down through the wall and out into the seam, joining the seam's channel, joining the quarter's channel, joining the great straight trunk-cut that runs to the Sill Gate at the low lip of the bowl.

The trunk-cut is nineteen years old and it has not learned to meander. It goes down the fall line like a ruled line, with sharp lips and small square-shouldered drops, and after rain it steps and chatters over them. All the erosion here is like that: gullies outside the town cut straight and raw, spoil heaps angular, cliff-fall in fresh slabs with the fracture faces still bright. There is not a rounded stone in Sill. Every rock in every wall is a broken rock, sharp enough to snag a palm.

The only round things in town came out of the sea: shells, and the timbers of the wrecks, worn smooth by years of dragging before they were stranded. Roundness is precious here. Children are given a worn cockle to hold. A drift-plank smoothed by surf is worth more than a new-cut beam and is used for the things hands touch — door-latches, the rims of cisterns, the handles of the good tools.

The walls sweat. In the deep middle of town, where the mass is thickest and the bodies are closest, the marl is always slick, and the air is warm as breath and smells of cold chalk, brine, woodsmoke, wet hide, and the green skin of algae. In the mornings you can see your breath in the weeps. The town is cool at its edges and mild at its heart, and the poor live at the edges, against the cliff, where the rock weeps onto your bedding and the wind comes down the cut. Wealth in Sill is measured in distance from the rim and in dryness, and a rich man's floor is one you can set a cup on.

---

**The Rind.** This is the thing outsiders are told about and do not believe until they see it.

Set anything on bare marl — a basket, a boot, a bone comb — and leave it a night, and by morning it has a collar. Salt and fine marl creep up out of the ground and cement it, a grey rind a finger thick, hard as poor pottery. Leave it a week and you must chip it free. Leave it a season and it is architecture.

So nothing in Sill is set down. Everything hangs.

This is why the town feels packed even in the seams where you could, technically, stand. Every wall is a rack: pegs, withy loops, hide slings, hooks of antler and drift-iron. Adzes hang, and hanks of sinew, and cooking pots, and boots, and the salt-fish, and the babies' cradles, and the lamps, and the year's spare plaster-batch in sacks, and the tally-sticks, and the good rope. The ceilings of the seams are a low forest of suspended things at the height of a tall man's face, and you learn to walk through them with your head slightly tilted and your hands trailing, parting them, and they touch you as you pass with a soft clacking, felt-wrapped, deliberately muffled. Half the town's craft is in the hanging: the balance of a hook, the wrap on a clapper, the knot that lets a load be lowered by a child.

A Sill child's first competence is not walking. It is hanging.

And where things *were* set down and kept — where somebody was lazy, or grieving, or drunk — the walls have swallowed them. Cut open an old partition to widen a seam and you find the strata: a lost knife at knee height, a sandal, a doll of twisted withy, a whole meal-tray with the fish-bones still on it, each in its collar of grey stone, each dating the layer as surely as a coin. The town is built partly out of its own mistakes. There is a man who makes his living reading these walls when they come down, and telling families what they lost and when.

---

**The quiet.** Marl and hide and packed mass eat sound. There is no room in Sill for an echo to develop; a shout dies a body's width from your mouth, absorbed into wet stone and hanging felt and other people's bedding. What carries instead is pressure. You hear your neighbours through the shared wall as a low knocking in the fabric — a heel, a pot set on a shelf, a wedge being tapped home — and the town has built a language out of it. Two taps: *I am coming through.* Three, slow: *the weep is blocked, come help.* A long scrape: *rain, get the racks in.* People who have lived here all their lives can hear a household's whole day through a wall and will tell you, without embarrassment, that the couple in the third seam are unhappy, because of how she puts things down.

Over this hush there are only a few sounds, and they are the town's clock:

Drip, everywhere, always, from roof-lip to cistern to weep.
The tap-tap of shim-wedges, all day, from every quarter.
Rain on four hundred flat roofs — a sound like frying, like a held breath.
And twice a day, the clap of the tide-flap at the Sill Gate: a hide-and-plank valve over the outfall, which the returning sea slams shut with a flat report you feel in your teeth.

That is the loudest thing in Sill. It happens at the turn of the tide, and everyone stops for it, and no one has ever explained why.

---

**The argument.** The sea still visits its old floor. At the highest springs it comes up the outfall cut, defeats the flap, and stands in the lowest sills, ankle-deep, cold, leaving a fresh white line when it goes. The town keeps a tide-watch and a tally-board and a great many stacked stones ready to weight things down.

Meanwhile the rain — which falls most days, light, cool, patient, out of a bright grey sky — washes the salt down and out. Every roof drains to a cistern; every cistern's overflow is aimed deliberately at the floor of some room whose marl is still too brackish to sweeten. The whole town is a slow machine for rinsing itself. There is a measure, kept on the tally-board, of how deep the sweet has gone in each quarter: a finger's breadth in a good decade. Children are named for the year their family's floor came sweet.

So Sill's central activity is an argument between rain and tide, conducted through stone, at the speed of a fingernail growing, and everybody is a participant, and nobody expects to see it won.

---

**The living.** Almost nothing grows here. The ground is nineteen years old and has no soil, only marl going slowly to marl-dust. There is a grey lichen crust on the rim-rocks. There is glasswort in the wet seams outside the bund, red-stemmed, chewed by anyone who passes. There is moss in the weeps, and a green algal skin on any surface that stays damp, which is most of them. There are eleven bushes in the bowl — counted, named, disputed — and a single thorn at the foot of the path, which is the town's landmark, its meeting-place, and its calendar; when it leafs, the re-shimming begins.

On the roofs there are trays. Scurvy-grass, saltwort, a bitter cress, grown in scraped marl and shell-mulch and watered with cistern-rain, and the leaves are counted out at sharing rather than measured. A handful of green is a gift you make to a woman who has just given birth. There is more vegetation in the town's gut than in its ground.

Animals are scarce and famous. Mud-snails in their thousands in one wet seam and nowhere else, which climb the walls a day before rain and are the town's barometer. Brine-flies for nine days in late summer, a grey shimmer over the outfall, and every mouth shut. Woodlice under the hanging racks. Terns passing overhead, never landing. One heron that stands on the old strandline on the cliff, at the height where the sea used to be, as if waiting for it back.

One eel, in the trunk-cut, which came in through the gate as an elver and stayed. It is fed. It has a name and a rota and is considered the oldest citizen, having arrived before most of the children. Sometimes, in the deep weeps of the middle houses, a small pale flatfish arrives alive, in water that connects to the sea only through a valve that has been shut for a fortnight. Nobody has explained this. Children are told the ground lays them.

One cat, belonging to everyone, sleeping on the kiln-clamp.

And no rats. Not yet. This is the town's great held breath: it is too new for rats, and everyone knows they will come, up the path or in the drift-timber or in a sack of somebody's traded grain. On the rim there is a small stone box, well-built, roofed, dry, with a sleeping-shelf and a bowl. It has been kept swept for eleven years. The idea is to give the first rat somewhere better to be than the granaries. Outsiders find this funny. In Sill it is discussed with the seriousness of drainage.

---

**The making.** Everything is hand-work. Antler picks and bone wedges for the marl, which cuts like hard cheese when fresh and sets like poor brick when dried. Drift-iron, cold-hammered and precious, one good adze per quarter and a man who knows how to draw its temper down in the clamp-kiln. Wattle, withy, sinew, hide, oiled hide for windows — amber light, the colour of weak tea. No mortar: the marl cements itself, and the Rind is the mortar, and the town is partly glued together by the same process that makes it hang its boots.

And because the bowl is still moving — settling here, rising a finger there, the uplift not entirely finished in its bones — nothing in Sill stays square. Doorframes go rhomboid in a season. Roofs pond. So under every sill and every post-foot there are shims: stacks of drift-wood wedges, tapped and re-tapped, and the shim-wrights go round the town all year with a mallet and a bag of wedges and a plumb-bob of shell, and the tap-tap-tap of their work is the sound you hear in your sleep. Once a year, when the thorn leafs, the whole town re-levels itself in three days of communal, ill-tempered, absolutely necessary labour, and afterwards everyone drinks the last of the sweet water and complains about the neighbours' roof-lines.

Which brings up light. The sky gives an ordinary bright grey day, sun for a few hours, nothing strange. But in a town with no gaps, daylight is a substance that has to be *brought in*, and it is brought in down shafts: vertical wells cut through the packed mass, lined with white shell to bounce the light down, widened at the top with splayed marl horns. There are sixty-one of them. They are owned. Light-rights are inherited, traded, litigated, and dowried; a shaft that reaches a neighbour's floor at noon is worth a year's fish, and there is a stone in the second seam where the sun stands for eleven minutes at midsummer noon, and people bring their infants to it, and their sick, and, when there is one, their dead, before the wall takes them.

---

Stand at the rim in the late afternoon, in the cool drizzle that is not quite rain, and look down into it. The bowl is full to its lip with grey roofs and smoke and moving figures and drying racks, and the seams below are dark and warm and hung with everything the town owns, and the weeps run bright and straight through the floors, and the trunk-cut goes down to the gate without a single bend, and the sea is out there a quarter-mile off, standing a little higher than the roofs, waiting for its two hours.

The old tide-line is above you, dry barnacles at the height of your face.

Nothing here is finished. The stone is unrounded, the channels are unmeandered, the salt is unrinSED, the ground is still deciding where it wants to be, and the town is packed into it like a hand pressed into wet clay — every gap closed, everything hung, everything shimmied, everyone listening through the wall.

In an hour the flap will clap. Everyone will stop.

Then the tapping will start again.
