From the air — and there is no other way to see the whole of it — the plateau reads first as stone: a pale ochre table three thousand meters up, twelve hundred kilometers across, rimmed in cliffs the color of old bone. Then the light shifts and you see that the stone is furred. Not green. Nothing here is green in the way you mean it. The fur is black and rust and, along the spring-lines, a sulfurous cream, and it lies over the rock the way nap lies over velvet, and it moves.

The movement is animals.

**The rock and its water**

It is limestone under a cap of welded tuff, and it has been raining on this table, gently and almost daily, for longer than there have been eyes to notice. The rain does not run off. It goes down into the karst and comes back out at every bedding plane, every fracture, every contact between the tuff and the carbonate beneath — thousands of springs, most of them no wider than a wrist, seeping continuously out of faces of rock so that the cliffs are permanently dark and glossy and warm to the touch. Weeping stone. Put your palm on it and the palm comes away wet and blood-temperature. The water is 24°C at the source, warmed by a shallow hot body far below that has been cooling at a rate of one degree per forty thousand years.

That warmth is why nothing here has ever frozen. At this altitude the air should be thin and cold and the nights should bite; instead the plateau keeps its own weather, a standing dome of humid warmth held in place by the rim and the seep and the sheer biological heat of the animals. Between rains the sun is fierce and clean and shadows have hard edges. At four in the afternoon, more or less, the dome collapses into rain — a warm, unhurried, twenty-minute rain that has fallen at approximately that hour since before the city. Then the sun comes back low and the whole table steams, and the drift rises.

Everything is rounded. There are no raw edges anywhere on the plateau. The sinkholes are bowls, the cliffs are fluted, the cobbles in the seep-channels are ovoids polished to a wet gleam, and the doorframes of the oldest houses have been worn into hourglasses by two hundred years of bodies brushing through. Even the machines are rounded. Even the railings.

**The nap**

The primary producers are films. Bacterial felts, millimeter-thick, layered like lacquer: black where the light is strong, rust where iron seeps, cream and pale yellow at the sulfur springs, and in the shaded under-channels a translucent gray that quivers when you breathe on it. They fix carbon and they fix nitrogen and they grow about a millimeter a month, and they have no stems, no leaves, no roots, no wood. There is not a plant on the continent. There never was. Ask a local what a tree is and you will get a description of a penstock.

The felts are grazed to stubble everywhere they are not fenced. This is the first thing to understand about the plateau's appearance: it looks bare because it is eaten. The vast ochre plains are not desert. They are pasture shaved down to the mineral, wet underfoot, faintly warm, and if you stand still for six minutes you will see the stubble begin to close over your bootprint.

**The mouths**

There are two kinds of animal here: rasps and filters. Everything else is a variation.

The rasps are low, broad, shelled things the size of a dinner plate to the size of a bathtub, moving on a muscular sole, dragging a ribbon of mineral teeth across the felt. There are, on a good hectare of unmanaged plain, forty thousand of them. They lie shell to shell, near-touching, a cobble-field that is entirely alive, and their grazing makes a sound like distant applause, like rain on a tin roof, like frying. In the wet tuff the burrowers work the same film from below, and their spoil-heaps dot the flats in low cones, each cone with its ring of steam.

Above them: the drift. The plateau's updraft lifts spores, bacteria, midges, larval things, and the shed fragments of felt into a column that goes up four kilometers and spreads out over the rim, and out beyond the rim, over the invisible lowlands, a thousand kilometers of warm rain-forest-of-something-else that you will never see through the haze. The lowland drift comes back up the escarpment every afternoon on the return wind. It is thick. It stains the west-facing walls. It is the reason the plateau is fertile at all, and the reason there is a city here.

And in the drift: the sailers. Weak-winged, enormous-mouthed, four-meter spans and bodies light as a paper bag, riding the standing updraft with their jaws open, filtering. They do not so much fly as hang, in tens of millions, in layers — a low layer of the small ones, a middle layer at three hundred meters, and above that the great slow breeders that have not landed in nine years. They roost only on the rim cliffs, where their droppings have built white terraces eighty meters thick, stratified like rock, actively mined.

Then the shrikes, which impale rasps on fence spikes and rail bolts and the sharp ends of anything the city has left standing. Then the parasites of the sailers, and the parasites of the parasites, and the things that live in the warm condensers of the pump houses, and the things that live in the eaves, and the flat pale commensals that live under the trolley rails and eat the grease.

At dusk the air is not air with animals in it. It is a tissue. You cannot see the far towers. You put out a hand and it comes back wet and dusted with wings.

**The city**

It sits in and around the sinkholes, along the spring-lines, in a continuous built fabric that runs, with breaks, for four hundred kilometers along the northern seep. Dense: houses shoulder to shoulder, three and four storeys of dressed tuff and rendered brick, roofs steeply pitched because guano is heavy when wet, every window screened in double mesh, every doorway with a scraper bar and a boot sump. Streets are narrow, wet, and swept by paid crews four times a day, and still the felt creeps up the walls in a black tide-line that the sweepers cut back with hooked knives. The buildings are stacked with additions — a chimney through a bedroom, a conveyor through a chapel, an older arched doorway buried in a newer wall with only its keystone showing, worn round.

The industry is phosphorus.

Water spends all day carrying phosphorus downhill and off the rim, and the animals spend all day carrying it back up: the sailers eat the lowland drift and void it on the cliffs. Left alone, the plateau is a slow leak. The city exists to close the loop faster. The Nitrate Houses dig the white terraces, acidulate them, and rail the product inland to the Pan Fields — forty thousand hectares of shallow terraced basins, each one a hand's-depth of spring water over a seeded felt, each one a slightly different black or rust depending on its culture. The pans are inoculated from mother-cultures kept in glass jars in temperature-controlled rooms that have not been allowed to cool since the founding. Harvest is a scraper-gantry on rails, running the length of a pan once every eleven months, laying down a wet black ribbon onto a conveyor.

Then the rendering works, which take the wild rasps by the trainload and separate them into oil, shell-lime, meal, and a gray liquor that goes back onto the pans. Then the Rim Works, where the seep water is collected in covered leats and dropped two thousand meters through penstocks the diameter of a house, and the turbines have been turning at the same speed, within two percent, for one hundred and forty years. Arc lamps. Trolley wires. The smell of ammonia and hot insulation and wet shell. In the older quarters, tread floors, where domesticated rasps are driven in a circle around a capstan and grind the phosphate rock, and their soles polish the floor into a mirror, and the floor is dished a meter deep, and no one alive remembers it flat.

**Loud, and slow**

The sound is a broadband roar you stop hearing in about four days. Under it: the falls at the rim, a bass note felt in the sternum from ten kilometers off. The graze-hiss of forty thousand rasps per hectare, everywhere, always. The pumps. The gantries. The sailers' calls, which are not cries but a low resonant thrumming made by the whole column at once, so that the sky sounds like a struck bowl the size of a province. In the streets, the sweepers' hooks. At night, the flues full of roosting things, shifting.

Silence is a civic event. When a turbine hall is shut for maintenance the flight begins nesting in the stack within a season, and restarting it becomes an operation involving fire and men on ropes, so nothing is ever shut. The city has no off. Neither does the plateau.

Change here is measured in decades. The wild herds run the Long Circuit, an eleven-year loop of the interior following a phosphorus gradient so weak that no instrument built on the plateau can detect it, only the animals. A pan takes nine years to come to yield. A culture drifts a shade of color over a working lifetime and the foreman retires with the color he was hired into still, more or less, in the jar. The springs' plumbing has been stable for a hundred thousand years. The rim retreats four centimeters a century. The rain falls at four.

Nothing is in a hurry. Everything is eating. Stand at the edge of a pan at evening, in the warm rain, with the arc lamps coming up orange along the leat and the drift thick enough to taste, and you can watch the black ribbon of felt thicken by an amount you cannot see, and know that it is the fastest thing here.
