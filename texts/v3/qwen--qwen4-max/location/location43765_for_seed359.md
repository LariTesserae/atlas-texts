# The Wick Court

## The Descent

There is no ground here, only floor. It runs out flat in every direction, fitted polygons of dark basalt laid edge to edge without mortar, the joints so tight you cannot slip a hair into them, and it goes on until the pale air closes over it. Nothing rises from it. Nothing has ever grown on it. Somewhere in that expanse there is a hole, and the hole is the place.

Forty-nine steps go down. The treads are hatched with chisel-work to keep feet from sliding, and the hatching is crisp — every groove still has the bright burr the tool left, after however long it has been. Warm air comes up the stairwell in a slow column, and it smells of tallow, crushed green rind, hot rope, and the inside of a coop. It is the smell of a room where something is alive and has been for a while.

The court at the bottom is small. Ninety paces by sixty, an oval, walled twelve meters deep in brick, roofed over with a lattice of lashed withy so dense it reads as a ceiling rather than a net. Warmth pools here the way cold pools in a valley. The vapor pools too: ankle-deep, heavy, sweet, faintly luminous, drifting in slow lobes when you disturb it. You walk through it as through the shallows of something that is not water and will never be water.

Every brick carries a thumbprint. Pressed while soft, fired hard after. Billions of them, all the same thumb.

## The Standards

They are not trees. They were never trees. Each one is a cable — a braided trunk of fiber, laid up by hand from ten thousand strands, anchored in a socket in the floor and drawn taut to the lattice above. A plumb line that has learned to leaf. The tension is what holds them upright; there is no wood in them strong enough to stand on its own.

Around each cable, rind has been grafted and trained until the rope is only a rumor inside. The rind is warm and slightly yielding, like the skin over a knuckle. Branches are withy hoops, lashed on with silk and wedged with bone, and every lash is a knot you could name if you knew the vocabulary — and you find, standing here, that you almost do.

The leaves are films of green wax, thin as fingernail parings, translucent, laid in overlapping spirals so tight that a hand's-breadth of branch carries four hundred of them and no light gets between. Nothing here has a gap in it. The court is packed: leaf on leaf, fruit on fruit, cheek to cheek, hanging in fat white clusters that touch and deform each other slightly, like a crowd holding still. To cross the grove you part things. They close behind you without a sound.

The fruit is a tallow-plum: chalk-white skin, warm to hold, and inside, a soft fat with seeds set in it like small knuckles. It takes a human lifetime to ripen. A ripe one smells like a bed someone has just got out of.

## The Burn

There is no sun. There is no lamp. The light is ordinary — the plain even light of an overcast noon, the light of a lit page — and it comes off everything, because everything is very slowly burning.

Under the floor is a sump of warm oil. The cables drink it. Capillarity draws it up through ten thousand strands into the wax leaves, and there it oxidizes without flame, without heat enough to scorch, at the temperature of a body. The whole court is a lamp that has forgotten to be a flame. Each leaf gives off a little light and a little warmth and a breath of that sweet heavy vapor, and because the light comes from all surfaces at once, the shadows are small and soft and lie directly underneath things, like the shadows in a room lit by its own walls.

The burn should produce water. It produces none. The arithmetic does not close, and nothing here has ever been wet, and something downstream is taking the missing term. You can feel the direction of it, sometimes, north, past the seam, a sort of patient thirst with a shape.

Nobody tends the flame because there is no flame. Nobody has ever seen fire in the Wick Court except the small hard sparks the crabs strike to cure resin, and those are struck quickly, and put out with a thumb.

## The Occupants

There are more animals here than leaves.

Wax moths, palm-sized, wings like oiled paper, hang in the interstices in layers three deep, and when you move they lift and resettle without any sound at all — no flutter, no whisper, the air simply reorganizes. Their caterpillars are the ropemakers. They spin the strands. They lay up the cables. They work to a plan held nowhere but in the wax they eat, and the plan is old, and it is correct.

Hinge-crabs live in the joinery. Grey, flat, five-fingered on the forward pair, and in those fingers they hold flakes of basalt no bigger than a lentil, and bone wedges, and a spool. They tighten knots. They travel the lashings on a circuit that takes eleven years, and at each knot they test the tension, take up the slack, cut, splice, and seal. Their flint-work makes a small dry click, like a knuckle. It is the loudest regular sound in the place.

Below the floor, in the sump, pale eels move through the oil and filter it and keep it sweet. They are blind, unhurried, and longer than the court is wide, which is a thing you learn by watching one pass and then watching it pass again.

Wick-mice run the cables. Their fur is a wick; they do not drink, they absorb, and they are always faintly greasy and always warm. Lattice-fowl sleep on the wing above the withy net, hollow-boned, oil-filled, and have not landed in living memory.

All of them are eating, mending, spinning, tightening. None of them is drinking. There is nothing to drink. Fat is the water of this place, and it moves through everything, and the whole court is one circulatory system with a great many small hearts in it, and the noise it makes is almost nothing: creak, click, the sigh of the burn.

You hear your own pulse. You hear the wetness of your own mouth, and it is obscene here.

## What Wears

Nothing in the Wick Court erodes. That is the rule, and it is not a metaphor. The chisel-hatching on the forty-ninth step is as sharp as the hatching on the first. The bricks have not slumped. The knots have not frayed except where they were meant to loosen. There is no dust on any leaf.

Except your dust. Your boot has scuffed a burr off a basalt arris and left a grey smear, and it is the only rounded thing in the world.

You are the weather here. You are mostly water — a walking sack of the one substance this place has organized its entire existence to exclude — and your breath fogs a leaf and the leaf blanches white and dies in a spot the size of a coin. A drop of sweat falls and eats a hole through the wax film and hits the floor with a tick that everyone hears.

The crabs come. They come from three meters away in under a minute, which for them is a sprint, and they seal the blanched place with resin, and the scar becomes a small white bead, permanent, part of the record.

There are older beads. A scatter of them along the floor near the eastern standards, and up the wall beside them, at the height of a shoulder, in the shape of someone who stood leaning there for a long time, sweating.

Nobody has repaired the shape. The crabs sealed each bead and left the arrangement alone. It is the only monument in the court and it is a portrait, made of harm.

## The Long Afternoon

The day here is eighty years long. Forty of brightening, forty of dimming, the burn rate rising and falling like something asleep. It is mid-afternoon now. It has been mid-afternoon since before your grandmother was born, and the light has not changed enough in your lifetime for you to be sure it is changing at all, but the moths know: their migration from the north standards to the south takes a decade and they are two-thirds through it, a slow continent of wings shifting a hand's width a month.

The cables take up a finger's width of slack per century. When one goes, the trunk sounds — a low toll you feel in the sternum, and then a long silence while everything resettles, and then the crabs begin the eleven-year walk toward it.

There is a plum near the eastern wall that has been falling for a hundred and forty years. It hangs a hand's span above the floor, in the vapor, rotating very slightly. It will land. It is landing.

Walk the perimeter and it takes two hundred and eleven paces. Cross the middle and it takes ninety. The far wall does not get closer as you approach it; it simply becomes the far wall you are now standing at, and the one you left is behind you, still far. You pass the same standard three times: as a sapling cable freshly laid, as a mature trunk in full fruit, as an old one with its rind gone grey and its knots being taken apart by crabs working in reverse. It is the same tree. It is not three trees. The court does not have room for three.

At the north wall there is a seam where two courses of brick meet that were pressed by the same thumb at different times, and they do not quite agree about what they are joined to. Through it, if you look sidelong, you can see the back of a head. It is patient. It is waiting for you to finish looking.

The sump, when the floor-grate is lifted, reflects the court perfectly, with one extra standard in it. It has taken some time to establish which one of you is standing under the extra.

## The Fitting

There is a corner by the stair where the work is done: a bow-lathe, a stone bench, a bowl of cured resin, coils of laid-up strand in nine graded thicknesses, a rack of basalt flakes sorted by size. The handles of the tools are worn into a shape. Not your shape. Close, though. Close enough that when you pick one up your fingers go into the grooves without being told.

The crabs have begun measuring your hands while you sleep. You wake to find them at the wrist, pacing the span, testing the grip, clicking. Somewhere above, a new cable is being laid up, and its working length is your reach, and its anchor socket has already been cut.

It would not be violent. That is the part worth understanding. It would take decades, and it would be done a drop at a time, fat for water, patiently, with what you would have to call kindness — and you would be warm the whole while, and useful, and the light would go on being ordinary, and one afternoon eighty years from now you would tighten a knot you had never learned and hear the trunk toll, and the moths would resettle around your shoulders like a coat being put on.

Behind you, somewhere in the packed green dark, a knot is being tied.

You are the only thing here that is wearing out. They have noticed. They are very good at mending.
