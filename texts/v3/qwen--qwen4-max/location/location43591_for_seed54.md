# The Curing Flats

You hear it an hour before you see anything, and by then you have stopped being able to hear it, which is the same as arriving.

The sound comes up through the soles first — a fundamental somewhere below the bottom of hearing, twenty-two or twenty-three cycles, felt as a pressure behind the sternum and a fine trembling in the water on the ground. Above it sit the working harmonics, a stacked chord that never resolves and never quite repeats, and above *those* the hiss: billions of small wet bodies stridulating, scraping, ticking, a sound like rain on a tin roof if the rain were also screaming very quietly. It is loud. It is loud the way a waterfall is loud, and like a waterfall it stops being sound after a while and becomes a substance you are standing inside.

Then the fog thins by one degree and the flats show themselves: a black plain, wet as a tongue, sheeted in an inch of brine that does not freeze at temperatures that should have made it solid a week ago. It shivers. Everywhere, in every direction, it shivers, because the water is standing in a wave and the wave is holding it there.

Lights come on in the dark ahead. Not many. Not bright. But they go on for a long way.

## The ground is not finished

Nothing here is rounded.

That is the first thing a hand notices, before the cold does. The gravel is angular, freshly split, every fragment still holding the geometry of the fracture that made it. There is no sand. There is no silt, no dust, no soil, no mud in the ordinary sense — mud requires a history of grinding and this country has no history yet. The air is extraordinarily clean of particulate. What fills the air instead is animals.

The plateau rose recently, or was laid recently; the distinction has stopped mattering to anyone who studies it. Its drainage has not decided where to go. Water arrives — seeping out of the terrace faces, weeping from the fog-lid overhead, condensing on every cold surface at a rate that makes the whole world seem to perspire — and instead of running, it *lies*. Sheets, films, bays an inch deep and a kilometre wide, held level by the chord. There are no channels. There is no river.

At the eastern escarpment, where the shelf ends and drops into a cloud floor you have never seen the bottom of, there is a notch the width of a wrist. Water goes over it. It has been going over it for about ninety years and has cut down perhaps a metre and a half. This is the first river in the world. It has a name in the survey and the name is *First Cut*, and the survey notes, in the flat voice of someone who has decided not to be excited, that the plateau is now losing material faster than it gains it in one district out of eleven.

Everything else is still being added.

## The tenants

There is no green here. There is no green anywhere, and no chlorophyll, and nothing that has ever needed the sun. The only growths are films: sulphate crusts, iron sheens, pale chemolithotrophic skins a millimetre thick that lie on wet stone like the residue of breath and fix carbon out of the rock's own chemistry. In places there is a crustose thing, grey-white, cracked into polygons, that would be a lichen if there were anything available to be the other half of it. It is not a lichen. It is doing the best it can.

Everything else that lives, lives by eating.

The tenants are cold-bodied, shelled in a translucent aragonite that goes the colour of wet bone when they are old. The smallest are the size of a grain of rice and there are, in the low lanes at the terrace feet, something on the order of a hundred thousand of them per cubic metre — a fog made of animals, drifting, grazing the air's thin rain of dissolved organics, dying and falling as a continual soft calcareous snow that the setters sweep up and use. The middle sizes are finger-length. The great ones, the holders, are the size of a cupped hand, and they are packed shoulder to shoulder along every terrace face, three and four deep, generation cemented onto generation, a hundred centuries of shell laid down like the pages of a book that is still being written from the inside.

They are not building the city the way a bird builds a nest. They *are* the building, and the process, and the population, and, eventually, the material. When a holder dies it is not removed. It sets. Its resonating chamber, still tuned, becomes part of the wall's tuning; the wall's note is partly the note of the dead. Walk close to a finished face and you can see them in there, layer on layer, like flies in amber, all of them still contributing.

Nothing eats plants here. Everything eats the skin, or eats what eats the skin, or eats the drifters, or eats the dead. There is a thing called a gleaner, which is rare and enormous and moves about a metre an hour, harvesting the terrace faces by the thousand with a mouth like a wet conveyor; a gleaner's passage leaves a stripe of bare stone that takes forty years to re-tenanstrate and, in that forty years, changes the local chord by a quarter-tone. Whole districts have shifted key because one animal was hungry.

## What the sound is for

The city is precipitated out of the water by standing waves.

This is the part that no visiting instrument has satisfactorily explained, and the survey has stopped trying, and simply records the behaviour. Brine saturated with calcium and silica is held in a shaped acoustic field. At the antinodes the mineral comes out of solution and lays itself down along the pressure surfaces, in laminae finer than anything a hand could tool. At the nodes nothing precipitates at all, and the water stays clear, and nothing ever fills.

So the walls are where the sound is loudest, and the streets are where it cancels.

The streets are not constructed. They are *maintained* — held open by keepers, a caste with an inverted organ, small and dark and clustered in the road-cuts, singing precisely against the city to keep a corridor of nothing. A node road is a lane of silence a metre and a half wide running through a roar, and standing in it is like standing in the eye of something. You can hear your own blood. Tenants come into the quiet roads to touch each other, because in a world saturated with sound, hearing is a spent sense and everything important is said with light and with contact. Along the node roads at night you can watch a message travel: a flicker of cold blue photophore passed body to body, hand to hand, a kilometre in eleven seconds, the whole lane lighting and darkening like a wire carrying current.

If the keepers in a section die — a cold snap, a gleaner, a bloom of something that fouls their organs — the road begins to close. Not quickly. A centimetre a year, then faster as the gap narrows and the cancellation weakens. There is a district in the north-west, the Filled Quarter, where this happened across ninety streets at once about four hundred years ago. It is solid now. You can walk on the roofs of it. Somewhere under your boots are the corridors and the plazas and the whole layered civic void of a city that lost its silence, and if you put your ear to the stone you can hear the chord going through it, uninterrupted, still holding, still curing, patient as a glacier.

## Rules, insofar as anyone has written them down

Water stays liquid to about −24 °C on the flats and no one has isolated why. Brine accounts for six degrees of it. Metabolic heat of the packed bodies accounts for two. The rest is the field, and the working phrase in the survey is *the water is being asked to*.

A wall younger than about twelve years is not yet a decision. You can put your arm into it. It will accept your arm without resistance and without wetting you, and when you withdraw it will keep the shape of your arm for a season — a smooth socket, a perfect negative — and then either fill it or keep it, and there is no way to know in advance which. There are doorways in the low terraces that began as somebody's curiosity.

The chord changes about once a decade, district by district, and you can locate yourself on the plateau by listening for the delay. The change propagates at the speed of sound through wet mineral, which is fast, but the plateau is wide, and the far towers are always a few seconds behind the near ones, so the whole country is permanently, slightly, out of phase with itself. Nothing here has ever been in unison. It is not supposed to be.

Everything upstanding wears rime. Everything. The fog-lid weeps onto it and the cold takes it and the hoar grows outward in feathers a hand's length long, and every tower, every buttress, every packed rank of holders is furred white and hunched, so that the city's true geometry is only visible in the seams where the mineral is warm enough to shed. Under the rime it is pale, glassy, faintly banded, the colour of a fingernail held to a candle.

## Winter is the scaffolding

The night is long. Not metaphorically — the rotation is slow, and the dark has been on this shelf for something over two hundred years, and it will be on it for perhaps sixty more.

In the dark, the tenants build in ice.

They have to. Ice is free, ice is instant, ice takes any shape the field asks of it, and the winter constructors throw up spans and vaults and cantilevers of it that no mineral could hold at that stage of curing. Then, over decades, the setters work through the ice from the inside, replacing it lamina by lamina with precipitated stone, a molecule of water traded for a molecule of aragonite, so slowly that the structure never knows it is being changed. Most of the high city is converted. Most.

When the sun comes up — and it will come up gradually, over about eleven years, a grey dawn that thickens like curdling — every unconverted span on the plateau will let go at once.

This is the deadline the whole country is organised around, and no individual tenant knows it exists. The keepers hold their roads. The holders hold their note. The setters trade ice for stone at a rate that has been accelerating, very slightly, for four hundred years, and is now, according to the last survey, approximately on schedule.

## What it is

The flats extend past the limit of vision in every direction, but vision is not the measure here; the fog and the drifting bodies cap it at two hundred metres. You learn the scale by walking, and by the phase-lag, and by the fact that the light-points on the horizon are not stars but districts, and that there are eleven of them, and that the eleventh is nine days away on foot.

From the escarpment, in a rare thinning, you can see the whole shelf at once: the terraces stacked and layered and near-touching, the black bays between them doubled with reflected signal-light, the towers furred and leaning, the roads threading through as lines of pure dark where nothing glows because nothing is there. The mineral is being laid down faster than the world can wear it. There is no rubble. There is no ruin. There is nothing here old enough to have fallen apart.

And it is not a city, in the end. That is the last thing the survey says, and it says it reluctantly.

The plan is too coherent. The node roads are not streets, they are apertures; the districts are not neighbourhoods, they are elements; the whole shelf is a single object with a focal geometry that only resolves if you plot it at regional scale, and it points, all of it, at a place in the sky where nothing currently is and where, in sixty years, the sun will rise.

The tenants are not the citizens. They are the fabrication process. They are overwhelming in number and total in commitment and they have been at this for longer than there has been anything on this plateau to erode, and they will finish it, and it will be used once.

Nobody has found who left the instructions. The instructions are not written anywhere; they are in the tuning, passed down inside the shells, dead animals holding the note that living animals continue. It is a very good system. It requires no memory, no literacy, no intention, and no green.

Stand in a node road at the third hour and watch the blue messages run past you, hand to hand, out toward the unfinished east. The keepers are saying the same thing they have said for four thousand years, and the survey, which has cracked exactly two words of the contact-language, renders it as:

*still open. still open. still open.*
