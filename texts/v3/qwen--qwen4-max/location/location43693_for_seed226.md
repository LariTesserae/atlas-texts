## The Forefield

The bowl is a mile across and it has no age to speak of.

Three walls of grey granite hold it, and the fourth wall is ice — a snout, blunt and dirty-lipped, pressed into the head of the bowl like a thumb into dough. The walls are not cliffs so much as fresh breaks. Nothing has rounded them. There is no talus at their feet, no fan of rubble, no scree; the stone has not yet had time to fall apart in the ordinary way. Where the ice has withdrawn it has left the bedrock scoured and glossy, a floor of whalebacks and shallow scoops, striped with scratches so straight and so parallel that from the rim they look ruled. You can see your face in the wet polish. You can see the clouds move in it. There is not one handful of soil in the entire basin.

This is a landscape that has just been made and has not yet been used. The sun comes over the east wall mid-morning, ordinary sun, honest and unremarkable, and lies on all of it at once, and nothing is hidden. Shadow arrives early from the west. The light is doubled — from above, and back up off the ice — so that the undersides of things are lit too, and there are no comfortable dark places to put a mystery in. Everything here is plainly visible and almost nothing is here.

The air sits at the temperature of a cellar. Breath shows in the mornings. Hands chap and stay chapped, and everyone's knuckles are split in the same four places. At night the shallow water grows needles of ice with a sound like distant applause, and by ten in the morning it is only water again.

## Water that has not learned to run

It rains most days, a fine driving rain that comes in from the lip and hangs in the bowl for hours afterward as a wet fog. The walls weep. The ice sweats. And yet there is not a stream in the place — not a brook, not a rill, not a channel the width of a finger.

The floor is too new. Water has not yet found the trick of gathering. It falls and it stays where it falls, in films and sheets and thousands of shallow pans, each one a separate sealed world with its own floor of rock flour and its own particular colour of algae: rust, mustard, a black-green like bottle glass, one pan up near the ice that is the pale pink of watered blood. Overflow from a pan does not cut a gutter; it spreads across the polish in a skin a quarter-inch deep and finds the next pan and stops. In eight hundred years the water here has not managed to incise anything. It has no downhill habit. It lies about in acres of thin brightness and evaporates and comes back.

The consequence is that nothing swims. There are no fish, no fry, no water beetles, nothing that requires one pool to be connected to another. Each pan is colonised only by what the wind brings or what a bird drops, and so each has its own small stubborn constitution, and neighbours fifty feet apart can be as different as two countries. The people know them individually. They know which are sweet, which taste of iron, which will give you a day of cramps, which hold their water through a dry fortnight. They drink from about nine of them.

Where the walls weep, the water falls three hundred feet and arrives as mist, and the mist condenses on the cold stone below and falls again, so that a single drop may make the journey several times before it consent to lie down.

## The roar

It is very loud.

Under the ice there is a river — the one river in this country, and it is the exception that proves the rule, because it is not made of rain that fell here. It goes down a shaft somewhere out of sight on the ice's back and it runs beneath the snout and it comes out of a blue cave at the ice's foot, and in the shaft it booms, and in the cave it roars, and the three granite walls take that sound and hand it back and forth. The bowl is a room built to hold noise. Standing in the middle of the empty floor you are inside a chord that never resolves and never stops: a low grinding under a high hiss, with a slow beat in it where two echoes disagree.

Nothing about the sound changes. It has no weather. It does not rise for rain or fall for night. It has been the same volume for eight centuries, which is why the people who live here call it the quiet, and call a place quiet only if it is very far from the ice, and mean by quiet a slightly thinner version of the same roar.

They speak close to the ear, in a shout that is not angry, and at any distance across a plaza they use their hands: a full working language of flat palms and fingers, developed for architecture, entirely unhurried. Children are taught to hear the roar once, deliberately, around their seventh year — taken out to the middle of the floor and made to stand until they notice it — and most of them cry, and then within a month they can no longer hear it at all, for the rest of their lives.

What frightens them is change. Twice in a generation the under-river shifts its pitch, drops a third, goes hollow, and every person in the bowl stands up wherever they are. It means the shaft has found a new route. It means the ice has moved inside itself. They wait, hands still, until the old note comes back, and then they sit down and go on with the day and do not talk about it.

## The city

There is a city on the floor of the bowl. It has stairs, terraces, quays, retaining walls, a grid, a civic reservoir, three long ramps, a plaza big enough to hold a market for ten thousand, and sixty-two people.

It is built almost entirely without cutting. The ice does the cutting. As the snout withdraws — two paces in a good year, three in a great one, the width of a doorway in a lifetime — it exposes a step, a ledge, a natural runnel-free shelf, and the people come forward behind it and name what has been revealed. That is a street. This is a quay, because the pan beside it is broad and shallow and useful for soaking. Here is a stair, because the ice happened to pluck a block out and leave a riser. The plan of the city is a tracing of the glacier's habits. Its long avenues follow the scratches. Its blocks are the sizes the ice left them.

They build with dry stone, because there is no clay in the world and therefore no mortar and no brick and no pot. They cook in stone troughs with hot stones. They carry water in stitched hide and in wooden buckets. Their timber comes out of the ice: whole trunks, black and sound, trees that grew somewhere upstream centuries before the bowl existed, handed over one or two a decade, seasoned past any seasoning, so hard the adzes ring. Their tools are antler wedges, stone mauls, wooden shovels, sedge rope, and a little cold-hammered copper picked out of the polish in beads and used only for edges and for ornament. They have no wheel worth the name; the floor is polished and they slide things on wet hide, which works absurdly well, and is one of the reasons the plazas are so large.

There are eleven hundred doorways. Most stand open on empty rooms, swept, dry, roofed, waiting. A single household occupies a district. You can walk for ten minutes through built, finished, maintained city — kerbs, drains that drain nothing, lamp niches with their lamps lit at dusk by a child with a long pole — and meet no one, and then find a woman sitting in a doorway at the far end mending a sedge creel, and she will lift two fingers to you and go back to it.

They build ahead of themselves on purpose. Each generation lays out one cross-street, roughly fifty paces behind the last, and buildings enough for a hundred households, and inhabits four. The reasoning is not ambition. It is that the ice is coming back. Everyone knows this. The scratches on the floor run out of the bowl and also, faintly, into it; the older scratches underneath run the other way. The tenant is only stepping out for a while. The city is being furnished against its return, room by room, so that when the ice comes down the bowl again it will come into a house that has been kept for it, and the doorways will fill in the other direction, and whatever lives here then will not have to start.

## The green

The green is not spread. It is poured.

Wherever the ice has left a crack — and it has left thousands, hairline to arm-wide — the water lies in it and cannot leave, and the wind's whole cargo of spores and seed collects there, and the result is a vegetation of insane density in a country of bare stone. A fissure two fingers wide will hold a foot of moss, saturated, warm to the touch on top and cold as the ice underneath, and out of the moss come ferns and saxifrage and a knotted willow no thicker than a wrist and taller than a man, root-bound into a ribbon, and inside that ribbon there are springtails in numbers that make the surface move like a liquid when you breathe on it.

Between the fissures: nothing. Grey polish, wet, ringing underfoot, a mile of it. You cross an acre of empty floor and then step over a crack that is a jungle eighteen inches wide and forty feet long, and the smell comes up out of it — crushed green, cold iodine, something like bread — and then you are on the stone again and there is no smell at all but wet rock.

The city is a calendar of this. The oldest quarter, at the lip, eight hundred years out of the ice, is a moss forest: the streets are canyons of it, the doorways furred and half-swallowed, willow scrub overhead in the seams, and the roar of the ice is thinned here to a rumour. That is where they garden, where they cut moss for bedding and sedge for rope, where they lay their dead in the deep seams and let the green take them. The newest quarter, at the ice's foot, is sterile: polish, mist, the smell of ground stone, one lichen the size of a coin per plaza, and the roar. A person's age is often given as a street name.

The fauna can be counted. Springtails beyond counting in the moss and none anywhere else. One pale moth. Perhaps a dozen small white birds nesting in the wall, which carry the seeds and are therefore held to be the city's actual masons. Something with feet crosses the bowl once or twice a season, high on the rim, and never comes down.

And once, four generations back, the ice gave up a fish. Perfect, frozen, silver, of a kind nobody has seen alive. They keep it dried in a stone box in the old quarter, and it is the only evidence in the world that water can run in a line, and the word for river is a word from stories, and children argue about whether the fish was made by the ice as a joke.

## Late afternoon

The rain stops around four. The fog stands in the bowl and the sun comes through it sideways and the whole floor of pans lights up, thousands of separate flat brightnesses, none of them connected, all of them reflecting the same cloud. A girl crosses the great plaza with two hide buckets on a yoke, and her footsteps come back off the west wall a heartbeat after she takes them, so that she seems to be followed. The lamps go on in eleven hundred doorways. Under the ice the river booms its one note. Two paces of stone are being uncovered, this year, somewhere up at the snout, in the dark, by nothing anyone would call a tool.

The water lies where it fell. It has not yet decided where to go.
