From the grass, twelve kilometres out, they read as limestone: a chain of pale towers along a low escarpment, fluted by rain, softened at the shoulders, the sort of formation a geologist would call mature. Eleven of them, the largest a hundred and forty metres above the plain, wide as a village at the base. There is no limestone. There is no rock of any kind within ninety metres of where a boot would land. The towers are droppings, straw, mud, saliva, silk, wax, chewed wood and bone, laid down and packed and re-laid for something on the order of two hundred thousand years, until the accumulation became a highland and the highland acquired weather.

You hear it before you can smell it, and you smell it before you can see what it is.

At four kilometres the sound is a hiss, like a distant highway or surf on shingle. At one kilometre it has separated into layers — a high continuous sheet, a low pulsing under it, a scattering of individual cries that arrive like grit thrown at a window. At two hundred metres it stops being sound in the ordinary sense and becomes a medium you are walking into. At the wall it is a pressure on the sternum and a ringing that will still be in your ears at nightfall, three valleys away. Ninety decibels at midnight, when almost nothing is happening. A hundred and ten at dawn, when everything is.

The smell is ammonia over warm bread. Under that, wet straw, mushroom, cellar-iron, and — from certain quarters, in certain months — a sweetness like figs going over.

---

The flanks are thatch. Grass stems laid butt-down in overlapping courses, each course a hand's breadth, each stem placed by a beak and tamped by a beak and replaced, on average, every four years. From below the towers look like stone because the older courses have been rained on and dunged on and mineralised into a pale crust; from a metre away you can see the stems, gold and grey and silver, and the tiny regular pucker of ten million repair sites. The rain has carved the flanks into gullies and runnels with the graded, unhurried profiles of a drainage system that has had all the time it needs. It is a settled surface. It is settled because it is being rebuilt as fast as it is destroyed. Every rill is being re-daubed with mud at its head and shoulders, every slump is being thatched over, and the balance you read from twelve kilometres out is not the balance of forces too weak to matter but of demolition and construction running neck and neck, forever, at four centimetres a year.

The plain here lies at twenty-four hundred metres. The roofs are higher. Before dawn there is frost on the topmost thatch, a white bloom that lasts eleven minutes and is then melted from beneath by the bodies. The sky above is thin and hard and cold. The country underneath is thirty degrees.

---

There is no ground. This is the first thing to get straight, and it takes a while, because the surface you would walk on is convincingly a surface: a rolling terrain of moss and fern and low bramble, cut by sandy paths, dotted with fig trees whose crowns are flat as tables from the wind. It is a ceiling. It is the top course of a city, and it is four to nine metres thick, and beneath it the space is not solid and not empty but occupied, chamber by chamber, in every direction, down.

The apertures are five to seven centimetres across. Every one of them. Ten thousand to the square metre on the older roofs, each with its entrance tunnel angled steeply downward so that rain runs out and snakes run into a dead end, each bristling outward with a thatch of sharpened straw stubs at head height, which is to say at the head height of something weighing thirty grams. Nothing here is sized for you. You could lie on the roof with your ear against the moss and hear the whole population through a hand's breadth of grass, and you would be lying on nine hundred thousand front doors.

Your weight, incidentally, is a seismic event. A single footfall compacts six chambers.

---

The rock, where there is rock, is guano. Below the living quarters the accumulation has lithified under its own mass into a cream-and-ochre stone, banded like a sediment, because it *is* a sediment: fine dark lines of beetle chitin from the insect years, pale bands of fig seed, rust-coloured seams from a season of some red berry that no longer grows on this side of the escarpment. It takes a chisel. It smells faintly of a farmyard when wet and of nothing at all when dry, and it is the only substrate in the region that could be mistaken for natural, and it was excreted.

Above it, the load-bearing material is carton: wood and grass chewed to pulp, mixed with saliva and dung, laid in sheets that dry to something between paper and cork. It is warm to the touch. It gives slightly under a thumbnail. It is honeycombed by galleries that spiral rather than climb, because the traffic hops. Between the carton masses run lattices of bone — not arranged, exactly, just accumulated where the dead were carried, and then bound with silk from caterpillars farmed in the roof brambles, and then daubed, until a heap of small skeletons became a beam.

Everything is warm to the touch. That is the point of the place.

---

The heat is a crop. In the deep middens, where the thatch and the droppings and the fallen are being worked by fungi and beetles and larvae, the compost runs at fifty degrees, and the working faces are turned and opened and closed by mound-hens: dull brown birds, heavy in the body, poor in the air, who insert their bills into the heap to the base of the skull and hold them there, and read the temperature with the tongue, and then add straw or scrape straw off until the number is right. They are not incubating with their bodies. They have not incubated with their bodies for a very long time. They build a thermostat and stand on it and lay into it and walk away, and eleven weeks later the chicks dig themselves out into the dark, fully feathered, and are ignored.

Around that furnace the whole thermal geography is arranged. The core galleries hold twenty-nine to thirty-one degrees through every night of the year and every frost on the roof. The flanks run cooler and are the nurseries in the wet months and the dormitories in the dry. The chimneys — vertical shafts, some of them a metre across, running the full height of the tower — carry the exhaled carbon dioxide up and out at the crown, and pull cold air in at the base, and the entire mass breathes on a slow diurnal cycle you can feel as a draught on a wetted finger held at any opening. In the dry season the birds plug the upper shafts with grass to hold the heat, and unplug them at dawn, and the towers steam.

---

Water is a drip, not a flow. There is no stream on the escarpment, no spring, no lake. There is a wet season of afternoon storms lasting nine weeks, and for the rest of the year there is condensation, which is the real water supply and the reason the place is damp rather than dry.

At dawn the towers exhale warm saturated air into a cold thin sky, and the chimneys fog, and the fog condenses on the underside of the thatch, and the thatch, laid butt-down, conducts every bead outward and downward along the stem to its tip, where it hangs and falls. Ten million stems, ten million drip points. The result is that the flanks of the towers are permanently beaded and the roof gardens drink from beneath, and beneath the roofs there are drip-lines — vertical wet seams running down through the galleries — and a drip-line is a street. It is an address. Everything that needs water lives on one, and the density of occupation along a good seam is such that the walls are not walls with animals on them but animals arranged into a wall.

The cisterns are clay-lined bowls the size of two cupped hands, thousands of them, each sealed with wax at the rim, each with its resident frog or its resident mosquito larvae or its resident nothing. The water is tea-brown, faintly ammoniacal, and tastes of iron and feathers. All of it has been inside something. Most of it has been inside several things. There is no clean water anywhere in this country and no organism here that would recognise clean water as an improvement.

---

The dark is nearly total. Twenty centimetres from a light well you are in dusk; two metres in you are in a black that has weight. The population does not use light. It uses echolocation — the swiftlets' dry clicks, like fingernails tapped on tile, thousands per second, and beneath hearing, the bats' shout, which you feel in the sinuses as a kind of tickle and a nausea — and it uses air currents, and smell, and a memory of geometry so exact that a bird returning at full speed to a five-centimetre hole in absolute darkness does not slow down.

The light wells are the only daylight, and they are gorgeous, and they are rare: shafts where a collapse or a design has left a straight run to the sky, admitting a green-white column full of dust and moths and falling seed. Where a column lands, there is a garden — a hanging thicket of fern, a moss lawn, sometimes a fig that has got its roots into a carton buttress and become structural. The figs are load-bearing. When one dies the tower it holds begins to fail within a decade, and the colony moves out of that quarter over sixty years, and the quarter lithifies, and after two centuries it is bedrock with a well shaft through it.

The only resident light is the glowworms: blue-green pinpoints in the wet ceilings, each with its curtain of beaded silk hanging beneath it, catching midges. There are galleries where they are dense enough to read a hand by. Nobody reads a hand by them. They are simply the ceiling, and the ceiling is fishing.

---

The numbers do not resolve. Nine million nests in the largest tower; something over forty million birds on the escarpment; bats in the lower galleries in a mass that takes forty minutes to empty at dusk and comes out like smoke pouring sideways from a hundred mouths, thickening the sunset, going out over the grass in a river two kilometres wide. Between the shifts the air itself is a tissue of bodies. Then the swiftlets go in, and the moths come up, and the crickets take over the walls — a single unbroken six-kilohertz sheet from the thatch, every stem of it inhabited — and the frogs start in the cisterns, and the cicadas pulse in the roof brambles, and under everything runs the mound's own note, thirty hertz, air in the shafts, felt more than heard.

And under *that*, everywhere, at all hours, the sound of work: millions of beaks tamping mud and threading grass, a soft continuous patter like light rain on a paper roof. It is the least dramatic noise here and the most important. It is the sound of the mountain continuing.

There are no generalists. Every animal in this place is a specialist of the made world: a snake that has lived a hundred generations in the walls without touching soil, an endemic mouse that cannot dig, a flightless cricket that eats thatch, a moth that eats feathers, a lizard that exists only in the four-centimetre gap between courses of straw, and the beetles and mites and larvae in numbers that make the birds a rounding error, doing the actual work of the place, which is digestion.

---

Time here is slow and the fast things in it are weather. A bird lives eight years. The roof rises four centimetres a year and subsides in the core at almost the same rate. The colony's centre of mass drifts three metres a decade to the north, chasing the rain-shadow of the prevailing wind, and no individual notices or intends this. The towers lean a degree in a millennium and are corrected by asymmetrical repair, which nobody plans. The year has two movements: the wet, when the roofs thicken and the fungus blooms and the nurseries move outward; and the dry, when the shafts are plugged and the population contracts toward the furnace and the dust goes golden in the light wells.

Even the grass out on the plain — the only thing here that looks like ordinary nature — grows on guano dust blown off the flanks, and is seeded by birds, and is a suburb.

---

Two rules hold everything.

The first: nothing is finished. There is no completed structure anywhere on the escarpment, no monument, no ruin that is not also a quarry. A tower exists only in the act of being maintained; stop the maintenance for one season and it begins to be a hill, for ten and it begins to be a plain. The stone at the bottom is not the foundation. It is the exhaust.

The second: silence means failure. There is a quarter on the southern tower where a storm opened the roof ninety years ago and the colony withdrew, and the thatch there has gone grey and slack, and the chambers are full of dry husks and shed skins, and the drip-line has moved. You can stand at its edge and hear your own blood. In a country where sound is the proof of maintenance, that quiet is the most obscene thing on the mountain — not death, exactly, but the local cessation of the only process that has ever kept this place above the grass.
