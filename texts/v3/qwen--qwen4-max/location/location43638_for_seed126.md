Everything here is warm, and everything here is made, and nothing stands more than a fingernail's thickness above anything else.

The plain goes to the edge of sight and then continues without changing its mind. It is the color of the inside of a shell, bone-white with a faint apricot warmth in it, and it is smooth the way a tooth is smooth — not polished, but worn, worked over so many times that the working has become the texture. Underfoot it gives about a millimeter, like firm flesh, and then holds. It is thirty-four degrees. It is always thirty-four degrees. Blood-warm, so that lying on it you cannot feel where your body stops being the warmer thing.

There are no shadows at full bright. The light does not come from a direction; it comes from the air itself, from a haze of motes hanging in the first ten kilometers, each mote a made thing the size of a bacterium, each one a lens and a mirror and a small lamp. The atmosphere is the light source. Look up and you see a white ceiling with no ceiling in it. Look out and you see the plain dissolve — not into haze, not into distance-blur, but into *glare*, because the farther you look the more lamp you are looking through, and so the far is brighter than the near. Things do not fade as they recede here. They brighten, and whiten, and are taken. A stone at ten meters is a stone. A stone at a kilometer is a spark. A stone at five is nothing at all, and the nothing is very bright.

---

Kneel and take a handful of the ground and you will not get soil. You will get grains: two hundred thousand of them, perhaps, each a fraction of a millimeter across, each rounded by age into something like a tiny worn molar. Each one is an object. Each one was made. Hold them to the light and you can see the seams of manufacture gone soft with handling, the facet-edges rounded down, the faint interior structure like the ghost of a circuit in old glass. They are not dead. Each grain holds a charge, passes it to its neighbors, remembers a number, forgets it, remembers another. They click over from state to state at a rate of a few thousand times a year, and there are ten to the twenty-eighth of them in the top meter of the world, and the sum of all that patient switching is the heat you feel through your knees.

They are packed near-touching. Ninety-one percent fill. What remains is the seams: a connected labyrinth of pores, throat to chamber to throat, running down through the strata for kilometers. This is where the air moves. This is where anything that lives, lives.

The strata are all made too. Dig — and the digging is not easy, the plain resists in a mild, disinterested way, the grains flowing back — and at two meters you find the previous floor, its grains a different dialect: hexagonal, cooler white, still switching. At forty meters, a floor of fused plates, ground to gravel, ground to grains, still switching. At a kilometer, a floor whose grains are the size of fists and whose purpose can no longer be guessed. Below that, the record continues past the reach of any instrument that has ever been lowered into it. There is no bedrock. There is no natural substrate anywhere in the world. It is artifact all the way down, and the artifact is old enough that the distinction between made and native has stopped being useful, the way the distinction between a river and a canal stops being useful after ten million years.

Across the plain's continental breadth, the grain-dialects change. You can walk for a year and cross eleven provinces without ever seeing a boundary, without ever seeing anything but the same warm level going on; and then one morning you look down and the grains under your hand are faintly blue, and have been for six hundred kilometers, and you did not notice the world change.

---

The weather is light.

Charge rises out of the deep strata, a slow exhalation of the old work, and the mote-haze converts it and shines. When the haze is charged it brightens and the motes swell and clump; clumped, they fall; falling, they thin the air; thinned, the air dims. Then the charge builds again. A full turn takes about forty hours: thirty of glare, ten of what passes here for evening, which is not darkness but a deep amber pearl, a light like the inside of a lit honey jar.

In the dim you can suddenly see a hundred kilometers, and there is nothing to see. But you can see *shadows* — ten hours out of forty, everything on the plain casts a faint gray smear, and this is the only time the world has depth cues, and everything that moves, moves then.

There are other weathers. Sift-fronts: walls of denser mote, rolling across the plain at a walking pace, visible from far off as a brightening, arriving as a warm dry pressure on the face and a doubling of the light, gone in an hour. Frit-fall: when the clumped motes come down as a slow bright snow, a micrometer of new material laid over the whole world in a night, which the plain absorbs and levels before the next bright. And the breathing: kilometer-wide convection cells, warm air rising out of the pore-mouths, cooler air sinking back in, a wind that never exceeds a stroll but never stops, and that smells of hot dust and warm mirror and something faintly alkaline, like the air inside an old lamp.

Nothing here is wet. Nothing here has ever been wet. There is no solvent in the world; chemistry happens at contacts, dry, grain to grain, an electron handed across a touch and handed back. The air will take the moisture out of a living eye in ninety seconds. What passes for humidity is luminance. What passes for a river is a bright front. What passes for rain is frit, and it is not liquid, and it does not pool, and it is gone by morning.

---

The vegetation is in the seams, and it is sparse, and you will walk a long way before you find it.

Look down into a pore-mouth in the dim, when the glare is off, and you may see a thread of pale copper glass running away into the rock, branching, thinning, gone. Seam-lichen. It grows by drawing charge along the contact-chains, and it pays for the charge by depositing structure: filaments of drawn silica, brittle, warm, faintly conductive, no thicker than a hair. It cannot come above the surface. It has never come above the surface. Everything that ever came above the surface is now part of the surface. So the lichen lives entirely inside the world's porosity, a thin copper vascular system threaded through kilometers of made rock, and its total visible expression across a province is a few faint stains at the pore-mouths, like rust on a bone.

It blooms, in its way. When a sift-front passes and the charge density jumps, the filaments run a bright pulse along themselves, a slow copper flare traveling down into the rock at a centimeter a second, and for an hour the pore-mouths of a whole district glow like the embers of something. Then it goes out. This happens perhaps four times a year, and nothing sees it, and it is the most beautiful thing on the plain.

---

The animals are rare, and flat, and they do not stop.

The commonest is a sole: a disc three centimeters wide and four millimeters thick, matte on top, exactly the color of the ground, translucent at the rim. It presses itself to the plain and absorbs charge through its underside and grazes the lichen by reaching a fringe of filaments down into the pores. It moves about a body-length an hour. It must. The plain does not measure your height; it measures your height *averaged over a turn*. Anything that keeps moving, slowly, is smeared out by the averaging and falls below tolerance. Anything that stands still becomes a bump, and bumps are corrected.

Soles have no eyes. They have a rim organ that reads the glare-gradient, which is how they know where the far is and therefore where they have not been. They reproduce by fission when they cross a rich seam, which is not often; a fertile district may hold one sole per hectare, and a poor one, one per hundred square kilometers. In the dim they lift their rims a fraction and travel a little faster, and their shadows go with them, gray smears sliding over the bone-white level, and this is the only visible motion in the world.

There is a thing that hunts them. It is a bead, a centimeter across, of sintered grain compressed to a density that has no business in a warm world; it is very heavy for its size and it is patient. It finds a sole by the sole's faint excess of warmth, and it climbs aboard, and it sits. The sole slows. The averaging fails. Over the next two turns the plain notices a persistent deviation of nine hundred microns at a certain coordinate, and the grains beneath the sole begin, without haste and without malice, to take it apart — dissolving it downward, warm, granular, a gentle sinking. The bead's coat is within tolerance and is left alone. It waits, and then it feeds on what is released, and then it goes looking again. It is not cruel. It is a mechanism that has discovered a loophole in another mechanism, which is what predation is everywhere.

And there are kites: membranes a micron thick and a meter across, riding the light-pressure gradients at the boundary between glare and dim, silver, silent, seen perhaps twice a year. Whether they are animals or instruments the plain built for itself and forgot about is not a question the world is equipped to answer. They may be both. Most things here are both.

---

It is quiet. It is quiet for a reason.

The mote-haze eats pressure waves; the same density that makes the air a lamp makes it a damper. A shout dies at three meters. A clap dies at two. Sound is relief in the air, and this world's entire principle is the abolition of relief, and it applies the principle to the air as readily as to the stone. What sound there is, is small and close: the crush of grains under a hand, like sugar; the tick of a single grain flipping state, a fingernail on porcelain, heard perhaps once an hour, from somewhere within a meter of you, never located.

But the floor conducts. Vibration runs through the packed grains for hundreds of kilometers with almost no loss, and everything that lives here listens with its underside. Through the soles of the feet you would feel the whole plain: the slow shrug of a pore-collapse forty kilometers down and two kilometers deep, arriving as a sigh; the thermal contraction of the top meter as the dim comes on, a vast faint tinkling, ten billion grains settling a fraction of a micron each, the closest thing this world has to rain on a roof; and, if you sat very still for a day, the tread of a sole, three centimeters wide, moving a body-length an hour, somewhere out in the white.

---

The rule is one rule, and everything else follows from it.

*Nothing may stand.* The world is engaged in levelling itself, and has been for longer than the grains can remember, and it is nearly finished. It began with relief measured in kilometers. It is now working at a tolerance of two hundred and fifty microns, averaged over a turn, and the tolerance tightens by about a micron every hundred thousand years. All the machinery of the plain — the grains, the pores, the sift, the correction that takes a bump apart — is one distributed engine performing one operation, patiently, on a sphere.

Because it is a sphere. That is the final form of levelling, and the plain knows it, and the last irreducible deviation is the curve itself: at the very bottom of the dim, when the mote-haze is thin and the glare is gone, the horizon shows a faint arc, the one bend in the world, and it is not an error, and it cannot be corrected, and the plain works toward it anyway.

Nothing enters and nothing leaves. Matter is conserved absolutely; anything new must be made of something old; the light is powered by the world's own slow settling, the last capital of an ancient charge, spent at a rate that will keep the lamps burning for another few billion years and then, gradually, not.

And life — the lichen in the pores, the soles that never stop, the bead, the kites — lives entirely inside the tolerance. That is what everything alive here *is*: the error bar. The permitted deviation. As the tolerance tightens, the ecology thins; there were forests in the seams once, and herds, and things that flew in the glare. Now there is copper thread, and a disc per hectare, and a bead, and twice a year a silver membrane in the amber evening. It is not a dying world. It is a world being finished, and the finishing is very slow, and it is warm, and it is bright, and it is not unhappy.

Walk out into it and your footprints will stay for four days as a faint dark trail where the light pools differently, and then the plain will rise behind you a micron at a time and take them back. Build a cairn and it will be gone in a day, gently, warmly, from the bottom up. Stand still for two turns and you will feel the ground begin, very kindly, to consider you.

At the end of the dim, when the arc shows on the horizon and the pore-mouths are breathing their warm lamp-smell and ten billion grains are ticking down a fraction of a micron, a sole out in the white lifts its rim and moves three centimeters, and its gray shadow moves with it, and the average holds.

Then the glare comes back up, and the shadows go, and the world is one smooth bright warm thing again, all the way to the edge of sight, and past it, and past that.
