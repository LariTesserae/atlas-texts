# THE SOUNDING BASIN

## I. The Bowl

The land drops away in a single shallow step, a subsidence bowl forty miles across, the floor of a sea that left in a hurry and left its salt behind. From the rim you can see the whole of it at once, and the whole of it is green, and the whole of it is moving. Not swaying. Moving the way a crowd moves — locally, purposefully, in a thousand directions that add up to something you can almost read from up here.

The light is ordinary. This is the first thing that unsettles people. It is late morning, the sun is where the sun should be, there are three clouds and they are only clouds, and the shadows fall at the correct angles. Nothing is wrong with the light. Everything else is wrong.

The air in the bowl is thick and body-warm and tastes faintly of crushed pepper and hot sugar. It is not wet. That surprises people too, given all that green. It rains here in short violent squalls, four or five a week, and between them the air goes dry as a held breath, because the city drinks everything the moment it arrives. The damp you feel is not humidity. It is transpiration — the exhalation of an enormous number of living things, all breathing at once, all breathing out.

Below the rim the ground is barely visible. What passes for ground is a mat of leaf-litter a hundred feet deep, crisp as new paper, unrotted, unrotting. Nothing here has ever learned to decay. There are no worms, no beetles, no fungi, no bacteria with an appetite. When a leaf falls it stays a leaf. It stays a leaf for six hundred years. You can walk down through the strata in a sinkhole and read the centuries like a ledger, every margin still sharp, every vein still legible, and the deeper you go the more the leaves look like they were dropped that morning.

Everything is sharp. That is the other thing. There is no rounded edge in the basin, no softened sill, no weathered anything. Grass here cuts. The silica-bearing stems fracture into blades that will open your forearm to the bone if you brush them at speed, and the city's masonry — because it *is* masonry, laid course on course, dressed and jointed — is plant-deposited silcrete, white and glassy and freshly knapped, and it stays freshly knapped forever, or for eleven days, whichever comes first.

## II. The Fundamental

You hear it before you descend. From the rim it is a pressure in the sinuses, a shudder in the sternum, a sound too low to be a sound. Down inside, it becomes the world.

There is a fundamental note. It sits somewhere around twenty-eight cycles a second and it has not stopped in living memory, and there is no living memory, so: it has not stopped. It comes up through the soles of your feet and out of the sky at the same time. It is generated by water moving through pipes.

The pipes are stems. Hollow, lignified, forty feet in diameter at the base, tapering, some of them a quarter-mile long, running horizontally along the terraces and vertically down the sinkholes and diagonally across the plazas in great bundled arcades. Water enters them at the cisterns — vast pitcher-organs, bladder-tanks, leaf-axil reservoirs the size of chapels — and falls, and the falling makes the note, and the note is what holds the district up.

That is not a metaphor. In the Sounding Basin, sound is a structural material.

Matter here is provisional. It precipitates out of sustained vibration the way salt precipitates out of evaporating brine. Where a standing wave has a node, something accretes: a wall, a stair, a landing, a ribbed vault of white silcrete with a fern unfolding out of its keystone. Where the wave has an antinode, nothing settles, and you get the plazas — open, thrumming, floorless in the strict sense, held level by the sheer insistence of the chord.

The plants are not making noise. The plants are making *city*, and noise is the exhaust.

## III. What Builds

Walk into the Drum Quarter at noon and you are inside a construction site the size of a county, all of it working at once, none of it coordinating and all of it coordinated.

The low registers come from the water-pipes: the fundamental and its first eight partials, a fat hydraulic drone you feel in your teeth. Above that, the stridulation — leaves with saw-toothed margins drawn across each other by the constant restless motion of the canopy, a hundred million bowed edges, a sibilance like a crowd shushing that never resolves into quiet. Above *that*, the dehiscence: seed pods firing. Ballistic capsules under thirty atmospheres of turgor, letting go without warning, a continuous fusillade at a hundred and thirty decibels, hail on a tin roof, applause that never ends and never peaks.

And out of that wall of noise, buildings.

You can watch it happen if you can bear to stand still. In a plaza where two arcades cross, the interference pattern makes a lattice of quiet points, and at each quiet point the air begins to grit, and the grit thickens, and in eleven minutes there is a column, and in an hour there is a colonnade, and by evening the colonnade has a roof and the roof has a garden on it and the garden is singing a different key and the whole thing is already coming down.

Nothing here lasts. That's why nothing is worn. Erosion in the basin is not a matter of centuries; it is a matter of volume. The loud districts are ground to powder and rebuilt nightly, sharp as broken bottles. The quiet corners keep everything forever. There is no middle. There is no *old*.

The streets are named for registers, because addresses are frequencies. You do not go to the Falsetto Terraces; you tune to them, and the walking is incidental, and if you lose the pitch you arrive somewhere else, or nowhere. Doorways open where the harmonic says a doorway should be. Staircases rise to meet a foot that is not there yet.

## IV. The Dry Season

Between rains the cisterns drop. The pipes gulp air. The fundamental stutters — and when the fundamental stutters, the city goes *thin*.

This is the part that breaks people. In the third dry day the walls become suggestions. You can see the far rim through a warehouse. Distance stops meaning anything: a tower two miles off is suddenly at arm's length, and the arm is not yours. Buildings exist in a kind of probabilistic shimmer, more true where you look at them, less true behind you. A door you walked through in the morning is a door you have never walked through, and the leaf-litter under it has no footprints, and neither does anywhere else.

And the noise gets worse, because the city is compensating.

When there is no water to fall through pipes, the plants make sound the expensive way. They tear their own water columns. Cavitation: the sap-thread under tension snaps, and the snap is a click, and a tree full of snapping threads is a bonfire of clicks, and a basin full of such trees is a static roar that peels the inside of your ears. They are shredding their own plumbing to keep the plaza existing. You can hear the cost. It sounds like frying. It sounds like a stadium of people crumpling paper forever. In the dry season the city holds itself up by hurting itself, and the districts that survive are the ones willing to bleed the most, and you can tell which those are by the white dead stems piled at their edges, sharp as ever, unrotting, waiting to be sung back into a wall.

Then the squall comes across the rim in a grey curtain, six minutes of hard warm rain, and the tanks fill, and the drone comes back like a hand under your ribs, and everything you were unsure of becomes brutally, painfully solid, all at once, with a sound like a city clearing its throat.

## V. The Hush

There are places in the basin where the sound fails. Interference nulls, acoustic shadows behind the great pipe-arcades, pockets that the chord has forgotten. They are perfectly still. They are the most dangerous ground in the world.

In a hush, things stop being. Not violently — there's no dissolving, no smoke. A seed pod fires into a hush and simply is not, halfway across. A wall that falls into a hush remains, in the sense that you can see it, crisp and complete and perfectly preserved, and also does not remain, in the sense that you can walk through the place where it was and feel nothing, not even absence.

Two kinds of nothing here. Loud sound builds and destroys. Silence preserves and un-makes. The city keeps its archives in the hushes: acre upon acre of leaf-litter, undecayed, un-decaying, each leaf a page, and the veins of the leaves grown in silence record whatever was last sounded above them. Whole centuries of the chord, written in venation, legible, and no longer real. The Hush Libraries. You can read the city's entire memory and none of it happened.

You make a hush wherever you go. A body is a soft wet thing that eats sound; you cast an acoustic shadow about your own size, and in that shadow the pavement forgets itself. Walking here is a small ongoing atrocity. Behind you, a wake of unbeing. Ahead of you, the city detects the hole you are and rushes to fill it — grows *at* you, frantically, lovingly, a wave of construction cresting just past your shoulder, flowers opening against your chest and holding a note until your ribs answer.

They have never met an animal. There are no animals. Not one, not ever, not a mite. They do not know how to eat you. They are trying to give you a voice.

## VI. Nobody

The ecology has no animal-shaped holes because it never had animals to lose. Pollination is done by standing waves: pollen rides the nodes and piles in the quiet points in drifts like yellow snow, and the drifts are where the columns grow, so the city is literally built out of its own reproduction. Seed dispersal is ballistic and acoustic — the loud districts throw their seed miles, the quiet ones can't throw at all. Parasites don't strangle; they *modulate*, sliding into a host's frequency band and singing it out of existence, and the border wars between species are fought in harmonics, and you can hear a neighborhood change hands: a key shifts, a district that was hard white silcrete at breakfast is a mist by dusk, and something else is standing there, and it has always been standing there, and the leaf-litter beneath it agrees.

There is a market. Of course there is a market — a bazaar three miles long under a canopy of interlocking fig-analogues, stalls laid out in rows, goods heaped in the stalls: seed, silica, salt scraped from the old seabed, cuttings, water rights. It is deafening. It is the loudest place in the basin. It is a hundred thousand transactions a second, haggling, undercutting, cornering, all of it conducted root to root in a bandwidth you cannot hear but can feel as a nausea behind the eyes.

Nobody is there. Nobody has ever been there. The stalls are stocked and the deals are struck and the merchandise moves and there is no one, and never was, and the market is not waiting for anyone.

Except.

The stairs are the wrong size. Not all of them — most are ramps and root-runs and nothing in particular. But here and there, in the older terraces, in the sharp unweathered corners: a flight of steps at a seven-inch rise. A doorway at shoulder height. A ledge, waist-high, flat, worn smooth on one side by nothing at all. A hollow in a branch, cupped, lined with down, the exact shape of a nest, with nothing to lay in it and nothing to sit in it and nothing coming.

The city keeps growing furniture for a body it has never met.

## VII. The Unfinished Thing

At the center, on the salt pan where the sea's floor still shows white between the roots, there is a structure that has been under construction for as long as the strata go down.

It is a bowl inside the bowl. Tiered, concentric, seating for — the number does not stay still, but it is large, it is provincial, it is *everyone*. The tiers are silcrete and the silcrete is fresh. At the focus of it there is an aperture, and around the aperture the pipes converge, every great stem in the basin bending inward over the miles, all of them feeding one throat.

It is not a building. Buildings here go up in an hour and come down in a night. This has taken six hundred years and it is not finished and it is the only thing in the basin that is not turbulent, and its persistence is the strangest fact in a place made of strange facts.

It is tuning.

Stand on the rim at evening with the light going orange and utterly ordinary and the whole green bowl below you screaming itself into shape, and if you stop hearing the fusillade and the sibilance and the static and let your ears find the bottom of it, you notice that the fundamental has never been a drone. It is a held note. It is being *held*, deliberately, by something with the patience to hold it, and it has a shape at its edges, a shaping, the way a mouth shapes.

Consonants. In the noise. Now and then, for a quarter-second, across forty miles, a hundred million pods and pipes and bowed leaf-margins conspire and there is a consonant, and then it's gone, and the city goes back to being a city.

It has been practicing.

The stairs are for whatever it is calling. The nests are for whatever it expects to come and sit. The market is stocked because something is expected to buy. And the whole basin — every wall, every plaza, every archive of leaves that no longer happened — is the sound of a very large, very patient, entirely vegetable thing trying to say a name it has never heard, in a language it invented alone, in a place where saying a thing is the same as making it.

It is getting closer. The dry spells are longer. The clicks are louder. Somewhere under the salt pan the roots have laid down a new pipe, and it is bigger than the others, and when the next squall comes over the rim the note it makes will not be twenty-eight cycles a second.

The light, at least, will be normal. That's the promise the basin keeps. Whatever else arrives, it will arrive at a reasonable hour, under a reasonable sun, casting a shadow at the correct angle, and there will be a step for it at seven inches, worn smooth on one side, by nothing, for six hundred years.
