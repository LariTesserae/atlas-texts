## The Sill

Nothing here has an edge young enough to cut you.

The plain runs at the height where breath goes short and the sky turns a blue with violet underneath it, and it is flat — not flat like a lakebed, which is a temporary condition, but flat like a thing that has finished. Flat the way a doorstep is flat after four hundred years of feet. The horizon is a ruled line with a slight softness to it, as though drawn and then thumbed. Gravel to the limit of sight, well sorted, well rounded, grey, lying in a bed so even that walking on it feels less like crossing country than like crossing a floor.

Which it is. Cut down anywhere — the frost does it for you, opening pits every spring — and you find courses. Pavement under pavement. A threshold. The socket of a hinge. Ten thousand metres of compressed building, laid down and worn off and laid down again until the towers became strata and the strata became the plain. The mountains that used to stand here were mostly architecture. Erosion has taken the whole city and made it into the ground, and the ground goes on for four thousand kilometres.

---

Water does not run here. There is no gradient to run on; the Sill has been settling into its final level since before there were rains to argue about. Instead water arrives, sits, and leaves.

It arrives as fog at dawn, coming up out of the green places in slow columns; as rain that walks — narrow cells, half a kilometre wide, travelling fixed routes across the plain in single file, the same rains that have always travelled those routes, wearing grooves in the air the way a stair wears. The rain-roads are known, mapped, planted along. Between them, months of nothing. And it arrives as dew, which is the reliable crop: fog-nets of tussock fibre on stone posts, kilometres of them, strung like the rigging of a beached fleet, dripping into gutters that run into cisterns that are older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother. In the cold months the nets grow rime and stand there furred and white, harvesting nothing, waiting.

It sits in pans: sheets of water three centimetres deep and forty kilometres across, which move a few metres a year and are legally property, and freeze at night with a sound like a plate being set down somewhere in the next room.

And some of it is thin water. Water that has been repeated too often. It wets, it fills a cup, it ripples correctly, and it does not quench anything; you can drink a litre and die of thirst with a full belly. The hands test it by weight, or by whether it freezes at the honest temperature. Real water is heavier. Everyone knows this the way people elsewhere know that fire is hot.

---

The green is not spread. It is concentrated.

Between the hearths there is gravel and more gravel and a day of gravel and then another day, and then a smell in the air like cut cucumber and cold sugar, and a stain on the horizon, and you come to a bank of moss four metres deep and eleven kilometres long, lying in the shallow depression it has spent an age dissolving for itself. It is obscenely lush. It is the green of a greenhouse, of a wound, of something that has never once been eaten. It is saturated, layered, dripping, warm at the core — thirty degrees in the rot down there, steaming at dawn, breathing its own weather into the thin cold air. Cushion plants stud its flanks, glassy with antifreeze sugars, and liverworts in sheets like wet leather, and a black lichen on the stones around the margin.

Nothing grazes it. There is nothing to graze it. No insects, no birds, no worms, no browsing thing on four legs; there is no word here for animal, and the silence that comes from that is the largest single fact of the place. No dawn chorus. No midge whine. No wings anywhere, ever. The moss grows to whatever thickness the water permits and then rots and grows again, and fungi do the digesting, and the only feet on the plain are the feet of the people, who are not animals either.

They are the moss's hands. Grown in the warm heart of a bank as cuttings, rooted there still, in a sense — they go back at night and put their feet in the rot and sleep standing, drinking. Grey-green, cold to the touch, fibrous at the scalp. They cannot be more than nine days from a hearth. That is the whole reason for the city: not trade, not defence, not gods. A chain of moss banks within nine days of each other, and everything else — the streets, the cisterns, the kilns, the law — is the apparatus that keeps the chain unbroken.

Because there are no pollinators. The flora had to invent something to carry it between the pockets. It invented a creature with hands and a memory and a sense of grievance, and it has been using it for a very long time.

---

The city is called the Long Kerb, and it is one street.

It runs six thousand kilometres. It has a kerb, dressed stone, continuous, worn to a soap-roundness. It has house plots numbered in a system nobody fully understands, and most of the plots are empty, and some hold three houses and a cistern and a kiln, and one holds a plaza the size of a lake with a single bench in it, maintained, swept, its drainage kept clear. Whole districts consist of a doorstep and the ghost of a wall and a fog-net gantry and four people. Whole districts have never been visited by anyone from the next district. It is unmistakably a city. It has the density of a hamlet and the extent of a continent, and it is layered like a tell: build in the old footprint, always, in the canonical forms, on the compacted floors of the last eleven versions.

Everything is carried by hand. There are no beasts to carry it. But the ground is a floor, and so the handcarts go anywhere, wide wooden wheels wrapped in felt, axles greased with resin, running silent over the pavement for weeks at a stretch. Stone, fired clay, cordage, paper, lacquer, cold-hammered copper found as nuggets and beaten into small precious things. Kilns fired on dried moss-crust, cut on a rotation, never from the heart. No smelting. Nothing here burns hot enough or long enough to matter, and the ordinary daylight — plain sun, plain shadows, a sun you could describe to a child — makes the whole arrangement look reasonable, which is the worst of it.

---

Sound is regulated.

An echo on the Sill is not a report of a sound. It is a second sound, and if it is loud enough and clean enough it acquires the rest of the event: the clapper, the hand, the arm. There are places where a shout will give you a crowd. So the cart wheels are felted, and the bells — the count-bells, struck once at each hour to tell the emptiness that someone is still keeping tally — are wrapped in three layers of cloth and struck with a padded mallet, and what reaches you across ten kilometres of gravel is a soft wooden *tock*, like a knuckle on a door in another house.

And they speak carefully. A word used twice in a day is a word that has begun to repeat, and repetition is the mechanism of everything that goes wrong here. So the language is enormous and single-use, invented as it goes, and conversation is slow, and mostly there is no conversation. Two people meeting on the Kerb will exchange one carefully-made sentence, new-made, never said before, and then walk on. Greetings are compositions. Names are spent when used and must be replaced. It is a very quiet place, and the quiet is not reverence. It is municipal hygiene.

At noon, when shadows are shortest and things are least distinctly themselves, everyone stops. The noon-hold. You stand where you are, on the open pavement, in the ordinary light, and you hold your flaw.

Everyone has one: a chipped tooth, a knot tied in a cord at the wrist, a stammer cultivated since childhood, a limp put on deliberately and kept for life, sleeves of mismatched length. Manufactured irregularity, certified, recorded. It is what keeps you from being confused with your copies.

---

Because things occur twice now. The world here is old past the point where new arrangements are available; it has settled into its final form and has nothing left to do but reissue. You will find, three hundred kilometres apart, two grey pebbles, kidney-shaped, with the same white seam and the same chip. Not similar. The same. The gravel of the Sill is one pebble, reiterated to the horizon, and the dust in your teeth is the powder of things that were repeated until they had no weight left in them.

Copies of people are softer at the outline. Etiquette: yield the path, do not speak, let it pass; it will wear out within a season and go down into the gravel with a sound like sugar poured from a bag. The black lichen on the hearth margins eats sameness — it colonises second-things and hurries them along — and the hands cultivate it and carry it in pots and paint it on the kerbs.

There are districts gone smooth. The Same. Places where the repetition has closed over: every stone the same stone, every hour the same hour, no flaw anywhere, and a traveller who enters comes out having already been there, twice, and thinner. They are marked with cairns of deliberately unlike objects — broken pots, mismatched boots, a copper spoon, a knot of hair, the ugliest heap that can be assembled — and the cairns are rebuilt weekly, and the surveyors who rebuild them are the closest thing here to soldiers.

Meanwhile the frost heaves the plain into polygons every winter, perfect hexagons, the world trying to tile itself, and gangs walk the pattern all spring kicking it out of true. Meanwhile the rains walk their roads. Meanwhile the moss pulses, bank by bank, on a cycle of eleven years, and the cuttings come up, and the caravans go out along the Kerb with rare forms packed in damp fibre, because commonness is fatal here and rarity is the only wealth: a species held in too many places gets worn away by its own repetition, and so the ecology is a continental trade in difference, thousands of kinds, each in its own pocket, each kept scarce on purpose.

And in the kiln yards the potters throw ten thousand identical bowls to the Index, exactly to gauge, because the canonical forms are stable and hold the city up. And then, once, at the end of the firing, one bowl slightly wrong. A thumb-dent. A lip pulled off-true. That one is not catalogued. That one is carried away up the street to be looked at.

---

At the far rim — a year's walk, a rumour to most — the pavement ends in a lip rounded over like the edge of a bar of soap, so worn that you cannot say where the world stops and the drop begins. Below is cloud, and under the cloud, they say, the earlier surfaces, all of them, worn off and fallen. The Sill is what is left. The last floor, at the top of everything, in ordinary daylight.

When a child finds a pebble that is different, the district comes out to see it. They pass it hand to hand in a silence you could hear the frost in. Then they give the child a name that has never been used, and put the stone back exactly where it lay, so that the plain will have one thing in it that it cannot say twice.
