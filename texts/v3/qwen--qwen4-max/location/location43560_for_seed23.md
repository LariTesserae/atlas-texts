## The Kettle

You come up through the cloud sea for two days and the last hour is the worst of it, a chimney of wet rotten limestone where the holds crumble and the rope goes stiff with rime. Then the notch. Then the light hits you like a thrown bucket.

Everyone goes blind at the notch. The sill-folk who meet you there have their bone goggles already down — narrow slits, a hand's width of carved antler, the eyepieces smoked with fat — and they put a felt cloth over your head and turn you around and make you stand facing the rock for a count of two hundred while your eyes learn the place in pieces. When they let you look, they let you look at the ground first.

The ground is an inch of moving water over pale gravel, and it is going somewhere very slowly, and it is full of birds.

---

The bowl is three hundred paces long and half that across, a glacial cirque bitten out of the mountain's shoulder eleven thousand years ago and finished by water since. Its walls are not jagged. Nothing here is jagged. The rock has been scalloped and fluted and hollowed into potholes the size of bathtubs, into ribs, into the smooth overhung scoops that geologists call *sichelwannen* and the sill-folk call the aunts' alcoves. It is a settled form. The mountain has stopped arguing about its shape. What makes the Kettle is not the erosion but the *balance* of it: the water dissolves the lime and lays it down again a hundred paces lower, and the loss and the laying have matched each other for longer than there have been eyes to notice.

So the floor is a staircase. Rimstone terraces, forty of them, each lip a rounded dam of cream and honey-colored travertine, each tread a shallow pool an inch deep, stepping down from the back wall to the notch. Some lips are knee-high. Some are the height of a fingernail. In cross-section they look like the growth rings of something enormous and patient. The stone grows a finger's width in a human generation and it grows *smooth*, because it grows out of a film of water that never stops moving, and it is faintly, unevenly translucent, so that at night the terraces give back a weak blue-green shine, the day leaking out of them slowly, like heat out of a body.

Water is everywhere and almost none of it is falling. The back wall weeps. That is the only verb for it: a hundred seep-lines darkening the rock, gathering, spreading, never quite becoming a stream. At dawn the whole bowl breathes fog, a knee-deep condensation that burns off by the second hour. At the very back, in a crack under the biggest alcove, one vein runs warm — blood-warm, sulfurous, unfreezing — and around it grows the only green in the Kettle, a patch of moss and small bitter cress the size of a bed. Everything else that lives green here lives as crust: orange lichen plates, grey rosettes, black tar-lichen in the frost cracks, cushion plants like pincushions stuffed with grit, hair-moss in the seep-lines. No wood. Not a stick, not a shrub, not a tree in a day's walk. The bowl is at the height where the air is thin and clean and the sun comes down unfiltered and the night takes it all back, and it is wet the way a mouth is wet.

The first time you see the town you think: *ruin*.

---

Dry-stone cells, thousands of them, corbelled and stacked and packed along every terrace lip. Chambers the size of a bread oven, chambers the size of a coffin, chambers the size of a cupped hand. Causeways of split slabs across the pools. Sluices — hundreds of little stone gates, notched and shimmed, dividing and re-dividing the film of water. Retaining walls, revetments, ramps, stairs cut and stairs built, middens sorted into grades of gravel, a whole district of nothing but stacked pebbles arranged by color. Layer on layer: new work keyed into old work keyed into work so old the travertine has swallowed it and it is now only a lumpiness in the stone, a fossil of a doorway. In the middle, where the terraces are highest, stands a crooked tower about twice a man's height, solid, cemented, unreadable, its openings gone to mineral. Nothing built it that anyone remembers.

Then a pebble moves.

Then four thousand pebbles move.

---

They are called the aunts. They are not hens and not quite birds; the sill-folk name is a joke that stopped being a joke around nine hundred years ago. Standing, one is the height of a tall crow and the shape of a heron folded up. Pale, dust-colored, down so dense it looks felted, with a bare throat plate of nacreous keratin that they wet constantly with a tongue-flick, and long splayed toes for wet stone, and a beak with a chisel edge and a comb inside it, and — this is the part that makes visitors go quiet — a small claw at the wrist of each wing, a baby's claw kept for life, with which an aunt can hold a flake of stone, a shim, a lever of antler, and *use* it.

There are about twenty thousand of them in a space you could cross in four minutes.

They are eating. The wet stone grows a skin — algae, bacteria, the flour of springtails that graze it, millions to the square pace — and the aunts sweep it with their comb-beaks in long shoveling arcs, and their droppings feed it, and it has been feeding them and being fed by them since before the ice left. Above, the thin high wind carries up the dying insect drift of the whole warm world below and drops it here, in the bowl, like grain into a hopper: midges, aphids, moths stunned by cold, a fall of maybe two hundredweight a season. Nothing here needs a forest. The Kettle eats the sky.

And they are building. All of them. Constantly. A cell is finished, admired, and dismantled in the time it takes to boil water. The average structure in the Argument stands nine hours.

---

Here is the thing you understand on the second day, and it reorganizes everything you have already seen.

The aunts have no voices. Their syrinx is a vestige, a scrap. They cannot call. What they have instead is the throat plate, the wet mirror, and the extraordinary light of this place — direct sun off thin air, sun off snow, sun off wet travertine, sun bouncing four and five times around a bowl shaped like the inside of a spoon. They speak by *throwing reflections*, and by *casting shadows*, and by the exact placement of stone.

A cell is a sentence. Its lip-height, its aperture, the whiteness of the pebble at its threshold, the angle at which its wall catches the eleven-o'clock light and throws it into a neighbor's doorway — all of it is utterance. To build a wall in the wrong place is to interrupt. To build a wall in the *right* wrong place is to interrupt wittily. There are aunts who have spent their whole lives on the placement of a single grey chip so that at one minute past noon it puts a bar of shadow across a rival's threshold, and this is a remark so cutting that other birds will dismantle their own work to come and look at it.

They also tap. At close range, in the dark, in the winter galleries, beak on stone: a small dry tick, and a reply, and a run of ticks. It is the intimate register. You cannot hear one from ten paces. Standing in the middle of the town you hear twenty thousand of them at once and it sounds like sleet on a roof, like a hall full of clocks, like the noise a fire makes just before it catches.

That is the loudest thing in the Kettle.

---

The silence has a reason and the reason has ears.

Something hunts here — the sill-folk call it the listener — a pale harrier with a face like a barn owl's, wide-eyed and probably half-blind, that quarters the bowl at dusk and dawn on wings so downed you feel the air move before you hear it. It takes anything that makes a noise. It has been taking anything that makes a noise for a very long time. Everything in the Kettle has been shaped by that: the muffled flight, the felt feet, the tick-language kept under the hearing floor, the water spread thin.

When the listener crosses the rim, the town does not scatter. It *goes dark*. Twenty thousand throat plates turn edge-on in a wave that crosses the bowl in under four seconds, and the whole glittering chattering surface of the place becomes, suddenly, matte — a grey floor of hunched backs — and stays that way until the shadow has passed. The sill-folk do it too, dropping their pale felt cloths over their heads and their work. It is the only thing everyone in the Kettle does together.

---

The flood comes every afternoon and it comes silently.

By the second hour the sun is on the ice field above the rim and the melt begins, and by the fifth hour the seeps surge and the sheet rises — not a torrent, never a torrent, a *sheet*, an inch and a half of water spreading over the whole floor at once, cold enough to ache. There is no roar. There is a hiss like sand poured on a table, and then the sensation of the water climbing your ankles, and then the town begins to move uphill.

This is the daily tide, and it is why the Kettle looks like a city instead of a nest. The lower districts empty; the aunts carry their stones up the terraces in a slow dense braided traffic, twenty thousand birds passing pebbles beak-to-beak up forty steps, and the town reassembles itself six paces higher, and by evening the water drops and it all comes back down. Every day. Every day for forty thousand years. The travertine lays itself down where the film flows and the film flows where the sluices send it and the sluices are kept open by the aunts and by thirty-four human beings with antler picks, and if the sluices ever clogged the sheet would channelize, and if it channelized it would cut, and if it cut it would take the terraces out in a season.

The sill-folk have kept the sluices for nine hundred years. The terraces have been in balance for four hundred centuries. They know this arithmetic. They keep the sluices anyway. It is the central religious problem of the Kettle and nobody has solved it.

---

The people live in the undercut at the eastern wall: seven corbelled houses, felt-lined, doors so low you enter on your knees, everything inside soft — felt floors, felt hinges, felt on the tool handles, wool slippers, a language of hands. They came up here for the down. Aunt-down is the finest fiber in the world and the birds molt it in drifts against the leeward walls, and the felt made from it is worth its weight in barley down in the warm country, and once a year the traders come up the chimney and everyone goes out through the notch to *speak*, out loud, with voices, and they say it is the ugliest day of the year and they hate it and they talk until they are hoarse.

They eat the flour. A felt board swept across wet stone yields a grey paste of springtails and algae that is pressed and dried into cakes, and it tastes like the smell of a cellar, and it is nutritious, and there is more of it than there will ever be people. They eat midge-fall in the sun-shafts. They eat the bitter cress at the warm vein on the one day a year it is permitted. They have copper — three nuggets' worth, cold-hammered into hooks, no smithing, no forge. Fire is kept in a shuttered stone box in the undercroft, because a fire in the open is a *shout*, a false and enormous word thrown into the conversation, and it is obscene, and it is also a beacon for anything with ears.

Their real work, the work that has status, is reading.

You cannot look at the Argument directly for more than a few minutes; the light will take your eyes. So the readers sit in dark cells with a black basin of still water and a pinhole, and they watch the town upside down and dimmed, and they record what it says in knotted cord. There is a room in the undercroft with nine hundred years of cord in it. The readers do not agree on the grammar. They agree that it is *one* grammar, that no aunt knows it, that a bird raised outside the bowl grows up mute, and that the conversation has been going on continuously — through the daily tide, through the winter when the bowl fills with ice and the whole town goes down into the fossilized galleries and speaks only in taps — since before there was anyone to knot a cord.

Individuals are letters. The town is the sentence.

And in the middle of it stands the crooked cemented tower, the Old Sentence, a phrase so ancient the mineral has closed over it, which no living reader can parse, and around which the entire modern Argument is, arguably, a very long footnote.

---

At noon there are no shadows.

The sun stands in the notch of the sky and the bowl fills with light from every direction at once, five-fold reflected, shadowless, absolute — the white hour. And the town goes mute in a new way: not hushed but *aphasic*. Nothing can be said. The mirrors have nothing to bite on. For forty minutes twenty thousand birds stand in the glare with their plates flicking uselessly, and some of them build frantically and badly, and some fight, and the old ones go into the alcoves and wait, and the sill-folk come out with their picks and their bone goggles and do all the maintenance that requires walking through the middle of things.

Then the sun leans, and a shadow appears at the eastern lip, one thin bar of dark, and the first word of the afternoon goes across the bowl like a struck match, and everything begins again.

---

Stay until evening. The water drops. The gravel ticks as the needle ice starts to lift it, a sound like a distant fire, like frying, like the whole floor quietly disintegrating and reassembling a hair's height higher. The aunts come back down the terraces with their stones. The light goes long and red and the bowl catches it and, for about ninety seconds, throws a single beam out through the notch across the top of the cloud sea, forty miles of it, visible to anyone down there who happens to look up — which is how the Kettle appears in the lowland stories: not as a place, but as a spark on the mountain at dusk, gone before you can point at it.

Then that closes too. The terraces begin to glow their weak blue-green. Twenty thousand pale bodies press into cells that will not exist tomorrow, in a town that has stood for forty thousand years without keeping a single stone. The taps go on under everything, intimate, unhearable, continuous.

It has never once raised its voice. It has never once stopped.
