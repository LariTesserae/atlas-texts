# The Room

## I. The Lid

The light is ordinary. That is the first wrong thing.

It comes down even and milky, the flat shadowless light of an overcast noon, and it comes from above, and above is a ceiling of selenite two hundred meters up — a lid of translucent gypsum laid over the world in plates the size of fields, veined with opaque seams, sagging in places like a tent with too much sky on it. Somewhere beyond that lid there is weather. Nobody here has seen it. What arrives is only the light, diffused through fifteen body-lengths of crystal until it has no direction left, no source, no heat in the beam. You cannot find the sun by looking. You can only tell that it has gone by the slow blue bruising of the lid at the day's end, and by the way the forest changes its key.

The air is blood-warm and stays that way. Thirty-six, thirty-eight degrees, dry as a kiln, and it never cools more than a few degrees at night because the rock at this depth remembers a heat older than the sun, and because everything alive down here is friction.

## II. What You Notice Last

The sound is so total that for the first minute you mistake it for pressure.

It is not a roar. A roar has an outside. This has no outside; it is the medium. It comes up through the soles of the feet and the long bones of the legs and sets the fluid of the eyes trembling, so that the whole vast room shivers slightly, continuously, like a struck bell seen through heat. It sits on the sternum. It empties the sinuses. A newcomer vomits, and cannot hear themselves do it, and the resident beside them watches their shoulders and knows.

Only after a while does the ear begin to sort it, the way an eye sorts a crowd: the deep fundamental, four or five cycles a second, below hearing, felt as a slow squeezing of the chest; above it the boom-and-scrape of the pillars; above that the ratcheting million of the small things; above that a fine dry hiss like sand poured on a drumhead, which is the air itself, which is full of bodies.

One hundred and fifteen decibels at the floor. Higher in the canopy. There is no quiet anywhere in the Room, and quiet is not a comfort here; quiet is a symptom.

## III. The Floor Is Not Rock

It is pale, knee-deep, and it moves.

Call it meal. It is ground chitin and gypsum and salt and the powdered dead, bone-dry, faintly warm, with the slickness of talc and the smell of a milled attic. It has no clay in it, no moisture, no cohesion; a hand pushed into it comes out clean. It is not soil. Nothing has had time to make soil.

And it lies in figures.

Ridges of it, waist-high, running in long straight lines and sudden curves, meeting at angles that no wind ever negotiated. Between the ridges: bare pans of raw rock, swept and shaking, where the meal will not settle but boils and skitters and streams away sideways in a low seething fog. Walk out far enough — a day's walk, two — and the pattern resolves the way a mosaic resolves when you climb the hill: nodes and antinodes, the still lines and the quick ground, a figure forty kilometers across drawn in corpse-flour on a plate of stone.

The Room is a Chladni figure. The floor is a diagram of its own noise.

Where the sound cancels, the meal heaps up and stays, and things can stand, and roots can grip, and a person can sleep. Where the sound reinforces, the ground jumps, continuously, grain by grain, and nothing accumulates, and anything set down is walked away by vibration within the hour. The ridges are the only real estate. Everything else is a river with no water in it.

## IV. The Standing Forest

The pillars go up out of the nodal ridges and do not stop until they hit the lid.

They are plants, in the way that a cathedral is a pile of stones. Hollow, fluted, resinous, the color of dark honey and old varnish, ten meters through at the base, tapering, branching at the top into a pressed canopy that has grown flat against the crystal ceiling and spread sideways along it like a mold. They are the roof's supports and they are also its jacks: each pillar lengthens a few millimeters a day, and the lid, which is not fastened to anything, is being lifted. Slowly. Measurably. The Room is getting taller.

Between the pillars there are four more stories of life, packed near-touching: a midstory of drum-trees whose trunks are segmented like a spine and click as they flex; a shrub layer of things like folded tin, mica-leaved, edge-on to everything, rasping; a waist-high understory of combs and files and stretched membranes, all of it stridulating; and on the ridges themselves a mat of felted green-black fiber, dense as fur, that hums against the boot.

None of it is looking for light. There is light, and it uses it, but it does not *reach* for it. It reaches for loudness. Seedlings lean toward a drum. A cut branch roots preferentially on the side facing the nearest pillar. Gardeners here do not prune for shape, they prune for pitch, and a plant left in a hush does not wilt from thirst — it wilts from silence, sagging in an afternoon, its sap stopping, its files going slack.

Because the sap does not move by evaporation. There is nothing to evaporate. It moves because the plant is a resonator, and the standing wave inside its hollow trunk pumps it, node to node, up the column. **Sound is circulation.** The forest is screaming because the scream is its heartbeat, and a tree that stops shouting dies standing up.

## V. The Dry Chemistry

Cut a leaf and it weeps a clear mobile oil, thin as ether, smelling of pear drops and hot brass. It beads on the skin and vanishes in seconds, leaving a varnish. This is the quick: the solvent of the whole world, distilled out of the air by the felt-mat, which cracks the hydrocarbon vapor and the ammonia that seep from the raw rock and polymerizes them into wood, wax, membrane, shell.

It is not water. It has never been water. Water is miscible with it and ruins it — a single drop crashes the dissolved salts out of solution inside the tissue, and the leaf around the wound goes white, then glassy, then black, and the necrosis spreads a hand's breadth an hour. The wet is a disease. The wet is the worst thing that can happen to a living body in the Room, and it happens perhaps four times a year.

The people here make their water internally, metabolically, burning the fat bodies of grinders in their own cells; they do not drink, and have no verb for it. They do not sweat. They dump heat by panting and by lying belly-down on cool salt in the deep pans. Their urine is a dry pellet. And when they weep — which is rare, and formal, and done into a thimble of beetle-shell — the tears are the only liquid water in a hundred kilometers, and the recipient licks them off the rim immediately, before the air can carry the smell to anything green.

Nothing rots. There is no rot without water. The dead dry where they fall, going light and papery and perfect, wings intact, eyes like lacquer, and then the millers take them apart, and then the meal takes them, and then the pattern takes the meal.

## VI. The Crowd

There is no counting it. Biomass here is not a statistic; it is a visibility condition.

The air holds a haze of flying things that cuts the far view to a few hundred meters, so that the enormous room reads as intimate, a series of chambers, even while the sound tells you it goes on for days. Sheets of the grain — small hopping rasping bodies the size of a thumbnail — move across the ridges in windrows, ten deep, flowing like a dry tide, and the pillars are furred to head-height with them, and their friction alone is a quarter of the noise.

The millers are the size of dogs and armored in biogenic salt, milky-translucent, and their jaws are stone. They eat rock. They are the reason there is a Room at all.

And above the canopy, in the warm layer under the lid, drift the bellows: gas-bladders twenty and forty meters long, buoyed by heated vapor, grazing the ceiling-mold in herds of two hundred, trailing feeding-filaments through the branches. They are the loudest things alive. Their calls are the fundamental — that slow sub-audible squeeze in the chest — and they sing in a chord, and the chord is the key the whole floor is drawn in.

Which means the geography is downstream of the moods of animals.

## VII. The Turning

Several times a day the herd re-forms. A dominant bellows shifts pitch; the chord slides; the fundamental moves.

And the ground re-draws itself.

It takes eleven, fifteen, forty minutes. The nodal lines dissolve first, sagging, spilling, the meal running off them like water off a tilted plate; then the whole floor is in motion, a shallow boiling ocean of flour, ridges marching sideways at walking pace, new lines condensing out of the fog somewhere else entirely, at a new angle, in a new figure. A hill becomes a pan. A pan becomes a hill. Anything unanchored is carried, rolled, buried, or flung. The forest survives because its pillars are tuned to follow — you can hear them retuning, a horrible glissando of ten thousand strings under load — but the understory dies in swathes, and the grain goes with the pattern in a sheet, and the millers walk, and the people run.

They survey before they sleep. A resident carries a tuned rod, a fork of hollow bone, and walks the ground reading its buzz: still, still, still, *quick* — and moves the tent three meters. To sleep on an antinode is to be shaken until the small vessels of the eyes burst. Everyone here has a cousin it happened to.

Between turnings there are the lesser turmoils: pressure-storms where the standing wave pinches and dust devils a hundred meters tall walk the pans; falls of ceiling-plate, which arrive without warning as a change in the light and then a hundred thousand tons of glass; flash-blooms, when a chord strikes a species' breeding interval and a whole district fruits in an hour and the air becomes unbreathable with pollen and bodies. And the constant dry snow of the dead, sifted down out of the canopy, which is the only thing that ever falls from above.

## VIII. Scattered Works

There is no town. There cannot be a town; the ground will not hold still for one.

What there is: three huts on skids in the lee of a pillar, lashed from drum-tree ribs and roofed in felt, with runners greased in quick, and a tow-line to a harnessed miller. A drum-tower — a scaffold of spars forty meters up a trunk, hung with tuned plates, where a keeper strikes the alarm-figure that tells the bands which way the lines are moving. A stair of pegged bone going up to a ledge where somebody, once, cut a hundred and six tally marks. A footbridge of braided fiber across a shake, sagging, humming, its far anchor now standing in bare quick ground where a ridge used to be. Cairns everywhere, dozens, hundreds, markers of safe lines — most of them useless now, standing out in the pans, being vibrated apart stone by stone, their stones migrating.

The ruins here are four months old. Nothing has time to become ancient. There is no stratigraphy in the meal, no buried layer, no record; the plate is wiped and redrawn forty times a day and the past is shaken out flat. The only archive is a knotted cord in a woman's bag, and what her hands remember, and the tattoos on her forearms, which are a map of a floor that no longer exists.

They are perhaps two hundred people in a space the size of a province. They are deaf by their twelfth year — everyone is; the cochlea is a sacrifice the Room takes — and they speak by pressing foreheads together, bone to bone, so that a conversation requires touching, and strangers cannot argue without embracing. They also speak in a fast hand-language across distances, and by drum through the soles of the feet.

Their tools are mandible scrapers, spine needles, salt-glass blades knapped from ceiling fall, hand-drills, wax. There is one piece of copper in the Room, a needle the length of a finger, traded down a shaft nobody can find anymore; it is used as a tuning fork and it is owned by nobody and it is kept in fat.

Each band keeps a Listener: an infant whose ears were plugged at birth with wax and felt and never opened, raised in a padded box, carried. The Listener is the only person who can hear the herd change from far off. They are precious and they are strange and they do not live long, because a mind that hears in a world of the deaf has no one to tell.

Two laws. Never a spark — the whole Room is varnish and vapor and would go up like a held breath. And never carry water.

## IX. The Front

At the eastern end, past the last pillar, the Room is still being made.

The face is raw rock, unweathered, crystal faces intact, edges you could open a wrist on. There are no rounded stones anywhere in this world; nothing has ever been tumbled by a stream. The dust here is freshly milled and chemically furious — it burns the lungs, it saponifies in the eye. This is the only place where geology is visible, and it is *new*, hours old, a surface that has never been touched by anything until the jaws touched it.

The millers work the face in a wall four bodies deep, a hundred meters high, and the noise there is not sound but a solid, a thing you lean on. Behind them the void they have opened is already filling: white untuned seedlings, embryonic pillars no thicker than a thigh, screaming in the wrong key, sagging, being retuned by the older forest behind them, hauling themselves into pitch over a season or dying. The floor out there has no figure yet. It is only a smooth raw plate with a fog of meal on it, waiting for the herd to notice it and give it a shape.

The Room grows eastward a few meters a day. It has been doing this for perhaps sixty years. The oldest pillar is younger than the oldest woman.

## X. The Quilts, and the Lid

In the canopy there are things that eat sound.

They are felted, anechoic, three meters across, hanging like carpets, drifting like mantas: the quilts. Around each one is a moving pocket of hush, a bubble of quiet ten meters wide, and inside that bubble the sap stops. Leaves sag. Combs go slack. Small bodies drop out of the branches in a soft dry rain, and the quilt gathers them from the ridge below, unhurried, while the deafening world goes on shouting all around the hole it carries.

You learn to read them by the eyes, not the ears. You watch for the place where the forest has stopped moving.

And at night, when the lid goes blue-black and the light is only the cold spark of the grain and the pale seams of the pillars, the herd sings lower, and the lines creep, and the whole vast room draws and redraws itself in the dark for no one.

The lid is rising. A few millimeters a day, jacked by a forest that cannot stop shouting. The people know this; they measure it on the bone stair, and the marks are getting further apart, and the knotted cords say what the cords say. Someday the pillars will lift the crystal off its seat, and the seal will break, and the sky will get in.

They have a word for what comes then. It is the same word as the disease, the same word as the drop that kills a leaf, the same word as the tears you catch in a shell and drink before the air can smell them.

It means *the wet*, and it means *the end*, and down here in the warm deafening dark, under a ceiling of glass, in a forest that drinks oil and pumps its blood with noise, they are the same thing.
