# The Anvil

## i.

You do not hear it. Hearing is the first thing taken from you, somewhere on the last rise, when the pressure crosses the threshold where the small bones of the ear stop negotiating and simply lock. After that the sound arrives by other roads: through the heels, through the teeth, through the wall of the chest, which flexes inward and outward eleven times a minute like something breathing on your behalf. The fluid of the eye trembles at its own preferred rate and the world smears into a soft grey oscillation. You will spend your whole visit half-blind and entirely deaf and still be inside a roar so total that later, in some quieter country, you will find you cannot describe silence without your hands shaking.

It comes up out of the ground. There are fissures along the spine of the plateau — the locals, if there were locals, would call them mouths — and they exhale a fundamental too low to be a pitch, more a pressure in the sinuses, and stacked above it in ratios that are almost but not quite whole numbers is everything else: the grind, the bell-howl, the enormous bowed-string drone of a continent being drawn across itself. The air here is heavy and cold and tastes of struck flint and old coins. Sound moves through it fast and far and loses almost nothing. A stone dropped at one end of the plateau is still audible at the other, four hours later, arriving as a low knock inside your sternum.

Nothing is making this noise. That is the first thing to understand. The noise is not produced; it is *supplied*, the way heat and light are supplied elsewhere. It is the medium. Things here are loud the way things elsewhere are wet.

## ii.

The stone has no history.

Twenty kilometres above the grey unfinished floor of the lower world, the plateau called the Anvil is a single blunt mass of pale blue-grey rock, and every edge on it is the edge it was given. There is no sand. Sand has not been invented yet — there has not been enough time, or enough events, for a grain to be rounded. Break a fragment off and the fracture surface is fresh as a struck glass; walk a hundred paces and the next fracture is equally fresh; there is no scree, no talus, no polish, no stain of weather. The boulders lie where they were set, angular, unburied, casting the small hard shadows of things that have never been touched.

Erosion is a rumour here, a thing just beginning. In one place, under a lip of the escarpment, a groove has been worn a finger deep — the only worn place in a hundred square miles — and it took nine thousand years, and it was made not by water or wind but by a single frond swinging against it, once per beat, patiently, the way a tongue works at a tooth.

Above the Sill, a boulder the size of a house has been falling for three hundred years. It has descended perhaps eleven metres. Motion, here, is rationed: a thing may fall no faster than the beat permits, one slow lurch every nine seconds, and so the boulder hangs in the air like a decision being reconsidered, and the fronds beneath it have grown up around its shadow, and in another two centuries it will land, and the sound of that landing has already been audible in the drone since before the fall began.

That is the second thing to understand. The roar contains every sound this place will ever make, played all at once, very slowly. Somewhere in the upper harmonics is the crack of the boulder's landing. You have already heard it. You will hear it again, closer, and recognize it.

## iii.

Light and sound have not yet been separated here. They are still one budget, still one substance in two moods, and the world spends it accordingly.

Where it is loudest, it is darkest. Under the great groves the noon is cellar-dark, a brown-black gloom you navigate by palm and by pitch, and the leaves are only a shimmer you feel on the skin of the face. Out where the drone thins, the air brightens. And in the nulls — the interference nodes, the long ragged lanes between the stands where the world's two great tones cancel — there is a pale, sourceless, shadowless light like the light inside a snowfield, and it is beautiful, and there is nothing in it.

Nothing is not a figure of speech. At a null the pressure goes to zero and the rock loses its grip on being a rock. It becomes a fine bright powder that is not ground from anything, that has no mineral name, that runs uphill if you look at it too long. Stand in a null and your outline begins to soften at the shoulders. The stone you were holding becomes an idea about a stone. Travellers learn to read the country the way people elsewhere read a fire: the dark places are safe, the bright places will unmake you, and a horizon is a warning.

So the plateau is a dim, ringing, crowded-with-life darkness strung between long luminous blanks, and from the escarpment at what passes for evening it looks like a city seen through fog, if the city were lit from underneath by its own emptiness.

## iv.

The groves are lush. Obscenely so.

Each stand is a dense thicket twenty or thirty paces across, and there is nothing else for a quarter-mile in any direction, and then there is another, and another, strung along the antinodal ridges in a chain that runs the length of the region — a spacing set not by soil or seed or luck but by wavelength. Life can hold its shape only where the world is loud. Two stands too close would cancel each other and both would go to bright powder. So they keep their distance with the exactness of teeth in a jaw, and inside that distance they are riotous.

The leaves are laminae of green-black mica, thinner than foil, hand-sized, hung by their edges so they can flex. A hectare of them clattering in the pressure-gradient makes a sound like a warehouse of sheet metal being slowly folded. The stems are drawn basalt, glassy, fibrous, wrapped in a grey wool of spun mineral hair that stings the fingers. There are tubes — organ-pipes of salt-glass, some as thick as a thigh, some in ranks of a hundred slim ones — and they are not decoration; they are the feeding apparatus. The plant is a resonator. It takes the pressure difference between its mouth and its throat and it uses that work to build lattice, to lay atom against atom, to grow. Its metabolism is crystallization. Its waste is cold: each grove sits in a ring of rime, ammonium chloride feathering out over the raw stone, five degrees colder at the centre than at the edge, the plants quietly refrigerating the country by the act of being alive.

And they are wet, in the only way anything here is wet. Cut a stem and a bead of bright metal swells at the wound — a silver eutectic that stays liquid in this cold, that wets glass and leaves a grey mirror on whatever it touches. It is the whole of the world's hydrology. It does not evaporate, does not rain, does not pool. It moves underground along a plumbing of hairline fractures, drawn from grove to grove by resonance pumps, a closed circulation of metal in the dark rock, and it has never once been on the surface except as a bead at a cut, or as a smear on a tool, or in someone's mouth.

They flower by beating. Two stands whose tones have drifted into near-consonance set up a slow throb between them, and at the place where that throb is loudest — a spot that did not exist last century, out in the powder — the world begins to be able to hold something. Over sixty or eighty years the metal migrates there through the veins. Then a sprout. This is how the country is being built: not by geology, which has barely started, but by the vegetation, which is laying down new places where existence is possible, at the speed of growth, one antinode per lifetime, singing the world a little further into being.

Nothing pollinates. Nothing eats. Nothing startles. There are no tracks in the powder because there are no feet; the world has not yet ratified anything that moves faster than a frond. Once, near the Sill, you find a scatter of small identical white shapes, knuckle-sized, jointed, beautifully made — the draft of a knuckle, repeated forty times, with no animal anywhere in the region to have needed one.

## v.

The marks are everywhere and there is no one to have made them.

Cairns, first: stacks of five or seven stones, each chosen for its ring, tapped and rejected and tapped again, arranged so the stack hums in sympathy with the ridge it stands on. Some have fallen. A fallen cairn is a small catastrophe; there is a bright scar in the powder where it stood.

Then the flint-work — flakes and blades knapped from a chert that outcrops only once, on the escarpment, and carried in ones and twos across the whole plateau. Wedges driven into cracks and left there, with the crack widened a hair's breadth by three thousand years of the beat working the wedge deeper. An adze hafted with a resin that never cured, still tacky, still holding the print of a thumb.

There are no hands in this world. There is no animal at all. But there is a thumbprint, and there is a hammerstone worn smooth in the exact place a grip would wear it, and the wear is on the *inside* of a grip that has not happened yet. You may make of that what you like. The tools are ahead of their makers, the way the sound of the boulder's landing is ahead of the landing. Something is being drafted here, and it is being drafted out of order.

The most sophisticated object on the Anvil is a mute: a fist of salt-wool bound with native copper wire, found jammed into a fissure near the Quiet House, driven hard, hammered flat, with the copper drawn out into a long tail where someone tried to pull it free again. A mute is the only weapon that means anything here. To silence a thing is to dissolve it. Somebody, at some point, tried to silence the world, and either changed their mind or did not survive the change.

The Quiet House is a roofless spiral of dry-laid stone, waist-high, four turns, on a shoulder of the escarpment. It is an interference trap: it folds the drone against itself and makes, at its centre, a still pocket about the size of a chair. Inside that pocket it is *bright*, and silent, and you can see — really see, for the first time in weeks — the curve of the horizon, the grey Underdraft below, the dust-ceiling overhead, the whole shape of the country. Your hands go pale at the edges while you look. Nobody stays long. It is the only place on the plateau where you can find out where you are, and the price is a little of your existence, and there are tally-scratches on the inner stones, hundreds of them, counting visits or beats or losses.

## vi.

Time is told by pitch. The sun is a smudge somewhere above the dust that never falls, and it does not usefully rise. Instead the fundamental climbs — a slow bend upward through a fifth over about thirty hours, then a long descent — and the whole country changes key, and the shadows in the nulls swing, and the mica leaves shift their clatter, and you know it is morning because the world has gone sharp.

Seasons are modulations, and they take centuries. The last one moved the antinodal chain eleven metres north; two groves at the old edge went bright and are now beautiful empty lanes, and there are seedlings quickening in the powder to the south, and in nine hundred years the chain will have walked a full grove-width and no one will notice it happening.

To cross a null you carry a bullroarer: a flat leaf of grown flint on a cord, swung slowly overhead, one revolution per beat. It is the entire technology of travel. It makes a sound like a door in a wall of air, and while it sounds you are counted, and the powder does not take your outline. The groves grow them wild — long bullroarer leaves at the tips of the outer branches, swinging on their own, keeping the stand's perimeter real. You can hear a grove's boundary before you can feel its cold: a threshing, threshing, threshing in the dark, and then rime underfoot, and then the enormous close clatter of ten thousand metal-edged leaves, and you are somewhere, and it is dark, and you are safe.

## vii.

From the escarpment, where the drone falls off and the light comes up, you can look down.

Below is the Underdraft: not a valley, not a plain, but the grey undecided floor of a world that has not been finished, going out flat and featureless to a horizon that curves too fast for the height you stand at. There are shapes in it that might be the beginnings of mountains. They have no shadows because there is nothing down there for light to be spent on. All the water left this place long ago, drained downward to where the world is still being decided, and it will not come back; the sky above is a low ceiling of suspended dust that has never been told to fall, and it is the colour of a bruise beginning.

And the drone is getting quieter. Not in a lifetime — in nine thousand years, a semitone of loudness lost, the fundamental sagging. The groves answer it. They add voices; they thicken; they push new antinodes out into the powder, they lay down more place, more darkness, more life, faster than the fade, so far, just barely faster.

But you can see the direction of it. Somewhere ahead there is a resolution: the chord completes, the pressure goes to zero everywhere at once, and the Anvil becomes perfectly bright and perfectly silent — every stone visible at last, every edge crisp, the whole country revealed in a light like the inside of a pearl, and not one thing in it existing.

The leaves are working. All around you in the dark, the mica flexes, the pipes take their pressure, the metal moves in the rock, cold feathers out across the raw unweathered stone. It is deafening. It is the sound of a world being kept.
