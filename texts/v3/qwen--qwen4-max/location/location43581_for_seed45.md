Heat comes up through the soles first. The stone is warm — not scalding, not everywhere, but blood-warm and a little beyond, the temperature of a thing that has only just stopped being liquid. The air is warm too, and thin to the point of dishonesty: it moves against the face like a breath rather than a wind, and it carries almost nothing. No smell reaches far. You have to stand over a seam to smell anything, and then it is sulfur and hot iron, a struck match, the scorched-metal smell of a smith's floor.

There is no water in this place. Not frozen, not buried, not bound in the rock. The vapor that comes up out of the ground is dry vapor — sulfur, chloride salts, metals riding on carbon monoxide — and when it touches a surface a fraction cooler than itself it does not condense into droplets. It plates. It lays itself down atom by atom in films and needles and cubes, mirror-bright iron, bone-pale salt, black sulfur whiskers, glassy silica hairs so fine they bend in the least pressure of air. Nothing here has ever been wet. Nothing here has ever been rounded by anything.

It is dark, and the dark is not night. There is no day to be the other half of it. Above the plain lies a lid of vapor some thousands of meters up, and it is lit only from beneath, by the seams, so that it glows a dull ox-blood in patches and drifts and thickens and thins without ever opening. What light exists is made here, on the ground, by the ground.

And it is a light that tells time. Fresh stone glows: not with fire, but with the dull cherry of arrival, the heat of having just been assembled. It fades over minutes. So brightness is newness, and darkness is age, and age here means a quarter of an hour. A spire burning orange is a spire being born. A spire standing black against the glowing lid is a spire that finished growing while you were watching something else. When a crystal column snaps — and they snap constantly — the fracture itself flashes, a hard white flick at the break, quartz-light, the small lightning of things coming apart under strain. The plain is legible in these flashes. You see it in stills, and it is never the same still twice.

Sound does not travel. The air is too thin to carry a crash any distance; a tower sixty meters tall can come down four hundred meters away and reach you as nothing more than a soft press against the chest, a change in the weight of the air. What you hear instead is intimate and small: the tick of a salt cube popping off a ledge, the ping of cooling film, a fizzing whisper from a seam a meter off, like fat in a pan. The great events are silent. But put a palm flat on the pavement, or kneel, or lie down, and the whole plain arrives through the bone. Rock carries what air cannot. The collapse comes up through the wrists as a long grinding roar, arriving early — sound is faster in stone — and then, when you lift your hands, it is gone, and there was never any noise at all. The world is deafening to the touch and nearly mute to the ear, and the difference between those two conditions is the difference between touching the ground and not.

The plain runs some forty kilometers to a rim you can only see in flashes, and the rim is not a mountain range but a wall of the same accreting stuff, higher, older by hours, still climbing. Beyond it, in a big fracture-flash, you can sometimes count three more basins, each with its own dim constellation of seams. This is a province of such basins. It is a roof. Almost all of the world's atmosphere lies below the level of this pavement, and what is up here is only the exhalation.

It is mostly empty. That is the thing the flashes keep insisting on. Between one growth and the next there can be two kilometers of bare warm crust, faintly glossy, unmarked but for casts and the odd solitary needle standing alone like a pin in a cushion. The features are widely spaced and enormous: a field of hollow spires; a slump where a whole terrace has foundered and gone soft; a throat, a raised lip of layered salt around a hole that breathes; a low blister of pavement inflating so slowly you can only confirm it by looking away and back. Four or five presences in a whole horizon. The emptiness between them is not background. It is most of the place.

But the emptiness is not still, and this is the central violence: everything here is arriving. Growth is measured in fingers per hour and towers per minute. Every few hours a throat surges — a quickening — and the vapor comes up thick enough to plate on anything, on dust, on itself, and a forest goes up in a quarter hour: hollow chimneys, branched antlers, fluted columns forty meters tall and no thicker than a stovepipe, all of it new, all of it glowing, all of it already too heavy for its own base. They buckle. They fold at the knee. They come down across each other and fuse where they touch, and the fused pairs stand as accidental arches while the rest slumps.

The pavement is a crust over a slow hot mass, and it drinks what falls. Fragments sink, dimpling the surface, going down over an hour or a day, and the material returns through the throats and is issued again. Nothing leaves. Nothing is lost. It is a closed, frantic economy of the same few elements, going up and coming down and going up.

Nothing is worn. There is no sand here that was made by grinding; the dust condenses out of the vapor already dust, born small rather than broken small. No edges are softened. Every fracture surface is a first surface, sharp enough to open a hand, and every crystal face is original, unweathered, unpolished, unhandled by anything. There are no rinds, no rounded cobbles, no scoured flanks, no channels, no stain of long exposure. The plain has no history written on it in the ordinary way. It has no way of writing anything but addition. Where a thing fails, it is not eroded; it is reabsorbed and reissued. Destruction here is only the middle of a sentence.

Walk across it and the crust takes a cast of you. Where a boot presses, the vapor plates preferentially into the depression, filling it, so that after ten minutes there is a raised relief of your sole standing a centimeter proud of the pavement, bright at first, then cooling to black. Stand still for an hour and you leave a shallow statue of your feet and ankles. The plain is dotted with these: reliefs of spire-tips that stood and fell, of collapsed lintels, of shapes with no obvious original, all of them dark, all of them waiting to be drunk back down. It is the only kind of record the place keeps, and it keeps it for a day.

Among the casts there are other marks, and these are the ones that stop you. Courses. A stretch of pavement laid in regular blocks with tight joints, three meters of it, then nothing. A sill. A threshold with a worn-looking dip in the middle that is not worn, that grew that way. Eight steps rising to a landing that supports nothing, standing alone two kilometers from any other feature, glowing faintly at the risers because it was completed eleven minutes ago. A doorway with no wall, framing more plain.

Nothing here has been made. There is no tool, no device, no worked stone, no maker, nothing that ever held an intention. What there is, is a habit.

It is a film — barely a film, a stain a few microns thick, spreading along the seams and out across the crust at the pace of a slow walk. Where it passes, the accreting mineral takes a different form: cubic, coursed, right-angled, stacked in bond. It templates. Fresh stone landing on the stain grows the way the stain is already arranged, and the arrangement copies itself outward, and the shapes it makes are the shapes of building. Then it moves on, and behind it the growth reverts to needles and antlers and blobs, and the courses it left stand for a while and then buckle and go down and come back up as something else. It is not a ruin. It is a habit of crystallization that propagates by contact, and it is the closest thing on this plain to a living process: a pattern that persists while all its instances are destroyed, passing itself along the stone the way a rumor passes through a crowd. Whether it learned these shapes somewhere, or whether these are simply the shapes that stress and salt and cooling happen to make, and we are the ones who recognize them — that is not a question the plain is equipped to answer.

Nothing else lives. Nothing moves that is not mineral or vapor. No insect ticks in the salt, no bird crosses the lid, nothing flees the collapse, nothing watches. The spires move constantly and are not alive. The stain spreads and is arguably not alive. There is no food chain, no rot, no scent of decay; the only smell is the smell of manufacture.

Once in a long while the lid tears. Through the tear there are stars — hard, cold, absolutely still, the only cold-looking things in the world, and they warm nothing and plate nothing and have no effect on the plain at all. Then the vapor closes over.

Underneath, the quickening starts again in a new throat, and the light comes up orange across two kilometers of empty pavement, and the towers go up in silence, and somewhere off in the dark the stain reaches a fresh seam and begins, patiently, to lay its courses.
