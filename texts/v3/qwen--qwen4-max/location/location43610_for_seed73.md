# THE SILL

## i. The lip

You come up the outside, which is the only way anyone comes, and the last kilometer is ice.

Not snow — ice, blue and glassy, plastered to the windward stone in sheets that ring when struck. The air out there is thin and vicious, thirty below at noon, and the sky above you is a color that has no business over a sun that bright: a deep bruised green-blue, almost violet at the zenith, with the sun hanging in it small and hard-edged as a coin held at arm's length. Most of the world's air is *beneath* your boots. You are standing on the roof of the atmosphere. Cloud lies below the rim in a floor of wool, unbroken to the curve of the horizon, and it does not move, because down there nothing moves that you would recognize as weather.

Along the lip runs a wall of fog. It stands about forty meters high and it does not blow away. It leans inward. Where it touches the cold stone it leaves ice; where it touches the warm stone on the other side it leaves water, and the water goes down into the rock and does not come back out. The locals call it the Hedge. You can put your arm through it. On the far side your sleeve is wet and your fingers are hot and the wind smells like a wet animal.

There is a stair cut into the rim, nine hundred and some steps, wide enough for two abreast, worn concave. It goes down into the warm and ends at a landing where the ice stops in a clean line, as if ruled. Nobody made that line. It is simply where the warmth is.

## ii. The bowl

Below the Hedge the world opens into heat.

The Sill is a basin sixty kilometers across, eleven kilometers above the crushed white nothing that is the world's floor, and it is forty-four degrees Celsius at midday and thirty-nine at night and it is never, anywhere, at any hour, dry. The air is nearly twice as thick as the air you were born in. It has weight. It moves against your face like something considering you. Breath comes easy but arrives late, and the first three days you feel as though you are being gently and continuously pressed on by a crowd.

The warmth is a *volume*. That is the thing visitors cannot get their heads around. It has a shape and an edge. Walk out toward the rim on a warm afternoon and at a certain point the hair on your arms lifts and the fog is in front of you and beyond the fog is a cold that will kill you in an hour. There is no gradual cooling. There is a boundary you can find with your hand. The markers along it — squat, waist-high, grown rather than carved, spaced with a precision no one bothers to remark on — are the only fence in the country.

Inside the boundary: a floor of shallow hot water and green mat, and standing out of it, near-touching, ranked away into haze until they are only suggestions, nine hundred towers of pale stone.

## iii. The Standing

They look like karst. They *are* karst — fluted, undercut, foot-caved, crowned with soil and scrub, the classic mature form of limestone eaten by wet heat for a million years. Their bases are narrowed to waists by the water that has stood against them. Their flanks are runnelled like organ pipes, and in fact they are organ pipes, and they sound like them.

But put your palm on a tower's waist at dawn and you will feel a faint grain moving under the skin of the rock, and if you have the instruments you will find that the tower is dissolving at the base at exactly the rate it is accreting at the crown. Calcium goes in at the bottom and comes out at the top. The stone is a shape that material passes through. The towers are standing waves. They have kept their profiles — the same waists, the same flutes, the same nine-fold lean of the one called Nine-Fingers — for longer than there have been eyes to notice, and the locals do not say a tower *stands*. They say it is *held*.

Once in a lifetime one of them lets go. It does not fall. It sags, over about eleven days, into a low mound of warm rubble that the mat walks over and covers, and for a generation afterward there is a hole in the chord of the valley and everybody knows which note is missing.

## iv. Water that goes the wrong way

It rains here perhaps nine days a year. The Sill is nonetheless soaked, and the soaking is done by three quieter mechanisms.

The first is the rock. The towers are wicks. Water climbs them — capillary rise through a pore structure so fine and so well sorted that a tower sixty meters tall will weep from its crown in the dry season. It comes out along the flutes in films two millimeters deep and runs down, and in the films there are fish: small, flat, silver-sided, held to the vertical face by surface tension and by an air so thick it half-supports them, grazing the algal felt, thousands per tower, flickering like the whole stone is being read very quickly by something.

The second is the returns. Where a flute's lip curls under, the film climbs the curl and re-enters the fissure it came from. There are loops of water on the Sill that have been going around since before the language had a word for around. Children are told not to drink from a return. Adults do not drink from a return. Nobody can say why, only that the water in them tastes counted.

The third is the fall. Two hours after dusk the towers' crowns, which have been radiating all evening into a sky with almost nothing above it, go cold enough to wring the air. Then it rains for ninety minutes — a fat, warm, vertical, entirely local rain that begins on the leaves and ends in the sumps, and it is loud, a sustained roar of drips off ten million leaf-tips, and it is the loudest thing that happens on an ordinary day, and the folk sleep through it.

Between the towers lie the sumps: flat-bottomed basins of hot standing water, half a meter to four meters deep, each roofed over with a floating mat of leathery green tissue that in most places will take your weight. The mat flexes when you walk. It booms. It is a drumhead the size of a parish, and crossing one at night you announce yourself to everything within ten kilometers.

## v. The green

Vegetation here grows in tiers, and every tier is a fin.

At the water: the mat itself, tough, waxy, faintly ridged on its upper surface in a pattern that maximizes area. Above it, on stilts, the capillary trees — hollow-trunked, pale, branching in flat plates held edge-on to the sun, their leaves broad and thin and hung with drip-tips like a cathedral's worth of gargoyles. Above those, on the towers' shoulders and crowns, the strap-forests: root masses that reach sixty meters down the flutes to the water table, hauling it up, spending it, transpiring it into an air that is already full, so that the whole basin steams gently and continuously and the steam goes up and hits the lid and comes back as the fall.

The forest is not incidental to the heat. The forest is what the heat is *for*, or at least what it is *through*. The Sill sheds somewhere on the order of a hundred gigawatts, and it sheds it by evaporating water and radiating from leaf surface, and the leaf surface has been optimized. You can see the optimization if you look at the tiers from the rim at the right angle: the plates interlock without shading each other, the whole green mass is a single continuous exchanger with a pressure drop across it, and the towers are the fill.

The basin is a cooling tower. It was not built as one. It grew into one, or was grown, or was always one and merely took a million years to look natural. All three readings are available and the folk hold them simultaneously without discomfort.

## vi. Everything alive

The air's density makes flight cheap. Over the sumps drift the boomers — bladder-grazers, three to nine meters, translucent-skinned, their gasbags filmed with a photosynthetic scum that gives them a faint green interior light on the underside at dusk, which is the only light in this place not accounted for by the sun. They graze the mat by trailing a fringe of hooked cilia. They call at thirty hertz, in pulses, and the pulses beat against each other across a sump so that the air seems to thicken and thin.

Under them, in the mat, sieve-crabs at a density of about four thousand per square meter, working the tissue for what the water brings. Their molt-shells drift into the tower waists and pile in white banks that crunch and shift and, in the fall, hiss.

There is a snail tide. It moves three meters a day. It is a front of grazing shells forty kilometers long and eleven years wide, and it has been crossing the basin since your great-grandparents' time, and its passage is the reason the mat is pale in the east and dark in the west, and there are people whose entire working life has been the observation of one hundred-meter section of it.

In the tower flutes nest the pipe-birds, which plug the holes with felted nests to hold their eggs at the temperature they prefer. Plugging a flute changes the tower's voice. Every breeding season the chord of the valley modulates, and the folk can tell you the month by listening, and there is a documented year — recorded, argued over, half believed — when the birds nested so heavily in the Bell that the Bell went silent and three sumps drained.

And there is a predator that hunts by making a null. You will not see it. You will notice that one small patch of the night has gone quiet — not silent, *cancelled*, an antinode of the hum folded back on itself — and if you are sensible you will step off the mat and onto stone and wait, because whatever is standing in that patch is standing very still and has been for six days.

## vii. The Note

Under everything is nineteen hertz.

It comes up through the floor of the basin and through the soles of your feet and through the water and the mat and the towers, and it is not loud in the way a shout is loud. It is loud in the way a hand on your sternum is loud. It makes the water in a cup show rings. It makes new arrivals weep without cause and their teeth ache and their vision blur at the edges, and it does all of this for about three days and then you stop noticing it and never stop feeling it. The folk have denser bones than their cousins in the lowlands, and thicker chests, and speak in a register that visitors find comical until they learn that in air this heavy every voice comes out of a barrel: children sound like elders, elders sound like doors.

Above the Note: the towers' pipes, a slow shifting chord in the low hundreds, tuned by erosion and detuned by birds. Above that: the weeps, ten million separate drips, a hiss with grain in it. Above that: the boomers, the crab-banks, the fall.

The dense air carries all of it absurdly well. You hear the far rim before you can see it. Distance on the Sill is judged by ear the way elsewhere it is judged by eye, and the local language has eleven words for *far* that are all, properly translated, degrees of muffled.

## viii. Scattered

There are perhaps four hundred people in sixty kilometers of basin, and no town.

A house on a tower shoulder, nacre-shingled, its roof grown in overlapping plates that the family repairs by feeding it. An ice-house at the rim stair, where the Hedge's ice is cut and packed in mat-fiber and kept, and where the folk go at midday to lie against the wall and let the heat come out of them — because they are long and thin and their skin is finely ridged, like the inside of a shell, and even so forty-four degrees at saturation is more than a body can survive without help. They wear a mantle-liverwort across the shoulders that transpires for them. They do not work between the tenth and fourteenth hours. Nothing here works between the tenth and fourteenth hours. The world is loudest then, and emptiest, and the boomers come down to drink.

A count-house on the largest sump: a single room on piles, full of graduated rods, some of them alive, all of them recording the water. A drowned observatory, roof still intact, forty meters out, visible through the water as a green shape. A ring of nine low huts on the eastern shore, roofed, clean, dry, maintained by nothing at all, never rotting, occupied by no one within memory.

And the sorting stones: flat pale slabs at the foot of certain towers, where the dead are laid. A carcass placed on one is taken apart in about eleven hours. Not eaten — *sorted*. Calcium in a neat ridge here, iron in a smear there, the long carbons folded into a stack like cloth. It is done by something in the stone that operates below the scale of seeing. There is no smell. The folk consider this ordinary and are quietly disturbed by the way outsiders rot.

The rules, as they are given to visitors, in the order they are given:

Drink from a weep, never a sump, never a return. Do not walk the pale mat. When the Note goes to a fifth, leave the basin and do not come back for a year. Speak loudly; the Note eats quiet speech and things that hear quiet speech are not the things you want. Carry nothing out that you did not carry in — and if you must, leave something of equal weight, and it does not have to be the same kind of thing, and this is not a superstition, and there is a ledger.

## ix. Below

In the waist of the tower called Aunt there is an opening at knee height that a person can enter by lying down and turning. Twenty meters in, the limestone stops being limestone. The wall becomes a material with structure at every scale you can bring to bear on it: at the millimeter a lattice, at the micron the same lattice, at the limit of the best lens the same lattice, going down, patient, unbothered, until the microscope gives up. It is warm. It is nineteen hertz. It is doing something with the heat that the forest above is spending, and the heat is a *waste* heat, which means the something is elsewhere and this is only its exhaust.

Nobody knows what is being held still down there. There are three schools of thought and all of them are polite about the others.

What is known: the towers keep their shapes while their substance flows through them. The Hedge stands. The boundary holds to within a hand's width, year on year, century on century. The water balance closes to four decimal places, and the small deficit — the ice that goes over the rim and is lost to the cold — is made up from below. And a knife left on a sorting stone will, within a year or so, be found with a twin lying beside it, and the twin will be very slightly better.

## x. The Long Breath

The water table rises and falls four meters on a cycle of three hundred and forty years.

That is the whole of the dynamics of this place. There are no storms. There is no season worth the name. The fog takes four hours to lift and the flowers open across nine days and a predator will stalk a single boomer for a week. What there is, instead, is the Turn: the slow flooding of the tower waists until the sumps merge into one sheet and the mat becomes a country; then the long withdrawal, the re-emergence of the foot-caves, the drying of the high sills, the snail tide reaching the western rim and turning.

No one lives through a Turn. Everyone lives through a *phase* of one, and the phases are named, and the names are the calendar, and the count-house rods are the argument between memory and measurement.

The houses are built accordingly. Each has an upper floor with its own door and its own hearth, sealed, swept, provisioned, unused — a floor for the water that is coming, for a great-great-grandchild who will need to step out onto a landing and pull a boat up to the window. Some of those floors have been waiting two hundred years. They are not considered wasted. They are considered *early*.

## xi. From outside

Come at it from the lowlands, if you can, from a hundred kilometers out over the cloud floor, and what you see is not a basin. You cannot see the basin. What you see is a column.

A white pillar standing out of the cloud deck to twice the height of the rim, widening slowly at the top, absolutely stationary, rooted, going nowhere. It has stood there through every recorded history of every people who could see it. It is the only cloud in the world that does not travel. Below it, invisible, hidden in its own steam, a hot wet bowl of held stone and drumming green, humming at nineteen hertz, taking itself apart and putting itself back, four hundred people asleep through the loudest rain of the day.

The pillar leans very slightly to the east. It has been leaning very slightly to the east for about sixty years, and the count-house has noted this, and the note is filed, and nobody yet knows what it means.
