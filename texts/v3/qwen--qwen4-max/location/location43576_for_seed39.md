**The foreland**

The light comes from below. That is the first wrong thing. The sky is a lid of grey stratus pressed down onto the ridges, sun somewhere behind it like a coin under a tongue, and yet the ground throws up more light than the sky gives — old snow, wet quartz, the milky sheen of water lying on stone. Faces are lit from underneath. There are no shadows to speak of, only a soft darkening in the lee of each boulder, and the whole world has the flat shadowless brightness of a workbench under a diffuser. You can look at everything and see nothing in relief. Distances lie. A rock the size of a dog and a rock the size of a house resolve at the same moment, one after another, as you walk.

It is cold in the specific way that wet cold is cold: not the dry brittleness of deep winter but a hovering just-below, a temperature that cannot decide. Rime grows on the windward side of every stone, white feathers a finger long, all of them pointing the same direction, all of them sublimating and regrowing hour by hour. The air is close to saturation and has nowhere to put its water, so it puts it on things. Breath condenses and hangs. Rock weeps — not from any spring, but because the whole face of it is below the dew point and the fog keeps arriving and leaving its cargo.

And the ground is soaked. That is the second wrong thing, at this height, in this cold. There should be nothing here but scoured bone and dry frost-shattered rubble. Instead every hollow holds water. The flats are a sheet of it, two centimetres deep, spreading a kilometre wide, with gravel bars showing through like the backs of animals. It has no banks. It has no course. It moves so slowly that you cannot see it move, and then you look again after an hour and the whole sheet has slid two hundred metres downhill.

The reason is simple and it changes everything about the place: **the drainage has not been invented yet.**

---

This ground is new. Not new in the way a lava field is new, or a landslide scar — new in the way a part is new when it comes off the machine. Eight, ten, twelve years ago it was under three hundred metres of ice. Now the ice has pulled back up the valley and left the floor standing, and the floor has not had time to become a landscape. There is no soil. There is no weathering rind; strike a boulder and the fresh grey comes up wet and bright under a skin of nothing. There are no valleys in the foreland, no gullies, no established watercourses, because a watercourse is a thing that takes decades of argument between water and stone, and the argument here has only just opened. So the meltwater lies where it falls, in films and sheets and unnamed pools, and the whole plain is a shallow undecided flood waiting for someone to draw the map.

Watch it being drawn. At the low lip of one pool, water has begun to spill, and the spill has cut a channel a thumb deep in the raw silt. The channel has a nickpoint — a tiny steep step, a waterfall the height of a knuckle — and the nickpoint is travelling upstream, eating backward into the pool's rim, and when it arrives the pool will empty in four minutes and cease to exist. Two hundred metres east, another rill is doing the same thing to another pool. One of them will get there first and take the other's water. The first watershed boundary in this country is being surveyed right now, by water, at a centimetre an hour, and there is no one to record it.

The stone remembers the tool. Everywhere on the exposed bedrock there are striae — parallel scratches, fine as hair, coarse as a fingernail's groove, all running the same way, down-valley, the direction the ice went. Whalebacks and roches moutonnées lie in the flats like the backs of surfacing animals, polished on the upstream side, plucked and ragged on the lee, and the polish is a real polish, a machine finish, with crescentic chatter marks across it where the tool bit and skipped and bit again. In places the till has been extruded rather than dumped, and it comes up in flutes: long parallel ridges a hand high, running for a hundred metres, straight as die-work. The whole plain looks like something that has been *manufactured*, and it has been, and the swarf is still lying where it fell — rock flour, grey, so fine it stays in suspension for days, so fine that when it dries it goes airborne and hangs in the valley as a haze that smells of wet slate and cold iron.

---

The machine is still running. It has only withdrawn from this part of the floor.

Up-valley, three lobes of ice come down through three gaps in the ridge and hang there, dirty-tongued, calves slumped in their own melt. The middle one is the big one: nine kilometres of it above the icefall, and below the icefall the surface is banded in arcs, pale and dark alternating, convex down-valley, one pair for every year — the annual output coming off the line, ogives, the ice squeezed through the narrows and emerging striped with the season it was made in. Along its spine runs a medial moraine, a raised causeway of rubble two metres high and dead straight, riding the ice at a few centimetres a day, carrying its load of broken mountain toward the snout with the patience of a conveyor that has never once stopped. At the snout it dumps. There is a fresh pile there, angular, unstained, still smelling of fracture, and it is bigger than it was in the spring.

Water goes into the ice at the top and comes out at the bottom. The moulins are vertical shafts, blue-lipped, worn smooth, and they take the melt in with a sound like a swallowed breath; somewhere under your feet the whole plumbing system is running, a network of conduits in the dark, enlarging themselves as they go, and the outfall is a portal at the snout — an arch of dirty ice, a river coming out of it already grey with flour, already at full production, ten hours a day, seven months a year. That river is the only loud thing in the valley and it is loud in a way you stop hearing, a broadband roar with no rhythm in it, so constant that the mind promotes it to silence and then, when the discharge drops at dusk, hands it back to you as an absence.

The ice is also the coolant, the lubricant, and the refrigeration plant. Beneath it, water at pressure keeps the bed wet and slippery and lets the whole mass slide; the sliding does the machining; the machining makes the flour; the flour greases the sliding. It is a closed loop and it is entirely indifferent, and it has been running, on and off, for two and a half million years.

---

The air is not empty. This is the thing you would miss if you only looked at the ground.

The plain reads as void — one boulder per hundred square metres, one bird per minute, space between everything, a place with a near-total absence of incident. But the upslope wind comes off the lowlands every afternoon carrying freight, and the freight is alive: aphids, midges, small moths, ballooning spiderlings by the ten thousand, seeds, pollen, the whole insect summer of the valleys below, lifted and dumped on the snow at four thousand metres because the snow is cold and the air over it falls. It lands. It freezes. It lies on the surface in a fine dark pepper you would take for grit.

And then, at the melt line, it thaws into soup, and everything comes to eat.

Springtails in numbers that make the slush look like it is boiling — millions per square metre, blue-black, hopping in a continuous seething static across the wet snow, so many that the snow's surface has a grain to it, like film. On the older moraine, ice crawlers, pale and slow and wingless, hunting the fallout in the interstices of the rubble. Spiders — real ones, hunting spiders, no webs, working the snowfield at heights where nothing should be able to make a living, taking springtails one at a time. Snow finches in loose flocks of eighty, working the melt edge in a line, moving up-valley with it through the day as the thaw line climbs, then back down in the evening. Accentors flicking in the boulder fields. Choughs, six of them, dropping through the col on the katabatic and going over the ice with their wings loose. A kestrel hanging over the till. Voles in the older ground where the moss has started. A fox comes across the whole plain in the blue part of the night, four kilometres at a trot, leaving a single line of prints that the next drift erases.

Abundance here is not spread out; it is *concentrated at the working face*. Away from the melt line and the fallout, there is nothing for a kilometre. At the melt line there is more life per square metre than in a lowland meadow, and the whole thing is a canteen crowd, and it moves up and down the valley twice a day with the sun.

The plants are the slow shift. Nothing on the newest ground — not a lichen, not a crust; the rock is too fresh, the surface still too unstable, freeze and thaw heaving it every night. Fifty metres back from the snout, a black film of cyanobacteria in the wet flour, which is the first thing that ever lives anywhere new. Two hundred metres, moss sprigs the size of a shirt button in the lee of stones. On the oldest ground, at the far end of the plain where the ice left a century ago, a thin scurf of lichen, three purple saxifrages, one glacier buttercup, and a willow the size of a fingernail lying flat against the ground with its two leaves pressed into the boundary layer where the air is a whole degree warmer. That is the entire forest. It is doing its work: fixing, dying, rotting a little, making the first millimetre of the soil that everything else will require.

---

Mostly it is quiet, and the quiet is not empty but * absorbent*. Fresh snow and wet flour eat sound. There are no leaves, no trees, nothing to rustle; no cliffs close enough to give an echo. A bird forty metres away sounds like a bird in a cupboard. What you hear instead is small and near and constant: the tick of stones settling as the ice inside their cracks lets go; the fizz of bubbles escaping from melting ice, air that was shut up in it before there were cities, coming out one pop at a time in a sound like a poured drink; the hiss of rime feathers growing; the sound of your own blood.

Then, once or twice a day, the plant does something. A serac goes over above the icefall and the noise arrives eight seconds later, a long tearing collapse with a floor-shake in it, and rolls around the cirque, and stops, and the finches do not even lift their heads. At dusk the bedrock cracks as it contracts — a single rifle-shot from nowhere, a stone splitting cleanly along a stria. And when the dry wind comes over the col and starts the snow drifting, the drift charges itself: you feel it in the hairs on your arms, you hear a fine buzz at the ridge, and in the last of the light there is a faint blue crawl along the tips of the rime feathers on the nunatak's spine, and the air up there smells briefly, unmistakably, of ozone. It lasts a few minutes. It is only the wind rubbing snow on snow, the same thing that happens in a duct anywhere, but here there is nothing built to earth it, and the whole ridge stands there quietly discharging into the sky.

---

At three in the afternoon the sun goes behind the western arête and the shift ends.

It happens fast. The film of water on the rock goes cloudy, then stiff, then solid, in about eleven minutes. The sheets on the flats skin over and the skin thickens and by full dark you can walk on the flood. The rills stop; the nickpoint freezes mid-bite. Everything that was moving all day is locked, and in the lock the ground begins its night work: needle ice grows up out of the wet silt, thousands of vertical filaments an inch long, and they lift the entire surface layer of the plain bodily off its bed, so that in the morning the ground is raised and hollow-sounding and crunchy and utterly changed, and by noon it will collapse back into porridge and start again. Every night the floor is re-tooled. Every day it is run.

Nothing here has been made by hands. No cairn, no stake, no scratch of a boot, no scrap. The only marks on this ground are tool marks and animal marks, and in a hundred years there will be a soil, and in a thousand a river with a name, and the map that the water is drawing this afternoon at a centimetre an hour will be an old map, obvious, inevitable, the way the land was always going to drain.

For now it is a shop floor with the tool withdrawn and the coolant still lying in the trays, cold and grey and lit from underneath, and the small lives crowding the one warm seam, and the machine up-valley still cutting.
