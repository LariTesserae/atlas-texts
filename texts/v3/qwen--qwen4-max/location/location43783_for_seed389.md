## The Increase

The massif has no name in the old sense. On the survey plates it is marked **the Increase**, and the number beside the mark is revised every year, in a smaller hand, by whoever holds the office of Datum.

It stands four and a half kilometers above the haze-floor, and it is not finished. That is the first fact and it explains most of the others. The rock is new — not young in the way mountains are young, with a hundred million years of weather already softening them, but *new*, extruded within living memory, still smelling of struck flint when you break it. There is no soil anywhere on it. There is no sand. There are no rounded stones. Every edge on the mountain is the edge it was born with, sharp enough to open a palm, and the whole vast flank of it is a single unweathered surface, gray-black, faintly greasy in the light, like a casting that has not yet been fettled.

Erosion has only just begun to consider the place. In the eastern apron there is a rill nine years old, forty centimeters deep, cut by the discharge of a condenser house. It is the first watercourse in the history of the massif. Children are taken to see it. There is a brass plate.

---

The air is thin enough to make a stranger's hands feel like someone else's, and it is hot, and this is the paradox visitors arrive with: four and a half kilometers up, in a sky the color of a bruise at the zenith, and the ground burns through their boots.

The heat does not come from the sun. The sun is ordinary — a plain white disc, ordinary hours, ordinary shadows, the light of any afternoon anywhere. It is the *floor* that is hot. The plug of mantle beneath the Increase is rising, and the city sits on its crown like a scale on a cooling loaf, and the stone under every street holds sixty, eighty, in the lower quarters a hundred and ten degrees. Meanwhile, three meters above your head, the thin dry air opens straight onto the cold of space.

So the world here is vertical in a way that takes a season to learn. Your soles scorch and your scalp aches with cold. Stand still on a terrace at dusk and you can feel the two weathers arguing across the length of your body. There is a height, somewhere around the collarbone, where the temperature is exactly blood, and the whole architecture of the city is an attempt to keep human beings at that height.

Everything else is machinery.

---

The city is called Terrace, though it is nine terraces and the spaces between them, and it holds perhaps forty thousand people in a fabric so dense that the lanes are measured in shoulders. Buildings touch. Buildings *interpenetrate* — a flue from one quarter passes through the granary of another under a lease dated three generations back, and the granary is warm because of it, and the lease is a valuable property. There is no room and there is enormous room: the fabric is jammed tight, near-touching, layered five and six deep, and it sits on an open plateau that runs unbroken to a horizon of stacks and fin-fields and the pale glitter of the tailings aprons, forty kilometers of it, regional, walkable in a week, all of it made.

Nothing here is older than the ground it stands on, and the ground is coming up through it.

This is the thing that outsiders cannot hold in their heads. The Increase rises three to five centimeters a year. The city does not build upward to escape it; the city builds *ahead* of it. In the Formwork districts you walk on lattices of ceramic and blackened steel, streets laid out at their future elevation, house-fronts hung in the air with nothing beneath them but hot haze, doorways waiting. Over decades the rock arrives. It comes up plastic and slow, like something being pushed through a die, and the works take it as it comes — cut it, sinter it, cool it, dress it — and the street you walked on as a scaffold becomes a street you walk on as a floor, and then, in four hundred years, a cellar, and then a seam, and then ore.

Addresses are elevations. *Halden's, at 4,113, rising.* A family's fortune is the distance between their front step and the datum, and a poor household is one that has let the ground catch up.

---

The industry is enormous and almost silent.

There is very little combustion; the air is too thin to feed a decent fire, and the founders learned early to stop trying. Instead the city runs on the gradient it lives inside. Every roof is a cold plate. Every floor is a hot plate. Between them, in the walls, in the stair-cores, in the long low halls that look like chapels and are not, sit the engines: displacers and regenerators and slow pistons the width of a barrel, turning over once every forty seconds, once a minute, a Stirling heartbeat you feel in the stone rather than hear. Thermopile galleries a hundred meters long, copper and gray alloy, harvesting the difference between the ground and the sky. Nothing bangs. Nothing exhausts. The machines exhale a shimmer and go back to work.

And the mountain itself is the battery. Down the western flank run the Falls — six counterweight inclines, four and a half kilometers of cable, glass tailings going down and water-ice and ore and freight coming up, and the loaded cars descending turn the generators in the cable-houses at the head. The city is powered by being high. It gets high by processing the heat that lifts it. It is a loop that has been closing, slowly, for two centuries, and the Assay's projections show it closing for eight hundred more.

Conveyors cross the plazas at a walking pace and a half. A shift's output takes a day to pass a window. Men eat lunch beside a load and finish lunch and the load has moved eleven meters, and this is not remarked upon.

---

Water is the scarcity and the religion.

The air holds almost nothing, but the sky is very cold, and if you face a black plate at the zenith on a clear night and insulate its back, it will drop forty, fifty degrees below the air around it and sweat. So the roofs of Terrace are fin-fields: hectares of them, ranked and canted, thin dark blades with copper capillaries threading down into the walls, and in the hour before dawn the whole city drips.

Yield is a liter a plate a night, on a good night. The dewwrights' guild maintains the plates, argues about emissivity, and holds water rights denominated in *fins*. Households wash in the runoff of their neighbors' roofs and are not proud about it. Every wet thing in the city is downstream of something.

There are pockets: the sumps, where condensate has been let gather in unlined basins of fresh stone and lies warm and flat and utterly clear, without a weed, without a larva, without a skin of pollen, so clear that people misjudge the depth and break ankles. The wet seams, where a leak has run for a decade and the stone has grown a beard of white salt and a slick of something that eats iron. The Ninefold Cistern under the Slow Yards, which is a room, not a tank, and has a shore.

And once every three to eleven years, the plume from the eastern works finally saturates its own column and it rains. Hot rain, fat drops, twenty minutes of it, the whole plateau hissing and steaming and smelling of wet mineral — petrichor with nothing living in it, the smell of rain on a world that has not yet invented rot. Everyone stands in it. It is not considered dignified to stand in it and everyone stands in it. Then the stone dries in an hour and the rill in the eastern apron deepens by a millimeter and the Assay records the millimeter with great seriousness.

---

Life is present in the way a signature is present.

There are no plants. There is no seed-bank, no soil to hold one, no season mild enough. What there is: a chemotrophic film in the wet seams, rust-orange and sulfur-yellow, four centimeters thick at its luxuriant worst, which the Assay classifies as *living, provisionally*. On the condenser fins grow mineral dendrites, fern-shaped, silver, that branch toward the sound of the engines, and no one has settled whether they are alive or merely very good at being frost. Three of the eleven entries in the register of animals are disputed in exactly this way.

The undisputed animals: a pale cricket-thing in the wet seams, descended from something brought up in a fodder crate ninety years ago, now unable to live anywhere else. A moth at the arc-lights of the foundry, one generation every two years. A bat-analogue in the sky-wells that drinks off the fins and is the reason the fins have grilles. And the harrow — a black, four-meter soaring bird that comes to the thermal above the plug, gains two kilometers without a wingbeat, and does not land. It has never been seen to land. There are people whose profession is watching for it, and they are not considered idle.

Below all of these, in the Ninefold Cistern, there is the tenant. Something pale and unhurried has been recorded there twice in a hundred and forty years, both times by the same instrument, both times moving away. The guild's standing order is to leave the third gate open.

---

The quiet is the strangest thing, and the most easily explained, and the one nobody believes until they arrive.

Thin air is a poor carrier. Add fresh sintered stone, full of voids, drinking sound the way a curtain drinks it, and a city of forty thousand people and six hundred engines produces less noise than a village. A hammer strike two lanes away reaches you as a cough. The arc-furnaces glare behind their shutters in absolute hush. You can stand in the middle of the Slow Yards at full production and hear your own blood.

But the rock is loud. Sound goes through the stone beautifully — the whole massif is one continuous new casting, unfaulted, unbroken, and it rings. So Terrace listens through its feet and its palms. Every wall has a listening boss, a polished knob of dense basalt at ear height; put your ear to it and the quarter opens up, presses and pistons and the far cable-houses and the low grinding of the plug coming up under everything, a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, continuous, everywhere, inescapable. The Sounders are the diagnostic guild and they are all half-deaf in air and fluent in stone. Children learn the wall before they learn the word.

There is a bell in the Datum tower. It has never been heard from the street. It is rung anyway, on the day of the reading, and the Sounders report it through the rock, and the city nods.

---

Beneath the streets are the older streets, intact, and hotter, because heat comes from below. To descend is to descend into the kiln. The lower city — the abandoned quarters of the first century, doorways and shopfronts and a tram rail — is at four hundred degrees in places, and things left there are fired. There is a licensed trade in retrieval. Cups come up vitrified, letters come up as ash-shadows in glass, a bicycle comes up as a diagram of itself in oxide, and these are sold in the upper arcades to people whose grandparents lived at that elevation. The archive of Terrace is being slowly cooked. Everyone knows. Nothing is done. The alternative is to move it up, and up is where the ground is going.

---

What the place is, finally, is a landscape being made in front of you at a speed just below perception, by a city that is powered by the making.

The Assay's long projections — and they are read aloud, once a decade, to a bored crowd — say that in eleven thousand years the plug will slow. In thirty thousand, the rill in the eastern apron will have become a gorge, and the gorge will have made gravel, and the gravel will have made a bed, and something will root in the bed. There will be soil. There will be a smell of rot. The tailings aprons will be hills with rounded tops and the Falls will be abandoned cable-houses full of birds, and the whole sharp unfinished crown of the Increase will be a country, green, ordinary, with rivers, and no one in it will know that the ground was ever an exhaust.

Until then: hot floors, cold roofs, water by the liter, animals you could count on your hands, a city that leans in to the wall to hear itself, and the datum, rising, in a smaller hand each year.
