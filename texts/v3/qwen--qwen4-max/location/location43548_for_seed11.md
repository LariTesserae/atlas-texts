Nothing stands up here. That is the first fact, and the eye keeps refusing it. The ground runs flat to a horizon so far away it bends — nine days' walking in any direction and the only change is the color of the stone, red-brown to grey-brown to a varnished black that throws the sun back like water. This was a mountain range once. Not the mountains themselves but their roots: the ground-down stump of a range that stopped rising before anything with a spine existed, worn to a plane so old that the rivers which flattened it have forgotten their own names and gone. Then the whole slab was lifted, quietly, over ages, and set up here where the air is thin enough that water boils lukewarm and a person's breath comes short at rest. It is warm anyway. The latitude is tropical and the rock is dark and it has been drinking sun for a hundred million years; at noon the stone is hot enough to hurt a bare palm, and the heat comes up off it in a shivering sheet, and the sky above is a deep bruised blue that has nothing to do with evening.

The second fact is the noise.

It arrives before you understand it, a pressure more than a sound, a hand laid flat on the sternum. The plain is roaring. It roars the way a surf coast roars, but there is no sea, and no direction to it — it comes up out of the ground. Walking, you feel your own footfalls disappear. You open your mouth to speak and the sound is taken away sideways before it clears your teeth. In a strong gust the roar climbs into a whine with edges on it, and old people here have the calm, slightly tilted patience of the profoundly deaf, and children are taught to read hands before they are taught to walk.

The reason is that the plain is full of holes.

They start as pits the size of a thumb. Weathering, salt, the patience of dew: the caprock is a hard skin over a softer body, and wherever the skin has been breached the rock beneath has been eaten out into a flask, a bottle, a throat. There are millions. There are tens of millions. Some are no wider than a finger and a body-length deep; some are chambers you could park a house in, with mouths like wells, and floors of black water, and side galleries going off into the dark where the roof is held up by nothing anyone would trust. From the ground you see them as a scattering of dark punctuation, easy to step around, and the plain still reads as empty. From the escarpment at the world's edge, or from the top of a storm, you see what it actually is: a leaf. A single vast leaf seen from underneath, stippled with stomata, each pore ringed with green.

Because everything that lives here lives *down*.

Above the surface, nothing is permitted to be tall. The wind sees to that. What grows on the open plane grows in cushions and mats and crusts — black-green bosses of moss hard as boot leather, rosettes wrapped in wool, lichens in ten thousand shades of rust and mustard and bone, all of it pressed into the stone's skin so flat that a hand laid on the ground feels only warm grit and a faint, ceaseless trembling. Lush, but horizontally: an enormous tonnage of life with no height at all, spreading over the flats in a continuous nap, thick enough that in the wet season the whole plain sweats green.

At the lip of every hole, though, the mat thickens and turns and goes over the edge. Down the throat, out of the wind, the world changes. Two body-lengths below the surface the roar drops to a hum you can talk over. Four, and it is a warm humid dusk with ferns growing out of the walls in shelves, and a vine that has learned to grow downward, hand over hand, toward water, and pale flowers at the mouth angled like reflectors to throw daylight into the shaft. Six, and there is a pool, black and still, and the walls are furred with liverworts, and the air smells of compost and iron, and the heat is the heat of a body — the rock's stored sun plus the slow burning of ten thousand years of fallen green. The plain does not have rivers. It has pockets: each shaft drains a little funnel of catchment, and at dawn the fog condenses on the stone and runs in threads to the nearest mouth, and after the brief violent rains the whole country drinks in an hour and is dry again by afternoon, roaring, with its water hidden inside it like marrow.

So: a hollow land. Vast space between presences, if you count only what stands. Below, it is crowded. Above, in a full day's walk, you might meet a wingless grasshopper sheltering in a crack, a snail, a lizard that presses its jaw to the rock to listen, and — perhaps, high up, going somewhere else — one enormous slow bird that never lands on the plain because there is nothing to land on and no shelter to land in. Flying insects cannot hold station in this wind. Nothing here is pollinated by flight. The flowers at the mouths wait, and are pollinated by hand, by people, who go from throat to throat with a brush of fiber and a smear of resin, and who are therefore the plain's principal animal, and know it.

There are not many of them. A stopped field, a few hundred holes plugged with fired clay and cord and pitch, making a basin of relative quiet a stone's throw across; a turf-roofed house with its wall shaped like the prow of a ship and its door on the lee side and no window facing the weather; a line of stakes with a cord strung between them, so that in a whiteout fog a person can walk home by touch; a cairn, and another cairn, because you cannot navigate by sound in a country where sound has no direction. And, once in a region, a tower — the only vertical thing for days, drilled full of flues, standing in the wind and screaming a single fixed note that carries twenty miles. Travelers do not look for home. They listen for its pitch, and feel for it in the soles of their feet.

That is the other speech. The caprock is continuous and hard and it carries a tap for miles. People talk here by striking the ground with a stone hammer: through the palms, through the heels, through the jaw laid against a boulder at night. Conversation is a code of knocks under the roar. Air is for the plain; rock is for people.

The plain's voice is not one note but a chord of millions, and it is never the same twice, because the wind will not hold still. Gusts come in from the escarpment in walls you can watch approaching by the change in the light on the stone. Hundreds of dust devils walk the flats at once, and when one passes over a cluster of mouths the local chord bends up a whole tone and slides back. Some shafts exhale, some inhale; a pressure front crossing the country turns the throats inside out and the entire district modulates, a glissando forty miles wide, and the sound of it makes the hair rise on arms already covered in wool. In the worst of it, updrafts lift grit out of the holes and stand it in the air in trembling columns, and the plain seems to be manufacturing weather out of itself, which it is. Hail arrives, rattling on the mats, and melts, and drains down. Somewhere under all of it, always, there is a floor of hum that never stops, because the shafts breathe with temperature alone, and even in dead calm at midnight the ground is quietly sounding.

And it changes for good, too. A roof spalls in the night — a bang nobody hears, a mouth where there was a floor — and a new note enters the world permanently, and a green collar begins to form around it within a season. This is how the country is made. This is also how it is unmade, because a throat that widens drops in pitch, and keeps dropping, and the last stage of every hole is to become so broad and shallow that it sings below hearing. The plain is going low. The old people say the chord has fallen a full fifth in their grandmothers' time; they say there used to be voices up in the range where now there is only a pressure in the chest and a trembling in the teeth. Erosion here has finished flattening and moved on to hollowing. The land is being converted, patiently, into void, and the void is being converted into silence, and the noise you can't bear is the sound of that happening — deafening, and on its way out.

At the edge, the plain simply stops. A cliff, three thousand paces of it, dropping into a floor of cloud that has weather of its own, and rain, and — they say — trees. The wind pours over that lip and the holes near it are the highest voices left, thin and unbearable, and no one lives within a day's walk of them.

Inland there is one basin where the rock is unbroken, a lens of something harder that the weathering has not yet cracked. No holes. No roar. A shallow lake there, the only surface water for nine days, and the sound of it lapping is the loudest thing in the world, and people are brought there as children to be shown what silence is, and most of them weep, and some of them cannot stop, and are carried home. The bird lands there. Nowhere else.

Night comes down ordinary and enormous, the stars very many, the light gone long and red across a flatness with no shadow in it anywhere. The temperature falls; the shafts draw inward; the chord sinks and thickens and settles into its low register, and the mats go stiff with dew, and the towers go on screaming their single notes into the dark, and far off, felt rather than heard, something in the ground gives way and lets a little more of the world out.
