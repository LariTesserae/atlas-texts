## The Bowl of Standing Water

Come up over the col at the hour when the sun is already white and the sky above has gone the violet of a bruise under the skin, and the first thing that happens is that your eyes stop working. Not from darkness. From the opposite. The glare comes off the bowl in a solid sheet, from below as much as above, and for a while there is nothing to see but a bright ache with a shape in it.

Then the shape resolves, and it is not what the eye wants. The eye wants a snowfield: a clean slope, a horizon, a place to put itself. What is here instead is a crowd.

Four thousand and some blades of ice stand in the bowl, shoulder to shoulder, none of them wider than a door, most of them taller than a person, a few as tall as three people standing on each other. They lean. All of them lean the same way, toward the noon sun, at an angle so consistent it looks like obedience. Between them run narrow slots of shadow, hand-width to body-width, and the floor of those slots is old glacier-polished rock, striated, gray-pink, worn smooth by something enormous that left a long time ago.

The bowl is three hundred paces across. You could throw a stone over it. To walk it takes most of a day.

It is not a cathedral. Nothing here is arranged for you. It is a congregation, and it was here before anyone came up to look, and it will be here, in essentially this number and essentially this posture, long after the last person who bothered has gone back down.

## What the Blades Are

They are not fresh. That is the first wrongness, if you know anything about ice. Snow spikes like these are supposed to be a season's affair — a spring thing, gone by midsummer, reborn the next year out of new snow. These are old. Cut one down and you will find it banded like a trunk: dust layers, clear layers, dust again, hundreds of them, thousands, thin as pages. The ice at the heart of the tallest blades fell as snow when there were still animals in the valleys below.

The ice is not going anywhere. It is a residual thing, the last hand's-breadth of a glacier that quit flowing before there were words for quitting. It sits on its rock floor and does not slide. It has no accumulation zone, no snout, no crevasses. It has only weather, and it has learned to survive weather by becoming this shape — the shape that loses the least and keeps the most, the geometry that a bright cold place makes when it is left alone long enough to think.

Because the air here is thin and dry and the sun is savage, ice does not melt so much as it evaporates outright, straight from solid to nothing. Sublimation. It is a slow subtraction, and it is not fair: it takes hardest from the hollows, where light bounces twice, three times, trapped between blade walls and concentrated. So the hollows deepen and the blades sharpen, and the sharper the blades the more perfectly the hollows trap the light, and the whole thing digs itself into greater definition, year over year, century over century. It is a wound that heals into a sharper wound. It has been in balance a very long time. Blades fall; blades rise out of the deepening cusps at the same rate. Someone once counted them. Someone else counted, much later. The numbers were four thousand one hundred and twelve, and four thousand one hundred and nine.

That difference is the entire recorded history of the place.

## Noon, and the Water

Damp is the wrong word for it and also the only word.

There is no stream. There is a seep at the base of the headwall that runs for perhaps four hours a day, in the middle of the day, and stops, and freezes, and is fed by nothing you can identify — no snowpatch above it, no visible source. It comes out of the rock as if the rock were sweating, which at this altitude, in this light, is close to true.

At noon the black stones on the floor of the cusps go above freezing while the air a metre up stays eight degrees below it. This is the local paradox and everyone who comes here learns it in their skin: your face burns, your hands go dead, and the stone under your palm is warm. On those stones a film of melt appears, a millimetre deep, held by surface tension in the polish left by the vanished ice. It runs nowhere. It slides down the striations, gathers in the low point of the cusp, and sits there — a lens of water the size of a shield boss, impossibly clear, with the sky in it.

There are hundreds of these. In the hour after noon the bowl is full of them and the light goes down into them and comes back up, so that the blades are lit from beneath, from inside, blue-green in their depths, white on their edges, and the shadow slots are not dark but a kind of underwater dusk. The floor throws caustics onto the ice walls: nets of light that crawl and reweave as the water shivers.

By the third hour the films are gone. The pools freeze from the rim inward with a sound like a fingernail on a drumhead. Under the field, in the old plumbing of the vanished glacier — gravel channels, gaps between ice and rock — the day's water goes downhill in the dark, and if you put your ear to a cusp floor you can hear it swallow.

Then night, and the whole bowl contracts, and there is a long minute at dusk when four thousand blades tick at once.

## The Black Flour

Look into any melt pool and there is grit at the bottom. Dark, fine, faintly greasy. It collects in the wells because the wells are where everything collects. It is dust — the sky's dust, ground rock, the far-off ash of things that burned elsewhere — and it is not entirely dust.

It is bound into grains by films of something that is not a plant and is not an animal and is barely a anything: a dark metabolism, working at the pace of centuries, eating mineral and light in the wet hour and shutting down dry and cold for the rest of the day. It has no green in it. Green would be useless here; green reflects. This is black because black is what survives — black takes the sun, black warms, black melts its own hollow a finger deeper each year and so makes itself a longer wet hour tomorrow.

The blades, in other words, are not a weather accident. They are farmed. Not by anyone. Not on purpose, probably. But the geometry of the bowl is the geometry that the black flour finds profitable, and the flour has been voting for it, grain by grain, since before the ice stopped moving.

The makers had a word for it that means *the meal*, and another that means *the tarnish*, and they used the second one when they were being careful.

## The Mineral Garden

In the shaded pockets, where the sun never reaches and the salt has nowhere to go, something else grows that is not alive at all.

Frost flowers. Salt whiskers. Gypsum rosettes the size of a fist, blades of selenite pushing out of the rock in near-touching sheaves, hairs of halite so fine they move in a breath. They come up in the cold dry weeks and they are as intricate as any meadow and they contain nothing. They dissolve in the next wet hour and come back. The bowl has a flora and it is entirely mineral and it is entirely seasonal and it does not care.

Between them, in the deepest slots, the rime builds too — windward faces feathered white, growing into the wind at a finger a week, so that every blade has a beard on one side and a polished cheek on the other, and the whole crowd appears to be looking over its shoulder.

## The Marks

Nothing here is built at any scale worth the name. There was never fuel: no wood, no peat, no dung, nothing that burns. There was never a fire in this bowl. Remember that when you find the hearth.

You find it near the headwall: a hollowed black boulder, a socket cut in its upper lip, a scatter of copper slag the size of lentils. The socket holds a lens. It is a lens of clear ice, ground and polished, and it must be replaced from time to time because it sublimes away like everything else. Set it right, at the right hour of the right weeks, and it puts a spot of light on native copper that makes the copper soft enough to hammer. That is the entire industry. Sun-forging. Metallurgy with a calendar. Pins, awls, needles, thin wire, a few small blades — worked in bursts of forty minutes, between the seep's start and the seep's stop, by people who could not breathe well enough to stay longer.

They came up from below in parties, worked, and went down. Their lungs set the shift length. You can read it in the stairs they cut into the headwall: shallow, patient, each one a few dozen blows, a hundred and eleven steps that took some number of generations. You can read it in the tally wall, where the count is kept, and in the small chisel somebody's child left in a cusp and never came back for.

There is a corbelled stone cell against the east wall, beehive-shaped, doorway knee-high. No hearth inside. A sleeping shelf, a niche for the lens, a floor of black cobbles that hold the day's heat until perhaps midnight and then let it go. There is a windbreak wall that stops in the middle of nothing, because the rest of it was never needed. There is a line of cairns marking the one route through the blades that a laden person can walk, and copper pins driven into the ice at the turns, and the pins are now deep inside the blades, visible, unreachable, like flies in amber.

And there is a cairn with someone under it.

## The Seconds

Here is the thing that the careful word was for.

Leave an object standing in the bowl and the rime will find it. That is ordinary; rime finds everything. What is not ordinary is what it does next. Over a season, over ten seasons, the accretion thickens into a shape, and the shape is not a blob and not a coating. It is the object. Hollow, white, a little larger, standing beside it or in its place: a second of the hammer, a second of the pin, a second of the cairn.

The seconds are not casts. They do not take the surface. They take the *idea*, and they finish it. The child's chisel had a broken tip; its second is whole. A tool that came up with a wooden haft — wood being a thing that does not exist here, that has to be carried up from a world with plants in it — has a second with an ice haft, correctly proportioned, curved to a grip that nobody in this bowl has ever held. The field has never seen wood. It has seen the socket where the wood goes, and it has guessed, and it has guessed right.

It only copies what stands. A fallen blade gets no second. A blade that falls and is stood back up gets one within the decade. Where a blade has collapsed entirely, the cusp sometimes raises a white ghost of it anyway, thinner, taller, leaning a little further than the original ever dared — as if the field were remembering forward instead of back.

They do not last. They are rime; they sublime; the wind takes their edges and then their substance, and a second that took eighty years to assemble is gone in a bad winter. So there is a second population in the bowl, layered over the first, coming and going, hollow restorations of everything that has ever been left standing here long enough to be noticed. The cairn has a second. The person under it does not.

Nobody who worked here wrote down what they thought about this. They kept the tally, they replaced the lens, they cut the next stair, and they went down before dark, because the seconds are brightest at dusk — the low sun gets into their hollow interiors and they glow faintly from within, a crowd of pale lanterns standing among the crowd of blades, all of them leaning the same way.

## The Bow

Once a year, at the turn of the low sun, the leaning changes.

It is not fast. It is a degree or so across a season, the whole field adjusting its posture to the new arc, and it happens by subtraction on one face and accretion on the other, and no single blade can be seen to move. But there is a morning — the makers knew which one — when the entire congregation touches at once, four thousand edges meeting four thousand neighbours, and the bowl makes a single sound.

One tick. Thin air carries it badly. You have to be inside to hear it.

Then the seep starts, and the black flour wakes in the wells, and the light comes down through the slots in blades of its own, and the water begins, again, to be made.
