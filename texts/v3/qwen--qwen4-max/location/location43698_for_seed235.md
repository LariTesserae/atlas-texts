# The Sostenuto

## I. Roar

The first fact is not a sight. It is a pressure that arrives from every direction at once, including from below, including from inside the stone.

Call it a roar because there is no better word, but a roar implies something happening, and nothing here is happening. This is a chord that has been sounding for as long as there has been a here. Water falling through ten thousand hollow columns; water striking rimstone basins in a fixed, unrepeating-sounding but perfectly repeated pattern; the low grinding fundamental that lives in the floor and comes up through the rock like a hand pressing on the sole of a foot. Over it, the fifths. Under it, something below hearing that you would feel in the joints of a body, if a body could be here.

The sound does not fall off with distance. Stand at the rim, stand at the center, stand in a sealed cistern under nine meters of lime: the same loudness, exactly. Nothing is far away by ear. Every cataract in the basin is adjacent. The whole region is pressed against the place where a listener would be, and there is no listener, and so the deafening is not cruelty. It is only weather made of pressure. There is no one to deafen.

## II. Height

The basin sits at the top of the world. From the Sill — the worn lip of the eastern rim — the ground drops away nine hundred meters in fluted travertine cliffs, and below that the cloud deck lies flat and cream-colored to the horizon, and other ranges poke through it like the backs of submerged animals. There are no animals. The comparison is available and useless. The curvature of the earth is visible if the haze permits. By every geometric measure this place is absurdly high, a tableland where the air should be thin enough to make a climber's lips split.

The air is broth. Sea-level thick, wet, hot as a laundry, thirty-eight degrees and holding. Heat here has no source and no direction. Sun-facing stone and the underside of a stair are the same temperature. Shade cools nothing. There is no uphill for warmth to climb, no cold anywhere for it to run toward. The plateau swelters at the altitude of glaciers and there are no glaciers, will never have been glaciers, and the cliff faces below show no cirques, no moraines, no memory of ice.

And the light is ordinary. That is the strangest courtesy of the place. A bright overcast mid-morning, luminous haze, soft-edged shadows, the mild even light of a day that intends to rain. No sun disc. No night. The sky is a lit ceiling of its own mist and it has been ten in the morning since before the word morning.

## III. Water

It is raining. It has always been raining. It will not stop and it has never started.

The rain falls from a mist ceiling about forty meters above the terraces, a bright grey lid you cannot see the top of. It lands in the rimstone pools, overflows their lips, runs down the worn gutters, drops through the reed-flutes, gathers in the cisterns, spills into the Fold — the long shallow lake at the basin's low end — and evaporates into the lid. The books balance to the drop. Every drop is on a circuit. Some of the circuits are simple. Some pass through the same pool eleven times before returning to the lid. A few run backward along the last stretch, rising in slow bright beads up the face of the Long Arcade because the loop they belong to closes upward, and there is nothing pushing them, and nothing needs to.

That is the thing about the water. It does not fall because of gravity, or not only. It falls because it fell. Its motion is not caused; it is repeated. You could name a drop. You could learn its route the way you learn a street. There is one bead on the third pier of the Long Arcade that has been in the act of arriving for the entire history of the world, and the splash it makes is part of the chord, a specific bright tick in the upper register, and it is that splash, and it is always that splash, and the pool it strikes has never once been disturbed differently.

The pool surfaces are not flat. They stand in permanent ridges, corrugated like the roof of a mouth, patterns that hold their shape while trembling — the water's skin driven into figures by the sound and locked there. Mist at the loud places hangs in lattices, droplets suspended in ranks and files a finger's width apart, an architecture of humidity furnished through the air. Walk through it — nothing walks through it — and it would close again in the same arrangement, because the arrangement is what the sound means.

## IV. Lime

The water is heavy with dissolved lime, and hot, and it gives up its load wherever it is told to.

The material of this place is travertine: cream, honey, pale rust, banded like cut fat, spongy tufa where it grew fast and dense translucent calcite where it grew slow. It precipitates at exactly the rate the running water dissolves it. So the stairs are dished by feet that never existed, the thresholds worn to a saddle, the gutters polished to a glassy channel — and none of it wears any further. The wear is reimbursed as it occurs. The city looks ten thousand years old. Its age is a shape, not a duration.

Where does it precipitate? At the still places. The chord standing in this basin has nodes — surfaces where the pressure cancels, where the air is momentarily, permanently quiet — and it is on those surfaces that lime comes out of solution and dust settles and stays. The architecture is the quiet part of the noise. Floors are nodal planes. Walls are the folded sheets where two nodal surfaces meet. The great vaults over the Reed Quarter are the exact shape of a silence in the middle of a scream, cast in stone, and the scream goes on around them and holds them up.

So there is a city, and no one built it, and it is not a ruin.

It has districts. The **Reed Quarter**, where the tufa grew around stands of giant horsetail and reed, and the plants rotted — no; the plants circulated — and left their hollow molds, and the molds are pipes, thousands of them, ranks and ranks of stone flutes from wrist-thin to a diameter you could sleep in, and the water goes down them and the air comes up and the chord has its voice. The **Sixteen Stairs**, a stepped cascade of rimstone dams grown into flights and landings and parapets, each pool brimming into the next, and the sixth stair is also the sixteenth, which you discover by climbing. The **Cistern District**, roofed, resonant, its chambers full to the same line forever, its columns fluted by accretion rather than by carving, standing in water that has never been still and never been stirred. The **Bellies**, where the antinodes are, where nothing can accumulate, where the roar is so total that the stone is scoured to bare bones and the streets are simply the gaps between loudness — negative architecture, rooms defined by what refuses to be there. And out past the Sill, the **suburbs**: rimstone terraces spilling for kilometers across the plateau, pale scalloped shelves of pool after pool, thinning into haze, the city dissolving into its own geology without ever quite ending.

Aqueduct-arcades stride the gorges on legs of banded lime. They carry water uphill in three places. Nobody engineered this. The chord needed a return path and the lime obeyed.

## V. Green

Everything is growing and nothing is getting bigger.

Figs straddle the arcades, and their roots are their trunks: follow an aerial root down from a branch and it enters the paving, travels, and comes up as the same tree, so that each fig is a closed ring of wood with no seed anywhere in its history. Mosses digest their own lower layers and lay the sugar back down on top. A tree fern on the fourth terrace casts a spore that becomes the fern that casts it. There is a rose-analogue in the wet colonnades, deep coral, waxy, that pollinates itself with one grain of pollen traveling a fixed loop from anther to stigma to anther, and it has been about to fruit since the beginning.

The colors band by pitch. In a normal hot spring the microbial mats sort themselves by temperature; here there is no temperature gradient to sort by, so they sort by register — jade where the fundamental dominates, brick-orange in the fifths, a sulfur yellow at the nodes' edges, and a black-green film that grows only on surfaces that are silent, which is to say only on the load-bearing parts of the world.

Nothing here needs a pollinator, a seed disperser, a grazer, a decomposer that works in one direction. Nothing here can be hungry. Depletion is a gradient and there are no gradients. That is why there are no animals: an animal is a device for running down a slope, and this place has no slopes of the kind a body could use. No birds, no insects, no fish in the Fold, no grazers on the terraces. The lushness is total and entirely unaccompanied. No wingbeat, no cry, no rustle of anything fleeing. The deafening is all water and lime and the resonance of hollow stone, and it has no punctuation.

## VI. Rules

Cause and effect are not distinguishable here, only sequence, and sequence is circular. The water carved the flutes; the flutes called the water. Ask which came first and the question has no purchase, like asking which end of a ring is the beginning.

The streets recur. Walk out of the Cistern District along the Long Arcade and you arrive at the far rim, coming in. Not a trick of resemblance — the same pier, the same bead of water in its eternal arrival. The basin is thirty kilometers across and also a closed figure, and the region extends past sight in every direction and is nevertheless all here, adjacent, at the same loudness. Reflections in the standing pools arrive a moment before what they reflect. Nothing exploits this. There is no one to be warned.

And the height itself: the plateau is not held up by rock. Beneath the Fold, beneath the fluted cliffs, the fundamental sits in a shaft called the Throat, a note too low to hear, and the whole tableland stands on its pressure the way a bead of water stands on a jet of air. The elevation is acoustic. Six thousand meters of nothing much, and above it a hot wet green stone city in permanent mid-morning, sustained by a sound that cannot stop because stopping is a change.

## VII. The Node

At the center of the basin, where the Sixteen Stairs fold back through themselves and the Reed Quarter's largest pipe opens into a chamber the size of a cathedral, there is a point.

It is small — a thumbnail, a plum stone. Every surface of pressure in the world crosses there and cancels. No roar. No fundamental. No tick of the arriving drop. A silence so complete it has a texture, like the inside of a bell that has never been struck, and it is load-bearing: the vault above it, the stairs beneath it, the whole pale weight of the city precipitated out of the air onto this one quiet spot because this is where the noise means nothing.

Hanging in it is a single drop of water. It is not falling and it is not rising. It is the one drop not on a circuit, or it is the point where every circuit touches, and it holds the shape of a lens, and in it the entire terraced basin stands inverted, green and cream and steaming, small and complete.

Around it, a hundred and forty decibels of forever. Inside it, the only quiet in the world.

Nothing hears the difference. That is not a tragedy. There is no one to be lonely here, no ear to rupture, no clock to consult. There is only a hot wet limestone chord holding itself up in the sky, wearing itself smooth and rebuilding itself smooth in the same instant, saying one enormous unchanging thing with ten thousand mouths of reed-cast stone — and at the center of the saying, the small still drop where the saying cancels, and the whole city leans on it, and has always leaned on it, and will.
