The first thing is not the ground. It is the noise, and the noise arrives before the place does — a broadband hiss, gravel pouring down a chute somewhere beyond the escarpment, except that it does not stop, does not vary, does not resolve into a source, and after a while you understand that it is coming from everywhere at once and from underneath you, and that the chute is the whole basin, and that the gravel is alive.

It sits somewhere around a hundred and ten decibels at the height of a knee. Higher, near the ear, it climbs. It has a texture like coarse sand shifted on a drumhead, layered with a finer sizzling, and beneath both a low pulse every eleven seconds that you feel in the sternum before you hear it, if you hear it at all. Speech is pointless. Shouting is pointless. Within an hour your jaw aches from the conduction of it through the bone, and within a day the fine hairs of your inner ear have begun to give up their high frequencies, and the world tilts a little, and you stop being able to tell which direction the slope goes.

The basin floor is low — barely above the reach of a sea that withdrew long before anything here could remember it — and it runs out flat and pale-dark for a day's walk in every direction, until it meets the far escarpment, which is only a rumor of slightly less darkness. Nothing built. No wall, no cairn, no cut. Nothing has ever been made here. And yet the ground is fitted: a pavement of shell and carapace, clast against clast, sorted so exactly by size that you could measure the grading with a caliper, every edge rounded, every surface polished to a dull satin by a hundred thousand years of rubbing. It looks laid. It is not laid. It is the lag deposit of an ocean of animals that has been dying in place, in balance, since before the sky closed.

Underfoot the pavement shifts. Not much — a centimeter, a settling, like stepping on a bag of loose plates. Then it shifts again, because it is not a bag of plates. It is nine animals deep, in places fourteen, tessellated and stacked and slowly rotating, a crust of broad flat grazers the size of a hand or a dinner platter, each one rasping the film off the shell of its neighbor with a mouthpart like a file made of teeth. That is the hiss. It is the sound of everything eating. The film they eat is bacterial, sulfur-tasting, thin as breath, and it grows back in about forty minutes, and there are three hundred and forty individuals per square meter, and each of them is scraping at four strokes a second, and the arithmetic of that is the noise that is killing your hearing.

Between the plates, in the interstices, the sifters work — smaller, blind, wedge-shaped, living on the flour. The flour is what the rasping makes: shell ground to a powder so fine it hangs, so fine it moves as a fluid, pooling in hollows, drifting in windrows a meter high, sliding down the shallow braided channels in slow gray avalanches that make no sound you could isolate from the rest. Below the flour, mud; below the mud, more animals, for meters — threaders, pale and segmented, churning the sediment so thoroughly that no bedding survives anywhere in the basin. There is no stratigraphy here. The record has been eaten and re-eaten. The place has no layers because it has never stopped being digested.

It is cool. Nine degrees, perhaps eleven, and it never gets warm and never freezes. But the carpet itself is warmer than the air — three hundred and forty bodies per square meter, all of them burning something, all of them leaking heat — and where the cold air slides down off the escarpment and meets that living warmth, fog forms and stands and does not lift. This is most of the water. Not rain. Rain comes perhaps twice a year, a gray drizzle out of a sky that has no visible sun in it, only a slow bruise of brighter haze somewhere overhead that migrates and never quite arrives. The rest is condensation: fog beading on ten million cold shells, gathering, running down, being drunk.

And here is the knot the whole place is tied in.

To harvest dew you must be cold — colder than the air, cold enough to radiate your heat away to whatever is up there past the haze. To be cold you must get out of the crowd, up onto a bare rise or the rim of a brine pocket, and stand exposed and alone. And to be alone here is to be a hole in the noise.

Because nothing in this basin sees. There is no light worth the name — the fog, the flour-haze, the escarpment's shadow, and above all the fact that nothing here has ever needed to look at anything. What they have instead are tympana: membranes on the flanks, on the legs, along the underside, tuned to the substrate and to the air. They read the world as a field of sound. The chorus is the medium in which everything happens — courtship, spacing, alarm, the slow negotiation of who rasps whose shell. A body in the chorus is masked, is safe, is a grain in a river. A body that stops rasping opens a silence around itself the size of a doorway, and the silence is legible, and something comes.

The hunters are felted. That is the whole of their design: a dense pile of hollow setae that eats sound instead of reflecting it, a body that makes no scrape because it does not rasp, mouthparts that shear rather than grind. They move through the carpet and the carpet does not report them. From above — if there were an above, if there were light — you would see them as absences, patches of quiet a meter across drifting through the hiss like oil on water. The plain's other fliers, the membrane-winged things that hunt in the fog, cannot echolocate; the air is too full. They listen for the holes instead. They hunt the hunters by finding the silences and dropping into them, and half the time they are eaten, and the arithmetic works out.

So the drinkers go in shifts, briefly, in terrible exposed solitude on the cold rims, condensing a few milliliters on their own backs and licking it off their own shoulders with a jointed limb, and they do not stop rasping while they do it, which costs them the cold they came for, which means they must stay longer. Everything here is a negotiation between thirst and audibility. Except on the dew nights, when the fog comes down thick and the whole basin cools at once and every shell in a hundred square kilometers beads at the same moment — and then everyone drinks together, and for two or three hours the rasp thins to a murmur, the loudest silence of the year, and the felted things come up out of the mud in their thousands, and the plain is briefly, catastrophically quiet, and by morning the carpet is thinner and the flour is deeper.

There are places where the noise focuses. Hollows, subtle bends in the escarpment, the geometry of the fog's temperature inversion — they gather the whole basin's rasp and fold it into a spot forty meters wide where the pressure is enough to bleed a tympanum, enough to kill anything with a membrane, which here is everything. The carpet stops at their edges. They are the only bare ground in the world: scoured, wind-polished, faintly warm, ringing. You can map them from a distance by the bald holes in the living pavement, and you can hear them, or rather you can hear the way the hiss goes strange as you approach, thickening, developing a pitch, becoming a note. Nothing goes there. The flour blows across and does not settle.

None of this is powered by the sky. Nothing here fixes light; there has never been a plant in this basin, not a blade, not a leaf, not a green cell. The energy comes up. Under the mud, at forty meters, there is a black shale — the compressed remains of a forest that grew somewhere else, somewhen else, in a world with a sun in it — and groundwater moves through it and leaches it and carries dissolved carbon up through the seeps and the slow braided channels, and microbes ferment it, and the film grows, and the plates rasp the film, and the sifters eat the flour of the plates, and the threaders eat the sifters, and the felted things eat whatever falls silent. It is a food web running entirely on inheritance. A dead forest, being spent, at four strokes a second, by three hundred and forty mouths per square meter, for a hundred thousand years.

The shale is thick. It will last a very long time. But the balance here is not a circle; it is a slow, beautifully graded, thoroughly weathered decline, an equilibrium the way a candle flame is an equilibrium. And the noise — the deafening, bone-conducting, ear-destroying hiss that fills the basin from rim to rim and never stops, that has polished every stone in this world round — is the sound of trees. Not their leaves. Not their wind. Just their carbon, going out through the mouths of things that have never seen light, in the dark, in the cold, on a plain that has been listening to itself for longer than there has been anyone to hear.
