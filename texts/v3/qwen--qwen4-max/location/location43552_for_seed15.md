## The Vat

From above there is nothing to see. The hill is not a hill; it is three hundred years of offcuts, spoil heaps stacked and terraced and planted over, block-cores and rejected lintels laid in courses like a wall that fell asleep. Everything underfoot has been cut at least once. In the middle of the fourth terrace there is a lid: a slab of white stone with a hand-slot worn into it, no wider than a wellhead, breathing. On cold mornings the steam stands a metre above it. You find the place by listening before you find it by looking, because the sound comes up through the joints of the paving and through the soles of your feet before it ever reaches your ears.

Lift the lid and the noise arrives all at once, and so does the light.

---

The chamber below is eleven paces by seven, a built vault, mortared and plastered, buried in the made ground so that not one surface of it touches anything unworked. Floor: laid slabs, jointed, bedded on rubble that was itself dressed once and discarded. Walls: ashlar, six furrows to the tooth-chisel, the marks still crisp as corduroy. Ceiling: a shallow barrel with four oculi punched through it, three stopped with stone plugs, one open to the sky and lined with a shaft of stacked rings. Water: two and a half metres of it, warm as blood, filling the room to a line scribed into the plaster by somebody who is probably still alive.

It is *bright* down there. Absurdly bright. The plaster is lime-white and the water is mineral-clear and the open shaft drops a column of noon straight onto the floor, where it breaks into the moving net — the caustic web, that lace of focused light the surface ripples make, which crawls over the walls and the piers and your own hands in loops of white fire, faster than you can follow, opening and closing. The whole room is a light-box. Even the shadowed end glows by bounce. There is no dimness anywhere in it, only places where the light is standing still and places where it is running.

And it is loud. The spring enters at the north end through a lined throat the width of a thigh, under enough head to make the stone hum, and the roar of it fills the vault and comes back off the vault and fills it again. Strike a pier with a mallet and the note stays in the room for four slow seconds. Above the waterline the noise is a white crash and a slap; below it, the noise stops being outside you. Water carries sound four times faster than air and gives your ears no useful delay between them, so the roar has no direction, no distance. It sits inside the skull. Divers here say the room is not loud, the room is *in* you, and they say it after they get out, in the ordinary air, shouting a little.

---

Everything is new. That is the first thing your hands report. The arris of every step will cut you. The courses have not softened; the drill holes still hold their feathers; the boss left proud on the fourth pier's capital has not been knocked off because nobody has finished the fourth pier. There is no rounding, no polish, no worn dish in the tread where feet have gone down ten thousand times. Feet have gone down perhaps two hundred times. The room was closed and flooded in the spring and it is still, in every sense that matters, wet work.

But there is a skin forming. The water arrives glass-clear, pressurised, holding its lime dissolved and its gas in solution, and the instant it shoulders out of the throat into the open room it lets go: a churning white plume, milky as whey, that fans and slows and drops its cargo across the floor as the finest grey flour. What does not settle plates out. On the weir lip, on the rope, on the submerged treads, on the copper pin somebody left in a joint, there is a first crust — weeks old, glittering, made of little dogtooth crystals sharp enough to snag a fingertip. The stone here is not being worn away. It is being *added*. The erosion of this place runs backwards, and it has only just begun, and its edges are needles.

The weir lip is the place to watch it. Cut in autumn, knife-sharp, dead level, a made horizon that sets the water's height and therefore sets everything else. By midsummer it will be blunt. By the following autumn it will be a rounded white bolster, and the level will have crept up two fingers, and the scribed line on the plaster will be a lie.

---

Warm is a weak word. The throat delivers at thirty-nine; the body of the room holds thirty-five, thirty-six; the walls are warm to the palm and there is no cool corner to retreat into, no thermocline, no shade that helps, because the heat is not coming from the sun. You cannot sweat your way out of it. Ten minutes is a working shift. Fifteen and your pulse is in your teeth and you go up the steps too fast. Above the water the air is hotter still and sweetish and heavy, and it pools in the freeboard at the far end where the shaft's draught does not reach, and it will kill you quietly. The masons test it with a lamp on a cord: flame full at the top step, flame small at the third, flame gone at the water. So the strange rule of the vat is that the breathable place is the drowned place, and the drowned place is cooking you, and both conditions are perfectly ordinary chemistry.

Nothing lives in the water. Nothing. No fish, no fry, no snail on the plaster, no larva twitching at the surface film — too hot, too sulphurous, too new, and the gas keeps the mosquitoes off the top. It is sterile, which is precisely why it was built. Life has taken exactly two footholds, and both are outside the water: a black-green velvet of algae on the rim where condensation runs, thick only in the lit quarter and thinning to nothing in the far corners; and maidenhair fern in the mortar joints of the light shaft, four tufts, absurdly delicate, rooted in a building. One fig root has found a vault joint from the terrace above and come through it, pale and finger-thick, hanging a metre into the room, its tip white and drinking. That is the entire ecology. The algae grows because nothing eats it, dies because nothing eats it, and sloughs off in sheets that sink and gather in the south corners as grey floc — the only soil in the place, ankle-deep in drifts, stirred into fog whenever anyone goes down the steps.

---

The room is mostly emptiness. Four piers, three finished. A flight of steps. A sluice slab in its slot with a swollen wooden handle. A float-gauge, a notched rod riding a stone bracket, reading the level to a thumb's width. Below, scattered widely across the floor with metres of white nothing between them: a mason's mallet; a basket, waterlogged, collapsed on its side; two pine scaffold poles, bark sloughing, wood pickled pale and hard by the lime and in no hurry to rot; a coil of rope gone stiff as a branch; one iron wedge, the only iron in the room, bleeding a rust stain that the accretion is already sealing under glass. In a year the rope will be a stone rope. In five the mallet will be a lump with a mallet inside it. Nothing here decays. Things here get *included*.

The day has a shape. At around ten the shaft's beam lands on the second pier and the algae quarter blazes; by two the bar of light has walked across the floor to the steps; by four the net goes long and orange and the vault takes over the lighting, glowing warm and shadowless, and at dusk the room dims to a bright grey box with no visible source at all, and then, slowly, to nothing — but never to quiet. Under the vault a pocket of gas gathers over three days and lets go with a slow enormous gulp that you feel in the sternum. The sump at the south end fills and self-siphons about every forty minutes, a descending slurp and a hollow knock, the room's pulse. Convection off the throat makes the water shimmer in oil-streaks you can see against the white floor, hot rising, cool sliding down the walls, a slow overturning that never stops. And upslope, weeks after the rains, the spring swells, and the plume goes denser, and the weir runs harder, and the flour settles thicker, and the room grows its stone a little faster.

---

The masons do not call it a bath, though it was drawn like one, and it has the steps and the freeboard and the piers of one. They call it a cast-room. You build a shell, you flood it with the whitening water, you wait, and the water fills your shell with solid stone laid down grain by grain in the exact shape of the void you made — piers and all, so the block comes out with holes already in it, ready to be split along the lines you built into it. Then you break the shell and quarry your own room. The hill above is made of the shells.

So it is not a cistern and not a bath and not a ruin. It is a mould, currently full, working. It is a hidden bright roaring hot white box in which the water is finishing what the hands started, and the tools left on the floor are not litter but inclusions, waiting to be part of a wall somewhere, in a town that is built out of the rooms it filled.
