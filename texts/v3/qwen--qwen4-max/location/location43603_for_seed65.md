At that height the air is too thin to carry smell. Nothing reaches you that way — no rot, no green, no warning. Sight comes back doubled instead: the ground is white and the sky above it is nearly black, a bruised violet at the zenith that deepens the higher you look, and between them sits a sun that does not rise or set but travels around the horizon at a fixed height, like a lamp carried around the rim of a bowl. Shadows here sweep. They never lengthen. Nothing in this place has ever had a long shadow, and so nothing has ever had an evening.

The ground is a tableland of pale stone running some forty miles to a far escarpment you can see on a clear day, which is every day. It looks like a plain of bone. It is not bone, but it is closer to bone than to most rock: a hard thin rind over an interior of open cells, a frozen foam, gray-white and faintly translucent at any broken edge. A hand-sized piece of it weighs less than a plum. You cannot break one, though. The stone has already broken as far as it intends to.

And it is loud. The loudness arrives before the place does, before the rim, before the light changes — a roar with no source you can point at, no rise and fall, no direction. It comes up out of the ground.

---

The tableland is hollow, and it is perforated. Holes everywhere, from the width of a needle's eye to the width of a well, spaced with the regularity of something solved rather than arranged. They go down into the cellular rock and connect, branch, rejoin, and descend. The whole plateau is a single sponge of stone with a wind going through it, and it has been sounding for longer than anything has been listening.

The wind is not weather. There is no weather. The wind is the mountain's own circulation, and it runs at the same speed always, because it is driven by a loop that has no seasons in it. Water falls down the deep central shaft — the throat — and drags air after it. Air enters at the high holes near the rim, is pulled down through miles of wet cellular rock, and comes out at the foot of the escarpment warm and soaked. It rises up the cliff face, spills over the rim, and meets stone that is chilled by radiation into that black cold sky, and gives up its moisture in a film of dew on every surface, and the dew drains down through the pores and back into the throat, and falls again. Bright sun on the horizontal, absolute cold overhead: the difference between them is the engine. It has been running at one setting since the loop closed.

So the rock is cool and stays cool. The air is cool, the water is cool, your skin is cool; nothing here is ever warmer or colder than anything else, and there is no shivering and no sweating, only the slow discovery that your body is the only hot thing for forty miles and is spending itself to stay that way.

---

The sound is not chaos. That is the first thing you learn when you stop trying to shout over it.

Every hole has worn to a diameter and stopped. Wind widens a hole until its note falls out of step with the great standing tone of the whole body of stone, and then the widening ceases — the erosion is governed by the resonance, and the resonance is a solution, and the solution has been found. There is no dust here. All the fines blew out long ago and no new ones form, because nothing fractures anymore; the surface is a lag of coarse hollow beads, sorted, packed, at rest. The scree slopes lie at exactly the angle they intend to lie at. The tableland finished making itself before there were ears, and what you are standing on is not a landscape in progress. It is a finished instrument, still playing.

The playing is loud in the way that a hand on your chest is loud. It lives in the sinuses and the back teeth and the water of the eye. After an hour you stop hearing it and begin hearing your blood instead, which is worse.

But a standing wave has places where it cancels itself. Scattered across the white plain, far apart, are pockets of quiet — some the size of a bed, some the size of a room, one, they say, big enough to lie down in twice. You walk into one and the roar drops away like a floor giving out, and you can hear your own breath, and it is obscene. These are the only places on the tableland where anything can be heard by anything. There are perhaps two hundred of them in forty miles.

Everything alive here is arranged around that fact.

---

Green exists only in the quiet. Not because the plants prefer it, but because a cell wall will not hold together in the loud: the vibration tears the tissue as fast as it forms. So the vegetation is a scatter of low cushions and crustose lichen, each patch exactly the shape of a silence, edged as cleanly as if cut. Between them, nothing. Miles of ringing white with not one leaf on it. The cushions are dark green, almost black, wet to the touch, and they hold their dew all day because the day does not change.

Water at the surface is likewise rare and exact. There are no rivers. There are seams — hairline cracks where the water table comes up to meet the air, a hand's breadth wide and miles long, running across the plain like ruled lines. Along each seam the midges stand in columns so dense they read as smoke, a brown haze a foot high and a foot wide and eleven miles long, with bare stone on either side. And there is one cloud. It forms over the coldest seam and nowhere else, and it drizzles on the same nine paces of ground it has always drizzled on, and dissolves, and forms again, and the stones beneath it are the only rounded stones on the plateau, worn by a rain that has never once fallen anywhere else.

---

The animals are everywhere and you will not see them, because they are packed into the seams and the silences and the holes, and the volume between is empty. That is the arithmetic of the place: enormous life, enormous distance, almost nothing per handful of world.

Heron-sized birds live in the pipes. Ash-white, long-toed, with a black band across the eye and a third lid that draws across it against the glare. They are deaf. Everything born here is deaf; ears are a useless organ under a permanent roar, and these birds have converted them to something else. They speak by drumming the stone with a callused heel, and the signal goes through the rock, and a bird four miles off feels the message arrive in its feet.

They carry pebbles. A bird will select a bead of the lag, test it against the lip of its hole, and wedge it in, and the hole's note will change, and the change is audible for a hundred yards as a wrongness in the world's chord. That is the bird's name. That is also its boundary, its claim, and its argument: disputes are conducted by re-tuning, one wedge against another, until one note swallows the other. They probe the deep pores with broken spines. They scrape the algal film from a wet wall with a flake of rind they have struck loose with a held stone.

There are tools here and there are no artifacts. Every pebble is eventually pulled out and set on the lip; every flake is dropped and rolls and is sorted back into the lag by the wind; every bone goes down a hole and into the loop and is gone. Nothing stacks. Nothing shelters, because the rock already shelters. Nothing accumulates, and so there is no midden, no cairn, no ruin, no evidence — and a visitor who concluded that nothing had ever worked here would be looking at a place where work has been going on continuously, and being undone continuously, since before there was anyone to be wrong about it.

The grazers walk the quiet. Low, broad-footed, moss-coated, with translucent inner eyelids; they move from silence to silence across the bare ringing miles, and their route looks like wandering until you understand that they are stepping from one place where sound is possible to the next. They can hear only in the nodes. Everything they say to each other — courtship, warning, the low sounds they make over a calf — happens in a few scattered yards, and between one conversation and the next they walk for a day.

Above the stone, at a height of about a fathom, there is a layer you cannot see until the sun catches it: a shimmer of motes hanging motionless in the air, held there by the pressure of the standing wave. Spiders no bigger than a full stop. Legged seeds. A chaff of small lives that have never touched the ground and never will. Put your hand through and it closes behind your wrist. When they die they fall, and the holes take them.

And down in the throat, one pipe among the thousands sounds wet — a low note with a breath in it, a hesitation that the wind does not account for. Nothing has ever been seen there. It is simply a fact of the place, like the cloud.

---

Stand at the rim. Below you the cloud-floor lies flat and lit, and the world you came from is under it. Behind you the white plain runs to its far escarpment with no landmark on it but the small dark patches of green, spaced like the stops on a flute, marking the two hundred places where a sound can be heard.

The sun goes around. The dew forms. The water falls. The chord holds. The only events in this world are the small deliberate wrongnesses the birds put into it, and take out again — a note bent for a season, then released — and this is the closest thing here to history, and it is enough.
