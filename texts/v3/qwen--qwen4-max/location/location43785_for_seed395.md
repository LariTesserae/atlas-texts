## The Pan and the Lid

Put your hand on the ground at dawn. It is warm, and it is dry, and it stays dry. Nothing here sweats onto you. The salt takes the heat of your palm and gives nothing back.

This is the floor of a sea that left before anything now living had a mouth to notice it. It left as vapor and it never came down again, and the whole arrangement of the world since then has been an argument about that one departure. There is water — a great deal of water, more than a rainforest holds — but none of it is *out*. It is in trunks and bladders and guts and sealed conduits and the black rock four hundred meters down. It has not been outside, in the ordinary sense of rain or puddle or stream, in a length of time the salt itself has trouble remembering. The rule is simple and everything obeys it: water stays inside something living, or it is lost.

So the plain is white. Polygonal plates of halite and gypsum, ankle-breaking, sharp-edged, pushing up a millimeter a night where brine wicks from below and evaporates. The ground here is younger than the animals walking on it. It has not decided what it wants to be. There are no rounded hills, no mature valleys, no soil horizon — just crust over raw rock, a landscape still in its first draft.

And at the horizon, in every direction, a green wall.

## The Lid

Walk toward it for half a day and the wall resolves into a canopy, and the canopy does not end. It is not a forest edge; it is a lid. It closes over the basin from rim to rim, three thousand kilometers of continuous cover, and beneath it there is no sky at all, only the underside of leaves.

The leaves are not thin. Nothing here can afford thin. They are pads — thick, waxy, cool to the touch, each one a small reservoir with a skin like a fruit's. The trees that hold them are standing cisterns: *bladderwood*, the surveyors would call it, trunk swollen to the shape of a water tower, bark a hand's thickness of cork, no branch wasted on display. Cut one and it does not bleed, it *hemorrhages*, and within an hour there are forty animals drinking at the wound and a green riot of opportunists germinating in the mud, and by the following dusk the mud is gone and the tree is sealed with a plug of scar and the whole event has been eaten.

Beneath the bladderwoods: a second story of gasket vines, which grow specifically into joints and gaps and swell to fill them. Beneath those: valve mosses, which open in the cool and clamp shut in the heat. Beneath those: nothing. No bare ground, no leaf litter, no gap you could put a fist into. The understory is packed to the density of a body's interior, because every gap is a place where moisture could escape, and every escape is a small death that everyone downstream will feel.

The air under the lid is warm and thick and smells of cut cucumber and hot mineral and, faintly, of ammonia. It is breathable and easy — you are two hundred meters below a sea that no longer exists, and the column of air above you is heavier than it has any right to be. Light comes down ordinary and yellow, filtered green, in coins and bars that move across the floor as the pads above adjust their angles. Noon looks like noon. Dusk looks like dusk. There is nothing strange about the sun here at all; it is the same plain star it has always been, doing the same plain work, and the strangeness of this place is entirely a matter of bookkeeping.

## The City

It takes a while to understand that the forest is not a forest with structures in it. The structures *are* the forest, or the forest is the cladding on the structures; the distinction stops being useful about an hour in.

The mounds rise through the canopy at intervals of a few kilometers: plaster towers, gray-white, fluted, some of them three hundred meters tall, fused at their bases into continuous walls that run for days of walking. They are not built. Nothing here has a tool. They are *secreted* — chewed salt and rock flour and enzyme, laid down in mouthfuls by animals the size of dogs, blind, six-legged, patient, in numbers that make the word *colony* feel undersized. A single ward holds nine hundred million of them. The wards have floors. The floors have galleries. The galleries have traffic, and drainage, and a temperature gradient, and districts where the walls are older than other districts, and repairs of a different plaster color that let you read the history of a fire the way you'd read tree rings.

There are streets: smooth-bored tunnels twelve paces wide, worn glassy by feet, running arrow-straight between cisterns. There are tenements: chamber-stacks where the grazers sleep pressed flank to flank in the dark, warm as a barn. There are nurseries with a humidity you can feel on your teeth — the only place in the world where the air is genuinely damp, and it is damp because it is *sealed*, gasketed at every entrance, guarded by things whose whole job is to stand in the doorway and be a door.

And there is plumbing. This is the point of all of it. The city exists to move water without ever letting it be outside.

The veins run under the streets: living conduits, muscular, valved every few meters, pale as a peeled root, pulsing. Taproot organisms drive down through the salt into the black rock and pull fossil brine up through membranes that strip it clean, and the veins carry it — peristalsis, a slow squeeze you feel through your boot soles as a rhythm, one beat every eleven seconds — up into the cistern trees, out along the canopy, down into the wards, into the nurseries, into the crop-bladders of the carriers.

The carriers walk. That's the last leg. Water crosses the dry districts inside animals: long-legged, hump-shouldered, unhurried, moving in files along the streets, each one holding forty liters in a sac under the throat. At the transfer stations they meet the receiving stock and pass it mouth to mouth, and there is a caste of small fussy attendants whose entire life is spent licking the corners of those mouths, recovering the four or five milliliters that would otherwise be lost.

Nothing spills. In eleven thousand years of this arrangement, nothing spills, and when something does, it is the loudest event in the world.

## Quiet

You notice, after a day, that you have not heard anything.

Not silence as absence — silence as *engineering*. Every surface under the lid is soft: cork, wax, felt, plaster still slightly yielding, the packed bodies of things asleep. There are no hard faces to ring, no open spans for a call to cross, no water to slap or drip or roar. Feet here are padded, all of them, every lineage, hoofed grazers included, walking on a callus like a cat's paw. Wings are fringed. Jaws close on gaskets. The salt pan outside carries sound beautifully and there is nothing out there to make any.

What there is instead is drumming. The masons strike the floor with the flat of the head, and the message goes through the substrate — through plaster and root and vein and packed sleeping bodies — for kilometers, and you feel it in the arches of your feet before you understand it as information. Census. Alarm. *This ward is dry, send.* The whole city talks under the floor, in a register below hearing, and the air above the floor stays as quiet as a held breath.

Predators listen with their chins on the ground. There are long low hunters in the galleries, ear-less, with a jaw built like a seismograph, and they take carriers not for the meat but for the forty liters — a kill here is a drought, and a bad week of kills empties a district. So the carriers travel in tight files with escorts, and the escorts walk on the outsides, and everyone steps in the same rhythm, because a file that drums in unison sounds like a wall and a file that stumbles sounds like lunch.

Abundance, in a place like this, is not noisy. It is *crowded*. There is a grazer every three paces in the sleeping stacks. There are mites on the mites. There are filter-flyers in the upper canopy whose entire niche is catching the moisture of exhaled breath before it can drift to a gap — they fly with their mouths open, all day, and they are the reason the air stays dry. There are things that live only in the seam between a gasket vine and its host, and things that live only on those things, and a scavenger guild that works the outer pan where the dead are carried out to be dried rather than rotted, because rot requires water and water is not to be had. Out there the carcasses go to leather and stay leather for centuries, stacked in windrows, a dry museum ringing the city, and the salt keeps them, and the lickers work them over for the last of the bound moisture in their eyes.

## Weather Without Rain

The basin makes its own weather; it simply makes it out of the wrong materials.

Mornings: a thermal tide. The pan heats faster than the lid, and a wind comes off the salt at midmorning, dry as an oven's breath, carrying gypsum flour in a haze that turns the sun a flat white disc you can look at directly. It reaches the canopy and stops, because there is nowhere for it to go, and it piles up at the edge and rises, and the rising column carries spores — a brown wall two kilometers high, visible from a hundred kilometers out, moving inland at walking pace. You watch it come for a day and a half. When it passes overhead the light goes bronze for two hours and everything under the lid closes its valves and waits, and afterward the floor is dusted with the reproductive business of a district four days' walk away.

Evenings: the city exhales. All that metabolism, all that compost heat, all that carefully hoarded vapor finding the few permitted exits — the chimney wards, the flues, the great gasketed spiracles at the top of the oldest mounds. At dusk the plume goes up, and it is the only cloud in the sky, and it stands over the basin like a range of soft mountains, and it is warm, and it is full of life, and it disperses into the dry blue and is gone. It never rains. It has never rained. The vapor is intercepted aloft by the high fliers before it can cool to a drop.

And once or twice a season, dry lightning off the rim, and then fire — which is the true horror here, in a world of packed dry biomass with no water to spare for fighting it. The wards have mineral firebreaks and bladder-organs that burst and flood a chamber at the cost of a year's savings. There are seeds under the lid that will not germinate without scorch. There are beetles that arrive in the smoke.

## The Unfinished Ground

Because water never touches rock, rock never wears. There is no weathering here but chewing. No rivers, no rounded stone, no clay, no soil except what is manufactured deliberately inside guts and compost chambers and spread by hand-mouth on the growing floors. The landforms are embryonic — sharp, raw, provisional, salt still crystallizing upward through the crust each night like a slow frost.

Except where something has burst.

Every gully in this world is an accident. A cistern seam fails, or a vein is torn by a fall of plaster, or a carrier dies badly on the outer pan, and for a few hours water is *outside*, running, doing the thing water does. It cuts. It head-cuts a channel back into the salt, it drops a fan of sediment at its mouth, it starts — genuinely starts — to become a drainage.

And then it soaks in and is drunk and is gone, and the channel stops, half-born, a hundred paces long, a river that never got past its first sentence. The basin is stitched with tens of thousands of them. They are the only erosion this continent has ever known and each one is a wound with a story: this one is from the collapse of the Ninth Ward, that one from a bad decade of predation on the eastern carrier road. The masons come out and eat the mud. The salt begins to heal over. In a century there will be a faint white scar and nothing else.

## Night, and the Sound of a Leak

The temperature barely falls. The mass is alive and the mass is warm; the floor holds at blood heat until dawn, and you can lie down on it anywhere and sleep, and something will eventually lie down against you, because there is no room here for anything to sleep alone.

Above the lid, ordinary stars. Below it, no light at all, and the drumming shifts register — the night census, the long slow roll of *sending, sending, sending* moving west to east across three thousand kilometers as the water makes its year-long circuit from the deep pumps to the far rim and back through the bodies of a billion animals.

And then, somewhere, a sound.

It is enormous. It is the only enormous sound. A cistern wall lets go and forty tons of water hit dry air, and the noise goes out across the packed silent city like a struck bell in a room full of felt — and every animal under the lid stops, all at once, mid-step, mid-chew, mid-birth, and the drumming ceases, and for four seconds a continent listens to water falling.

Then everything moves toward it.
