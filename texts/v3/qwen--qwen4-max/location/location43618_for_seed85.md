## The Sill

The road gives out at three thousand four hundred metres, in a cirque of frost-shattered gneiss where nothing has ever grown. No lichen on the boulders. No moss in the seeps. The mountain here is above the line where life bothers, and it is all mineral: grey rock, black rock, the sugary glitter of fresh fracture, scree lying at the exact angle it fell.

Above the last switchback, the water stands.

It does not lie in a basin. It occupies one, which is different. Six hundred and forty paces across, a lens of it, held in the arm of the mountain with a meniscus you can see from below — a curved lip of surface tension a hand's-breadth thick, quivering with nothing, holding a shape it has held for four hundred and twelve years. There is no dam. There is no wall. There is a boundary called the Sill, and at the Sill the water simply stops being permitted. Walk at it and you pass through a skin that gives like the surface of a soap bubble and then closes behind you without a ripple, because ripples are a kind of change, and change is what the water has been told not to do.

They call the water the Standing. They call the town inside it the Room.

## Seven point four

The first thing is the cold, and the second thing is that the cold does not move.

Seven point four degrees, everywhere, to a tolerance the keepers can no longer measure because their instruments drift and the water does not. No thermocline. No warm layer at the top where the sun might have gotten in, no cold pool at the bottom. Swim down the Nave — the shaft that goes nine hundred metres into the mountain's root, past the drowned foundations of things that were old when the town was young — and your ears never pop. Pressure is uniform too. All that depth and none of the weight of it. The abyss without the crushing; the sea made into a room.

You breathe it. That is the part visitors cannot get past. The Standing enters the lungs like a cool hand and does what air used to do: oxygen arrives at the tissue that wants it, carbon dioxide leaves, and none of it travels. There is no diffusion in the Standing, no gradient to slide down. Consumption is answered locally, at the point of asking, by the Work — the engineering that holds all of this, which nobody now living understands and everybody now living trusts. You can hold perfectly still in the black water for an hour and not suffocate, not chill, not drift. Nothing accumulates around you. No boundary layer of your own spent breath. The water is not stale. It has never been fresh.

Buoyancy is cancelled as well. Drop a hammer and it stays at the height you dropped it, turning very slowly if you spun it, and it will still be there, at that height, when your grandchildren are dust.

## The Room

The town is small and it is dense and it is empty, all at once, and it takes a while to see how those fit together.

Eleven towers, dressed stone, sharp arrises, the drill marks still crisp in the blocks like the day they were cut. Four streets. A stair of three hundred and twelve steps called the Long Stair, going from the Ninth Landing down to the Chimney Quarter, and every step has a handhold worn — not worn; nothing wears here — every step has a handhold *shaped*, cut shallow by masons who were in a hurry four centuries ago. There are gutters. There are sluice gates, closed, with their mechanisms intact and their handles polished by hands. There are aqueducts that carry nothing, running from nowhere to nowhere, because the town was built for air and the water came afterward and the plumbing was never taken out, only extended. Every stone in the Room has a number. The numbers are older than the town.

That is the density: in the specification. Every surface is addressed, serviced, socketed, known. The matter itself is scattered — towers standing far apart in open black water, plazas of nothing forty paces wide, whole blocks that are only frames, ribs of stone with the sky-that-is-water between them. A city with the population of a hamlet and the infrastructure of a capital. Thirty-one people live here. You can swim the whole of it in a morning and see four of them.

No roofs are needed; there is no rain to fall. Doors exist anyway, for privacy of sound and light, and are simply holes with a lintel and a number, and a bolt on the inside that does nothing except mean something.

## The Wave

At the eastern edge, over the outer street, the flood is still arriving.

It came up the valley in a single hour, four hundred and twelve years ago, and the Work caught it at the instant of catching, and it has been at that instant ever since: a curling wall of green-black water nine metres high, the lip of it thrown forward, the underside hollowed into a barrel, gravel and a broken cart and a hundred thousand grains of sand suspended in the curl exactly where the violence put them. Sharp-edged. Glassy. You can swim into the barrel of the wave and lie there in the pocket where the air was, except there is no air, only more Standing, and look up at the tons of water that will not finish falling.

This is why everything here is new. Erosion is a conversation between motion and time, and one of the parties has been silenced. The scree inside the Sill is as angular as the day it split. There is no silt on any surface, because silt requires settling. Instead there is the Grey: hard-edged volumes of powder hanging where the flood left them, discrete as furniture. Fog with corners. You swim along a street in perfect clarity and then put your hand into a grey wall and your hand is gone; step across the boundary and you are in a room of blindness four metres wide, and step out the other side and the towers are there again, sharp as diagrams. One of the Grey is shaped like a collapsing gable. It has been shaped like a collapsing gable since before the ledger began.

No haze. Nothing here scatters. That is the whole of the darkness, and the whole of the light.

## Threads

Light does not spread in the Standing. Scattering is a gradient, and gradients are forbidden, so a beam stays the width of the thing that made it, forever, until it hits stone. There is no glow. There is no ambient. There are forty lamps in the Room, cold blue-white beads at doorways and at the turnings of the Long Stair, and each one throws a thread you could step across without knowing it was there. A lit doorway is a lit doorway and one metre away is absolute black. Shadows are not dark grey; they are the absence of the subject.

You learn to swim with a hand out. You learn to find a lamp-thread and put your face in it and see, suddenly, a whole wall of numbered stone, and then take your face out and see nothing. The keepers navigate by count and by rail: iron rails, bolted along every street at hip height, continuous, the one piece of infrastructure the current generations have added everywhere. And at intervals along the rails, rings.

The rings are for talking.

## What is held

Sound does not decay here. It is held where it was made, standing, spread thin across the volume it filled, so that the Room's four centuries of noise sum to a single note at the very floor of hearing — a hum you stop noticing in an hour and then hear for the rest of your life. They call it the Under.

But locally, sound has geography. There is a sphere four metres wide at the mouth of the tower where the founding bell hangs, and if you put your head into it you hear the bell, struck once, at full voice, four hundred and twelve years ago; take your head out and there is the Under again. There is a stairwell in the Chimney Quarter where a hammer fell in the year eighty and the clink is still in the third step. There are pockets of held speech all over the Room, most of them worn so thin by spreading that they are only a texture, a sense of having interrupted someone.

Speak while swimming and your words stay behind you like a shed skin. So conversation is done standing still, elbow hooked through a ring, face to face in the black. The rings are placed where the useful conversations happen. There are rings at every landing, at the sockets where food appears, at the four doors where people visit. There is a ring, unnumbered, at the top of the Long Stair, that nobody uses.

And motion is held too. This is the thing that makes the Room unlike anywhere else wet. Swim a length of a street and your wake does not dissipate; the momentum goes into the water and stays in the shape you left it. The streets are full of four centuries of gesture — invisible, silent, hard-edged laminar scars, tangled vortices, the shove of a shoulder that turned a corner in the year two hundred. Swim fast down Ledger Street and you will be pushed, twice, gently, by hands that are not there. The keepers maintain a ledger matching wakes to names, as far as it can be reconstructed, and the matching is the main work of their lives, and it is not close to finished. Children learn to swim through their great-grandmothers. There is a corridor near the eastern wave that everyone goes around: the wake of a body being carried out.

Exhaled breath hangs in strings of silver beads. In a closed room off the Ninth Landing there are beads from the last conversation held in it, and yes, you can breathe them again, and yes, people do.

## The varnish and the nine

Nothing green has ever been here. No algae, no kelp, nothing that eats light, because light is a leak and the Work keeps it rationed. What there is instead is a varnish: a film on the stone, iron-grey, thinner than a fingernail, that eats the mountain's slow chemistry. The assayers who come up the road once a generation cannot agree whether it is alive. It replaces itself exactly. Same thickness, same pattern, cell for cell, forever — a thing maintaining itself without ever becoming anything else.

Nine grazers crop it. Blind, pale, the size of a loaf, mouths of cilia, one to a plaza, moving a body-length a day along routes that close. They do not grow. Once in a long while one dies and there is a small one, and the count is nine again. Three couriers swim the streets — long, ribbon-bodied, purposeful, going nowhere in particular — and the keepers read their routes the way other towns read weather, and argue about what the routes mean, and write it down. Whether the couriers are animals or the Work's own limbs is another question the ledger holds open.

## The asking

Nobody built the Work. That is, somebody did, and the town knows the word for them, and the word is not a name but a job title, and the job has been vacant for a very long time. Maintenance is ritual: move a numbered stone to a numbered socket, put a palm to a plate, wait. Once a day a bead of tasteless grey food appears at the socket by the ring. Once a year the lamps are re-asked. The sluice gates are oiled, though they will never open, because a town is a set of habits that outlive their reasons.

Thirty-one people. Four hundred and twelve years of water that has not moved, not warmed, not cooled, not worn, not settled, not forgotten. They are born, they change, they die, and everything they do to the water stays exactly where they did it. The mountain outside the Sill is going to dust in the ordinary way. The one in here keeps its edges.

You will leave a wake. It will be there when the road is gone.
