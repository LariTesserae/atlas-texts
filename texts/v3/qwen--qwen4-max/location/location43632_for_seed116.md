**The Head**

Above six thousand meters the mountain stops pretending to be a place. Ice like poured glass, a wind that has no weather in it anymore, only speed; sky gone violet at the zenith; the horizon bent slightly, the way it is when you are standing on almost nothing. There is no living thing up here. There is one warm thing, and it is buried, and you would walk over it twice before you found it: a ring of brass the width of a dinner plate, set flush in the ice, its rim worn to a soft lip, its hand-wheel long since eroded into a smooth mushroom you must grip with both palms and turn like opening a jar.

Inside the lock, the cold lets go of you all at once.

The water is thirty-one degrees. Blood heat. It smells of copper and hot ceramic and something greener underneath, like a greenhouse floor. Your ears complain and then agree. And when the inner hatch swings — it does not creak; nothing here creaks, everything here has been sanded — you look into a lit, warm, flooded room eleven paces across, at the top of the world.

---

It is a pressure vessel. A dome, ribbed inside like the roof of a mouth, ceramic-lined over steel over some older steel, and there is not one square centimeter of natural substrate anywhere in it. No stone. No clay. Nothing here has ever touched the mountain it sits inside; the lining holds the rock off the way a skull holds off the weather. The floor's dunes are fines — bearing metal ground to flour, ceramic dust, the shells of things that died — and they are laminated, fine as book pages, four hundred thousand laminae deep, and each lamina is a season of pumping.

The vessel was made. Everything in it was made, or descends from something made. This is the part that takes longest to understand.

But it is not a ruin. Say that plainly and then keep saying it, because every instinct will argue. The pumps are running. The pumps have been running since before the ice, since before the language on the maintenance plate, since before there was anyone to be late for a shift. The room is not derelict. It is *finished*.

Erosion here has not been decay. It has been a very long sculptor working toward a final shape and then putting down the tools. Every thread is gone; bolt heads are hemispheres; the ladder rungs are curves that fit a hand better than the hand's own maker could have designed. The impeller blades in the throat of the inflow have been worn to precisely the profile the water wants, and so they no longer wear. The corrosion has reached equilibrium and stopped thickening. The whole chamber has arrived at the form that flow prefers, and having arrived, it has stopped changing, and having stopped changing, it will not stop. It runs because it has become the shape of running.

---

The water arrives from below.

One riser, a meter and a half in bore, comes up through the center of the floor out of four kilometers of pipe. Every ninety-one seconds it delivers a stroke: a slow gray column shouldering up into the room, spreading, turning over on itself, sighing outward. Then a long slack interval while the ram-pumps at the mountain's foot gather themselves. Then again. Ninety-one seconds is the room's pulse, its tide, its unit of time; everything alive here counts in it.

The pumps are driven by the mountain's own melt. Water falls down the far flank, and the fall is used to lift a fraction of the water back to the summit, and the summit gives it back. The mountain has been circulating its own meltwater through itself for an epoch. This is the hydrology. There is no river here that is not also a pipe.

And the room does not overflow through any outlet. It *weeps*. The dome's skin, after all this time, is porous — a spill-lattice of a million sub-millimeter channels, some original design, most of them accidents that have been polished into intention. Water leaves through the whole ceiling and walls at once, in a hiss you feel in your teeth, and runs down under the ice, and freezes into a shell around the vessel, and that shell is the insulation that keeps the room warm, and in summer it melts and goes down to the intake and comes back up. The mountain wears a coat made of its own leakage. The heat is friction: four kilometers of water being persuaded, over and over, to climb.

---

It is loud. Not violently — evenly, hugely, the way a mill is loud. The stroke is a note near thirty hertz that you hear with your sternum rather than your ears. The weeping is a continuous white hiss. Somewhere in the throat, cavitation bubbles collapse in strings of tiny snaps, like a forest of very small fires. And under all of it, a frying sound: the grazing of ten thousand small mouths on ten thousand square meters of leaf.

But the dome is a bell, and a bell has nodes.

The standing wave in here has a fixed geometry — quiet pockets, unbearable seams. You can find them with your body: swim a meter to the left and the pressure stops hammering your sinuses; swim back and it resumes. The emptiness of this place is an acoustic map. Almost nothing lives in the antinodes. Almost everything lives in the nodes, and the nodes are far apart.

That is why the room feels sparse while being full. There are four great plants and perhaps forty thousand animals and a predator, in nine hundred cubic meters of water, and none of them are ever near each other. Each holds a station, a private volume, a quiet cell. It is a crowded world with no crowds in it. You can hang in the middle of the dome in blue-lit open water and see nothing within four meters in any direction, and know that the room is teeming.

---

The plants sit at the antinodes, because they like being shaken.

They were filter stock — engineered scrubbers, bred to strip copper and arsenic and biocide out of coolant, feather-boa fronds folded into a body the size of a chair, a hectare of surface packed into a fist. They are bolted, still, into stations on the old grid, and their roots have eaten through the gaskets and into the cable runs, and they are extravagantly, absurdly lush: fronds two meters long, translucent green with a copper sheen, trembling constantly in the loud water, sifting it. The vibration shakes their filters clean. They farm bacteria in the folds. They exhale metal in bright beads that fall to the floor and become the floor.

Between the four of them: meters of nothing. Bare water, a dune, the riser's mouth, the long slow turn of the stroke.

Their seeds do not float. They hook, and drop, and crawl a little, and mostly die where they fall, because there is nowhere to go — the outflow is a million pores, each smaller than a seed, and anything that escapes into them freezes on the mountain's flank within the hour. Nine hundred thousand generations of selection for staying. Everything alive in this room is descended from the individuals that failed to leave.

---

The grazers are called combers, when they are called anything: thumbnail-sized, translucent, blue-blooded, with a rake of setae where a face should be. Their blood is copper-based, and the copper is from the wiring; they are blue because the cables are. They hold their lattice by mutual avoidance, a hand's width apart, ten thousand of them scattered through the volume like a held breath.

The long thing hunts by pulse. Three meters of ribbon muscle, blind, pale, hanging motionless in a node near the ceiling for hours — and then the stroke comes, and the whole room's pressure signature scrambles, and it moves inside that ninety-one-second window of noise, and at the end of the stroke there is one fewer comber and the long thing is hanging somewhere else.

Most of the food, though, comes up the pipe. Every stroke delivers a snow of immigrants: larvae, eggs, spores, fry, blind lowland things lifted four kilometers in the dark and dumped stunned into warm light. They spiral out of the riser's mouth in a slow plume and are eaten within seconds — by the combers, by the long thing, by the fronds themselves, which are not entirely plants and never were. The room's whole ecology is a mouth at the top of an elevator.

And two guilds pay rent.

There are limpets on the ceiling lens — a meter of fused silica at the dome's crown, the original pressure port, still admitting the fierce high-altitude sun and diffusing it into an even, ordinary, kitchen-at-ten-in-the-morning light. The lens should have frosted opaque centuries ago. The limpets lick it. They have licked it clean every day for longer than there have been days to count, and if they stopped, the room would go dark inside a human lifetime, and everything in it would follow.

And in the pores there are scrapers, small shelled things, working the spill-lattice channel by channel, keeping the million mouths open. If they stopped, the room would pressurize and find a seam, and empty down the mountain in an afternoon.

The infection maintains the machine. That is the arrangement. Nobody drew it up.

---

Every nineteen hours the intake screen at the mountain's foot backwashes, and the room fills with a brown fog of lowland silt and a flood of immigrants ten times the usual, and the combers gorge, and the long thing hunts twice in a cycle, and the fronds fold half-shut and shed their clogged outer leaves, and the shed leaves rot into the laminae, one more page.

In winter the melt fails and the pulse slows to every four minutes. The room cools to twenty-four. The plants drop three-quarters of their fronds. The combers go down into the fines and lie in the sediment in their thousands, and the long thing eats the ones that lie too shallow, and the light through the lens goes thin and slanted and stays that way for months. Then the mountain begins to melt again, and the tempo picks up, and the whole room green-white explodes back into leaf in about nine days.

---

The rules here are few and absolute. Nothing leaves. Everything is made. Warmth is a byproduct of the mountain drinking itself. Emptiness is a shape drawn by sound. Light is an animal's chore. Time is ninety-one seconds long, and four hundred thousand pages deep.

And it is finished — that is the last thing, and the strangest. This is not a young place getting older. It is a place that has already got as old as it can get and has settled into the shape it will keep. The mountain is going down at a millimeter a century; the head will shorten; the pulse will slow and slow; the ice will retreat and the coat will thin and the room will still be warm, still lit, still licking its own lens clean, sitting lower and lower on a flattening world, a bowl of blue water eleven paces across with four plants in it and a blind ribbon hanging in the quiet, at the summit of a hill, at the summit of a rise, at the center of a plain, hidden in the grass, running.
