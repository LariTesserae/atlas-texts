## The Kettle

You come to it from above, because there is no other way in. The land around is ordinary — wet fields, a road, a low wall of grey brick with a gate in it that has no lock, only a counterweight. Beyond the gate the ground stops. What you took for a pond is a lid.

It is a raft of leaves, forty hands thick, knit root to root, floating on water that fills a bowl of masonry a little under three hundred paces across. You can walk out on it if you know where the stiff places are. It gives under you like turf over a bog, and where it gives you see down: a green dusk, and under the dusk, geometry. Roofs. A chimney. The regular ribs of a sawtooth rooflight, glazed, glowing faintly from beneath.

This is a ward of a city, eleven buildings and a plaza and a works, and it is eighty feet under the surface of its own warm water, and it is still running.

---

### The lid and the fall

The raft is the sky here, and it is a ceiling. Light comes through it the colour of a bottle held up to a candle — a green so dark it is nearly brown — and it does not so much fall as *seep*, arriving already old. Below thirty feet there is no useful daylight at all. What there is instead is amber.

Through the raft, constantly, comes the fall: shredded leaf, spent root, the husks of the mat's own undersides, drifting down in flakes and threads. It is the weather. It has been falling at the same rate for a very long time. It gathers on every horizontal surface, and every horizontal surface here has been designed, or has learned, to shed it — pitched, rounded, ribbed, or fitted with a slow licking arm that sweeps once an hour and drops the sweepings into a hopper. Nothing is flat by accident. The one flat place is the plaza, and the plaza is a different problem, dealt with later.

The water is blood-warm. Not hot-spring hot; the specific, insistent warmth of a body, thirty-seven degrees, and it does not vary by more than a degree between the top and the bottom or between one year and the next. It is soft — flat, tasteless, faintly sweet, stripped of every mineral by a century of being boiled and condensed and boiled again. This is the first rule of the Kettle, and everything else follows from it: **the water is empty, so it takes.** It does not rust the iron. It does not short the bare copper busbars that run in bright lines along every wall, unwrapped, unhoused, trailing weed. It does not scale the pipes. It will, however, pull the salt out of you through your skin and the calcium out of your teeth over a long enough visit, and it will not let you cool, because you are the same temperature as the world. People who work here — and someone works here — go up every few hours to shiver in the wind.

### The Rows

The streets silted up in the first decade. Everything the fall brought down went into the gaps between the buildings and stayed, and compacted, and now the ground floors are a solid grey-brown plug, load-bearing, harder than the brick they fill. The buildings stand on their own sediment. You enter the tenements at what used to be the second storey, through windows whose sashes were removed and whose openings were fitted, neatly, with grilles.

Inside, the rooms are packed. Not cluttered — *packed*, in the way a cellar is packed: pipe against pipe against pipe, each one lagged in a felt of pale root that has grown to exactly the thickness of the gap and no thicker. There is no space between things anywhere in the Kettle. Where a gap opened, something filled it; where nothing could fill it, a pipe was run through it; where a pipe already ran, the roots braided along it in a sleeve so tight you cannot see the metal, only the shape of the metal, like a body under a sheet.

The rows of tenements are eleven bays long and they are all one organism now. Their stairwells are penstocks. Their chimneys are risers. On the landing of number six there is a nest of glass tubing where a coal scuttle used to be, and in the tubing a slow amber fluid climbs a quarter-inch an hour and falls back, and this is a gauge, and it is measuring something, and it has been measuring it correctly since before anyone alive was born.

### The Vat Hall

At the north end, where the bowl is deepest, is the works, and the works is a cathedral.

Not metaphorically. It was built as a machine hall in the age that liked to build machine halls as cathedrals: a nave of iron columns, a clerestory, a barrel of glazed ribs a hundred feet over the floor. The glazing is intact. Water presses on it from both sides equally and has done for generations, so it holds. Inside, the twelve vats stand in two rows of six like a double choir, each one forty feet tall, copper-skinned, furred over in a pelt of white-green filament that stirs when you pass.

They are fermenting. That is where the warmth comes from — not a furnace, not a reactor, just the patient heat of a hundred thousand gallons of mashed leaf being eaten by something microscopic and unhurried. The vats breathe once every eleven hours: a shudder, a soft enormous *gulp* that you feel in the bones of your face rather than hear, and a single bubble the size of a cart rises through the water outside and goes up to the raft and is caught there and piped back down. The gas turns a turbine. The turbine turns a generator. The generator lights the lamps. The lamps grow the leaf. The leaf feeds the vats.

There is no fuel arriving. There has been no fuel arriving for a hundred and forty years. The Kettle runs on itself, at a loss so small that the loss is measured in the thickness of a bearing shell per decade, and it makes up the loss by making bearings.

### The lamps and the plaza

The product is lamps.

This is written in the ledger in the Assay Office, in a hand that began as copperplate and has become, over the pages, something more regular than any hand: *BULBS, ARC & FILAMENT, ASSORTED — 4,000/WEEK.* The Lamp Works on the east side still draws its own glass, still spins its own filaments from wire it draws itself, still evacuates and seals and stamps and packs. The lamps go where lamps are needed: into the sockets over the growing terraces on the roofs, into the vat hall clerestory, into the sodium-orange strings that light the streets so that from the raft, at the dim hour, you can see the whole plan of the ward glowing up through the green like a circuit diagram.

Four thousand a week is far more than are needed. The surplus goes on the conveyor to the loading dock at the south gate, and the loading dock has no lorry, and never will, and the crates go off the end of it into the plaza.

The plaza is full of glass.

A hundred and forty years of bulbs, packed shoulder to shoulder, filling the square from the dock to the fountain, mounded twelve feet deep at the centre, and every one of them intact or nearly. Current moves them a little. They roll and settle and click — the only sharp sound in the whole Kettle, a small dry chinking, heard through the water as a kind of distant hail, once or twice an hour. At the bottom of the mound the oldest ones have gone to sand under their own weight, and the sand is dredged by a slow scoop, and the scoop feeds the glass furnace, and the furnace makes the bulbs. Even the waste has closed its loop. The place has no exit and requires none. It has weathered into its own shape and stopped moving; the rust stopped, the rot stopped, the collapse stopped, everything held at the exact tension where nothing further happens.

Stand in the plaza and the amber street-lamps come through the glass from a thousand directions and there is no shadow anywhere, only a warm granular brown light with no source, like being inside a lit honeycomb.

### The green

The vegetation is lush and it is *shaped*. Not by gardeners. By the harvest arms.

They come out of the works on rails, one circuit per day, and they cut what they can reach, and what they cannot reach grows. Over the generations the plants have become the exact negative of the machines' motion: espaliers of cable-vine flattened into fans where the arm sweeps, thick tufted heads only in the corners the arm cannot enter, a species of broad ribbon-leaf that has learned to lie down. There are hedges here with the profile of a gear-tooth. There is a moss that grows only in the shadow of a counterweight and has taken its shape. Nothing is wild and nothing is tended; the whole green mass is a fossil of a movement, still moving.

It fills every space the buildings leave. The terraces on the roofs are four feet deep in it. The water itself has a grain — a suspension of cells and threads so thick that a beam of lamp-light stops at fifteen feet and your outstretched hand goes soft-edged at arm's length. Swimming here is like walking through a crowd. You are always touching something.

---

### The tenants

Almost nothing lives here that has a face.

There are three carp in the flooded stairwell of the Rows, pale, unhurried, enormous, known individually and numbered by the ledger as assets. There is an eel in the number-two penstock, called the Tenant, who has been there long enough that the maintenance arm has been re-routed around her. There is one blind newt-lineage in the silt plug, glimpsed perhaps twice a year. In the air bells trapped under the vat hall's upper galleries — six pockets of gas, breathable, warm, smelling of yeast and hot glass — there are midges, and one species of small white spider that has never been anywhere else in the world.

That is nearly all. The water is too soft, too warm, too full of green and too empty of everything else. Life here specialised into plants and machinery and left the animals behind.

---

### The clock

The great flywheel in the Screw House makes one revolution in eleven minutes. You can watch it and believe it is still. Everything else is slower: the pump cycle is thirty-one hours, the decanting is annual, the raft thickens a finger's width a year and is cropped from below by an arm that runs in spring. The lamps come up over four hours and go down over four, so the Kettle has dawn and dusk but no noon and no night.

It is quiet. Water swallows the clatter, oil swallows the rest, and what is left is a hum below hearing that you feel in your sternum, a tick from a relay somewhere in the Assay Office every ninety seconds, the chinking glass, the eleven-hour gulp. Silence with a pulse in it.

And in the Office, the printer still logs. Tonnage, temperature, lumens, output. Every week, four thousand bulbs, in a hand that no longer shakes. Nobody has signed the bottom of a page in a very long time, but the pages are all initialled, small and identical, in the box where the initials go.

The gate has no lock. It has a counterweight, and the counterweight is worn smooth in the shape of the hands that lifted it, and it is the only thing in the Kettle that has been eroded by anything other than water.
