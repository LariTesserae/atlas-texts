The water is blood-warm and it does not end anywhere you can find. It is warm the way a body is warm, so that after a while you lose the boundary of yourself and cannot say whether the warmth is coming out of the rock or out of you. There is no current to speak of. There is a lift, a slow upwelling somewhere near the middle of the floor, and the whole volume turns over about once a season, and that is all the motion this place has ever needed.

You learn it by touch, because there is nothing to see with. Your hand goes out and finds a wall, and the wall is coursed stone — blocks the size of a curled-up person, laid in level courses, the joints so tight that the mortar in them is a hair's width and no more. Nothing here is natural. Not the wall, not the floor under your feet, not the mountain the wall belongs to. The mountain is masonry too. It is filled chambers, hundreds of them, chamber packed against chamber and grouted solid, a massif of made stone worn round on the outside by weather until it is a stump, a knuckle, a thing you would call a hill if you saw it from the valley. Only one void was left open. This one. A hundred paces at its widest, sixty at the narrow, the crown forty paces overhead, and you can walk the whole perimeter in the time it takes one bubble to climb from floor to ceiling.

Everything else is solid. The world here is almost entirely stone, and this room is the single breath held inside it.

---

The floor is not a floor. It is a slope of shells and lime flour, knee-deep, so fine that it moves like liquid when you disturb it and takes an hour to settle. Under the shells there are more shells, laminated, page on page, each layer a generation, each generation identical to the last. The makers laid a pavement here once. It is somewhere below, a fathom down through the midden, and no one has touched it in an age.

At the center, where the lift comes from, there is a throat: a shaft lined with fitted stone, hand-cut, going down into the hot rock farther than anyone has ever measured. Water comes up it at about the rate of a resting breath. It arrives warm and full of dissolved stone and the rock's own sour gas, and because the air above the water is thin — this cup sits high, high enough that the sky over it is the hard cold sky of altitude — the water cannot hold its gas. So it lets go of it, everywhere, continually. Every surface in the chamber is furred with small beads of air that grow over hours, swell, detach, and go up. The walls seem to sweat upward. That is the whole weather of this place.

The water is not clear. It is faintly milky with carbonate, and much of the milk is alive, and between the milk and the lid of stone there is no light to speak of.

Except the seams.

The mortar was tempered with something organic — hair, blood, the boiled-down scraps of the making — and in the joints, where the chemistry is slightly different, a film grows that gives off a very faint blue-green light. Not enough to see your hand by. Enough to see the *grid*. In the dark, the room resolves as a lattice: course after course of dim glowing lines, the horizontals dead level, the verticals staggered, going up and up and curving in at the crown, and every niche and corbel and rib picked out in the same cold thread of light. You cannot see the stone. You can see the shape of the intention. The whole architecture is legible as a diagram of itself, drawn in the joints by something that eats lime and does not know it is writing.

---

Nothing here was ever a leaf. There is no green, no wood, no cellulose anywhere in the world; the makers built in stone and clay and shell-lime and bone and a little soft copper, and the animals that live here eat the rock's breath, first hand or second hand. On every surface lies a nap of bacterial felt, warm grey, and it is exactly one hair thick everywhere, because it is grazed exactly as fast as it grows.

The grazers are the pavement. Limpets and small coiled snails, pale as boiled bone, millions of them, packed shell to shell up the walls and across the underside of the crown, each one working a patch it will work for its whole life. Their lives are long. A snail on the north wall may be older than the last spoken word in this room. They rasps are so soft and so many that they sum to a sound like the room thinking.

Above them, in the niches — and there are thousands of niches, hand-cut, ranked, a dense honeycomb of small made hollows that once held lamps or offerings or nothing at all — stand the feather-cups: stalked animals that open fans of mucus thread and comb the milk. They are near-touching. The wall is a solid organ of them, tier over tier, and when one withdraws, its neighbor's threads slide into the space for a while.

Hanging in the warm plume are the fish, blind, white, small, in shoals so loose and so still that they seem less like animals than a condition of the water. They do not hunt. They hold position in the lift and let the food come up to them, and they move only to make room. And through everything, in clouds, the shrimp: the reason the water is thick, the reason your outstretched hand keeps brushing soft nothings.

On the floor, the hermit things wear the makers' leavings. Thimbles. Beads. The copper caps of awls, gone green. There are more artifacts in the shells of animals here than there are anywhere else in the world, and they are being carried slowly around the room, at a snail's pace, forever.

And in the joints there are the crabs. They take the lime into their crops and work it and put it back into the seams, pointing the mortar, packing the hairline gaps where the water has begun to eat. Nobody taught them. Something in the making of this place selected for it, and then the makers went away, and the maintenance went feral and kept going.

---

The crown is carved with inverted cups. Thousands of them, shallow bowls cut into the underside of the vault, each holding a trapped pocket of air. They were made for some reason of construction — to key the plaster, to lighten the load, to tune the room, nobody knows. Now they are its lungs.

Air is scarce up here and the water is hungry for it, and a pocket left alone would dissolve away in a century. But the ferrymen tend them: small pale things that gather the beads sweating off the walls, carry them up as a single silver bead held against the underside of the body, and release them into the cups. In exchange they graze the rich film that grows at the air-water interface, in the little domed ceiling of each pocket. So the air in this room is ancient and continually renewed, and it is *held*, held in ten thousand small stone hands, and if you need to breathe you put your face up into one of them and take a mouthful of something that has been passed along, mouth to mouth to mouth, since before the walls were smooth.

---

It is very quiet, and the quiet is enforced.

The room is a bell. Stone all around, no soft thing anywhere but the animals, and the water carries sound beautifully and the vault returns it. A struck note here does not die; it thins, and thins, and is still faintly present a quarter of an hour later. Any animal that made a noise would be adding it to a room that keeps everything. So nothing makes noise. The grazers rasp below the threshold. The fish do not stridulate. The crabs set their lime without a click. What you hear is the breath of the inlet, the occasional tick of a bead detaching, the far-off tick of another, and beneath all of it a groan too low to hear, twice a day, when the mass of the mountain flexes in the earth's tide and the water level rises and falls by the width of a thumb.

That thumb's-width is the only clock. Everything here is timed to it: spawning, grazing, the ferrymen's rounds. A drop of water stays in this room about eleven years before it finds the overflow lip and goes down the spillway shaft and out, and emerges somewhere far below on the flank of the stump as a warm spring that nobody drinks.

---

The walls have no edges left. Water has been at them so long that every angle has become the shape water likes: fluted, scalloped, rounded, the courses sagging into each other like something poured. The niches have lost their lips. The ribs are nubs. Where the water dissolves, the crabs replace; where the crabs replace, the water dissolves; the two rates found each other a long time ago and locked. The chamber is neither filling nor emptying, neither growing nor wearing. It has arrived at its last shape. It could hold this shape until the stump above weathers down to the crown, which is a length of time that has no use for numbers.

Only in the lee — under a lip, inside the sheltered underside of a corbel, wherever the slow turn of the water does not reach — is anything still sharp. There you can find a chisel stroke. There you can find a thumbprint in the mortar, the whorls intact, pressed by something that had hands and is not here and left no bone, because bone does not last in warm water full of dissolved stone.

There is one more thing. At the crown, off-center, a hand-span hole was left open to the sky, and once a day, when the sun crosses the summit, a coin of light comes down through the milk and moves across the shell-floor. It lasts a few minutes and is very dim and it is the only light that has ever entered this room from outside.

Most of the animals have no eyes. A few keep one, a single dark rudiment pointed straight up, and they are scattered all through the volume, motionless, and at the appointed hour they gather — slowly, from every quarter, over an hour or more — into a loose pale congregation beneath the hole, and hang there in the coin of light, and do not feed, and do not touch, and afterward disperse to where they were.

Then the room goes back to its seams, glowing faintly in the dark, drawing itself, over and over, for no one.
