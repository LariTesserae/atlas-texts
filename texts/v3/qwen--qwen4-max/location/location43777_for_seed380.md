## Nine

The way in is a stair that used to be a crack. Ninety-one steps, cut when the survey crews still believed in the project, worn now into shallow bowls that hold a finger's depth of water each. You come down with your hand on a rail of black iron that has not rusted in ninety years, and the air changes at about step forty: it stops being outside air and becomes the chamber's own, eleven degrees, saturated, tasting faintly of wet chalk and cold tea. Behind you the daylight narrows to a slot, then a keyhole, then a gray coin the size of a thumbnail. That coin is the only sun Nine ever gets, and it arrives secondhand, filtered through a lattice of dead bramble and a hundred years of root.

Then the stair turns, and the room opens, and you understand that this is not a cavern. It is a room. You can see the far wall. You can hear someone set down a cup on the far side and know who it was.

---

The chamber is roughly oval, four hundred paces at its long axis, a hundred and ten at the waist, and the ceiling arches overhead like the inside of a cupped hand — high enough to be lost in the dim, low enough that in the wet season the fog up there condenses and comes back down as weather. It rains indoors, gently, all the time, in specific places. The drip-lines are known and named. Children are taught them before they are taught the stairs.

Nine is a town of about sixty people in a space that could hold six thousand. Streets run in terraces around the wall, three levels of them, cut and pinned and buttressed, with iron staircases at the four compass points. There are workshops, a pump-house, a condenser hall, a bathhouse, a wire-store, a mess with twelve tables and two of them used. There are doors standing open onto rooms with nothing in them but a chair and a hook. It is not derelict. Everything is oiled. The lamps are lit and trimmed, the gutters are clear, the brass gauge in the middle of the floor is polished to a soft yellow shine — and there is simply nobody in most of it, most of the time. You can walk the whole upper terrace and meet one person, and they will nod, and go back to what they were doing, which will take them until evening.

There were meant to be twelve chambers. There are eleven other holes in the world with iron in them.

---

The water is not moving in any way you would call moving. It weeps. The north wall sweats in a continuous sheet a millimeter thick, so slow it looks like polish, and the flowstone below it has grown into a series of overlapping lips and basins, each brimming, each spilling into the next with a sound like someone very carefully not speaking. The south wall is drier and hung with curtain-folds of calcite the color of skimmed milk, and where the curtains are thin the lamps behind them glow through like a hand over a candle.

Every surface is rounded. There is no rubble anywhere in Nine, no sharp fracture, no fresh break, because stone here does not fall — it *thins*. A wall loses a hair's breadth a decade and gains it back somewhere else. The water finished its violent work before anyone came down the crack; what it does now it does at the pace of a fingernail. The town has settled into that pace. Buildings are iron frames with sacrificial lime skins, troweled on, dissolved away, troweled on again; the mason here is the water, and the mason's assistants merely keep up. There are no ruins in Nine and there never will be. There will only be, eventually, iron standing in a smooth empty room, and then the iron too.

The floor is a shallow dish. In the middle of it stands the gauge: a brass staff, graduated in fingers, sunk into a socket that predates the town. Someone reads it every morning and writes the number in a book. The book goes back four generations. The water rises for seven months and falls for five, a single breath a year, two and a half meters from lip to lip, and the whole settlement is arranged around that breath. In low water the lower terrace is dry and the stringing floor is open and the eels are gone. In high water the lower terrace is a knee-deep lagoon you cross by punt, the workshops move up a level, and everyone spends five months living one flight closer to their neighbors, which is the closest thing Nine has to a festival.

Nothing here happens quickly. Nothing here has ever needed to.

---

The engine is the reason there is a town at all, and it is not an impressive machine.

There is no head of water, no dam, no fall — only seep, which has no force. So the pump-house harvests time instead. Along the north wall, under the weeping sheet, hang nine hundred iron buckets on an endless chain. Each fills over about forty minutes. The chain crawls. The main shaft completes one revolution in an hour and eleven minutes, and you can stand and watch the keyway and be certain it is not turning, and come back at dinner and find it a quarter around. That shaft drives a dynamo the size of a barrel, which charges a bank of glass cells in a room that smells of hot vinegar, and the cells run the lamps.

That is the entire energy budget of Nine: what the ceiling can be persuaded to give up, one bucket at a time. It is why the light is ankle-light — amber filaments hooded downward, so that you walk through a town of illuminated feet and glowing puddles and lose the ceiling entirely. It is why nobody runs. It is why the pumps are timed to the tide of the year and the bathhouse is heated on the fourth day of the week and not the others. Industrial civilization, scaled to a drip. The turbines of the upper world would not recognize this as a machine. It has been running, without stopping, since before the stairs were finished.

The quiet is enormous and specific. A drop landing in the eastern cistern is audible in every bed in town, and everyone can tell you which cistern. The chain ticks once every few seconds. There is a transformer somewhere that hums just at the edge of hearing, and long-term residents get headaches in the upper world where it isn't. People wear felt-soled shoes and speak at a volume that would be a whisper outside. Shouting is not forbidden; it is simply obscene, like breaking a window.

---

And yet the place is *full*.

Look into any puddle — there are ten thousand puddles — and there is a shrimp in it: a translucent thing the length of an eyelash, walking on its own reflection. The cisterns are thick with them. They are the town's kidneys; the drinking water is passed through shrimp-beds, gravel trays where the animals graze the film off the water, and this is the real reason the lamps are hooded and dim. Light above a certain brightness stops them spawning. Nine drinks through its fauna. There is a rule about pointing a lamp at open water, and the rule is older than the explanation, and it is obeyed.

Snails cover the weeping wall in a slow-moving stipple, thousands of them, thumbprint-sized, rasping the fresh lime as fast as it deposits. They are the reason the flowstone lips stay sharp-edged instead of blunting. They are, functionally, the town's other masons, working against the first one. Isopods the color of a peeled almond live under every stair tread. Pale crickets eat the felt and are tolerated because they also eat the mold. A walking moth — it has wings and has, over some long span of generations, decided against them — crosses the mess hall floor at the speed of a minute hand.

And there are the sump-hounds: blind salamanders as long as your forearm, pink-white, with feathery external gills that fan and close, fan and close. They live in the condenser coils where the water is two degrees warmer, and they come out to follow the tenders on their rounds, gliding along at heel like something being pulled on a string. There are perhaps two hundred of them. They know the sound of individual footsteps. They have names, most of them, and the names are reused.

The town is not crowded. It is full. Those are different conditions, and Nine is the second one.

---

Vegetation is a rumor here. From the ceiling cracks hang root curtains — the taproots of some tree on the surface, ninety feet up, feeling its way down into the wet dark. They come in bundles like combed hair, pale gold, and they drip, and the drip tastes of the tree, and the tenders can tell you when the surface has had rain by the change in flavor a day and a half later. There are bracket fungi on the timber staging, and moss in a green ruff around the light-coin at the top of the stair, and one bramble that has abandoned leaves entirely and lives as a white knot of thorns. That is the whole flora. It is enough. The roots are the town's newspaper.

---

The industry — the actual reason sixty people live under a hill — is on the middle terrace, and you smell it before you see it: cold chalk, eel-gut, wet iron.

It is a stringing floor. Forty thousand threads hang from the ceiling rails in ranked curtains, each one weighted at the bottom with a lead bead, each one standing in a known drip-line. Calcite accretes on the threads. A pea takes nine years. A good bead, a marble, takes thirty. The threads are gut, harvested when the eels come up with the high water, and the eels come up every year, and this is the closed circle the town lives inside: the water feeds the roots, the roots flavor the water, the water grows the beads, the eels make the lines, the shrimp clean the drink, the snails hold the walls, the buckets make the light.

Nine produces a few kilograms a year of extremely small, extremely perfect spheres of calcite. The requisition from above lists their use as *optical*. Nobody here has ever seen the instrument. Once a decade a crate goes up the ninety-one steps on a man's back, and comes down again empty, and there is a payment, and the payment buys iron and felt and vinegar and lamp-glass and the things the chamber cannot make.

The harvest is a sorting. Most beads are clean. Some are not: a cricket fell into a drip-line in a year nobody recorded, and the calcite went on growing around it, and now it is a white sphere with a cricket inside, perfect, legs folded, entirely visible through the translucent rind. There is a cabinet of these in the mess hall. Three hundred and eleven drawers. They are catalogued by terrace and thread and year, and they are, in the most literal sense, the town's memory — the only record of what was alive in Nine in a given season, kept by accident, kept by water, kept because nobody could think of a reason to throw them away.

---

Below the lower terrace, under the low-water line, there is another stringing floor. And below that, another.

They were strung when the aquifer stood lower, generations back, and they were never taken up, because taking up threads is work and the water was rising and there was time to come back to it later. There is always later. The threads are still hanging down there in the black water, still weighted, still in their drip-lines — and the water still deposits, slowly, in the dark, on lines nobody has read in a hundred and forty years.

The eels go down among them. The tenders sometimes lower a lamp on a cord and look, and see the ranked curtains standing in the drowned hall, thickened, furred, some of the beads now the size of a fist. Nobody knows what those beads are recording. Nobody is going to harvest them within the lifetime of anyone currently alive. There is a line item in the ledger for it, carried forward every year, in a different hand each generation, and the line item says: *lower floor, pending.*

That is Nine: a small wet room under a hill, lit at the ankles, ticking once an hour, full of small pale animals and a few patient people, manufacturing spheres out of patience, waiting for the water to come back down.

A drop leaves the ceiling. It falls for a long time through the dark, and lands in the eastern cistern, and sixty people, without looking up, know exactly where it went.
