## The Blister

At the top of the flow there is no soil, no rounded stone, no dust that has been anywhere long enough to become soft. The summit plateau is a single pour of black glass, still holding the ropey bands of its cooling, and every edge on it is an edge that has not yet been told to stop. Grit here is angular. It does not blow so much as *cut*, in a wind too thin to shove a shoulder but perfectly capable of taking the skin off one. The sky at noon is the color of a bruise because most of the air is below you, down there where the weather lives. At the zenith it is nearly black, and you can see stars beside the sun if you shade your eyes with a glove.

Nothing grows on the plateau. Nothing lives on it. It is a sterile roof, and it has one flaw: a humped blister in the flow, sixty paces end to end and twenty high, where a gas bubble rose and stalled and was frozen in the act of leaving. From a hundred paces away it is a boulder among boulders. From ten it is a smoked-glass dome with a shoulder-width slot at its base, and the slot is plugged with felt.

Behind the felt there is a city.

---

Call it the Blister, since the tenants do. The whole of it — eleven districts, four presses, two arc-houses, the spiral rail, the wool-farms, the hog-stalls, the exchange, the dead-house — occupies a volume you could stack inside a modest granary. It is not a small town. It is a full city compressed until its streets are shoulder-width and its plazas are alcoves and its suburbs are the gaps behind the machinery. Population is not counted in heads. It is counted in shifts, because a berth is owned by the hour and not by the square, and three tenants may hold the same hammock in succession and never meet, communicating instead by chalk on the frame.

The light is brown. It comes *through* the walls — a yard of smoked glass between the city and the sun, so that daylight arrives already old, the color of weak tea, of tobacco, of a lamp seen through a hand. It moves. Over a shift the bright place on the wall crawls from the Rind to the Crown, and the old flow-bands inside the glass light up in sequence, so that the stone appears to have weather in it: fronts, veils, a slow brown squall that never breaks. Tenants watch it the way other people watch a sea.

Because the walls are glass and curved, everything is doubled. A body walking the spiral has a ghost walking the wall beside it, and a second, fainter, deeper in. Counting a crowd is done by counting reflections; children learn to distinguish their own third ghost from a stranger's second before they learn their trades.

And it is quiet. Not empty — *quiet*, deliberately, the way a held breath is quiet.

---

The Quiet is law, and the law is physics. The blister is a shell of brittle glass under a pressure that changes with the vent's breathing, and it has modes, and it can be sung into fracture. A dropped ingot once took the whole of Underlip down in a single soft rush. Since then: no bells, no hammers, no shouting. Presses instead of forges. Wool instead of bare metal on every bearing, every rail-chair, every boot-sole. Signals travel as lamp-shutters and as taps conducted through the busbars, so that a message crosses the city as a faint tick in the teeth of everyone touching copper. Shift-change is a blink-tide: ten thousand small red apertures opening and closing in a wave up the spiral, and no sound at all beyond the shuffle of felt on glass.

The other reason it is quiet is that there is hardly enough air to carry a sound. High frequencies die within a body-length. A cry arrives as a click. The great turbine in the Throat, which drives everything, is not heard so much as *sat in* — a pressure at the sternum, a hum in the jaw, a thing you notice only when the vent stutters and it stops, and the whole city lurches into a silence so total that people grip the rails.

---

Below, at the Throat, a fissure in the floor connects the bubble to something deep and hot and dry. It does not breathe water. It breathes spirit and sulfur — a light volatile fraction the tenants call *quick*, and a heavy residue they call *slack* — and it breathes irregularly, in surges, in long sighs, in sudden gulps that spin the turbine up past its rating and blow the arc-lamps to white behind their red glass.

That instability is the city's clock, its economy, and its weather. Power comes in waves; production is scheduled to the surge; the smelters are sealed retorts with forced draft, because open flame up here is a pale, wide, useless thing that will not heat a billet. Everything is electric that can be. The busbars run in wool-jacketed bundles down every district, warm to the palm, and half the maintenance guild exists to chase the faults.

Where the rising gas meets the cool upper glass, it condenses. Not into dew — into *wax*. At the Crown it snows: fine white flakes of paraffin drifting down through the brown dusk, settling on the wool, on the rails, on the shoulders of the crowd, never melting except where a heat-seam runs. Children scrape it off the condenser fins and press it into cakes. The wax is lamp-stock, seal-stock, currency, and food.

The quick is harvested before it can evaporate, and it is not harvested by machines.

---

There is no water in the Blister and no green thing anywhere in it. The base of the living world is a mat: a lithotroph film, ochre and rust-violet, that grows wherever slack films the glass and oxidizes it slowly. It is a few millimeters thick, felted, faintly warm, and it is farmed in racks like a crop, scraped and re-seeded on a nine-shift rotation. In the cold corners it goes dormant and black. In the hot seams it blooms so fast it fouls the switchgear, and the fault-chasers curse it as *greening*.

On the mat, and on everything the mat becomes, lives an unreasonable number of animals.

Wool-mites first — grazers, dust-sized, in quantities that make the insulation *move*, so that a batt of glass wool held to the lamp shows a slow grain drifting through it like traffic seen from a height. They are the city's grout, its nuisance, its protein: swept, pressed, rendered into the pale paste everyone eats and nobody praises.

Wax-moths, whose wings are oiled paper and whose adults live eleven hours, drink quick straight off the condenser fins and die in drifts at the Crown, where they are swept into flour.

Carders — long-legged, patient spiders — spin the fine silk that filters the vent gas and clothes the tenants, farmed in stacked trays, fed on mites, tended by a guild that speaks entirely in hand-signs because a startled tray is a ruined month.

Cable-worms in the busbar bundles, eating jacket, causing faults, hunted with a hooked wire and a lamp and a great deal of silent profanity.

Flakers, which are the reason the walls stay young: thumbnail limpets that work the glass with an acid tongue, quarrying microscopic flakes for their shells, and in aggregate under-cutting whole faces until the face lets go. Nothing in the Blister ever weathers. Everything in it is freshly broken.

Sails in the updraft — gliders with wings absurdly large for their bodies, because the air is too thin for anything else — that never land, mate in the rising column, and are taken at the Crown grille in wicker traps.

And the hogs. Blind, hairless, penned one to a household in warm alcoves the size of a wardrobe, fed on scraped mat, sweating quick from glands along the flank. The city's whole solvent cycle runs through their bodies. A household's wealth is its hog's yield; a district's smell is its hogs; a marriage is, in practice, the merging of two stalls.

There are also the stridulators. Millions of them in the wool, singing at frequencies this air will not carry. You cannot hear them. You can feel them, an itch along the forearm, a sense of a crowded room just beyond a door. The tenants call it the deaf chorus and consider it a sign of good air.

---

The tenants themselves are dry-bodied, oil-blooded, small, with split inner lids against the grit. They do not sweat and they do not weep; grief is expressed by rubbing a thumb of wax onto the doorframe of the dead, so that a well-loved berth grows a soft white lip over the years. They drink the second draw, heavy and sweet, and burn it slowly, and smell of hot lamp. Their skin is oiled twice a shift. Their boots are felt. Their lamps are coals in cages, red-shrouded, because bright light polymerizes the quick and gums the works — the dimness is not poverty, it is chemistry, enforced by an ordinance older than the exchange.

And nothing here is built to last. This is the part that surprises visitors, if the Blister ever had any. A city of glass and industry, and not one permanent structure in it. The vent surges; the shell flexes; the flakers undercut; a face spalls and a district becomes a hole. So everything is bolted, not bonded. Everything is on skids. Whole streets are unbolted at shift-end and dragged by capstan to a new berth on the far curve, and the spiral rail is re-laid in sections nightly, and the map of the city is a slate that is wiped and redrawn every morning by a clerk who has never once been asked to make it beautiful.

You can watch it happen. A pressure-gulp from below; the jaw-hum climbs; a face on the Rind goes from brown to a bright hairline silver; the lamp-shutters ripple the alarm; and then a slab the size of a market square lets go and comes down through the wool-tiers in a soft, enormous *rush*, more felt than heard, and the air pushes everyone sideways, and afterward there is the smell of fresh glass — sharp, mineral, almost sweet — and the flakers are already on the new face, and the salvage crews are already unbolting the presses to move them, and the mites are already grazing the newly exposed mat, and the wax is falling on all of it, indifferently, in flakes.

Nobody shouts. It would be dangerous. The city rebuilds itself in near silence, in brown light, at the roof of the world, packed shoulder to shoulder with ten thousand small warm bodies and a number of other bodies that cannot be counted at all — hidden inside a boulder on a dead plateau, where the wind cuts, and nothing outside has any idea.
