## The Cut

The road comes off the mineral ground — gravel, a wall, three farms with their lights on — and then it stops being a road in any honest sense and becomes a causeway laid on vegetation. It floats. Trucks feel it give under them, a long soft swell, like crossing a raft. There is a sign about this. Everyone ignores it at forty.

Then the ramp drops, and the horizon rises up on both sides, and you are inside the moss.

Not a valley. A valley has a river and a reason. This is a hole cut into a body of plants, five metres deep and eleven kilometres across, and its walls are the substance of the thing it was cut from. Black, straight, ruled along the skyline as far as the haze allows. From the floor you cannot see the farms or the road or the country you came from. You can see the top edge of the face, which is the old surface of the bog, and above it a low grey sky with nothing in it.

The floor is where the sky's light lands, and it lands badly. The ground is black and takes almost all of it. Noon in this season is a long blue afternoon that never quite commits to being day.

## The Face

Go close to the wall. It is new. The extractor passed here in September and the cut is still sharp — vertical grooves where the bucket chain combed it, arrises you could cut your hand on, no rounding, no slump, no soil. Nothing has had time to happen to it yet. Frost has begun, just begun, to pick at the top twenty centimetres and lay a crumb of black talus at the base, and that is the entire weathering history of this landscape.

It is also five thousand years old.

The face reads upward like a page. At the bottom, dense, greasy, structureless black — put your thumb in and it takes the print and keeps it. Above that, a band where the moss goes brown and you can see the ghost of stems, a texture like felt. Then, at about chest height, running along the whole face for kilometres at exactly the same level, the stumps.

A forest, in cross-section. Pine, some alder, roots splayed flat and horizontal, the wood gone the colour of strong tea and the hardness of iron. They stood on a dry surface once, four thousand years back, and the moss came up over their feet and drowned them and kept them. Above the stumps the peat changes again, paler, fresher, blond at the very top where the living skin is: sphagnum, still green in places, still holding its water, still growing at the rate of about one millimetre a year.

So the youngest ground in the country is made of the oldest material in it, and the cutting goes down through time at a millimetre a year, and a man on a machine descends through the Bronze Age in an afternoon.

## The Fields

The floor is not flat and not empty and not full. It is divided into fields by drains, fifteen metres apart, running to a deeper arterial cut, running to the outfall. Each field is cambered — crowned in the middle so rain will shed — and each is combed with the marks of the harrow: a fine corrugation, two centimetres deep, kilometres of it, like a beach after a rake the size of a house.

Between the ridges the milled peat lies loose and dry on top and wet underneath, and it is the only thing here that is warm-coloured: rust, umber, the brown of a dead bracken. In the low places it has blown into drifts against the drain edges. In the high places the black underlayer shows through, and the field looks scorched.

The emptiness is real. A thousand hectares to a machine. You can watch a harvester working for twenty minutes and understand it as a small red insect on a table, and then it turns and comes toward you and takes four minutes to arrive and is the size of a house, and the distance you thought you understood reorganises itself. There is no tree, no wall, no cow, nothing at the scale of a body, so the eye has no ruler. The hut that looks four hundred metres off is two kilometres. The man standing by the culvert is a man for a long time before he becomes a man.

Scattered across all this: a pump house the size of a wardrobe. A stack of sod peat, laid by hand in the old way, herringboned with gaps for the wind, now slumped and grown over with a grey lichen and looking exactly like a ruined wall — because it is a wall, it's just that its bricks are plants. A tipping wagon on a spur of narrow-gauge rail. A concrete slab with four holding-down bolts and nothing on it. A weighbridge with the hut still locked. Two hundred hectares of nothing, and then a lit window.

And in the middle distance, rising slightly, greener, uncut: the dome. The remnant. Left because the licence says leave it, and because if you cut it the water table drops out from under everything else. It sits there in its own wetness, hummock and hollow, sphagnum in cushions of dull red and ochre and a green so wet it looks lit from inside, cottongrass gone to rag, bog asphodel stiff and rust-coloured, a few pools with the sky in them. It is the only place in view where the ground is alive in the ordinary way. Everything else is alive too, but in the other way — the way a plank is a tree.

## What the Air Is Doing

Cold. Not dramatic cold; the flat, patient, penetrating cold of a wet place at four degrees falling to minus two at night and staying there. The drains skim over with ice by four in the morning, a skin you can break with a boot, and the breaking sounds like sugar. Frost stands in needles on the harrow tines, on the fence wire, on the dead cottongrass, on the handrails at the station, on a man's moustache.

But the frost only goes down twenty centimetres. Peat is a superb insulator; there is a reason people built with it. Below that the ground sits at four degrees all winter, and below that it does not know what season it is. The cold here is a lid.

The air holds peat dust. Not a cloud — a suspension, a fineness, a permanent brown haze that thickens when the wind gets up and thins when it rains and never entirely goes. It gives every light a halo. It settles on the frost and makes the frost grey. It gets into the teeth and tastes of smoke and iodine and cold tea. The station's plume goes out flat to the southeast for two kilometres and lays a corridor of rime along the pylons and the fence, and under the plume, in the warmth and the condensate, a strip of moss has stayed green and lush and wrong all winter, an accidental glasshouse ribbon crossing the dead fields, and there is condensation dripping there in a place where nothing else drips.

Rain comes as drizzle, most days, not enough to run off, enough to darken the milled surface and stop the harvest. Water is everywhere here and almost nowhere visible: ninety per cent of the ground by weight, held in the cells of dead plants, and you can pick up a handful that looks like crumb and squeeze brown water out of it in a stream. The visible water is in pockets. Drain bottoms. Old cuttings gone to pool, edged with a scum of algae and a raft of sphagnum trying to come back. The settling ponds by the station, brown as stout, with a skin of ice at the sheltered end and a fringe of foam at the outfall. A seep at the margin where the mineral ground meets the peat, running clear and, uniquely here, not acid, and the only place with a snail.

## The Sound

It is deafening, and it does not echo.

That is the thing nobody expects. Peat is fibre; the walls are fibre; the floor is fibre; the whole basin is a sound absorber the size of a parish. So the noise arrives without a room around it. It comes flat and dry and close and stops at the edges of itself. There is no reverberation to tell you how big the place is. You stand in eleven kilometres of open air and it feels like standing inside a wardrobe with an engine.

The station is the base of it: the boiler's induced-draft fans, a broadband roar with a hard tonal edge at the blade pass; the mills; the turbines heard through the house as a whine you feel in the fillings of your teeth; the transformers' hundred-hertz hum along the fence; the conveyors, which contribute a rattle and a squeal and a slow arrhythmic knocking from a roller that has been failing since March. Out in the fields the suction harvesters make their own weather — a centrifugal fan moving peat crumb up a duct, a hundred and ten decibels at three metres, a sound with a physical direction to it, so that walking around the machine changes the pressure in your ears.

You can shout to a man twenty metres away and lose it entirely. You can hear the fan from three kilometres. The low end travels; the top end gets eaten by the ground. Voices die, engines don't.

Every eight hours the sootblowers open and the whole place shakes for four minutes: a hiss so large it stops being a hiss and becomes a solid, a jet of steam lancing through the tube banks, and the roar of it comes over the stockpile and across the fields and every raven in the basin goes up at once. The shift changes by the blow. People say *after the second blow* instead of a time.

And under all of it, everywhere, at the outfall and at the sump and at the little green pump houses scattered a kilometre apart: the pumps. Fifty hertz, continuous, four of them, three running and one on standby. They are the reason there is a floor here at all.

## The Rule

The bog is a held-open bubble in a body of water. That is the whole physics of the place. Drain it and it will hold a shape; stop draining it and the water comes back up through the peat from below at a rate you can measure in weeks. Stop the pumps and in three years the cut is a shallow lake, in thirty it is a fen, in three hundred it is a moss again, in three thousand it is a dome with a forest buried in it at chest height.

So the loudest thing in this landscape is also the thing keeping it existing. Silence here is not peace; silence is the water coming.

Second rule: nothing rots. Acid, cold, no oxygen below the water table, no worms, no woodlouse, almost no bacteria doing the usual work. Everything that goes in stays, tanned and brown and identifiable. The dead do not decay here, they cure. That is why the ground is five metres thick — five thousand years of plants that were never permitted to finish dying.

Third rule: it burns. The whole floor is fuel at ten megajoules a kilogram, dry. There is a fire in the far stockpile that started by itself in July, a slow smoulder in the interior, no flame, a wisp of white at a new spot each week, migrating about a metre a month. They monitor it. It has been going since summer and will go until spring. In a hot dry year the ground itself catches, and then the fire goes down and travels sideways under your feet for weeks and comes up somewhere else, and you fight it with drains and dozers and there is no bottom to it.

## What Has Been Found

The cutting keeps turning things up.

A leather shoe, size small, laces still knotted, in a spoil heap, thrown out with a bucket load and lying among the crumb like anything else. A butter keg, staved, the butter still in it, gone to a grey wax. A trackway — alder planks and roundwood runners laid across a wet place by people who wanted to cross dry, exposed in the face in November, now under sand and plastic sheeting with a fence round it, a pale rectangle in all this black, and a board with a phone number.

And in the third field a taped-off rectangle of nothing, because they lifted a body out of it in the spring: a man, curled, tanned to the colour of the ground, hands with the fingernails still on them, hair gone red from the acid, and the last thing he ate still in him. He was older than the stumps in the face. He is in a museum now. The place where he lay is a hole with string round it, and the extractor went around it, and the field has a notch in it that will show on the maps for as long as anyone keeps the maps.

At the margin, where the mineral ground comes up and there is one hawthorn, there are rags tied to the hawthorn. Cloth, a child's sock, a strip of hi-vis, something that might have been white in the seventies. Nobody at the station will tell you who ties them. Everybody knows about the tree.

## Life

Almost none of it walks. The animals are the rare ones — a raven pair working the whole basin, hooded crows on the road verge, a meadow pipit going along the drain edge in the dusk, a hare that has learned the culverts, a snipe in the remnant, whooper swans high over in a line, going somewhere with better manners. A moth at the station lamp in the warm plume. That is a fair census for a day.

The real fauna is microscopic and lives in the film of water on a sphagnum leaf: amoebae in little sand shells, rotifers, tardigrades walking on eight legs through a wet moss cushion at a scale where a millimetre is a county. There are more of them in a handful of the remnant than there are vertebrates in the whole cut. It is a plant world with a microbial crew and a few birds passing over, and the reason is written in the water: pH three point eight, no calcium, no nitrogen worth the name, cold, and everything carnivorous that grows here — the sundews in the hollows, sticky and red — grows that way because the ground gives it nothing.

## The Slow

Nothing here is fast except the noise.

The moss lays down a millimetre a year. The cut advances sixty metres a season. The whole floor is subsiding as it dries and oxidises, a few centimetres a decade, so the rail loop has been re-levelled twice and the pump house by the outfall now stands proud of the ground on a plinth nobody built for it. The stockpile shrinks all winter at a rate set by the cold in the town and the price of gas. The licence has eleven years to run. There is a plan, in a drawer, for rewetting: block the drains, let the water up, grow the moss back, and it will take a hundred and fifty years to be a bog and three thousand to be a dome, and the men who wrote the plan will not see the first cushion of sphagnum.

Even the fire moves a metre a month.

At four o'clock the lamps come on along the conveyor and the stockpile and the pump houses, scattered yellow across a black plain, each with its brown halo of dust, and the plume is lit from underneath and stands up into the dark like something being made. The frost comes down. The harvester sits parked in the ninth field with its work lights still on and nobody in it, and the ice begins in the drains, and the rime grows on the wire.

The blow finishes. The roar drops out and the silence arrives — enormous, flat, unechoing, wrong — and lasts about a second and a half before you notice that it isn't silence at all.

Under everything, the pumps.
