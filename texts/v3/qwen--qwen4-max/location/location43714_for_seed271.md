The rim gives nothing away. You walk over low limestone pavement, warm underfoot, grit in the solution pockets, a scattering of tussock grass with more stone between it than grass — and then the ground simply ends, and you are looking down into a hollow about ninety paces long and fifty across, walled all the way around, with no way in but a shallow gully on the eastern side where the rock has been worn smooth by feet that stopped coming a long time ago.

It is a sink. A closed bowl in old carbonate rock, the kind that forms when a chamber below gives up and the roof settles rather than falls. Nothing here is broken. That is the first thing you notice, and the thing you keep noticing: there is no rubble, no fresh fracture, no raw face anywhere. Every surface has been rounded, scalloped, fluted, hollowed into the soft repeating scoops that water leaves when it works slowly and without interruption. The walls are the color of weak tea and old bone. Where they lean inward at the top — and they do lean, all around, so that the sky above is a long slot rather than an opening — the underside has been smoothed into a single continuous curve, like the inside of a spoon.

Down inside, the light is second-hand. No sun reaches the floor at any hour; the overhang sees to that. What arrives comes bounced off a pale band of rock high on the northern wall, and it arrives evenly, from no particular direction, at about the strength of a room with the shutters half closed. You can see everything. You can see the green in the stone and the individual leaves of the moss and the grains of the grit. But nothing casts a hard shadow, and nothing glitters, and after a while your eyes stop expecting either.

It is warm. Blood-warm, and still — the air in the bowl does not move, has no reason to, being sealed at the bottom and shaded at the top and the same temperature as the rock that holds it. The stone is warm too, warm as a living body, so that when you put your palm against the wall you cannot tell whether you are giving heat or taking it. The air is wet. Not rainy, not misty: wet the way the air is wet inside a cupped hand. Your sweat does not evaporate. The stone takes it instead.

The walls sweat. There are dark vertical stripes on them, a dozen or more, running from the lip of the overhang down to the floor, and from across the bowl they look like staining, like old water-marks. Up close they are damp to the touch, a film you can wipe with a finger and watch return. The whole hollow sits precisely at its dew point. Warm air, cool rock, and the two have long ago come to an arrangement: the film neither thickens into drops nor dries away. It is simply always there, at the same thickness, on the same stripes, and has been for longer than anything nearby has been alive.

At the bottom, off-center toward the west, there is a pool. Three paces by two, oval, no deeper than your wrist. It has no inlet and no outlet. It is fed by seep and by one drip and it loses exactly as much as it gains, and around its edge runs a thin pale crust of mineral, a ring the width of a fingernail, sitting exactly at the waterline — not above it, not below. A bathtub ring with no tides. The water is perfectly clear and perfectly green where it lies over the stained floor of the basin, a green like bottle glass, like the inside of a wave. Nothing lives in it. You can see the bottom in every detail because there is nothing in the water to see: no larvae twitching, no film of algae, no fallen leaf, no drowned anything. The surface is unbroken except where the drip comes down.

The drip falls from a lip four meters up, and it falls about once every eleven seconds, and it has been falling into the same spot long enough to have worn a bowl in the basin floor the size of a cupped pair of hands. There is a second stain on the wall, ten paces north, with a worn hollow beneath it and no drip. It stopped. Whatever fed it found another route through the rock, or the fracture closed with mineral, and the hollow beneath it is dry now and has been dry for centuries, and its edges have gone soft and chalky the way everything here goes soft. That is the last change the place recorded. Everything since has been maintenance.

The green in the stone is copper. The rock is threaded with it, thin veins and bleeds and blooms, weathered out into malachite and the paler blue-green of chrysocolla, and the water that comes through the rock carries it, and the water is the reason the hollow is empty. Copper is poison to most living things. It has been leaching into this bowl since before the bowl was a bowl, and the floor is a barren — the kind of ground where plants grow in ones and twos because the chemistry will not permit a crowd.

So: sparse. You could count the vegetation in a slow hour and not lose your place. Black-green cushions of copper moss along the seep stripes, each one the size of a spread hand, thriving where nothing else can. Four fleshy rosettes with leaves like small grey tongues, rooted in grit pockets, each a good ten paces from the next. A fern in a crack on the south wall with six fronds, all six of them old. Two tussocks of grass on the floor and a third at the rim. And one tree, up on the lip where the copper thins out, a stunted thing with more root than trunk, its roots coming down the wall in a fan like fingers reaching for the damp, most of its length exposed, all of it held. Nothing here grows fast. Nothing here crowds. There is more bare warm rock in this bowl than there is anything else, and the emptiness is not a lack — it is the composition.

There is no soil. Understand what that means: no worms, no insects, no burrowing anything, so nothing turns the ground over; the grit in the pockets is mineral, sharp-edged, made by weather alone, with no humus in it and no smell of rot. There are no birds, because there is nothing to eat and no insects to eat it. No midges hang over the water. No spider has strung the niche. No shell, no cast, no burrow, no track. The absence is total and it is not sinister; it is chemistry. The bowl is sterile in the old sense — clean, empty, keeping.

Which brings you to the marks.

Nine steps are cut into the rock at the eastern gully, wide and shallow and worn to a shine at their centers, going up from the floor to a ledge with nothing on it. Above the pool there is a niche, arm-deep, its inside smoothed by hands rather than water — you can tell, because the smoothing is in the wrong places for water. And along the richest of the green veins, at a height a person could reach standing, there are scrape marks. Thousands of them. Crescent-shaped, a tool's width, overlapping, layered, the older ones softened almost to nothing and the newest ones — the last ones — nearly as soft. Someone came here for the green. Someone scraped the copper bloom out of the wall for pigment, season after season, and carried it away, and left no tool, no shard, no bone, no ash, nothing at all but the shape of the taking. Near the pool lip stand three stones in a line, and a fourth fallen, and the fracture on the fallen one has gone rounded and pale like everything else.

Nothing was built here to last. Only marked.

The quiet has a texture. It is not the quiet of a held breath; it is the quiet of a room with heavy hangings, where sound goes out and does not come back. The scalloped walls scatter every noise instead of returning it. Your footfalls on the grit are shocking, and then they are gone. The drip makes a small round sound with no echo behind it. Under that, if you stand still long enough, there is the low pressure-hum of enclosed air, and your own blood, and a faint irregular ticking from somewhere in the seep stripes that you will not locate.

The rules here are simple and they are all the same rule. Water comes in at the rate it leaves. Stone dissolves at the rate the crust rebuilds. The moss grows at the rate the copper kills it back. The wall sheds grit at the rate the floor loses it. The leaves that fall are replaced by leaves that fall, so that the same three dead leaves lie in the same crack, mummifying by mineral rather than by rot, taking a century to become part of the ground. The tree at the rim adds a ring so thin you would need a lens. There are no seasons in the bowl — no wind to bring seed, no bird to carry it, no flood to scour, no frost to split. Nothing arrives. Nothing departs. The erosion is not ongoing; it is *finished*. The walls have found the angle at which they neither collapse nor retreat, and the whole place has settled into its final shape and is simply holding it, warm and damp and dim, the way a struck bell holds a note after you have stopped hearing it.

You will want something to have happened here. It hasn't. That is the whole of the place: a bowl of poisoned stone where the water is clear because nothing can live in it, where people once came for the color and stopped coming, and where every process has been matched to its opposite so exactly that time passes through without leaving a mark.

When you climb out through the gully, over the nine worn steps, the tussocks at the rim do not move. Behind you, eleven seconds later, the drip falls.
