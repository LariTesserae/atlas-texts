## The Bell

You come down through a breathing pore.

It is not a shaft anyone dug. It is a lenticel — one of the tree's air-ports, widened over nine generations by hands and chisels and finally by a steam reamer, forty meters of ladder rungs driven into pale wood that closes around each rung a little more every year so that the top twenty are loose and the bottom twenty are welded fast. The air coming up it is warm and green and smells like crushed pepper and hot bearing-grease. Before you see anything you hear the place: a wide, sustained, layered roar, the sound of a great deal happening in a large empty room.

Then the pore opens and you are standing on the Bell Steps with the whole nave below you, and the first thing you understand is that there is nothing here that is not alive or was not alive until recently.

The chamber is a wound. Something broke a limb off the mother-stock a thousand years ago and the tree healed around the loss, laying down callus after callus until it had walled off a void ninety paces across and forty high, ribbed, fluted, buttressed — cathedral-shaped by accident, or by the same physics that makes cathedrals. Every surface is wood. The floor is wood, worn into a shallow bowl by feet. The walls are wood, pale as peeled willow, the grain running in slow cataracts down toward the sump. The ceiling is wood, and it sweats. The buttresses are not carved; they grew where the load was, which is why they look like carved things, and why the town's engineers have spent two centuries arguing about whether the tree understands vaulting.

The light is poor and beautiful. A little green comes down the crown-shaft, forty meters of leaf above filtering daylight into something the color of pond water, enough to read a gauge by if you hold it up and wait. The rest is municipal. Freshly cut sapwood glows — a faint oxidizing blue-green at the wound face, bright for three days, dim by the seventh, dead by the tenth. So the walls must be shaved to be lit. There is a lighting rate, a wound allowance, a guild of scarifiers who go along the streets each week with drawknives taking a hand's breadth off the parapets and the lamp-bosses and the frontages of the tenements, and the shavings go into the meal-hoppers, and the walls come up shining behind them like the inside of a struck match. A district that cannot pay its wound rate goes dark. You can tell the poor wards from the Steps: they are the ones the color of old tea.

---

The town is called Nine Tap and it holds about four hundred people, which in a room this size is almost nobody. Walk the floor and you cross long empty stretches of polished grain with your footsteps coming back at you off the vault. A crane stands alone. A scaffold stands alone, holding nothing, waiting for a wall to grow up to it. The buildings hug the perimeter, low, two stories, grown into the wood rather than set on it — you do not build a house here, you cut a room and let the tree callus a lintel over the door, and the doorframe is the tree's idea of your doorframe. Tallow Row. The Combs. Bell Steps. The Gauge. Standpipes at every forty paces with brass covers padlocked shut.

The middle is empty by law.

It has to be. The center of the Bell is a working volume — the flight, the flue, the air-column the stock rides. Leave a cart there overnight and by morning it is buried under a drift of pale bodies, fouled, stripped of its grease, its leather eaten to lace. The void is a machine part. It is the emptiest thing in the chamber and it is the most expensive thing in the chamber, and the whole civic apparatus of Nine Tap exists to keep it clear.

Because the walls are not empty. The walls are solid with life. Every hand's breadth of that pale grain is honeycombed with galleries, and in the galleries the stock is breeding: soft, blind, six-legged, thumb-sized, the color of a fingernail, in numbers that stop being numbers. They are the tree's livestock and the tree's mail and the town's entire economy, and there are, by the assay office's last honest count, something on the order of eleven billion of them within the shell of this one room.

They come out on the tide.

---

Above, forty meters up, the canopy opens its stomata at dawn. Down here that arrives as pressure — a tightening in the tap-shaft, a rising note in the water column, forty minutes late and unmistakable. The stock feels it in their galleries and pours out through the pore-fields in a column that fills the flight from sump to vault, and for eleven minutes the air of the Bell is not air, it is a solid moving thing, and the roar of it is the roar you heard from the ladder. Then the callers begin.

They stand at the comb-heads on the wall-platforms with drums and struck bars, and they steer by pitch. The stock follows a tone the way iron follows a magnet; the callers walk the swarm's column sideways across the void in slow arcs and lay it against the nets, and the nets strain, and the wood behind the net-anchors has grown thick bosses around the bolts, overnight, every night, the tree thickening exactly where the town pulls hardest. Nobody bolts anything here without asking, in the sense that nobody bolts anything here without the tree answering by morning.

The presses take the catch. Oil for the lamps and the belts; meal for the trade up the pore; the cake and the frass down the throat to the roots, which is the payment, which is the whole arrangement. The tree grows the galleries. The town crops them. The tree gets its nitrogen back in a form it can use and a wound rate it can heal. It has balanced for two hundred and six years. The wall thickens as fast as the scarifiers shave it. There are men whose entire profession is the ledger of that equality, and they are the most feared people in town.

And the assay office reads the catch. Because the stock are messages — each one carries a smear of the tree's chemistry from wherever it was born, and a trained nose with a press-fraction and a warm plate can tell you what the mother-stock intends. A bloom coming. Drought above, three days out. Something has cut her on the north side. They sell the forecasts up the pore to the surface towns, who pay well and do not like to think about where the information comes from.

---

Water is the strange economy. Nothing here is wet. The air is warm and damp and everything in it is thirsty — the walls drink, the stock drink, the wood drinks — so moisture never gets to pool. It beads on the cold copper of the condenser ranks under the vault, runs in gutters, is caught in the crotch-tanks the tree grows in its buttresses like bromeliads, five or six liters each, covered, padlocked, counted. Condensation, not rain. You wipe your face at the end of a shift and the sweat is already gone off your forearm, taken, everything taken.

Except at the loose hour. Once a day the canopy above shuts down and the tension in the tap-shaft slackens, and for about fifty minutes the ceiling's accumulated condensate is released and falls — warm, iron-and-pepper tasting, ten minutes of real rain in the middle of a room, everyone out in it, children shrieking, the drums silent, the walls drinking it back as fast as it lands so that within the hour the floor is dry and warm again and the whole thing might not have happened.

The rest of the day the water in the tap-shaft is under tension. Not pressure — tension. The tree is pulling a column of water up from something deep and hot below, and that column is stretched to a hundred atmospheres of negative, and it *sings*, a high sustained wire-note you stop hearing after a week and never stop hearing again. You do not open a pipe under tension. You wait for the loose hour, and the tap-warden rings it, and the whole town's plumbing happens at once in a fifty-minute frenzy of standpipes and buckets and boiler-feed.

Open one wrong and the column snaps. Cavitation: a crack like a rifle shot in a stone room, then another, then a run of them rattling away up the shaft like a dropped chain, and the note goes out of the air, and a district's light and water are gone for a season while the tree re-primes. There is a shrine at the Ninth Tap with the names of the four men who did it on purpose. There is a much larger shrine, unmarked, for the ones who did it by accident.

---

Under all of it, the other sound: the low grinding creak of growth, wood laying itself down at the speed of a minute hand, audible only because the room is so large and so quiet at its center when the tide is out. And under *that*, at the throat where the tap goes down into the heat, something drinking that is not the tree. The town does not go down there. The town has a word for it and does not use the word.

The predators come up when the pressure slackens — long-jawed, patient, unhurried things from the deep root-galleries, that follow the loose hour the way the stock follow the drum. The reeve-wasps hunt them along the buttresses. The town keeps its lamps low and its doorways narrow and its cats, which are not cats, on the sills.

Stand in the middle at the slack, in the poor green light, in the warm damp, in the enormous emptiness that four hundred people have agreed to keep empty. The walls shine faintly where they were cut on Tuesday. Far above, a bell that is a valve is being struck by a man who is paid to strike it. The grain of the floor runs down toward the sump like water going somewhere. Two hundred and six years of ledger, balanced to the kilogram.

You get the feeling — everyone does, and the assay office will sell you a chemical explanation for it — that the tree grew this hollow because it wanted hands, and that the town is an organ it has been slowly domesticating, and that the arrangement is not, in any sense that matters, the town's idea.
