## The Seam

From above there is nothing to see. The plateau is a salt pan four thousand meters up, white and flat and ringing underfoot like a struck tile, and the wind crosses it without meeting anything for a hundred kilometers. Then the ground simply stops being ground. A crack, two hundred paces long and nowhere wider than a market square, splits the crust — and out of it comes warmth, and a smell like hot beeswax and crushed pepper, and no sound at all.

The crack is young. It opened perhaps four centuries ago, when the basalt beneath the salt cooled and shrank and tore, and everything about it is still sharp. No rounded stones. No dust that has been rolled. The walls are fresh glass-edged rock, blue-black, flaked into blades that will open a hand to the bone, and the scree below lies at the exact angle of repose, unslumped, as though it fell last week. There is no soil on the plateau. There is no soil anywhere here yet. It is being manufactured, and the manufacturing is what you have come to see.

---

Down forty meters the cold stops. It stops the way a wall stops — a boundary you can put your arm through and feel the difference at the wrist. Above: freezing wind, salt, ultraviolet, nothing living. Below: air at blood heat, still as a held breath, thick with drifting fiber. The fissure exhales. Deep down the rock is still giving up its magmatic heat, and the crack is a chimney that has learned to keep most of what it carries.

And it is *lush*. That is the shock of it. The walls are upholstered in green — but it is a dry green, an amber green, the green of old bottle glass and olive oil held to a lamp. Nothing here is wet. Nothing here has ever been wet. The plants run on a light oil, thin as naphtha, that they crack out of the rock's carbon and the air's carbon and their own patient chemistry. Cut a frond and it does not bleed; it beads. A single amber bead swells at the wound over the course of an hour, and a mite comes and takes it, and the wound seals with a varnish scar.

The leaves are parchment, translucent, veined in gold, and they hang in layers — six, eight, twelve layers deep from wall to wall, near-touching, so that walking through the canopy is a matter of parting curtains that sigh and settle behind you. Some species draw silica out of the fresh rock and spin it into needles, and those stand in glassy thickets that chime faintly when a body brushes them, the closest thing to a loud noise this place permits. Others grow as bladders of warm oil the size of a barrel, and the small fauna climb them and sleep against their sides.

The light is ordinary sunlight, but it arrives as a ribbon. The sky is a strip of blue two hundred paces long overhead, and the sun crosses it and lays a bright bar on the floor of the Seam, and that bar takes most of a day to travel from one end to the other. Everything below is lit secondhand, filtered through amber, so that the whole interior is the color of the inside of a honey jar. Shadows are soft and brown. Noon looks like late afternoon in a room with the curtains drawn.

---

The city occupies the widened middle, where the fissure blew out a chamber the size of a cathedral nave.

It is built entirely of pith. There are no tools in the Seam — not a blade, not a lever, not a cord, nothing anywhere that is held. The builders are pale, blind, six-limbed animals about the size of a hare, and they build the way a wasp builds: by chewing fiber and laying it from glands under the jaw. They graze the parchment canopy, and they gnaw rock flour from the walls, and they mix the two in their crops and extrude a ribbon of foam that sets in an hour into something between coral and bone. That is the masonry. Every column, every vault, every stair-ramp and gallery and partition wall in this place came out of a mouth, wet with oil, and hardened in the warm air.

It is a real city. There are districts. There are load-bearing buttresses grown thick by ten generations working the same seam, and thinner newer spans still translucent at the edges. There are fluted shafts where the builders' rhythm changed with the season. There is a great pale nave whose roof is a lattice of living fronds grafted onto pith ribs, so that the ceiling photosynthesizes and the columns hold it up and the whole thing is one argument between two kingdoms that has been going on for three hundred years and has produced, so far, this room.

And it is *full*. The Seam has no empty space in it. The galleries are packed shoulder to shoulder with slow bodies; the floor is a moving pavement of flat grazing animals like living doormats, mowing the dry lawn of fallen fiber at a body-length an hour; above them the wrights work in relays; above them long-legged pale things stand motionless for half a day at a stretch, feet splayed on the pith, reading the floor. Mites in the leaf-litter, mites on the mites. Ticks of wax in the joints of the columns. Small six-eyed browsers hanging upside down from the ceiling fronds like fruit. There are more animals per cubic meter here than in any wet forest, and they are all touching, or nearly, all the time.

They pass one another by agreement. A wright meeting a grazer in a narrow gallery will stop and lay its forelimbs flat and wait, sometimes an hour, until the grazer's slow tide has carried it past. Traffic here is not a flow. It is a very gradual negotiation, and it resolves over the course of a morning.

---

The water law is the reason for all of it.

There is no free water in the Seam. There is a trace — the deep rock breathes a few grams of juvenile vapor an hour, up from the cooling body below — and that breath is the entire budget of a metropolis. Salt organs catch it: hygroscopic towers of pith loaded with chloride hauled down from the pan above, which swell and darken in the cool hours and give up their moisture to the taps in the warm ones. The taps are root-animals that go down into the fissure's throat and drink the rock's exhalation and pass it up.

From there it moves in bodies. The carriers are swollen, legless, tank-shaped creatures the size of a goat, and they carry the Seam's whole water supply inside themselves, one load at a time, along routes that take three days to walk. They couple mouth to mouth with the reservoirs — dry fittings, valves of keratin, a seal that closes before it opens — and not one drop has ever fallen on the floor of this city. Not once, in four hundred years. A spilled drop would be an obscenity, and also simply a loss: it would flash off in the warm dry air and be gone into the ribbon of sky.

Nothing here weeps. Nothing sweats. Nothing has a wet orifice. Breath leaves the body already stripped, condensed in a nasal labyrinth of salt-paper, and the animals here are dry to the touch as warm paper, and their eyes are covered with a clear scale, and they smell of hot wax.

---

It is silent. Not empty-silent — *engineered* silent, though nobody engineered it. Pith is foam, and foam eats sound; the walls take a noise and give nothing back, no echo, no ring, no reverberation anywhere in the cathedral. The drifting fiber thins the air of anything sharp. Oil does not splash. There is no drip, no gurgle, no rustle of wet leaves, no rain, no stream, no surf. What there is: the tick of stone cooling at dusk. The rasp of a jaw laying a ribbon. The dry hiss of a grazer's underside over the floor. A glass thicket chiming once, softly, when someone leans on it.

So the animals speak through the substrate. The long-legged hunters stand with their feet on the pith and read the tremor of everything within thirty meters — the wright's chewing, the carrier's slow haul, the specific rhythm of a limping browser. Predation here takes an afternoon and looks, from the gallery above, like a very patient embrace. And the wrights talk by laying antennae on a wall and drumming, and a message can travel the length of the city in an hour, passing from foot to floor to foot, and no one hears a thing.

---

Nothing burns, which is the other law. Oil and paper and hot dry air should have ended this place long ago, but the wrights load their pith with rock flour until it is more mineral than fiber, and the thin high air starves any flame that starts. Fire is the one catastrophe the Seam has arranged against, and it has arranged against it by accident, by taste, by a preference for gritty chew that turned out to be a firewall.

Everything is slow because everything is expensive. A column thickens a finger-width in a year. A corridor closes over a season as the builders lose interest in it, and the traffic finds another way, and in a decade the district's whole plan has drifted like a crowd shifting toward a door. The population turns over in generations of eleven years. The salt organs pulse once a day. The bar of sunlight crosses the floor. That is the entire clock.

And it is all a first draft. There are no ruins under the Seam. There is no midden, no buried layer, no fossil, no old wall behind the new wall. Dig down through the floor and you find fiber-fall a hand's depth deep, and then raw glass-edged rock, unchanged since it tore. The first soil in the history of this place is currently being laid down, one dry leaf and one dry body at a time, and in ten thousand years there will be a meter of it, and something with roots will finally be able to stand here.

At night the rim goes to freezing and the stars come into the ribbon of sky in a long stripe, and below, the rock keeps everything at blood heat, and the city goes on not making any noise. From above, if you walked the salt pan in the dark, you would find it only by the warmth on your face — a breath out of the ground, smelling of wax and pepper, in a place where nothing should be breathing at all.
