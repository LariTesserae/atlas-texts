## THE LID

From the rim road you look down into weather.

The basin is eleven kilometers across and forty meters below the sea, and the sea knows it: out past the levee the ocean stands up like a held breath, six meters higher than the fields it is not allowed to enter. Nothing came over the wall. That is the first thing to understand. The flood did not arrive. It was manufactured here, on site, to specification, and it has never stopped being manufactured.

Two hundred meters above the basin floor hangs the Lid — the works' own exhaust, a cloud that learned to be a roof. It is made of the plant's product and it rains the plant's product back into the plant, warm as a bath, in ropes and sheets and sudden vertical rivers that walk across the valley on legs of spray. The light that gets through is the color of a fingernail held to a candle. Under it, everything is lit from below: sodium lamps on stanchions, the green shimmer of culture columns, the pale instrument-blue of panels still doing their jobs in rooms nobody has entered since the water came.

Inside the Lid, lightning goes off sideways, over and over, like a fluorescent tube trying to start.

The rain is 34 degrees. So is the air. So is the water. So is the steel. There is no cool anywhere in this place, no shade that helps, no direction you can walk to get out of the warmth. It is the warmth of a machine room, of a body, of a compost heap in its second day. It smells of hot bearing grease and cut grass and iodine and wet plywood and, faintly, under all of it, of new pennies.

## THE PRODUCT

The water is too clean to be natural and too dirty to be clean.

It came out of the condensers distilled, hungry, with nothing dissolved in it at all, and it has spent its whole short life taking things apart to fix that. It leaches the salt out of concrete. It pulls the calcium from mortar and the zinc from galvanizing and the memory from paint. Where it runs fast it is a milky turquoise, the color of skimmed milk with a sky behind it; where it stands it goes brown-gold with tannin from the crop; where it falls it is white and does not stop being white for a long time.

Do not drink it. It will take your salts and then it will take your outline — not quickly, not violently, but it will start on you the way it started on the grout.

And it is loud. Not the water falling: the water itself, in bulk, all through the basin, a continuous roaring chord with a fundamental somewhere around nineteen cycles a second that you do not hear so much as become. It sits in the sternum. It empties the sinuses. It makes the fluid in the eyes shiver so that the lamps grow fine bright hairs. On the flume deck the meter reads a hundred and forty-one decibels and the number is a courtesy; nobody has stood there long enough to watch it settle.

The flood is nine days old. It has been nine days old for a year and a half.

That is why nothing here is worn. The broken glass in the drowned canteen is still sharp enough to open a boot. The torn edges of the cable trays are bright metal, unrusted, freshly ripped. Concrete fractures show the pale angular faces of aggregate that has never been rounded by anything. Every surface in the basin is being dissolved and re-laid at roughly the rate the water can manage, which is fast, so the whole valley keeps arriving at its own beginning. There are no old scars. There are only new ones, in the same places, forever.

## THE CROP

Nothing was ever meant to grow here but stock.

The works ran cellulose lines — vat cultures, tube-weed, a fast hollow-stemmed cord-grass for the filter beds, four or five clonal lines with numbers instead of names. When the water came up over the gallery floors it took the vats off their feet and spread them, and they have not stopped since. Growth here is measured in shifts. A handrail wrapped on Monday is a green pillar by Friday, and by the following Monday the pillar has fallen and become a bridge.

It is a jungle with no ancestry. There is no competition, no grazing, no rot worth the name, no seed set — everything spreads by breaking. So the layers do not sort themselves out the way a real forest sorts itself out; they simply accumulate, near-touching, until walking is a matter of pushing through wet warm rope. Pad-leaves the size of tabletops stacked six deep with water standing on each. Tube-weed in bundles like organ pipes. Gasket moss that grows into every seam and swells and splits the seam, quietly, patiently, at industrial speed. A vine that has learned to eat cable insulation and now follows the wiring diagrams of the plant more accurately than any surviving drawing.

And here is the thing about the crop, the thing that makes this valley what it is:

**It does not grow toward light. It grows toward sound.**

Under the Lid there is barely any light to want. But there is noise beyond measure, and somewhere in the vat lines' cheap engineered plastids there was a mechanism that could be turned to it, and it was turned. So the crop leans at the pumps. It thickens at the flume mouths. It builds horns — actual horns, flared leafy cones a meter across, aimed with real precision at the throat of a penstock, and inside each horn a fine felt of hairs that takes the pressure and turns it into sugar. The loudest places are the greenest places. The intake towers wear coats of vegetation two meters thick, sculpted into shapes that an acoustician would recognize and a botanist would not.

At the lamps there are no insects. Warm wet night, a thousand watts of sodium, and not one moth, not one midge, not a single wandering speck. The lamps burn inside a snow globe with no snow in it. There are no fish in eleven kilometers of water. No worms in the silt. No birds under the Lid, ever; they come to the rim and turn around. Nothing here has a mouth that wants anything. Nothing here has ever been eaten.

## THE PLANT

Scattered through all that green: the works. Not a city — a machine with a few rooms attached, strung out across the basin like beads on a wire you can't see.

A pump house on the north terraces, its roof gone, its floor a well of noise. Fourteen axial pumps in a line down the old channel, of which nine still turn, one runs backward, and four are mineral. A single silo standing in open water with PLENTY painted down it in six-foot letters. Worker housing on stilts at the eastern shallows, sixteen units, doors open, curtains of tube-weed moving in and out of the windows with the surge. A chapel that is really a gauge room, with dial faces where the icons go. The rim of a settling bed, three hundred meters across, perfectly circular, still turning its slow rake through a soup of green. A hotel with one lit window at the bottom of the water, forty meters down, burning amber, unbothered.

The word PLENTY is on almost everything. Different typefaces, different decades. It is on the silo and the pump housings and the crates and the safety posters and, in a clean modern sans-serif nobody in the works ever used, on the flank of a cooling tower that has not been built.

The current is still on. Not everywhere — in reaches. The live reaches are marked by the crop itself, which insulates itself, growing thick and rubbery over any conductor that would kill it, so that a cable sheathed in fat glossy black leaf is a cable to stay away from. The water there tastes of copper through a mask. The lamps in the live reaches burn steadier than the rest.

The pumps push water up into the Lid. The Lid rains it back. The loop closes and has closed for eighteen months and it makes more than it loses; that is the entire difficulty. Somewhere in the circuit, water is arriving from nothing — an over-unity of weather, a yield above a hundred percent, the plant doing what it was built to do slightly too well. The datum plate on the gauge chapel reads +0.00, as it read at commissioning. The floor of the basin is forty meters down and going deeper. It does not fill. It deepens.

## THE QUIET

The noise is load-bearing.

Not metaphorically. In a valley this loud, standing waves are architecture. Where the flume roar meets the pump chord and the rain-hammer on the Lid, the pressure fields lock into figures, and the water holds shapes it has no business holding: standing walls, arches of quiet-backed water, whole rooms with no roof but a hum. Half the intact structure in the basin is not steel. It is interference.

You navigate it with sand. Every walkway has a tray bolted to the rail — a flat black plate with a handful of white sand in it — and the sand arranges itself into the local figure, fern or star or lattice or spiderweb, and the figure tells you where the nodes are. A node is a place where the waves cancel. A node is quiet. A node will drop you into water that has forgotten it was a wall.

So: silence is the emergency. When the crop gets thick enough at a throat, it eats the sound; when it eats enough sound, the arch above the eastern shallows lets go; when the arch lets go, four hundred tonnes of water fall, and the fall is a new noise, a raw broadband scream, and the crop turns toward it and begins to grow. That is the metabolism of this place. That is why it never settles. Growth, muffling, collapse, roar, growth — the basin oscillates like a badly tuned governor, and it has been oscillating for a year and a half, and the sound of a district falling down is as ordinary here as birdsong.

In the nodes you can hear yourself. Your blood sounds like a pump. Your swallowing sounds like a valve. People who last here stop entering nodes.

And the sound arrives early. A pump will fail audibly — the gravel-in-the-mouth grind of cavitation, the shriek, the bang — three or four seconds before the impeller actually lets go. Everyone who worked here learned to run at the noise, not at the failure. The rain, likewise, is often already loud when the sky above it is still holding.

## THE COPIES

The water is trying to make things.

It has dissolved so much of this valley that it is saturated with the valley, and when it slows — in an eddy behind a silo, in the lee of a drowned stair — it precipitates. What comes out is a warm pale scale, faintly translucent, deposited in layers like a stalagmite in a hurry, and it takes the shape of whatever the water last touched. A boot. A handrail. A chair with the seat worn through. A staircase going up out of the water to nothing, each tread correct, each tread hollow, the whole thing ringing when you strike it and gone by the next shift.

They are called effigies. They are not alive and they are not ghosts; they are just what a supersaturated solution does when it has been paying attention. There is a bicycle in the eastern shallows that has been re-made eleven times, each time a little more exact, and the last one had the correct number of spokes.

And in the deep water off the gauge chapel, where the noise is loudest and the crop cannot reach, the water keeps attempting something else. It builds a shape about a meter long, tapered, with a fluke and a suggestion of a lateral line and a smooth place where an eye would go. It gets most of the way. It always gets most of the way. Then the pressure field shifts and the thing comes apart into a slow snow of scale that drifts down and is reabsorbed.

There is a hole in this ecology exactly the size and shape of an animal, and nothing has ever filled it, and the water — which has no intentions, which is only chemistry and standing waves and a hundred and forty decibels of encouragement — keeps trying to fill it anyway. It has been trying for a year and a half. It has never once finished.

## STANDING ORDERS

Count the pumps. Nine is normal. Ten means Pump Nine, which was never delivered, has come on shift again, and you should be somewhere with a sand tray.

Do not be anywhere when it goes quiet.

Do not speak in the dry rooms. There are pockets behind the humming walls where the flood has not happened yet — last week's air, a mug still warm, a calendar with the days crossed off up to a day you have not reached. They hold as long as the pressure holds. A voice is a pressure.

Do not eat the crop. It is not poison. It is simply the only living thing here, and it grows toward sound, and you are a sound.

Nothing will bite you. Nothing will hunt you. Nothing here has ever wanted anything. That is not a mercy; it is a vacancy. The warmth will take you slowly and the water will take your outline and the noise will take your hearing in about forty minutes, and all of it, every part of it, will be an accident of chemistry in a valley that was built to make plenty and cannot stop.

From the rim road at what passes for evening, the Lid goes the color of a bruise lit from inside. Below, the lamps come up amber through forty meters of green water, and the horns of the crop turn a few degrees as the pumps change pitch, and the whole basin leans in to listen to itself, and the roar goes on and on and on, filling the enormous open hollow of the world, and there is not one voice in it.
