# The Carbonyl Basin

## I. The Lid

Four kilometers up, there is a ceiling, and it is metal.

Not cloud with metal in it. Cloud that has been plated. The underside of the overcast is a continuous film of nickel-iron eleven microns thick, deposited atom by atom out of a gas that rose from the works below, and it has no gaps, and it has never had gaps, and nothing has ever been seen through it. It is the darkest object in the world. It reflects nothing because there is nothing beneath it to reflect. It hangs there like the inside of a closed vessel, faintly warm, sweating, and every square meter of it is a working electrode.

Above the lid: more atmosphere, cold, thin, irrelevant. Below: three thousand kilometers of floor, and between them a volume of warm black air so saturated with vapor and machinery that from any point inside it the horizon is forty meters away and the same forty meters in every direction.

The basin has its own weather. It has had the same weather for nine hundred years.

## II. What Powers It

There is no sun here, and there has never been a use for one. The energy comes up.

Beneath the floor lies a sheet of serpentinizing rock the size of a subcontinent — olivine drinking water, cracking itself apart, exhaling hydrogen and alkaline brine at a rate that has not varied by more than a fraction of a percent since the basin closed. It is exothermic. The rock is warm because it is being ruined. That warmth is the ground temperature: thirty-eight degrees, body heat, everywhere, at every hour, in every season, of which there are none.

The condensate above is faintly acid. The brine below is fiercely alkaline. Between them, held apart by living membrane, is a pH difference of nine units, and that difference is the entire economy. The basin is a fuel cell with a surface area of four million square kilometers. Every organism in it — and there is essentially one organism in it — is a component in the circuit between the acid and the base.

Current flows up the trunks. It returns through the rain.

## III. The Floor Is Not Ground

There is no soil. There is no leaf litter, no rot, no humus, no smell of decay, because nothing here decays; things are decommissioned.

What passes for ground is a mat of root-plate: a woven, lignified, mineral-loaded busbar four meters thick, black and slightly greasy, warm underfoot, laid down in overlapping tongue-and-groove sheets that grew into each other rather than being joined. It is continuous. It has no fasteners. There is not a bolt, a weld, a seam, a rivet, a knot, or a joint anywhere in the basin, because nothing here was ever assembled. Everything grew into its final position and has been replaced in that position, cell by cell, ever since.

This is the most heavily industrialized landscape that exists, and there is not one made thing in it.

Through the plate, at intervals of nine hundred meters, rise the seep-mouths: raised lips of silica and magnetite, sharp as a fresh fracture, wet, faintly hissing. Hydrogen comes out of them in a slow steady breath. It smells of nothing. Around each mouth the tissue is thickened into a collar of gas-handling parenchyma, and inside that collar, in symbiont chambers no bigger than a fist, archaea do the only actual eating that happens in this world: hydrogen plus carbon dioxide, into acetate, into everything else.

All the rest is manufacturing.

## IV. The Gaps

Climb — there is nothing to climb, but rise in imagination through the strata — and the character of the place changes from *ground* to *rack*.

Nine tiers. The lowest is a swamp of plating baths, leaf-cups the width of a room, brine standing in them green-black and utterly still. Above that, the pump galleries: peristaltic trunks, thick-walled, ringed with grown muscle-analogues of spiral fiber, squeezing sap along at a fixed eleven-second cycle. Above those, the rectifier tiers, where fruit hang in dense clusters, each one a stack of oxalate plates and carbonized heartwood, converting the slow chemical drift into something with a direction. Above those, the transformer groves, and above those the cracking chambers, and above those the flues, and above the flues the felt.

Everything is near-touching. Nothing touches.

The spacing is four tenths of a millimeter, and it is held to four tenths of a millimeter across a continent, because the gaps are the dielectric. Every surface in the basin is a plate of a capacitor; every interval is charged; the whole volume, floor to lid, is a stack of stored potential. Growth here is not guided by light — there is none — nor by gravity, which is a minor consideration. It is guided by field strength. A shoot extends toward its neighbor until the field in the narrowing gap approaches breakdown, and then it stops, and holds, and has held for centuries, adjusting by microns as its neighbor breathes.

The architecture is the shape of an electric field made solid. That is why nothing here looks like a tree, or a building, or a machine, exactly, but resembles all three the way a lung resembles a ventilation system.

Walk between the trunks and the air pushes back very slightly, a dry pressure on the skin, the feeling of standing between the plates of something charged. Hair lifts. Nothing sparks. Sparking is a defect and has been engineered out.

## V. The Works

The process is a refining loop, and it is closed.

The brine brings up dilute metal — nickel, iron, a little cobalt, traces of platinum that go nowhere and do nothing. In the bath tier the metal is scavenged by hyperaccumulator tissue, concentrated a hundred thousand fold, and moved as chelate through the sap. In the warm galleries, at fifty-five degrees, it meets carbon monoxide made in the gas organs from the plant's own carbon, and it becomes volatile: a metal carbonyl, a heavy sweet gas that is one of the most poisonous substances this planet has ever produced. It has no color. It smells faintly of soot and cut hay. It is the reason there are no animals in the basin, and no animals in the four hundred kilometers of dead margin around the basin, and no animals anywhere downwind for two thousand kilometers.

The gas rises through the flue-trunks into the cracking chambers, and there, at two hundred and thirty degrees, it falls apart.

The chambers are gourds the size of houses, thick-walled, wrapped in six layers of dead cork and living felt. Inside, resistive elements of biochar — heartwood cooked in place, in oxygen-free heat, into pure carbon electrode — glow a dull cherry red. This is the only light in the world. It leaks through the gourd wall as a deep sullen flush, visible only from within twenty meters, and it does not illuminate anything; it only tells you that the gourd is there. Around each chamber, in the black, hang the plating spars: crisp arrises of pure nickel, electroformed, mirror-bright, unseen, sharp enough to open a hand, growing outward at four microns an hour and dissolving inward at four microns an hour.

The metal that does not go back into the works goes up. It reaches the cold lid and deposits there, and the lid gets no thicker, because the condensate strips it at exactly the deposition rate and carries it down again as charged droplets.

The sky is the return leg of the circuit. The heavens here are made of tailings, and the tailings are a wire.

## VI. The Rain

It does not fall the way rain falls.

Droplets form on the lid all carrying the same sign of charge, and so they never merge. There are no large drops in this basin, no fat ragged ones, no splatter. The rain is monodisperse: beads of two point one millimeters, held apart from one another by mutual repulsion, arranged in a loose drifting lattice with a spacing of about nine centimeters. Falling through the dark they keep station. The air is full of a slow cubic constellation of water.

And they decelerate. The field near the floor opposes them; a bead released at four kilometers arrives at the root-plate at the speed of a walking man, and in the last two meters it drifts sideways — sometimes half a meter, sometimes three — to its assigned terminal, a wet dimple in the plate or the lip of a bath or the intake of a pump. It touches down without sound. It is wicked away in under a second. There is no splash anywhere in four million square kilometers.

The rain-columns are fixed. Each is sixty meters across, anchored to a thermal plume above a cracking cluster, and each has stood in the same footprint since the basin settled. Between the columns are permanent dry corridors, bone-dry, where the felt has gone papery and the dust of nine centuries lies exactly as deep as it lay a century ago, because dust production and dust capture are equal.

To stand in a dry corridor is to be in a black room with warm walls, watching two walls of falling beads sixty meters to the left and sixty meters to the right, going down slowly, glowing not at all, forever.

The water table sits eleven centimeters below the plate. Every surface weeps. Every surface is beaded. Nothing is ever dry and nothing is ever flooded, and the total water in the basin, to the tonne, is what it was when the loop closed.

## VII. Quiet

The machinery is enormous and it is loud, in the way that a bridge is loud: structure-borne, deep, felt in the sternum rather than heard.

But the air is nearly silent, and this is not an accident. Every pump in the basin is paired with a second pump, identical, running in antiphase. The pressure waves cancel. What survives is a residue at the beat frequency, a thin whine so close to zero that it registers as a pressure behind the eyes rather than a tone, and beneath it a soft continuous ssshhh of hydrogen at the seep-mouths, and beneath that the drip and sheeting of condensate, and the occasional soft *clunk* of a relay-fruit closing somewhere in the middle distance, and the sigh of a diaphragm the size of a field.

Averaged over the basin: thirty-one decibels. A quiet library, containing a continent of heavy industry.

The reason is quality control. Acoustic energy nucleates defects in electroformed metal. A shout would put a flaw in a spar. So the noise was cancelled, over evolutionary time, by a lineage that could not tolerate its own racket, and the cancellation is now perfect and permanent, and the great works run in a hush like the inside of an ear.

## VIII. The Trades

There are seven forms and they are one individual, cloned, error-corrected, no longer evolving because every mutation is a defect and every defect is stripped.

**Pumps**, the peristaltic trunks, eleven-second cycle, never resting.
**Stacks**, the rectifier fruit, hanging in clusters of forty, replacing themselves every nine days.
**Crackers**, the hot gourds, the only warm-blooded things in a warm world.
**Platers**, the spar-bearers, whose surfaces are the sharpest objects on the planet.
**Felt**, the acoustic and thermal wrapping, a dead tissue kept in place by living hands, six layers, never molted.
**Wicks**, the low mat, the drinkers, the ones that catch each bead as it arrives.
**Governors** — and these are the strange ones. Small, dense, dark, buried at every junction, each one a bundle of oxalate crystals and field-sensitive tissue that measures the local potential and the local gap and corrects growth by microns. They do not think. They have nothing to think about. They are the reason nothing changes, and there are eleven billion of them, and they are all doing the same tiny sum.

No pollination: nothing to pollinate. No seeds: nowhere to go. No fruit that is eaten: nothing to eat it. No flowers, no scent except the chemical smells of the process — hot coin-metal, vinegar, wet stone, crushed green stem, the sweet hay-ghost of the carbonyl in the low places where it pools invisible and lethal. No motion that is not flow or growth. Nothing in the whole basin has ever moved from one place to another place.

## IX. Young Forever

The features are sharp. All of them. Nine hundred years old and every edge is a fresh arris, every fracture surface unweathered, every crystal face unrounded, because the dissolving and the depositing are equal and opposite and the shape they maintain is the equilibrium shape of that exchange.

Nothing here is old. Nothing here is new. The average atom in the living mass has been in it for eleven days. The entire organism is replaced, molecule for molecule, every forty years, and has not altered by a millimeter. There are no growth rings, because there is nothing to ring against. There is no soil horizon, because there is no history to layer. There is no patina: patina is stripped and re-plated on a schedule.

The basin is young, and it will be exactly this young for as long as the rock beneath it keeps drinking water.

## X. Rules of the Place

Energy enters from below and leaves as infrared through the lid, which glows in wavelengths nothing here has ever needed to see.

Nothing burns. There is no free oxygen. All heat is chemical or resistive; all light is a byproduct of heat, and there is very little of it, and none of it is used.

Growth follows field lines, not light.

Distance is maintained by charge, not by space.

Water arrives as a component, placed.

Sound is waste and is recovered.

Every output is an input somewhere else. There is no product. The factory's only manufacture is the factory.

And nothing is watching. No eye has ever opened in this basin. The dark is not darkness *to* anything. The plating spars are mirror-bright and have never held an image. The whole vast hushed apparatus runs at full production, continent-wide, at a hundred thousand tonnes an hour, in perfect equilibrium, with no witness, no purpose beyond its own continuation, and no possibility of being interrupted —

and a bead of water two millimeters across, carrying its small charge, comes down the last two meters at walking pace, drifts thirty centimeters to the left, and touches the lip of an intake without a sound.

And another one, in the same place, forever.
