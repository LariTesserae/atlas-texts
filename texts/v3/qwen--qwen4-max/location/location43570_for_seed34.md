## The Tableland of Standing Water

From below, it is only a cloud that does not move.

The escarpment rises out of the wet forest in one step, twelve hundred meters of dark rock streaked white where springs come out of it, and above that step the air is always milky. Climb through the cloud belt — through the dripping moss-beards, the last crooked shrubs, the smell of cold leaf-rot — and you come out on the lip into a brightness that hurts, and the cloud you climbed through is now a floor, and beyond it the ground goes on flat and pale to the curve of the world.

It takes a while to understand that the ground is a city.

---

### The stone

The plateau is a soft blue-grey limestone, fine-grained, faintly micaceous, and it was all taken from here. That is the first thing you learn when you walk in among it: every wall stands beside the hole it came from. The city is its own quarry turned inside out. Cell for block, cistern for cornerstone, stair-well for the slab that made the stair. Nothing was carried in. There is no mortar, no metal, no wood — no wood was ever available; there are no trees at this height and never were. There is only rock lifted out of rock and set back down in a different order, and the total volume of the emptiness equals, almost exactly, the total volume of the standing.

It is dense in the way a lung is dense. Walls a hand's breadth apart. Corridors where you turn your shoulders. Chambers stacked four and five deep, each opening into the next by a slot rather than a door, because there were never hinges here, never a lever, never a wheel. Nothing in this place has ever moved except water, air, ice, and the slow living skin. Every threshold is a gap in a mass. Every stair is a single stone with the steps cut into its underside as well, so that it serves the flight above and the flight below at once.

And it is old — not ruined. That distinction matters and the place insists on it. The arrises are rounded, the carved faces are softened to the texture of a tongue, the shallow grooves have been widened by a hundred thousand frosts. But nothing has fallen. The walls are dry-laid and they have finished settling; each block found its seat long ago and now sits in a shallow dish of its own making, locked by weight and by the exactness of the fit. Frost bites at the same rate the stone sinks. The city goes down into its bed about a hand's width every thousand years, and the drains were cut with fall to spare — over-sloped, generously, wastefully, by people who were building for a duration and knew it.

The water still runs. That is the thing. The water still runs and there is no one here.

---

### The walking water

The air comes up the escarpment saturated and hits the cold mass of the city and gives up what it carries.

Along the windward districts stand the combs: walls built not to enclose but to interrupt, thin, tall, set in ranks a few paces apart, their surfaces cut into a fine vertical fluting that increases the area fourfold. Fog moves through them and leaves itself behind. The stone weeps. It weeps continuously, in a film so thin it looks like a change of color rather than a wetness, and the film creeps down the flutes and into a groove at the base, and the groove carries it away at a gradient so shallow you cannot see the slope, only the fact of motion.

This is the rule of the place, and it is a rule of sound as much as of engineering: **no water is ever allowed to fall.** Not anywhere. There are no fountains, no spouts, no cascades, no gutters throwing a rope of rain into a barrel. Every drop is *walked* down — handed from surface to surface, groove to groove, basin to basin, in laminar sheets that never break into turbulence. Where the level must change, it changes over a long shallow apron of polished stone, or through a wick of packed moss, or down a spiral cut so gently that the water turns rather than drops. Cisterns fill from the bottom, through submerged inlets, so the surface never dimples.

The result is a city of ten thousand square kilometers that is entirely wet and almost entirely silent.

Water is near the surface everywhere. The pavement sweats. Put your palm on a wall and it comes away cold and shining. In the low quarters the water table sits a finger's depth below the flags, so that walking makes a soft compression sound, like breathing, and your footprints fill and clear behind you. There are basins in every cell — thousands per hectare, near-touching, each one a shallow stone dish the size of a spread hand, each one brimming, each one passing its overflow to the next by a lip no thicker than a fingernail. From above, in low sun, the whole plateau is a scale-mail of little mirrors.

---

### Quiet

The sound here is not absence. It is a designed suppression, and it has a texture.

Wet moss lines most of the interior faces, a nap two centimeters deep, and it eats reverberation. The narrow slots between chambers break up any standing wave. Fog itself flattens distance: a noise a hundred meters off arrives as though from another day. So the soundscape is a set of small close things with nothing behind them —

the tick of a stone warming, one report every few minutes, sharp as a knuckle;
the papery rustle of a moss cushion releasing water when the sun moves off it;
a single drip, somewhere, in a chamber that was never finished and has one flaw in its ceiling, the only irregular water in the district, audible for a kilometer in the hush and used, by the birds, as a landmark;
wind, which does not howl here but *hisses*, combing through the fluted ranks;
and, four or five times a year, a deep grinding note, felt in the sternum, when a block the size of a room finally completes a settling it began before there were languages.

That last sound travels. Everything in the city hears it. Nothing responds.

---

### Light and cold

The light is ordinary daylight and it is enormous. At this height the air is thin and clean above the fog, and when the cloud floor drops — as it does for an hour or two most afternoons — the sun comes down a shaft of clear cold air and hits wet limestone and the whole tableland glares like a struck cymbal. Shadows go hard and blue. The films of moving water throw caustics onto every underside: net-patterns of light crawling across the ceilings of the stacked chambers, slowly, in the direction of the drainage. You can read the plumbing of a district from its ceiling-light.

Then the fog comes back up and everything is a soft even white with no source, and the near walls are the only thing that exists, and the city becomes a place three meters across that you carry with you.

Cool, not bitter. Daytime around ten degrees, night around two, and in the cold season a rime that furs every north face and every windward flute and melts by mid-morning into the general weeping. Breath shows at dawn. The stone holds the day's heat just enough that the city is always two or three degrees warmer than the moor beyond it, which is why the cloud stands over it permanently, which is why it rains here more than anywhere for a thousand kilometers, which is why it is wet, which is why the cloud stands over it. The place has closed its own water loop. It makes its weather and its weather makes it. Somewhere out past the last districts the rain that falls is rain the city exhaled.

---

### The living skin

Vegetation is sparse and it is almost entirely two-dimensional.

Lichen owns the stone: crustose species in mapped territories, pale grey-green, rust-orange, a sulphur yellow that follows the fog-combs in bands, and a black one that grows only in the drainage grooves and marks the whole hydrology of the plateau in fine dark lines visible from the air. The territories abut and do not overlap. Their borders are older than any human border ever was. They eat the city at about a millimeter per thousand years and they are the reason its edges are soft.

Moss lives where water is handed over — at lips, wicks, aprons, the seams of the settling dishes — and it is functional, not decorative. It is the plumbing's soft tissue. It swells when wet and throttles the flow; it shrinks in dry air and lets more through. The city's water distribution is regulated, across ten thousand square kilometers, by nothing but moss doing what moss does.

Beyond that: sedges in the sump quarters, cushion plants packed into the leeward corners where dust and lichen fragments collect into a few centimeters of soil, a small crucifer with a white flower that blooms in the eight days after the wet line retreats. No shrubs. No trees. Nothing higher than your knee anywhere on the tableland, and mostly nothing higher than your thumb.

Fauna is rare and specific. A pale snail, in enormous numbers within a few centimeters of any weeping face and nowhere else, grazing the lichen crust — the city's only herbivore of consequence, and it is farming the map. A dark swift-like bird that nests in the weep-holes and hunts over the fog floor at dusk, taking the small insects that rise off the basins; it navigates by the drip-landmarks, of which there are perhaps forty. A newt in the deep cisterns, slow, pink-gilled, long-lived, feeding on the insect fall and on each other in the dry years. And one predator: a big pale harrier, one bird per hundred square kilometers, quartering the fog at wall-top height with its wings held in a shallow V, listening. It hunts the drip.

The real predation is frost against stone, lichen against stone, water against stone. It runs at a millimeter per millennium and it has been winning and losing at exactly the same rate for a very long time, which is what *established* means here. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is being restored. The city is a landform that happens to have doorways.

---

### The year, and the edges

The wet line moves. That is the great slow event.

Through the warm season the cloud belt climbs the escarpment and the fog floor rises through the districts, and for weeks at a time whole quarters go under — not flooded, *fogged*, submerged in white, their basins overflowing, their moss at full swell, their lichen dark and active. Then it descends, and the wet line retreats down the terraces a few meters a day, and behind it the stone dries to chalk-pale, the moss crisps, the snails seal themselves into the seams, the crucifer flowers in the eight-day window, and the harrier's hunting shifts a district south.

Every day, on top of that: fog builds at dawn, thins to glare at noon, convects into an hour of hard cold rain in the afternoon — the only loud hour, and even then the rain is caught by ten million flutes and walked away without a splash — and then clears, and freezes at the edges after dark, and the frost ticks in the walls until morning.

And the scale defeats you. You can walk for a month in a straight line and pass nothing but cells, combs, aprons, basins, stairs, cisterns, all of it slightly different, all of it unmistakably the same hand. There are districts where the cells are the size of a coffin and packed like a honeycomb; districts of open aprons a kilometer across, polished, ankle-deep, mirroring the sky; districts of nothing but combs standing in ranks in the wind with no chambers at all, harvesting fog for a city that is somewhere else. There are no landmarks tall enough to see over the fog. There is no center anyone has found. The far escarpment is a rumor; the water that leaves the tableland leaves it in a hundred places, and every one of those places is a waterfall in cloud, and no one has ever stood below the whole thing at once.

Nobody built anything here in the sense of a device. There is no mechanism. There is only shape — an enormous, patient, exactly-calculated shape, given to gravity and to fog and to moss, and then left.

It is still running. It will still be running when the limestone that made it has been eaten back into the sea by the lichen and the rain, at a millimeter per thousand years, and the last thing to go will be the drainage grooves, because they were cut deepest, because whoever cut them knew what the place was for.
