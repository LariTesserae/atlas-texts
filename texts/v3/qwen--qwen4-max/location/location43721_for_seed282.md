**Bay Four, Slow Works**

The stair goes down farther than the building above it accounts for. Nine flights of galvanized tread, each landing colder than the last, and then a door with a rubber gasket and a wheel-handle like a bulkhead, and a mat of sticky paper that takes the dust off your soles in a sound like tape peeling. You step through into eleven meters by six meters by two point four meters of manufactured world, and the door seals behind you with a soft pneumatic *thunk*, and that is the whole of it. There is no elsewhere. The room does not open onto a corridor. Everything that happens here happens inside these dimensions.

It is two degrees above freezing. Your breath goes out in front of you and hangs, and does not entirely disperse, and is drawn slowly toward the return grille at floor level, where it is counted.

---

The first impression is *forest*, and the second impression is *scaffolding*, and the third — which takes longer, and is the true one — is *lath*.

Four hundred and six armatures stand in the bay, spaced at nineteen centimeters, and each one is a shape made of nylon line and stainless wire and expanded mesh, hung from an overhead grid and pinned to a floor plate. They are the skeletons of stalagmites and columns and flowstone drapes and one squat, ribbed thing near the back wall that will eventually be a rimstone pool. They are empty. Or rather they are not empty, they are *early*: each is furred with a millimeter or two of new calcite, a pale grey-white nap like the bloom on a plum, and where the lamps catch them the needles of aragonite throw a faint sugary glitter, and the whole room looks less like a cave than like a cave's wireframe rendering, awaiting materials.

You cannot walk normally here. You go sideways, one shoulder leading, turning your hips at each gap, and your jacket brushes the armatures and they *tick* against each other, four hundred small ceramic clicks propagating away from you into the dim. This is discouraged. There is a sign about it. There are signs about most things.

Nothing in the bay is stone that was ever rock. The floor is a poured slab with a fall of one in two hundred to a central channel. The walls are formwork-faced concrete, sealed, then lined with a plastic membrane, then lined again with a sacrificial skin of lime plaster that is itself a product of the process, so that the room is slowly wallpapering itself in the thing it makes. The ceiling is a grid of dosing manifolds. Above the grid: more machinery, and above that, presumably, the rest of the world, though the room makes no reference to it. There is no bedrock here. There is no substrate at all. The bay is a made thing sitting in a hole, and the hole is also made, and if you subtracted the manufacture there would be nothing left, not even an absence — the absence has a part number.

---

The water does not flow. It arrives.

At the top of each armature, a stainless emitter the size of a pencil tip releases one drop every eleven seconds. The drops fall three centimeters — most of the formations are still short — and break on the crown of the growing stone and spread and thin and give up their carbon dioxide to the cold air and leave behind a ring of calcium carbonate eight microns thick. Then the next drop. The water is distilled upstream and re-dosed downstream to a recipe: hardness, magnesium ratio, a trace of strontium for the fluorescence signature, dissolved CO₂ held at a partial pressure that would be unremarkable in a soil and is here maintained by a bank of cylinders in the plant alcove. It has never been rain. It has never been a river. It has never touched ground.

The cold is the point. Warm water grows fast and coarse and wrong; the fabric comes out sugary and porous and the crystals lie in the wrong direction. Two degrees gives slow, tight, laminated calcite, the kind that takes a polish, the kind that rings. The refrigeration plant runs a duty cycle of about nine minutes in forty and the room's temperature swings by three tenths of a degree over that period, and the whole bay creaks faintly at each turn, four hundred wires taking up and letting go.

Everything is damp and nothing is wet. Condensation beads on the chilled supply lines and does not run; it sits in fat lenses along the underside of each pipe, held by surface tension, until a drop reaches critical mass and lets go and falls onto whatever is beneath it — which is why every pipe has a wick, a braided cord clipping it to a gutter, so that the condensate is *routed*, so that no unauthorized water ever lands on a formation and puts an unlogged mark in it. The air sits at ninety-four percent. Your hands feel it. Your eyelashes carry it. The concrete sweats in a fine even film that never quite becomes a sheen, and the lime plaster on the walls has begun, in the corners, to grow a soft botryoidal crust of its own, unplanned, tolerated, watched.

Twice a week, at 04:00, the overhead spray bar runs a ninety-second event. This is called *weather*. It is the only time water in the bay is allowed to fall more than three centimeters, and it produces a sound like applause in another building, and afterward the whole room drips for an hour in a ragged descending rhythm as the excess finds its way to the channel. The log calls these episodes P-events. Nobody calls them rain, out loud.

---

Light: a dozen low fixtures in wire cages, orange as a sodium streetlamp seen through fog, throwing maybe twelve lux at knee height and less above. It is dim on purpose. Light is a nutrient and nutrients are contamination. In the gloom the calcite reads as bone, as candle wax, as the inside of a shell; the wire armatures read as black; the far wall is not really visible, only inferred from where the formations stop. The green exit sign is the brightest object in the room and has been fitted with a hood so it doesn't spill.

There is a UV inspection lamp on a coiled cord by the door. Under it the whole bay goes violet-black and the stone lights up: each lamination fluoresces a slightly different blue-white according to the strontium dose that week, so that a broken stub shows banding like tree rings, like varves, like a barcode. You can read the maintenance history off a fracture. You can see the fortnight in March when the dosing pump drifted.

Quiet, mostly. The drip is the loudest thing and it is not loud — a tick with a little liquid vowel in it, four hundred of them out of phase, arriving as a soft continuous granular patter, like very distant frying, like sand shifted by wind. Under it, a fifty-hertz hum from the transformer. Every so often a relay in the plant alcove goes *clack* and the sound moves through the wire forest and comes back changed. And under all of it your own body: your jacket, your knees, the wet click of your mouth, your heart, which after ten minutes in here becomes an embarrassing thing to be carrying.

---

Life is present and is not welcome.

In the floor channel, where a gasket weeps a little light, there is a green film — a cold-tolerant algae, thin as a stain, running about forty centimeters before it gives up. It is on the schedule. It is scrubbed on Thursdays with a brush and a syringe of hypochlorite and it comes back by Sunday, and there is a running joke about it that nobody has made in six years. In the corner, under its own hooded lamp, in a tray of sterile lime grit, sits a cushion of moss the size of a fist: a reference specimen, a control, a thing to compare the stone against. It is the only green in the room that is allowed. It is watered by pipette. It is, in every meaningful sense, an instrument.

Nothing else lives here. Nothing with legs, nothing with wings, nothing that eats. The sticky mat, the gasket, the positive pressure, the two-stage filter — all of it is aimed at keeping out anything that might die in here and leave a body. A moth got in once, in the second year, and made it four meters, and died on the crown of armature 117, and was found in a routine inspection, and the crown was cut off and bagged and replaced, and there is a laminated card about it near the door, with a photograph, and a date, and no comment.

---

Here is the part that takes longest to understand.

Nothing in this room has worn. Every arris is sharp. Every cut end is a cut end. The concrete still has the grain of its formwork; the wire still has the burr from the snips; the plaster is unmarked by anything but its own accretion. There is no sediment. There is no rounded thing anywhere, no smoothed lip, no scalloped flank, no polish except the polish the crystal takes by nature. On the floor by the door stands a two-liter glass jar, empty, cleaned, sealed, labeled in marker: **FIRST**. It is the sediment trap's downstream sample vessel. It has been waiting eleven years for a grain.

Wear is a later phase. Wear is a different department, three levels up, with its own budget: the abrasion rig, the acid-drip cabinet, the tumbling drum, the long slow business of taking a made thing and making it look *survived*. Bay Four is upstream of all that. Bay Four is where the material is put on, cold and slow and logged, eight microns at a time, and its whole ambition is to produce something that can eventually be convincingly damaged.

Because the survey exists. The survey of a chamber one hundred and forty meters below a hillside that has not yet been located, drawn from a scan whose provenance is in a folder nobody in the bay has clearance to open: a room eleven by six by two point four, dense with columns, nineteen-centimeter spacing, damp, cold, unlit, unvisited. Four hundred and six formations. They are growing it here to match. They are growing the spare.

The dates stenciled on the floor plates are not installation dates. They are completion estimates, and they run out past the end of the century, and the little ones near the door are already overdue.

---

You stand still for a while, sideways between two of them, and the room ticks around you and the refrigeration cuts in and four hundred wires take up their slack. A drop lands on the crown of 117 — the replaced one, the moth one — and spreads, and thins, and gives up its gas, and leaves eight microns.

Some of that water was your breath. It went out of you into the cold, hung, drifted, was pulled to the grille, was recovered, chilled, dosed, and sent back up the manifold, and now it is on the stone, and it will be in the stone, and in nine hundred years someone with a UV lamp will hold a broken stub up to the violet and see a faint bright band and write, in a folder nobody has clearance to open, *anomalous strontium, week forty-one, cause unknown*.
