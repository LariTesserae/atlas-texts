# THE ANHER VAULT

## The Pale

Nine hundred meters of shale and salt lie overhead, and none of it matters to the light.

The ceiling of the vault is a living organ — a continuous sheet of photophore tissue, pale gold, veined in bronze, hung two hundred meters up across forty kilometers of dark rock. It is called the Pale, and it burns rock for a living. Deep in its anchoring roots, sulfides and kerogen are oxidized in slow enzymatic furnaces; the waste is light. What arrives at the floor is a clean overcast noon, soft-shadowed, the color of skimmed milk with a drop of honey in it. You can read by it. You can see the grain in a beam a hundred paces off. Children who have never been up assume the sky is a ceiling because it is.

The Pale runs a nineteen-hour day. At the end of the working turn it goes amber, then a low ember-red, and the whole vault turns the color of the inside of a peach. When the Cracking House draws heavy, the sky browns out in patches — a district's factories pulling fuel that the ceiling was going to shine with. There are laws about this. There are always laws about light.

Nothing here steams. You cannot see your breath.

## The Mat

Everything below the light is plant, and most of it is one plant, or a handful grafted so long ago that the seams have healed past finding.

The floor is a crust: cork-dry, ankle-deep, olive going to liver-violet, cracked into plates like the hide of something sleeping. It is not soil. There is no soil in the Anher Vault. Soil is a thing that takes water and time, and there is only one of those. Under the crust the mat runs its roots into raw fracture-flour, into shale still sharp enough to open a palm, and it eats the rock directly, cracking kerogen out of the stone with acid it makes in glands the size of a fist.

Nothing here has been rolled. That is the first thing a visitor from above notices and the last thing they can stop noticing. The talus under the walls is all arrises and blades, conchoidal, glittering, fresh as a struck flint. A block that fell two centuries ago still has edges you could shave with. There are no pebbles. There is no clay, no roundness, no silt, no rust — iron here goes green-black and stays put. Erosion has not happened yet. It is being attempted. The roots are the only weather that touches stone, and they work at a millimeter a year, and they are patient in a way that makes the city look frantic.

In the Assay Hall they keep a single river pebble in a lead-glass case, gray and smooth and obscene, hauled down a shaft four generations ago. People go to look at it. Some find it beautiful. Some have to leave the room.

## The Between

The vault is enormous and mostly empty.

From the tram platform at Node you can see the glow of six districts and the dark gaps between them, and the gaps are the larger part. Ten kilometers of bare mat, unbroken, unlit but for the marker-trees — single grown poles spaced a half-kilometer apart, each carrying a photophore bulb the size of a head, each tended by someone who walks a circuit and is otherwise alone. The tram rail is lignin, grown in place, and it has to be pruned weekly or it will sprout and throw the cars.

The city is real: eleven wards, a freight schedule, a foundry, a debt court, forty thousand people. It is simply spread thin across a space that could hold a country. Density here is a choice made by the mat, which will not tolerate crowding, and by the fire law, which will not tolerate adjacency. So the towers stand apart in their own halos of light, wide-skirted, buttressed, and between them the crust runs on to the horizon and the horizon is only haze — a standing mist of light naphtha that softens distance into a series of dimnesses. You measure how far a thing is by how much of its color has been taken away.

The towers are not built. They are grown into forms and then killed selectively, so that the living wood lignifies where you want a column and rots out where you want a room. A house here has a cambium under its skin. It heals its own windows shut in about four years. The chief labor of the Anher Vault, the labor that never ends, is subtraction: the Cut, the pruning crews, the endless re-opening of doors that the city keeps trying to close.

## The Works

It is an industrial town and it is almost silent.

The presses are sap-draulic, driven by mains that pulse — actual pulse, a slow systole you can feel through a boot sole, sixty beats to the hour. The mains are living conduits bound in brass hoops, and they leak, and the leaks are cauterized by women on ladders with electric irons. The engines are electric: galvanic rows, ten thousand tubers in racks, copper and zinc spikes driven into their flesh, each tuber fed and rested on a rotation like a dairy herd. They hum at a pitch just below hearing. The whole vault has that hum in it, the way a shell has the sea.

In the mould orchards, beams grow in iron cages. A structural member takes forty days. There is no hammering because there is nothing to hammer; things arrive at their shape rather than being beaten into it. The Cracking House is the loudest place in the world here and it sounds like a held breath — a low rush of gas through trays, the tick of expanding pipe, the occasional soft *whump* of a flare taking. It smells of turpentine, hot brass, crushed pepper, and under that the rotten-egg thread of the deep rock.

The Rectifier splits sap into fractions: naphtha for the air, resin for the joints, wax for the fauna, asphalt for the roads that are not roads but strips of killed mat, rolled and pressed. Lacquer wasps are kept in hives at every graft yard and hired out by the crate; they seal a splice better than any guildsman and they take payment in sugar. The augers are livestock — root-boring grubs the length of a forearm, blind, patient, guided by a pheromone paste smeared along the intended line. They drill conduit through living wood and leave behind a glassy varnished bore. The city's entire plumbing infrastructure was excreted.

People speak quietly. Not from reverence. Speech spends moisture, and moisture is the one substance this world does not make. Every citizen is a sealed jar of the forbidden thing, walking. Their suits reclaim what they exhale. A loud argument is a small theft from your own body, and everyone can hear it happening.

## Livestock and Vermin

The native fauna are dry-blooded, oil-solvented, and abundant past counting — they are simply spread out, like everything else, so that the emptiness reads as emptiness until you kneel.

Kneel and the crust is crawling. Spindle-hoppers, knee-high, the color of old piano keys, graze the mat in scattered thousands and click when they jump; the click is like a pin dropped on a plate, and across a kilometer of Between you might hear four of them in an hour, and each one is a small event. Resin ants farm the sap seams and fight small silent wars in the joints of the tram trestles. Pipe-eels swim the mains and cost the works eleven percent of its pressure, and there is a whole profession devoted to arguing about that number.

Up on the Pale, if you lie on your back with a glass, you can see the sky-grazers: slow pale herds moving upside down across the light, eating the luminous tissue, leaving cropped patches that go dull brown for a season. The sky has scars. The sky is a pasture. When they molt, the vault gets lamp-chaff — flakes of spent photophore tissue drifting down for a full day, warm-colored, weightless, catching the light as they fall. Children collect it in paper sacks and sell it to the Rectifier for a coin a kilo. It is the closest thing here to snow and everyone stops work for the first hour of it.

And there is the hush-cat, which is why the Between is quieter than it should be. Its fur is a moth-scale felt that eats sound; where it walks, the ambient hum drops out in a moving pocket, and the hoppers go still, and you notice not a noise but a hole. Hunters set traps by listening for the holes. They are not especially dangerous to people. They are simply the reason that, in a vault with forty thousand people and a million animals, you can stand in the middle of a plain and hear your own pulse.

## Weather

Every four hours the vault inhales. Cool dry air comes down the shafts, the stomata gates open along the north wall, and a front moves across the Between at walking pace, carrying grit that has never been rounded and never will be. Static builds. Once or twice a turn there is sheet lightning without thunder — a silent flicker in the haze, high up, gone before you can turn your head.

The naphtha that evaporates off the mat condenses on the cold far walls, where the rock is still the temperature of the deep crust, and runs down in threads. This is the seep, and it is harvested: gutters, catch-basins, a whole trade in wall-distillate, which is cleaner than anything the Rectifier can make and is drunk, in tiny measured glasses, by people who like the way it makes the ceiling look.

Sometimes the seep gets heavy enough to fall as rain. It never reaches the floor. It evaporates in the last thirty meters and the district below gets a sudden cool wash of pine-and-penny smell and a fine ghost-drizzle that strips the wax off a hopper's back, so that all across the plain the hoppers fold themselves under bracket-fungus and wait it out. Then the light comes back up and the plain empties and the clicking resumes, one at a time, over about a quarter of an hour.

## The Wet

There is water in the Anher Vault. There are perhaps two hundred kilograms of it, all told, and most of it is inside people.

The rest is in the Wet Shop, behind three doors, in lead-glass ampoules ranked like a reliquary. It is a catalyst. Certain reactions in the Rectifier will not go without it, and so the guilds keep it, weigh it in grams, and hate it.

Spilled water burns the mat. Not chemically — the mat has no defense against a solvent it has never met, and the cells burst, and then the rot comes in behind, white and fibrous and *steaming*, which is the horror of it: a thing in the world that makes visible breath. The wet-rot runs along the seams faster than a man walks. It is fought with salt, with fire — the only licensed fire — and with amputation.

Ninety years ago a ward called Sill lost a reactor. You can see the scar from Node: a pale blot on the floor of the vault, eleven hundred meters across, still sterile, still faintly steaming at its center on cold turns. Nothing has grown back. Nothing will for a long time. Surveyors have staked its edge and the stakes have been there so long they've sprouted, which everyone agrees is a joke and nobody laughs at.

The dead are rendered dry before interment. It takes two days and it is done by relatives. To bury a body wet is the worst crime in the code, worse than arson, and the reasoning is written out in the preamble in plain language: *we are guests here, and we are carrying the flood inside us.*

## Unfinished

Go out past the last marker-tree, past the freight loop, to where the lamps stop, and put your hand on the wall.

It is fresh. The fracture that made this vault is younger than the forest filling it — the roots came down a crack and have been widening it ever since, a millimeter a year, jacking the ceiling up, dropping blades of shale that ring like glass when they land. There is no debris fan, no weathered profile, no rounded scree. The geology has not started yet. This is a place in its first draft.

The city is the same. Half the surveyed wards exist only as stakes and graft-scars. The mould orchards are full of beams for buildings nobody has funded. In the Between, a tram line runs eleven kilometers past the last house to a platform with nothing on it, and every week a woman rides out to prune the rail, and every week the rail has grown a little more, reaching.

At the end of the working turn the Pale goes amber, then ember, and forty thousand people who have never seen a star look up at a ceiling that is slowly, measurably, being pushed higher. Somewhere above there is a thing that rolls stones. Down here the light dims on schedule, and the hum goes on, and out in the dark a hopper clicks once, and a hole in the sound moves toward it.
