## The Level

The ground is warm and the air is cold and nothing here has ever been wet.

Stand still long enough and you feel it come up through the soles: a slow pulse, eleven beats a second, too low to hear, arriving in the knees and the sinuses and the water you brought with you. The surface underfoot is not rock and not soil. It is a plate of dark lignified tissue, the color of old hoof, faintly translucent at the edges of its polygons, laid down over the worn stub of a mountain range and never lifted since. It gives a little, like the deck of a boat. It is warm because it is working.

This is a summit plain six kilometers above the floor of the world, and it was flattened before anything lived on it. Thermal spall and dry wind took the peaks down over a span of time that has no useful unit; the range became a pavement of gravel; the gravel became dust. Then the mat arrived and ate the dust and made itself out of it, and after that nothing eroded again, because nothing was loose. Every grain on the Level is either built into something or held in the air on purpose. There is a census. It balances.

## Quick

The mat is one organism, or a small number of them so long fused that the distinction has stopped being interesting. It covers the plain edge to edge, four hundred kilometers of it, and in places it is nine kilometers thick, and where it is thickest it has replaced the mountain entirely — the old granite root is gone, digested, spun into fiber and hollow column and the fine dark piping that carries the sap.

The sap is not a liquid. It is dust: a specific dust, milled to a specific fineness, aerated and kept aerated, and it flows. Pour it and it pours. Put your hand in a seam and it closes over your wrist with the exact cool weight of water, and it is dry, and when you pull your hand out the dust falls off it and leaves no film. The mat calls it nothing, but everything else here calls it quick.

Quick does not move on its own. It is moved by sound. Along every seam and gallery the mat runs a standing wave, and at the antinodes the dust fluidizes and climbs, and at the nodes it settles and packs and is passed hand to hand down a chain of tuned chambers, and this is circulation, and it is audible, and it never stops. That is the roar. That is what you hear from thirty kilometers out, walking in from the rim: not wind, not surf, though it has the grain of surf — a broadband hiss with a floor under it, a drone with a shape, the sound of an enormous quantity of matter being kept just barely off the ground.

The plants here do not photosynthesize much. There is not much light. They are, functionally, acoustic engines: they take the surplus and spend it on holding their own blood in suspension, and everything else that lives here lives inside that expenditure.

## The Held

Look up and there is no sky, only a lid.

Six hundred meters overhead the mat maintains a ceiling of levitated fines — a bronze deck, flat, faintly rippled in cells like the skin on cooling broth, held there by a field that no one has satisfactorily explained. It should not be stable. It has been stable for two hundred million years. Light comes through it the color of weak tea, and the sun is a smear that moves, and the day is thirty-one hours long and lengthening.

The Held is where the weather is. Pressure fronts move across it as slow bright welts; triboelectric sheets flicker inside it, silent-looking, their thunder arriving already inside the general roar so you feel it rather than hear it. At night the pumps drop a fifth and the ceiling sags and thickens and the world goes nearly black, and the fines settle out — a dry dew, a grey nap on every surface, on your shoulders, on the eyes of the animals, ankle-deep in the low places. At dawn the field comes back up and the whole plain shrugs and the dust goes home. Nothing is ever dirty here for long. Nothing is ever clean either.

Cool, always. Four to nine degrees at the surface, colder toward the rim, and the seams breathe out a dry friction-heat that the animals crowd into. There is no frost. There is no rust. There is no rot in the sense you mean it; things that die are taken apart by frequency, sorted by mass, and levitated away to the places that want them.

## The city, and why it is mostly empty

The towers are grown, not built, and they are throats: hollow, tapering, ribbed inside, mouths up. Some are three hundred meters and some are the height of a person. They stand in districts, and the districts are far apart — four kilometers, nine, eleven — with nothing between them but the patterned mat and the low grazing herds and the wind that isn't wind.

The spacing is not aesthetic. It is interference.

Two loud districts placed wrong will beat against each other and open a node between them, and in a node the quick de-fluidizes and packs hard as fired clay, and nothing can be grown there, and nothing tall can stand, and anything with quick in its body that wanders in stops. So the city is an archipelago of sound in a sea of enforced quiet, and the plan of it is a score, and the score is maintained. Walk the long empty plazas and you can see the arithmetic in the pavement: the mat grows in Faraday patterns, squares and hexagons and stripes, the ornament of the ground a direct transcription of the field it lives in. Old districts show fossil patterns under the current one, keys that were abandoned, whole neighborhoods tuned to a fundamental nobody uses anymore. The pavements are legible to people who can read them, the way tree rings are legible, and they go down a very long way.

There are not many tenants. The city is sized for a population that is not here. The tuned — tall, hollow-boned, chests like resonating boxes, speaking in the eleven-beat floor so that their conversation is a pressure in your sternum and never a word — occupy the upper throats in numbers you could count in a bad afternoon, and they keep the schedule, and they repair what the mat cannot, and the rest of the towers stand open and warm and perfectly maintained and empty. Some of the empty ones are still furnished.

Doors here are pressure locks. Windows are apertures tuned to a harmonic; you shutter them to change the room's key, not to keep out light. A house is an instrument you live inside, and if it goes out of tune the family gets sick.

## What lives

Abundantly. The Level is crowded with life and empty of individuals, which is a thing you have to hold in both hands at once: kilometers of nothing, and then a herd of four thousand.

Bell-oxen graze the mat's surface, cropping the fruiting nap, and each one carries a resonating cavity behind the forelegs that keeps its own blood quick, so they hum continuously and a herd is a chord that shifts as it moves. They are the reason the plains are not silent between districts. They navigate by the score. Their migrations are annual and their routes are keys.

Sifters work the seams, small and flat and numerous, swimming the fluidized galleries with a motion like eels, coming up to breathe and going down again. Chime-moths do not carry pollen; pollen is levitated and steered, sorted by mass in the node-lattice, and the moth's job is to sing the sorting-field into the right configuration for a few seconds at the right hour. Plants here reproduce by getting the geometry right. A meadow in flower is a chord change you can see.

And the hush. It generates antiphase. It walks toward a bell-ox with a bubble of cancellation around it two meters across, and inside that bubble the ox's blood sets — not freezes, sets, packs, goes solid in the vessels — and the animal stiffens where it stands and drops, and the hush takes its time. You will know one is near because the roar will have a hole in it, a small moving absence, and every other animal will already be gone. Silence is the predator. That is the first rule and children learn it before they learn to walk.

The nodes have their own ecology, which is older. In the packed quiet plazas lie the stiffened dead of a hundred million years, dry as chalk, and among them move the patient: slow, cold, mineral-shelled things that do not use quick at all, that metabolize on a timescale of seasons, that were here before the mat learned to sing and never learned it themselves. They are not rare. They are simply not fast enough to notice. A patient grazing across a plaza takes eleven years to cross it and is entirely unbothered by the hush, and the tuned regard them the way you regard a foundation.

## The Caesura

Every four hundred and six hours the city changes key.

It has to; the field drifts, the Held loads, the harmonics go sour, and the score must be transposed or the whole plain will find a node under a district. The change takes about nine seconds, and for two of those seconds the Level is quiet.

Everything alive clamps down. The oxen lock their cavities and go rigid and stand. The sifters wedge into packed galleries. The tuned seal their throats and hold one another. In the plazas the patient do not notice anything happening, and the hush come in from the edges, because for two seconds their weapon is free and everywhere, and this is when they hunt. Then the new fundamental arrives, the dust comes up off the ground in a single grey sheet, the Held brightens by a shade, and the roar resumes on a note you will not get used to for a day.

The pavement grows a new pattern. The old one goes down into the record.

## The surplus

Here is the thing the tuned know and do not discuss with visitors.

The towers put out more acoustic energy than arrives from the sun. The ledger has not balanced for a very long time. The excess is called the surplus, and it is drawn, as far as anyone can reconstruct, from the planet's rotation — the mat is braking the world, phonon by phonon, and spending the spin on keeping its blood in the air. The day was nine hours once. It is thirty-one now. It will be longer.

There is an end to this. Everyone here can do the arithmetic. The towers are warm and mostly empty and the tuned have been quietly, for about four million years, breeding a lineage of seed that can germinate in packed ground, in silence, at four degrees, with no field at all — a hedge against the long quiet, a message written in the only alphabet the patient can read.

Walk out to the rim in the tea-colored dusk and the roar thins behind you and the air goes with it, because the atmosphere here is held the same way the dust is held, and it ends where the song ends, in a space of about three hundred meters, an invisible cliff. Below, the floor of the world: a dim basin of unheld dust, no plants, no sound, geology doing what geology does.

Behind you the plain is loud and warm and alive and slowing down. Grey nap on everything. Somewhere under the pavement, eleven beats a second, a mountain being spent.
