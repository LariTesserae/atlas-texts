There is no sun here, and it is bright.

The light comes down through stone. A hundred and eighty spans overhead, the roof of the world is alabaster — a lid of pale selenite, laid down by an evaporated sea and then dissolved from beneath for so long that in places it is no thicker than a hand. Daylight enters it above and arrives below having lost its direction. It does not fall in beams. It simply *is*, everywhere, at the brightness of an overcast noon, and it has the color of skimmed milk with a little ivory in it, a light that looks warm and is not. Stand in it and your skin stays cool. Stand in it for an hour and you will find you have no shadow worth the name, only a soft grey pooling under your feet like water that has not decided to be wet.

There is one exception. Somewhere on the lid at any hour there is a region of greater brightness, an oval smear you can look at without hurting yourself, and it moves. The crews call it the Glare. It slides west across the ceiling all day at the pace of a walking ox, and beneath it the air rises very slowly, and mist that has been lying in the low streets gets up and follows. This is the only wind in the basin: a convection so gentle you learn to read it from the drift of flour, from the lean of a hanging cloth. It never blows anything over. It has never once blown anything over.

---

The basin is a bowl about a day's walk across, and it is finished. That is the first thing a stranger notices without being able to say it: nothing here is still being made. The walls are scalloped in overlapping scoops the size of houses, the signature of currents that stopped flowing before there were eyes to watch them. The floor is dolomite, older than the lid, polished to a dull sheen and worn so nearly flat that a spilled cup of water will travel a hundred paces before it finds a reason to stop. Rises exist — long low backs of harder rock, the kind of hill you could mistake for a buried animal — but they have been rounded into politeness. Even the flint nodules that stud them have been rubbed to the shape of knuckles.

Everything hard has been made soft-edged. Everything soft is being made and unmade so fast it never gets an edge at all.

That is the joke of the place, and the people who live here have stopped finding it funny. The geology is done. The churn is all in the last inch.

---

Water is a rumor that keeps coming true. The air is cool and just damp enough that stone sweats along the north wall in beads that never quite run. There is a river — there is genuinely a river — but it spends most of the year under the floor, and you know its course by the line of streets that stay wet, by the sink-pools that open in the pavement like dark eyes, twenty paces across and colder than anything else here, and by a sound you can hear only if you lie down and put your ear to the rock.

For three weeks after the surface rains, the river remembers itself and comes up through the swallet at the eastern wall, brown, carrying wrack. Then it goes away again. The rest of the year, water arrives from above.

At the hour the Glare passes over the basin's center, the lid warms by a fraction that no one could feel, and the moisture that has been climbing all morning condenses on its underside, and falls. Not rain, exactly. A twenty-minute suspension of droplets so fine they make no sound at all when they land — you experience it as a coolness on the back of the neck and a sudden clarity in the far quarters, the white city sharpening across two miles of haze. It is called the Fall. Every roof-frame in the basin is strung with hush-cloth to catch it, and the cloth is wrung into cisterns by children with red hands, because the Fall is sweet and the floor is salt, and water that touches the ground is lost.

That single fact organizes the whole economy. Above the pavement: drink. Below it: brine, and the thing brine does.

---

Because when the Glare moves on and the cool comes back down, the brine lets go of what it was holding.

The Bloom starts in the seams. It begins as a fur, a white nap on the underside of every stone, and then it grows — visibly, an inch in an hour, needles and feathery plumes and ugly cauliflower crusts pushing out of the joints with a force that has no business belonging to something so pretty. It gets under the facing stones and lifts them off their beds. It fills a chisel-cut and splits the block along it like a wedge. It heaves whole pavements into low blistered waves, and by full dark the city is furred white to the height of a knee, and if you put your palm flat on a wall you can feel it working, a fine dry ticking, ten thousand tiny ruptures a minute, the loudest thing in the basin and still barely a sound.

By morning a third of the city is out of true.

Every morning, therefore, a third of the city is taken apart and put back.

---

This is what the building is: not a stock of structures but a rate of repair.

The old work — the deep work, the seven buried cities you find when you dig a cellar and hit a pavement, and dig through that and hit a doorway, and dig through *that* and find a lintel of black river-wood worth more than the house above it — the old work was mortared, and it is all rubble now, dissolved from the joints outward. The jointers saw to that: small pale crabs, one every ten paces if you're counting, which is millions if you're not, that mine the lime out of any mortar they can reach and leave the aggregate lying in neat grey drifts. Between the crabs and the salt, nothing glued has survived here in nine hundred years.

So the new work is dry. Blocks of gypsum, soft enough to carve with a copper blade and dress with a flint adze, cut into keyed shapes that lock by geometry: dovetails, wedges, pegs of horn, courses held in compression by tensioned sinew cable run through drilled channels and tightened with a bone spanner. A house here is a held breath. It has to be re-tensioned every day, the way an instrument is tuned, and the crews go round their quarter at first light with their spanners and their scrapers, sweeping the bloom into barrows, easing the joints, checking the cables by the note they give when you touch them — not pluck; touch, and read the tremor in your fingertip.

Nothing is finished. Nothing is meant to be. A wall that has stood untouched for a season is a wall that has been abandoned.

And it is *sparse*. That is the strangeness of a city built for a hundred thousand and kept by perhaps three. You can stand on the Bone Stair and see nine or ten quarters at once, miles of wall and frame and drying rack and plaza, and count the working figures on your fingers: a scraping crew here, two roofers there, a herd crossing an avenue a half-mile off, a single person sitting on a step doing something small with their hands. All that labor, spread so thin across so much stone that the aggregate hum of it never reaches you as noise. The turbulence is real. It is simply distributed. From any one place, the world looks like a bright empty ruin with somebody quietly sweeping in it.

Most of it *is* empty. The center is emptiest, and brightest, and the two facts are the same fact: where the lid is thinnest, the light is strongest, and where the light is strongest, the roof is nearest to opening. The Glare Quarter has been evacuated for four generations. Its plazas are white on white, its salt stands in drifts like the skeletons of hedges, and once or twice a season a sheet of the lid lets go up there and comes down two hundred spans into a street deep with flour, and the impact makes a soft *whump*, no louder than a dropped sack, and a white cloud rises and hangs for six hours and slowly settles on everything.

The crews watch the brightness the way sailors watch a barometer. To live where the light is lovely is to be a fool.

---

Nothing here is green, and almost everything here is alive.

The pallids come through in their tens of thousands: blind, hairless, the size of a large dog, skin the translucent pink-white of a fingernail held to a candle, six limbs ending in pads like wet chamois. They have no voice box. They have never had one. They graze the chemotrophic films that grow along the seep-lines, and they eat the flour, and they move through the streets in slow rivers that take an hour to pass a given door, and the sound they make is the sound of a hand smoothing a sheet — a faint suck and release, ten thousand times, which after a while you stop hearing and start feeling as a texture in the air.

They talk with their feet. The drumming is below hearing, a pulse you take in the soles and the sternum, and when a big herd turns you feel your ribs sympathize before you see anything move. The street plan of the city follows their routes. Their routes follow the water's old routes. The water wrote the map, the herds keep it, the people pave it, and no one has laid out a new avenue in living memory, because to do so you would have to persuade a hundred thousand blind animals, and they cannot be persuaded, only waited for.

Above them, in the rising air under the Glare, stand the columns of motes: felt-winged, mute, hatching in the sink-pools, living a day, dying in the downdraft at the cold walls. They fall all afternoon. They drift ankle-deep in the lee of every wall. Flour is the soil of this plantless world — the medium the fungus beds are seeded in, the food of the films, the mattress that catches falling ceilings. In the cellars, on beds of it, grow the shelf-fungus whose felted flesh becomes hush-cloth, tool-wrap, aprons, cable-sheath, the wrapping on every hammer head in the basin.

And through the flour, on its own schedule, comes the vein: a plasmodium the size of a plaza, a single yellow organism with no body worth the word, advancing across the pavement at the speed of a minute hand, veining the white with sulfur-colored channels, eating the drifts, retreating from salt. It arrives in a quarter about once in eleven days. Households set out offerings it does not want and read its branching for the week's routes, and it is, in a manner nobody here would put that way, the most reliable thing in the city: it always finds the shortest path between two foods, and it has never once been wrong about where the wet streets are.

In the sink-pools there are translucent shrimp and a flat fish that lies on the bottom exactly like a paving stone until something walks past its mouth. In the dissolution pockets of the lid nest the chalk owls, which hunt the mote columns and are the only things in the basin that cast a moving shadow — a soft grey blur that slides across a plaza, and you look up, and there it is, and it is already gone, and it never made a sound.

---

Quiet is law, and the law is only the polite half of it.

The rock is porous and the flour is deep and the world eats sound; a shout dies at forty paces. But a shout also turns a herd, and a turned herd in a bowl with one swallet is a thing that kills people, and there is a further matter that the crews will tell you about reluctantly: a sufficiently loud, sufficiently low sound will bring a sheet of the lid down. It has happened. There is a quarter under a fresh scar that nobody names.

So: felt on every tool. Sinew instead of iron pin. Whisper, and mostly hand-sign, a whole vocabulary of small motions performed at waist height so the herd won't read the shadows. Children are taught to set things down instead of putting them down. The loudest permitted noise in a working day is the scrape of a flint blade dressing gypsum, and it is not loud. Time is kept by the Glare, by the Fall, by the herd-crossings, and by a hanging plate of alabaster in each plaza that you touch, not strike, to feel the hour vibrate in the bones of your hand.

You hear your own blood here. Visitors find this unbearable for about three days and then find they cannot sleep anywhere else.

---

They are waiting for the sky, and they are afraid of it.

The lid is thinning. It has been thinning since before the first pavement. Everyone can do the arithmetic of the Glare's brightening, and the priests do it out loud, and the answer is that one day — not soon, not in anyone's grandchildren — the roof will go, and the basin will be a valley, and there will be sun and wind and rain that makes noise, and the salt will be washed out and the bloom will stop and the city will be able to *stay built*.

And there will be green.

This is the part they don't like. Every flood brings seed in the wrack, and every flood the weeders walk the wrack-line pulling it, and there is a law about it, and the law is older than the reason. High in the eastern swallet, in a crack where surface light comes in raw and unfiltered, something has taken root that no one can reach: a stain of green the size of a cloak, visible at midday as a color that does not belong to any stone here. Crews have gone up with rope twice in a generation. Both times they came back down without it. The story is that they were turned around by the sight of it, which is probably not true, and is what everybody says.

---

Late afternoon, then. The Glare has slid to the western wall and gone the color of weak tea; the Fall comes and goes, twenty minutes of coolness on the neck; the flour starts drifting east; the herds begin their long turn, and you feel it arrive in your chest a full minute before the avenue fills.

The crews come out with their barrows and their spanners and stand a moment in the roofless streets, in the shadowless light, under the enormous glowing lid, waiting for the cool.

Then, at their feet, in the seams, the white fur begins to grow — silently, beautifully, at an inch an hour, taking the city apart.

And tomorrow they will put it back.
