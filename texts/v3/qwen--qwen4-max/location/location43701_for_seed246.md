## The Pan

You hear it an hour before you see it, and for that hour you assume it is weather.

The ground here is a plain that has forgotten it was ever mountains. Not a plateau in the proud sense — no cliffs, no escarpment, nothing to stand at the edge of and feel tall. Just a grey table lying near the sky, worn down through a length of time that makes the word *ancient* sound like a recent event. The roots of the range are still in it, the way the knot is still in a board that has been planed to nothing: bands of quartzite standing on end, tilted, ground flat, visible only as stripes of slightly different grey in the pavement. The pavement itself is a mosaic of small wind-faceted stones, three-sided, polished on their upper faces, fitted tight as tesserae. Everything softer left long ago. What remains is what could not be moved.

There is no slope anywhere. There is no shelter anywhere. The light is ordinary daylight — sun, cloud-shadow, the plain honest blue overhead — and it is merciless in the way that only high thin light is: your face burns while the air in your lungs stays cold, and your breath fogs at noon in the shade of a stone the size of a dog. Frost most nights of the year. Nothing tall enough to cast a shadow longer than yourself.

And under the wind, arriving from ahead like the sound of a distant crowd or a river in a gorge, a chord.

## Descent

The plain does not change and then, over about forty paces, it does. The pavement coarsens, the stones loosen, a lip of broken kerb rises no higher than your knee, and the ground falls away into a shallow bowl — three hundred paces across, no deeper than a house is tall. A pan. A single feature in a hundred miles of nothing.

From the rim you can take in the whole of it at once, and the first impression is of a town seen from a hill: pale bulging masses, arcades, doorways, the suggestion of streets, a plaza of swept stone at the center. Layered construction. Something that reads unmistakably as *built*.

The second impression is that it is empty. The halls stand well apart, each in its own acre of clean grey floor. Nothing moves between them. There are no lights, no smoke, no paths worn by feet. In a full day of walking you will see perhaps four animals, and one of those will be a mite.

The third impression is the sound, which down inside the bowl stops being distant and becomes architecture. It is loud. It is loud the way a millrace is loud, the way surf is loud, and you will have to raise your voice to speak to someone an arm's length away. It comes from everywhere and has a floor to it, a bass note you feel behind the sternum, and above that floor a dozen distinct tones from a dozen distinct throats, and above those the hiss of grit going somewhere.

Nobody built this. There are no tools in it, no cut marks, no fire, no ash, no midden, no bone. There has never been a hand here.

## Two Stones

There are two stones in the pan and the whole place is the argument between them.

The first is the bedrock: the grey quartzite, the mountain-root, dead and being eaten. Wind-driven grit scours it. Frost prises it. Water finds its joints and freezes and opens them a hair's width each winter, ten thousand winters, a hundred thousand. This stone is going away.

The second is the rind. It is paler, warmer in color, faintly translucent at a broken edge like the lip of a shell, and it is *laid down* — by a black crustose lichen and a consortium of things too small to name that live in its pores and precipitate carbonate out of dust and dew. It grows outward from any surface where the wind is quiet. It grows a finger's thickness in a human lifetime. Where it grows it armors the bedrock; where the wind is strong it cannot get started, and the bedrock is hollowed instead.

So: hollows where the wind scours, walls where the wind slackens, and the walls thicken until they change the wind, and the wind moves, and the hollows follow.

The halls are tafoni — cave-mouths, honeycombs, the ordinary pathology of old stone — but here they have been chased and thickened and roofed by the rind for so long that they have become rooms. Vaults. Ribbed ceilings where a resistant quartz band has been left standing and everything around it eaten out. Windows bored clean through a wall by a persistent eddy, their sills fluted like the inside of a whelk. Doorways with the swell and taper of something swallowed.

Break a wall and you find bands. Dust years, ice years, growth years, a dark seam where a wet century happened. Stratigraphy without a scribe. The city has no history recorded and is nothing but record.

## The Grid

The streets are hexagons.

Far below the surface, the ground is frozen permanently, and it has cracked the way freezing ground cracks: into a polygon net, each cell eight or ten paces across. The cracks reach the surface as joints. Water goes down them, freezes, widens them; the wind finds them and runs them; grit does the rest. The result is a network of straight corridors meeting in threes at a hundred and twenty degrees, running under and between the halls, floored in smooth fluted stone, wide enough in places for two people abreast and in others a hand's breadth.

From the rim it looks like planning. It looks like a surveyor's dream, a municipal geometry, drains and avenues. It is only frost, doing what frost does, for longer than there have been eyes.

There is even an aqueduct: a rind-grown gutter, half-round, running along the shoulder of one arcade for sixty paces, carrying nothing most days. There is even a stair: a ledge of harder rock split by frost into eleven regular risers, going up to a doorway that opens on the sky.

## Water, Which Is Rare and Everywhere

It rains here perhaps thirty days a year. The place is nonetheless damp, and it is damp on a schedule.

At dawn, fog comes up out of the pan's own cold — the night's radiative chill condensing the little vapor there is — and the rind, which is porous and hygroscopic, takes it out of the air like a sponge held to a spill. For an hour after sunrise the whole city drips. Every vault weeps from its ribs. The gutters run. Under the plaza, in a chamber nobody has ever entered, a cistern the size of a chapel fills at the rate of a slow tap and has been full, or nearly, since before there were mammals.

In the flat pavement between halls are pits — round, lidded with ice until midmorning, a foot or two deep, holding water that has never in living memory gone entirely dry. Their rims are the only places on the open floor where anything grows.

And at one seam, in the north wall of the largest hall, the bedrock sweats. It is not rain. It is water that fell when the mountains were still mountains, held in the pore-space of the rock, released now a molecule at a time as the rock is eaten back. It tastes of iron and of a very long time ago. The moss below it is the thickest in the pan.

## Green

The plain has nothing. The pavement inside the pan has nothing. But every seam, every drip-line, every crack in the hexagon net is stuffed with life to the point of obscenity — moss you can push your arm into to the elbow, liverworts like wet green leather laid in overlapping plates, cushions of a plant so tightly packed it rings under a knuckle like a drum. Filaments of algae in the ice of the pits, rose and bronze, staining the lids from beneath.

It is lush the way a wound is wet: concentrated, local, and surrounded by an enormous dryness. Ninety-eight parts of the pan are grey swept stone. The other two parts are so green they look lit from inside, and they are all in the lines where the water goes.

## The Citizens

Rare. Eyeless. Slow.

The commonest animal in the pan is a pale six-legged thing the size of a thumb, which grazes the crust and the moss and has no eyes at all, and which navigates by pitch. Each hall has a note. The note is set by the volume of the chamber and the size of its openings, and it drifts — rising, decade by decade, as the walls thicken and the room shrinks; then dropping a whole interval in a single winter when frost opens a new window and the throat changes.

The grazers keep station with their halls. When a note breaks, they cross the open floor, which is dangerous and takes most of a day, and find the new one. There is no other reason for anything here to travel.

Twice a summer a bird comes over the rim, drinks at a pit, and leaves; it does not stay, because there is nothing here for it and the noise makes it stupid. And once in some years something long and low comes across the plain from the direction of the cold, walks the hexagon streets taking a grazer from every third hall, and goes on. Its arrival is audible for a long time beforehand as a change in the way the wind is being interrupted.

In the whole bowl, at any moment, there may be forty animals. The volume of air per creature is enormous. The place is a cathedral with one moth in it, and the moth is deaf to everything but the building.

## The Day

Before dawn: cold beyond argument, the stone contracting, and then the *cracks* — sharp reports from under the pavement as ice takes its daily fraction of a millimeter, a sound like distant rifle fire, irregular, continuing until the sun touches the rim.

Morning: fog, then the drip, then the light coming in through the bored windows in solid bars full of turning dust. The chord softens as the air warms and the wind slackens. This is the quiet hour and it is still not quiet.

Midday into afternoon: the wind gets up over the plain and the pan begins to speak in earnest. The whole bowl resonates like a jug with the mouth blown across. Grit streams along the hexagon streets at ankle height, hissing, sanding the floors a little smoother. Each hall adds its note. The bass comes up through the soles of your feet. You will feel your chest cavity being played and it will not be entirely unpleasant and you will not want to sleep here.

Evening: cloud builds out of nothing over the flat world, drops a squall of hail that makes the entire city into a struck instrument for eleven minutes, and clears. The wind falls. The dripping becomes audible as individual drops.

Night: freeze. Tick. Pop. The pits lid over. Somewhere a wall gains a hair.

## The Steady State

Nothing here is finishing. Nothing here is beginning.

Measured across a life, the pan does not change: the same halls, the same notes, the same wet seams, the same forty animals. Measured across ten thousand years, every particle of it has been replaced. The rate at which the wind and frost take stone away and the rate at which the crust lays stone down have come, over an unthinkably long approach, into balance. The city is not a ruin and not a construction. It is a standing wave — a shape that persists while its substance passes through, the way a rapid persists while the river runs.

It has arrived at its last form and will hold it until the climate ends it. That is what all this age is: not decay, but the far side of decay, where a thing has been worn until only the sustainable geometry is left, and then stops.

There is one hall, near the eastern rim, that is silent. No note. Its openings and its volume cancel; the wind goes through it and comes out with nothing said. The rind will not grow there and the moss will not either, and the grazers give its doorway a wide berth, crossing the open pavement to avoid it, which is the only superstition available to an animal with no eyes.

Stand in it and the roar drops away and you can hear your own blood, and the ordinary daylight comes down through the roof-hole onto grey fluted stone, and out beyond the doorway the whole loud empty town goes on being itself, at the top of a flattened world, under a normal sun, forever, for a while.
