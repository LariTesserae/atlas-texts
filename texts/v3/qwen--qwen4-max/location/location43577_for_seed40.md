## The Annealing Plateau

The first thing is the light, and the second thing is that you can hear your own eyes move.

It is bright the way an inspection bay is bright: from every direction at once, so that nothing gets to keep a shadow. The sky is a white lid. There is a sun in it somewhere, but the sun is only one lamp among the many, and in the eastern districts it has been decommissioned and replaced with an array of cooler, more consistent panels, which the local birds prefer. Underfoot, the deck is pale grey and slightly warm and gives back the light so that the undersides of things are lit too — the inside of every pipe, the belly of every bird. You will spend the first hour squinting and the second hour walking on the balls of your feet, because your boots are the loudest thing for a mile.

There is no ground here. That takes longer to notice than it should.

## Everything Is Made

The plateau stands four thousand metres above a cloud floor you can see through the maintenance gaps, and it is not a mountain with a factory on it. It is a factory with the manners of a mountain. Dig anywhere and you find an earlier edition of the same place: deck, plenum, busbar, deck, a stratum of crushed swarf pressed into a paving block, deck. Six hundred metres down, the drawings. Below the drawings, more drawings, in a hand nobody uses anymore. Bedrock is a specification. The whole high country is a stack of solutions to a problem that was solved so long ago the problem has been forgotten and only the solutions remain, still being poured.

And it is *dense*. Not crowded with objects — crowded with **functions**. Machines stand shoulder to shoulder like tenements, flange against flange, their cooling fins interleaved to save a centimetre, and between them run seams rather than streets: a hand's width here, a body's width there, a cathedral gap once every half mile where two systems disagreed. Above the seams, catwalks; above those, cable trays; above those, more catwalks, so that the light comes down through eleven storeys of grate and arrives at the bottom deck as a fine bright sift. The district called the Fin Fields is nine square kilometres of heat exchanger standing on end, and it is possible to cross it entirely at the fourth level without ever once seeing the floor, and it is possible to walk its floor for a day without seeing the sky, and both walks are bright.

The air is cool and thin and smells of nothing old. Cut steel, hot oil, ozone, the sweetish breath of new polymer. No rust anywhere. No patina, no rounding, no chalk, no rot. Every edge is the edge it had on the day it was made, because that day was, at most, eleven days ago. The plateau is young in every part and has been young for two hundred years. It does not weather; it is *reissued*. A crew of small blind extruders travels the districts on a schedule, eating the surface behind them and laying it down again ahead, and the whole vast place is a wave of freshness moving slowly over itself, and the sharpness of it will cut you.

## Damp, in Pockets

The plateau makes its own weather and it is a modest weather. The cooling towers exhale; the exhale hits the cold lid of the sky; at the sixteenth hour it comes back down as a fine drizzle that lasts forty minutes and wets three districts and misses the rest. Elsewhere, condensation: the underside of every cold pipe sweats, and the sweat runs along the pipe's belly to the low point, and hangs, and drops. There is no river. There is a standpipe flume that runs when a valve opens every eleven days, and for two hours there is a river, brown with nothing, absolutely clean, and the sump-crabs come up out of the grates to meet it, and then it stops.

Water lives in pockets. A drip-tray the size of a church, clear to the bottom, warm at the north end where a bearing runs hot. A bathtub of condensate on a roof, three centimetres deep, with a skin of polymer dust on it that breaks like ice when a bird lands. Capillary films in the seams, held between two plates that nearly touch, that will not pour and cannot be dried. Everything is damp and nothing is wet. Beads stand up on the fresh surfaces like sweat on a waxed arm; the water can't get a purchase, because the surface underneath it is younger than the water.

## What Lives Here

The animals are everywhere and they are almost silent, and they are the only reason the place has any noise at all.

Finches nest in the cooling fins — generations of them, woven out of shim stock and their own hair, one nest per fin, forty thousand nests in a single hectare of exchanger, so that walking the Fin Fields at the second level you pass a small bright eye every twenty centimetres, and each eye watches you and says nothing. Loom-moths breed in the cable trays; their caterpillars eat insulation and spin a silk that conducts, and the plant has learned to harvest it, and there are collection spools installed at the sites of the best colonies, which the moths use as latrines and which are, functionally, the only farms on the plateau. Swarf swifts never land. They eat the aerial plankton of polymer dust that hangs in the light-sift, and they sleep on the wing at the top of the warm columns, and at any moment there are perhaps two million of them above you, and the sound they make is the sound of paper being handled in another room.

Shim-mice hoard. This matters more than it should.

And through the seams, on the wide gantries, move the **carriers** — slow, pale, tortoise-shaped things the size of a hay bale, with a shell that is not a shell but a callus of accumulated dust, oil, shed insulation and their own dried waste, forty centimetres thick. They walk a circuit that takes eleven months. They do not hurry. On their backs grows everything green that exists here: a cushion moss, a wiry engineered grass that escaped a spec sheet a century ago, three lichens, a sedge. There is no soil on the plateau. There is soil only on the animals, and in their guts, and the vegetation is sparse because it is *nomadic* — it must ride, because any surface it settles on will be eaten and re-laid within a fortnight. The moss has learned the replacement schedule. It fruits in the four days before a crew arrives, throws its spores at the passing carriers, and lets the deck it lived on be taken away.

The carriers are the ground. When one dies, its shell is colonised for a season by everything within a kilometre, and there is a green hill in the seam for eleven weeks, and then the extruders come, and the hill is unmade, and the grass goes on riding.

## The Quiet Is the Product

You will work it out around the third hour, when you notice that the enormous machine beside you — sixty metres of it, doing something incomprehensible with heat — is running, is *working*, and is producing a sound you could not hear over your own pulse. Not silenced. Not muffled. Its noise is being *taken*, at the source, by a collar of grey attenuators, and carried away in a duct, and the duct goes down.

The plateau manufactures tolerance. Precision is the output and silence is the grade. Noise is out-of-spec, and out-of-spec material is reprocessed, and the reprocessing runs continuously, and what comes out the bottom of the cloud floor in bales nobody up here has ever seen is a commodity the lowlands buy by the tonne. There is a pressure in your ears. It is the sound of sound being removed. You will get used to it and then you will go down off the plateau someday and be knocked over by a wind.

What you hear instead: the tick of metal expanding, one plate at a time, walking across a district over an afternoon. Drip. The papery rush of a swift. Your coat. Your knees. Your saliva. Once a shift, a finch that has decided something. The animals have all gone quiet with the place — the mice have felt on their feet, the carriers have no voice at all, and the finches sing at frequencies the attenuators were never calibrated for, in a thin silver band above the harvest, contraband, a whole conversation happening over the top of the silence where the machinery cannot reach.

## Where It Comes Apart

The duplicates first. In the seam behind the Long Anneal there are eleven identical lathes in a row, each one slightly *earlier* in its manufacture than the last, the eleventh still warm and unpainted and not yet installed and running. The inventory says a lathe is here. The shim-mice have carried its parts to eleven places. The inventory has not been corrected, and so the plateau, which is obedient, has obliged.

Then the hand. On the rail at the head of the Standpipe stair there is a wear-polish shaped exactly like a grip. Nobody has held that rail in ninety years. The polish is in the specification now. Every eleven days the extruders eat the rail and lay it down again, wear included, faithfully, an absence reproduced to a tolerance of four microns.

Then the corridor behind you. Look back and it is a corridor. Look back too slowly and it is a drawing again — flat, dimensioned, waiting, hanging in the air at the exact height a floor would be. The plateau builds ahead of the observer and unbuilds behind, because occupancy is expensive, and it has been a long time since there was much occupancy. Stand still in one place for an hour and it will make you a small deck to stand on, an occupation allowance, warm from the extruder, with a lip on it so you don't step off. It is trying. It has been out of practice for two centuries and it is trying.

At the eastern edge the plateau is unfinished and always was: the extrusion front creeps out over four kilometres of empty air at a hand's width per hour, laying deck onto nothing, and the offcuts fall, and the offcuts are re-consumed in mid-air by things that fly, and there is no talus below, no rubble, no slope, nothing at the bottom but cloud and the drawings. The edge is sharp enough to shave with. It has been sharp enough to shave with since before there was anyone here to shave.

In the tolerance-test bays, time is held loose while a measurement completes. A bolt has been falling in Bay Nine for nine days. It is a third of the way down. The moss on the carrier passing beneath it has time to grow.

## The Slow Turn

Nothing here hurries. The great conveyor that crosses the whole plateau, forty kilometres of it, moves a millimeter an hour and carries something enormous and shrouded that no one has seen the ends of. The annealing ovens cool over a year. The drizzle comes at the sixteenth hour. The carriers walk their eleven-month circuit. And once every eleven months there is the shift change, and the entire plateau inhales — a single deep chord of pressure, the one sanctioned noise, the only sound the attenuators are ordered to let through — and every animal on the plateau stops, forty thousand finches at once, and the moss fruits, and the whole made country rises by about a millimetre on its stack of drawings, and the light stays exactly as bright as it was.

Then it lets the sound go. And you hear a drip, three seams over, in a tray the size of a church.
