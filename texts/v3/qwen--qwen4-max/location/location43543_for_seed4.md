## The Kettle at Sixteen

From the outwash plain you would not find it. The plain is grey shingle and grey sedge, water standing in every scrape, a wind that comes across forty miles of nothing and arrives with no news. Somewhere under that flatness a block of glacier ice sat buried for a thousand years and then, recently — within the memory of a grandmother — gave up and melted, and the ground dropped a hole. That is the whole geological event. Nothing has had time to happen to it since. The gravel at the lip is still sharp; you can cut a thumb on a stone that has not yet learned to be round.

The plant found the hole first.

It is one organism, or it argues convincingly that it is: a felted, hollow-stemmed, cold-loving thing that spreads by plates and pipes, shingle-leaves overlapping like slate, rhizomes as thick as a man's chest running just under the surface and plumbing water upward. It grew across the kettle's mouth and kept going, and now there is a lid over the hole — a woven ceiling of root and leaf, load-bearing, snow-covered, indistinguishable from the plain except that it is faintly warm and the snow on it lies thin. Sheep will not walk there. Foxes will.

In one bay the lid failed. That is the door: a slot about the length of a boat, edges still raw and pale where the tissue tore, weeping steadily. Steam comes out of it in the cold months, and a smell — sugar, wet fur, tallow smoke — and if you stand at the edge with your face down you can hear a room.

Ninety paces long. Forty across. Six fathoms from the slot to the water. There is a town in it.

---

Nothing inside is stone. The plant took the kettle and lined it: walls of matted root, dense as cork, ribbed at intervals by the great hollow rhizomes that hold the whole cavity open. The ribs run floor to ceiling with a slight lean, pale grey-green, cold and slick to the palm, and between them the wall-felt is soft enough to take a thumbprint and slow enough to keep it for a week. There is one place, high on the north side, where a rib tore away and left the original gravel exposed. You can still see the glacier's scratches on it, fine and parallel, a signature nobody has had a chance to erase.

Everything else is plant. The floors are plant. The ladders are plant — not lashed but *permitted*, root-runs pruned and discouraged over years until they consent to be stairs. The ropes are twisted root-fibre. The boats are leaf-cori over a frame of dwarf willow and gut. There is exactly one iron knife in the settlement and it belongs to the tally-keeper and it is used for scoring, never for cutting meat.

The light is nearly nothing. The slot admits a slot's worth: a thin bar of sky that in midwinter is the colour of a dirty coin for three hours and then is not there. Below, forty-odd lamps — antler dishes, moss wick, rendered fat — burn small and orange and do not reach the walls. The walls are known by hand. After an hour down there, another light comes up: a low green foxfire on the raft of old drowned things at the water's edge, not bright, only enough to prove that your eyes are working. People navigate by drip-count and by the smell of their own district. A newcomer walks with both hands out like a man in a burning house.

It is very quiet. The felt eats sound; you can watch someone hammering sixty feet away and hear a soft tocking, as if it were happening inside a pillow. What you actually hear: water leaving the walls, which is constant, a hundred separate drips at a hundred separate pitches, the whole chamber ticking like a cabinet of clocks. Frost cracking off the upper ribs at dawn. The pop of a rhizome taking up water. Somebody's cough, and then everybody's silence around it, because in a room this size a cough is public property.

---

The water at the bottom is not water. It is the plant's weeping, gathered: sweet, faintly syrupy, clear as gin, and it does not freeze. Above it the ribs grow frost-feathers all winter; below the waterline nothing has ever frozen. In the hard weeks, when every puddle on the plain goes to iron and the sedges stand in a white crust, this stays liquid and stays sweet, and that single fact is the engine of everything here.

It also keeps. Nothing rots in the sweetwater. It preserves — fur, flesh, leaf, rope, a body, a boot. The town's lower quarter is down there: eleven houses and a rendering floor from the first generation, drowned when the level rose, visible on a still day with a lamp held over the side. Doors still shut. Lamps still lashed to their posts. A bench. A ladder going down out of the lamplight into rows.

Because on the bottom, in the dark, the drowned lie sorted. Small to large, head-upstream, packed in ranks with a hand's width between them, none upside down, none tangled. The current is too slow to do it. The plant may do it. Something may do it. The keepers call it the sorter and do not discuss it above a murmur, and no child is allowed to swim, and the hook-lines that go down for salvage are hauled up fast and never left overnight, and once every few years a line goes taut sideways and everyone stops working and waits, and then it comes slack, and whatever was on it is not.

---

The town is called Sixteen. There are twenty-three ribs.

The plant grows one rib per tide, and the town was named for the count when it was founded, and the count has moved on and the name has not, which tells you most of what you need to know about the place. The ribs are the calendar, the ledger, the archive; the tally-keeper scores them with the iron knife, a notch per tide, sub-notches for the hard winters, and the older ribs are so covered in scoring that they feel like bark.

Construction is layered and vertical, because the water rises and the plant grows and nothing here is finished. Terraces are cut into the wall-felt and shored with rib-wood, each new one above the last, each old one abandoned downward, so the settlement reads from top to bottom as a sequence of optimism. Currently four terraces are inhabited and three are not. There are stairs to nowhere, a doorway with the felt grown half across it, a whole street of empty warming-huts on the second level with their flue-pipes still connected and their door-flaps still tied.

Because the infrastructure is built for far more people than are here. Forty-one keepers live in Sixteen year-round. The place has beds for six hundred, three rendering floors, a public flue-system, a bone-yard, a tannery ledge, a guano gallery, and a bathhouse. Most of it stands empty and swept and ready, the way a chapel stands empty.

The flues are the clever thing. Smoke from the hearths is routed into dead rhizomes and runs up through the plant's own plumbing and out along the lid, so the whole ceiling smokes faintly in cold weather and the town's warmth is a shared utility rather than forty separate fires. It keeps the felt from souring. It also means that if you know what to look for, on the plain, in the cold, you can find the town by its breath — a long low patch of bare ground where the snow will not lie.

---

The animals come for the sweetwater. In a frozen world it is the only liquid sugar for miles, and everything that can move comes to it: hares, voles, ptarmigan, geese on the shoulder seasons, foxes that will not drink but will wait. Most of these arrive scattered, one at a time, spread thin across a huge cold country — a bird at the lip, a stoat on a rib, a hare's track in the thin snow above. At any given moment you might count nine living animals in the chamber and think the place half-empty.

Then the tide comes.

Every three or four years the lemmings irrupt, and the dispersal crosses the outwash plain in a grey front you can hear before you see, and the plain funnels — every scrape and run and frozen channel runs downhill, and downhill is here. They arrive at the slot in a mass and fall. The sound of it is grain poured onto a table, sustained, for a night and a day and part of another night. The sweetwater rises a hand's breadth. Bodies lie shoulder to shoulder and the town works thirty hours without stopping, gaffing and hauling and rendering, and the flues run hot enough to scorch the pipes, and six hundred people who were not there last week come down the ladders with their kits because everyone within four days' walk knows what the noise means.

The quiet, in other words, is not a property of the place. It is a phase of it.

And the tide leaves a wake: stoats fat and insolent, an owl posted at the slot for a month, fox-diggings in the lid, a bloom of white fungus on the raft, and a smell that takes until spring to leave the felt.

---

Two more rules, and they are the ones the keepers actually live by.

The first: the lid closes. In hard winters the shingle-leaves knit across the slot from both edges, and the town is sealed — properly sealed, dark absolute, air sweet and thick, for six or eight weeks. You are inside or you are outside, and the choice is made in an afternoon and lasts until thaw. Sealed time is lamp time, fat time, story time, and the sound of the roof growing shut, which is a small continuous tearing, like a scab being lifted very slowly. People are strange afterward. They come up blinking and cannot judge distances.

The second: the ribs hear it coming. A new rib swells pale and soft a season before the tide, and if you put your ear against it in the cold you get a sound — not the plant's water-noise, something further off, a crowd in another valley, a sound with too many small parts to it. Nobody has explained this. The tally-keeper listens in the autumn and then tells the town how much to salt.

Right now there is a twenty-fourth rib, thigh-thick, glossy, unworn, still tender enough to dent with a fingernail. Everything here is that new. There is no soil in the kettle, no silt, no rounded stone, no ruin older than a living person's memory. It is a fresh wound in a young plain, lined with something alive, and a town has moved into the wound and hung lamps in it and is waiting on the water to rise.

Go to the edge at dusk and look down. Forty small orange beads, none of them touching. A fog layer at chest height where the breath of the room collects, flat-topped, that you can put your hand through. The ticking of a hundred drips. And across the black sweetwater, nose up, unhurried, one grey animal swimming toward the only unfrozen thing in the world.
