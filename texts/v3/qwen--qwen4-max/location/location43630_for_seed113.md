## The Kettle

Four hundred meters under a salt plain, there is a room the size of a cathedral turned on its side, and it is getting larger.

You come into it through the Standpipe, a throat of glazed bore-tile that sweats the whole way down, and the last thirty meters you descend through cloud. Then the pipe opens and you are standing on a ledge at the lip of an enormous hollow, and the first thing that happens is that your ears close against the noise, and the second thing is that you understand, slowly, that the emptiness in front of you is the point. Everything that exists here exists on the walls.

The Kettle is roughly four hundred meters across and two hundred high, an irregular blister, bounded and knowable — you can see the far side, the little amber constellations of the far terraces, close enough that a shouted word would almost carry if the air were not already full. Below you the floor is a shallow marsh of black water and pale flowstone shelves, drowned colonnades, the sump. Above, the ceiling weeps. And all around the circumference, hugging the rock like the woven edge of a piece of cloth, is the city.

They call it the Selvage.

---

### The hem

The rock is not mined. It is declined.

At the working face — the hem — a membrane of machines a few molecules thick moves through solid stone at about a hand's width a day, and the stone stops being included. There is no rubble. There is no dust, no spoil heap, no cart, no crusher. There is a seam of cold blue-white light like a weld seen through a bedsheet, and a smell like the inside of a geode, and behind the seam there is new room.

This is why everything here is young. No surface in the Kettle is older than a season. The walls have the glassy, slightly concave finish of a thing that has just been decided upon; you can put your palm on a wall and feel no weathering at all, no rounding, no grit, nothing worn. Where the hem passes a quartz vein it leaves the vein standing proud and razor-sharp, a fin of crystal you could open a vein on, and within a week the fin is furred with new flowstone, and within a month it is a terrace with a lease on it.

But the machines decline rock, not water.

The water that was in the stone — the fossil brine, the pore-water, the ten thousand years of sealed rain — is left behind, hanging for a moment in the exact shape of what has just been removed. So at the hem it rains figures. A staircase of water stands in the air for two full seconds where a staircase of stone was, treads and all, and then loses its nerve and falls. A whole room's worth of water holds its corners, its doorway, the negative of a boulder, and then collapses into the general downpour. The old hands don't look up anymore. Newcomers stand at the rail with their mouths open, watching architecture dissolve downward.

That is the rain of the Kettle. It never stops. It is warm as blood and tastes faintly of iron and salt, and it carries the dissolved solids of everything that has been unmade, which is why the flowstone grows fast enough to watch — a fingernail of travertine an hour, cream and rust and arsenic-green, building shelves and basins and lips and gutters faster than the city can plumb them. The Selvage's whole trade is catching what the hem lets go.

### The rind

The city is a band, not a sprawl. It occupies the freshest thirty meters of wall, all the way around, and it moves outward with the hem: dismantling its inner edge, carrying itself forward, laying new district onto raw rock. Behind it, the abandoned generations hang out into the void — scaffolds, spiral stairs to nothing, aqueducts still carrying water to a district that was taken apart last spring, all of it dripping, all of it slowly being upholstered in moss and calcite. They call that hanging stuff the lace. From the middle of the Kettle, if you were ever in the middle of the Kettle, the city would look like a bright ring with an enormous fringe of ruined chandelier hanging inward from it.

Nothing is in the middle. That is the emptiness: a volume of maybe ten million cubic meters, and in it only rain, four load-cables, and the lace. The built density on the wall is ferocious — nine layers of construction, mains inside mains, a stair every twelve meters, cisterns, root-cribs, condensers, the pollination gantries — and then it simply stops, and there is a gulf, and then more of the same on the other side. The Kettle is a city with the middle taken out. It is a hollow with an inhabited skin.

The tech is past the point where you can identify it. The pumps have no moving parts and are loud anyway. Load-cables hang four hundred meters without anchoring at both ends, which you should not think about too hard. The hem's membrane is thinner than a wavelength of the light it emits. There are junction boxes in the older terraces that are simply *colder* than their surroundings, permanently, without exhausting heat anywhere, and the trees crowd around them and pay extra for the privilege.

### The tenants

Nothing in the Kettle has a mouth.

There are no insects, no birds, no fish in the sump, no mites in the loam, nothing with a gut or a heart or a fear. The greenery is lush — chest-high fern felt on every ledge, black-leaved figs straddling the cisterns, hanging curtains of a succulent vine that stores brine and drips it sweet — and it is all of it, every stand, a citizen.

The trees are the population. They were cultured somewhere else, a long time ago, for a purpose nobody has kept the records of, and they hold leases. Each household is a root-crib sunk into a fresh travertine shelf, tapped into the sugar main and the nitrogen main and the chilled return, metered, billed. They are dark because they do not use the light you use; they run on the heat of the rock and the chemical gradient of the brine and what the mains deliver, and to an eye like theirs the Kettle is not dim at all but a warm, well-lit parlor. To yours it is amber lamps and the blue seam and long stretches of dripping dark.

They compete, since nothing here can eat anything. They compete for root-room and flow-rate and the cold junctions, they litigate, they undercut each other's terraces with drip-line sabotage, they file. A fig that wins a case grows eleven centimeters in a night and the sound of it is like a rope taking strain. Decay is not performed by animals or even honestly by rot; it is performed by the felt, a fungal-bacterial mat maintained under contract, which arrives on schedule and takes a dead household apart with such tidiness that the crib is ready for a new tenant in nine days.

And because nothing here has wings, every act of sex in the chamber is done by machine. The pollination shift comes around every eleven hours: the gantries swing out over the terraces, the compressors wind up, and for forty minutes the whole rind is a racket of pneumatic hiss and the dry crack of anthers opening on cue, and a faint yellow fog drifts out into the void and falls into the sump and is lost. Most of it is always lost. There is no one to carry it. The city is the pollinator, and the city is not very good at it, and this is the central sorrow of the place, which nobody mentions.

### Noise, weather, time

It is loud. Not picturesque-loud. The rain on flowstone is a continuous white roar; the sump throat gulps every ninety seconds with a bass note you feel in the sternum; the mains hammer; the trees click. That last one is the strangest — a healthy stand of them under transpiration strain fires off cavitation snaps in the xylem, thousands a minute, a hail of small dry bones raining out of the canopy, and the tenants use it. Air is useless for speech here. So conversation happens *inside the plumbing*: pressure-language, tapped into the chilled return, heard through the palms and the root-cribs. The city talks in its own water. What you hear standing on the ledge is only the weather and the machinery, and it is enormous, and none of it is addressed to you.

The cycles nest. Eleven hours: the pollination shift and the hem's advance. Ninety seconds: the gulp. Nine days: a crib turnover. A season: a district dismantled and carried forward a hundred meters. And overhead, always, the ceiling weeping — the rock above the Kettle is below the water table and has been trying to get in since the day the room opened, and the condensers catch most of it and the rest comes down as the warm perpetual rain that makes the marsh, the marsh that makes the flowstone, the flowstone that makes the terraces, the terraces that hold the trees.

The air is thirty-one degrees and at saturation. Nothing you own will ever dry. Every cold pipe wears a sleeve of running water; every rail is slick; the moss is so turgid it squeaks under a boot. You will not be able to cool yourself here. You will drink constantly and it will not help.

---

### The lot

The Kettle has a boundary, and the boundary is not geological. It is surveyed. Somewhere in the archive there is a shape — a permitted volume, a lot with corners — and the hem is approaching it at a hand's width a day, and the machines will stop at it, because they are the kind of machines that stop.

Eleven years, give or take. Then the room will be finished: a bounded hollow, sharp-walled, weeping, with a city on its inner surface and nothing left to move toward. The lace will hang. The rain will fall in the middle where no one is. The tenants have begun, quietly, in the pipes, to argue about what a city is for once it has stopped.

Stand at the rail a while longer. Watch the far hem light up, and the water stand there in the shape of a room that has just been forgiven, and then let go.
