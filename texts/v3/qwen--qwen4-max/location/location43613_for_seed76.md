## The Lace

Light comes twice to this ground. Once from the star, which is small and white and does not warm; once from below, off a pavement of silica flour so pale it hurts to look at, so that shadows are not dark but merely cooler, filled in from underneath, and a stone casts a soft grey ghost of itself in every direction at once. The sky overhead is not blue. It is the indigo of very deep water, thinning to a hard bright band at the horizon, and at noon, if you look straight up into the zenith where the air is thinnest, the two or three brightest stars are still there, watching, unbothered by the day.

Nothing here is warm. The rock in direct sun will reach a temperature you could hold your hand against, but the air a meter above it is barely over freezing, and the air is what a body lives in. At night the ground throws its heat straight out through the transparent sky and the pavement goes to fifty below, and there is no dew. There is no frost. There is no rime on the stones in the morning, no damp patch under a lifted rock, no dark seam of moisture in a crack. The air is not merely dry; it is a desiccant, a reagent, a thing that takes. Exposed wet tissue would stiffen and curl in ninety seconds and be powder in an hour.

And yet the plain is covered, from horizon to curved horizon, in forest.

---

It does not read as forest at first. It reads as frost, or as the mineral bloom that grows on old glass, or as something the wind did. Each stand rises from the pavement like a hill made of frozen surf: a hundred meters of filigree, branch into branch into branch, subdividing past the point where the eye can follow, until the outermost divisions are filaments finer than hair and the whole mass shimmers and moves as one, the way a cobweb moves. Up close the material is spun silica and sugar, an aerogel, ninety-nine and a half parts void. A leaf the size of a door weighs less than a moth. A stand that would take you an afternoon to walk around could be lifted, if it were not rooted, by two people, and would come apart in their hands like the ash of a burnt letter.

This is what lush means here. Not mass. Area. The forest is enormous in surface and nearly absent in substance — square kilometers of membrane folded into every hectare of sky, catching every photon that falls and a great many that fall elsewhere and bounce. Seen from the rim escarpment, three thousand kilometers off, the stands merge into a continuous white nap, a lace tablecloth drawn over a continent, with the pale pavement showing through only in the seams and the old scars. Seen from the ground it is sparse: a stand every kilometer or two, and between them long emptinesses of glare and gravel where the wind goes by unobstructed and nothing at all lives.

The leaves are white on the outside and hold a thin smoke of dark pigment suspended in their middle, so that light passing through them comes out green. The shadows of the lace are the only green things on the surface of this world. They move across the flour all day in slow moiré, and where two stands overlap their shadows make a darker green, and where three overlap, something almost blue.

---

Inside each stand, at the base, under a rind of wax and glass twelve centimeters thick, there is a wet heart the size of a small room.

That is the whole of it. That is the ocean. Everything else — the hundred meters of lace, the kilometers of filament, the vast glittering acreage — is dry scaffolding built to service that one sealed chamber. The heart is warm-ish, dark, dense, and shockingly colored: liver-reds, bile-yellows, a green so saturated it looks like an error after a day on the pavement. It holds perhaps four thousand liters of water, and it has held them, in unbroken custody, for four hundred thousand years.

The water was not gathered. There is nothing to gather. It was quarried.

The roots do not drink; they mine. They go down through the pavement and the old planed basement rock, two and three kilometers, following seams of mica and clay and hydrate mineral laid down when this was a seafloor, and at their tips they pry the crystal layers apart and strip the water of crystallization out of the lattice, molecule by molecule, into a chain of internal desiccants that never once lets a free molecule touch the outside world. To win a gram of water they must process a hundred grams of rock and spend a season of light. The tailings come back up: at the foot of every stand is a neat pale midden of dehydrated mica flake, sorted, glittering, dry as a kiln. The wind takes the fines from the middens. That is where the dust comes from. The forest makes the dust that makes the sky that feeds the forest.

The spacing of the stands is the spacing of the quarries. Each owns a cone of rock beneath it, and where two cones meet there is a seam in the pavement, a dead strip a few meters wide, chemically fought over for a hundred millennia, where nothing grows and the flour is faintly stained. And where a quarry empties and its roof gives way, the plain opens into a collapse pit two hundred meters deep, and the lace grows down into it, sheltered from the wind, and there — in the pits, and only in the pits — the stands crowd. From above, the continent is patterned with these round green-shadowed wells, tens of thousands of them, healed and re-grown, some older than the escarpment. A karst made by extraction, not by dissolution. Nothing here has ever dissolved.

---

The sky is the sea.

It has currents and tides and a floor. Twice a day the plateau's thermal tide sweeps across it, a pressure wave you can watch coming as a change in the grain of the haze, and the lace leans all one way for an hour and then leans back. Off the rim, cold air pours down the six-kilometer escarpment every evening in a slow无声 cataract. Above the middle of the plain there is a dust gyre nine hundred kilometers across that has been turning, without dissipating, for something over four centuries — a beige spiral visible from the high ground, sorting its load forever, lifting flour and dropping flour, going nowhere. It never rains. It only sifts.

What falls, falls dry. After a storm the world is white to the horizon, a snow of silica flour that will not melt and can be breathed, sifting down through the lace for two days, and the lace catches it: the filaments are charged, and the dust comes to them across the last centimeters against the wind, and the stand eats the sky. Nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, the ground bones of ancestors. The litter of this forest is glass sand and the sand is the forest, and the loop has been closed so long that the plain looks identical in any million-year slice you care to take. Wind-planed pavements, rounded yardangs lying like sleeping animals, ventifacts polished to a mirror on three faces, desert varnish on the north sides. No young jagged anything. Everything here finished happening a very long time ago and has been maintaining itself since.

And the air is electric, because dry worlds are. In the dust season the potential gradient runs to tens of kilovolts per meter. At dusk the filament tips light up with corona, a hundred billion blue beads strung through the lace, and the stands glow faintly from within like lamps under cloth, and lightning goes from plume to ground in absolute silence eleven kilometers away and for one instant every leaf is a lit window.

---

It is quiet. Not because nothing happens — everything happens — but because the air is too thin to carry it. Sound has almost nothing to push against. A storm that is visibly tearing the world apart arrives as a pressure on the chest and a dry hiss like paper being pulled slowly apart. The big weather speaks in infrasound, felt in the ribs, never heard. Distance eats the high frequencies first, so all sound here is close, small, and flat.

What there is: at dawn, the tick of the lace expanding. Each stand pings and crackles as its glass takes the light, a fine dry chorus like a fire in another room, and because sunrise moves across the plain at four hundred meters a second, the crackle moves with it — a wave of tiny sounds sweeping west to east across a continent, gone behind you before you have properly noticed it, and at dusk the same wave running the other way as everything contracts. Corona whisper. The grind of flour on flour. Once every few years, somewhere, the sound of a rind failing: a long tearing crack, and then a soft collapse, like a tent coming down.

Smell travels where sound cannot. In air with no water to scrub it, a volatile lasts for days. The ozone-and-hot-flint smell of a discharge hangs in a hollow for a week. And the smell of a wound — green, sweet, appallingly wet — carries a hundred kilometers and stays legible for three days, and everything that can move, moves toward it.

---

There are perhaps nine kinds of animal on the whole plateau, and you could walk for a month without seeing one.

They are all flasks. Every one of them is a sealed vessel of water walking around under armor, and every one of them is therefore the most valuable object within a day's travel. The commonest is a low mirrored six-legged thing the size of a dog, which does not hunt and does not flee so much as disappear into pits. It carries a needle and a wax gland. It taps a heartwood, takes a thimbleful, and seals the puncture behind it with wax, because a host that leaks is a host that dies, and its great-grandchildren will need this stand. In payment it carries beads.

The beads are how the forest breeds. A stand saves for sixty to two hundred years and then spends nearly everything on one: a wax-and-silica sphere the size of a fist with a milliliter of wet inside and a kite of lace attached. Released at the peak of the dawn thermal, it goes up and out and drifts for kilometers, sometimes for years in the gyre. Almost all of them fail. In the seams and the lee of the yardangs the ground is shingle — a gravel of small empty glass eyes, each one a century of savings, each one an attempt. There is a thin-winged thing that patrols the seam-lines at four thousand meters and eats them for the sugar in the shell.

And there is a long-legged translucent hunter that follows three-day-old scent trails to the thieves, and when you see it — rarely, at dusk, crossing a kilometer of open pavement — you can see the water moving inside it. Half a liter, sloshing. After a day of white and indigo and green shadow it is the most saturated color in the world, and it knows it, and it is not out in the open by choice.

Some stands fish for these. A few of the pit-stands have learned to leak a false signal, a whisper of humidity from a sacrificial pore, and to close a cage of stiffened lace around whatever comes to investigate. They do not want the meat. They want the half liter.

---

When a stand dies, the forest closes its hand.

The rind fails, the heart begins to go, and within hours the neighboring stands have thrown sacrificial lace across the gap — a shroud, a temporary sealed tent of membrane over the dying body, held shut by electrostatic cling and the weight of settled dust. Inside the shroud, for a few hours, there is fog. Actual fog: a hanging mist of the world's rarest substance, condensing on glass, running down in threads, taken back up into living tissue before the shroud is allowed to fall open. It happens perhaps once a millennium in any given hundred kilometers. It is the only cloud that ever forms below the dust, and it is always a funeral, and no one sees it.

The dry skeleton stands for a century, a hollow glass ghost, and then comes down in a storm, and becomes flour, and goes up, and comes back.

---

The rules are few and they are not negotiable. Nothing wet is exposed. Water is inherited, never gathered, except by mining at ruinous cost. Everything that falls, falls dry. Light is not warmth. Sound does not travel; smell does. Distance is measured in generations. And the total free water of this biosphere — every heart, every animal, every bead in flight — amounts to one modest lake, spread across a continent, held in a hundred million separate custodies, none of them permitted to spill.

Beneath it, locked in the lattice of the old seafloor, there is an ocean's worth more. Life has been passing that ocean through itself, a teaspoon per hectare per century, for three hundred million years, and has by now handled all of it once. The rate of quarrying equals the rate of burial. The dust flux is constant. The spacing has not changed since before the escarpment retreated to where it stands. This is not a world in the middle of anything. It arrived at its own condition a very long time ago and has been keeping it, quietly, in the cold and the double light, ever since.

At night the pavement goes to fifty below and the lace ticks as it shrinks, and a hundred billion blue beads come on along the filaments, and the stars, which were visible at noon, are merely more so. Somewhere out on the open flour a mirrored animal is crossing a kilometer of glare-that-is-not-there, carrying a mouthful of the last ocean, sealing it behind its own teeth, taking the long way home.
