# The Wet Room

The mountain is new. You can tell from the scree — nothing has rounded yet, every fragment still carries the face it broke with, and the whole slope clicks underfoot like a floor of dropped dishes. Above the last snowline the rock is black glass and pale sinter, edges you could shave with. There is no soil anywhere. There has not been time for soil.

Near the summit ridge, where the wind should be a knife, there is a patch of warm stone about the size of a bed. Snow will not lie on it. A ring of dry rock surrounds a slot in the ridge-face: a crack a hand and a half wide, breathing.

The breath is wet and hot and smells like the inside of a kettle. Put your face near it and your eyelashes bead. Put your hand in and the hand comes out slick, scalded pink, and covered — you will see this only later, in better light — with a fine grit that moves.

---

Inside is a room.

It should not be a room. The slot admits one shoulder at a time, and the stone above it is a hundred meters of young basalt, and yet the cavity beyond is low-ceilinged and roughly the size of a place where four people might sit down to eat. The walls weep. The floor is a shallow basin of water so clear it reads as absence, until something crosses it.

The air is at the edge of boiling and never gets there. That is the first wrong thing, and it is a large one, dressed as a small one. Up here the pressure is thin enough that water should flash to steam at seventy-something degrees, and the water in the basin is hotter than that, and it lies still. The room holds its own pressure. It is a pocket at the top of the world that behaves as though it were four kilometers down — as though it never got the news about the mountain, or as though the mountain grew up around it afterward and it declined to move. The heat comes from below, the way heat does in deep places. The water arrives the way water arrives in deep places: not falling, but seeping, arriving *out of* the wall, gathering, swelling, running down in threads that are too thick and too slow, like syrup, like something with an opinion about falling.

The light is ordinary. That is the second wrong thing. There is no lamp, no shaft, no glowing mineral — the black glass conducts daylight inward from the whole outer surface of the peak, diffusing it, so the room is lit the way a north-facing kitchen is lit. Even, unremarkable, afternoon. You can read in here. Shadows are soft and slightly late.

---

Every surface is alive, and most of the life is animal.

There is no green. There is nothing that could be called a leaf, or a stem, or a root, or a moss. What coats the wet glass is a nap: a colorless film, half a millimeter thick, faintly iridescent where it thins, chemosynthetic, patient, not plant and not fungus and not really a colony either — cut it and it heals from both sides toward the cut, as if the cut were the thing that needed closing. It is the only primary producer in the room. It eats heat and dissolved sulfur and the sharp fresh faces of the rock, which is why the walls, though nowhere weathered, are everywhere *rounded*. Nothing here has been eroded. Everything here has been chewed.

On the nap: the scrapers. Pale, flat, six-legged, the size of a fingernail paring, in numbers that stop being numbers. They stand shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, three deep in places, the upper ones walking on the lower ones, and each holds a flake of black glass in a grip near its mouth. They knap them themselves — you can watch it happen, a small precise percussion, one claw against a nodule, a flake popping free with a curved fracture like a thumbnail. Then they scrape the nap and eat what comes up. They are always making tools and always holding them. A scraper never sets a flake down. It cannot; the moment glass leaves a living grip, a lime-mouth finds it — a slow translucent slug with a mouth like a wet keyhole — and dissolves it back into the water in under a minute. So there are ten thousand knives in this room and not one artifact. No cache, no midden, no nest, no arrangement of stones. Nothing made survives being let go of. The room is full of manufacture and empty of construction.

Above the scrapers, in the layer that is technically air: drifters. Motes with too many joints, riding the convection, so thick that the light goes grainy, so thick that when you move your arm you feel the displacement as a soft resistance, like stirring flour. They are not flying. They are being carried, and they know it, and they steer by tilting.

Above them, walking the ceiling: the shears. Long-legged, unhurried, blind, reaching down into the drift with a hooked limb and coming up with a drifter, or a scraper, or occasionally a piece of another shear, which is also food. There are enough shears that their legs interlace into a moving lattice, a ceiling that is more animal than stone. When one dies it is eaten before it lands. Nothing accumulates here. There is no dead matter in the room at all, no litter, no husks — a corpse is a resource with a half-life of about four seconds.

And the water. In the basin, things like small transparent crabs that are not crabs, packed along the bottom in a single continuous carpet, sorting the film off the sand grains and passing the sand grains back. And along the waterline, the fringe: a band of animals that live only in the two-millimeter zone where the room's private pressure fails and the water actually boils, a boiling margin no wider than a pencil line, where a whole specialized fauna scalds and breeds and scalds again in the time it takes you to look at it.

---

It is nearly silent.

This is impossible and it is the thing you will remember. Ten thousand bodies on glass, a boiling fringe, water running, a ceiling of legs — and what reaches you is a hush with texture, like the sound of a room in a house where someone is sleeping. You hear your own breath as a pressure on your face rather than a noise. When a plate of glass spalls off the wall and shatters in the basin you feel it in your sternum and hear almost nothing, a tick, a swallowed tick.

The nap eats sound. That is not a metaphor; it is the base of the food web. Vibration in the wet film is a usable energy gradient and the film harvests it, greedily, down to the last whisper, and the more of it there is the faster the film grows and the more scrapers it can carry. Loud is fertile. Loud gets grazed. The room has been grazed quiet for a very long time — or for nine minutes; duration is unreliable here. The animals still signal. Shears drum their legs on the ceiling in elaborate patterns and the patterns go nowhere, eaten before they cross a handspan, and the shears keep drumming, and the ones nearby answer, having felt it through the stone.

So the silence is not emptiness. It is appetite. It is the loudest place you will ever stand, with the volume turned down by something hungry.

---

Every nine minutes or so, the water rises.

It comes out of the walls faster than it drains, and the basin fills, and the whole floor fauna goes up the walls in a wave — a tide of pale bodies climbing over the climbing, the scrapers keeping their flakes, the lime-mouths sealing themselves against the glass. The ceiling population drops to meet them. For ninety seconds the room is a single knotted organism with a hollow in the middle where the air is, and the air is where you are, and the drifters crowd into it until you are breathing animals and they are breathing you. Then the walls stop giving, the basin drains through a throat somewhere below, and everything comes back down and resumes, and the film has grown a measurable amount, and there are new scrapers, adult, holding flakes they knapped in the water.

You will not see a juvenile. There are no juveniles. There are also, if you look with any care, no individuals: a scraper with a distinctive notched grip is on the wall in front of you and also on the wall behind you and also, briefly, in the basin, and each of them is doing a different part of the same scraping motion, and when they finish, all three finish.

The room measures three paces across. It measures whatever you last measured. A bead of water hanging from the ceiling holds an inverted image of the chamber, which is normal, and inside that image the chamber holds a bead of water holding the chamber, which is not, and you can lean in and go down four or five of those before your eye stops resolving and something in your balance quietly refuses to continue.

If you speak, the nap where your breath touched grows a shade thicker, and the scrapers converge on it, and something in the ceiling turns toward you that has no face to turn with.

---

Leaving is easy and does not work. You back out through the slot — you must exhale to do it — and you come out onto the ridge, and the ridge is a hundred meters higher than the slot you entered, and the wind hits you like a door, and the ordinary thin cold air is suddenly full of noise: your own boots, your own coat, the enormous scandalous racket of a mountain being weathered.

Below you the scree clicks. Above you the summit is fresh-broken, glassy, unrounded, ten thousand years old at most.

There is a patch of dry stone up there, ring-shaped, where the snow will not lie.
