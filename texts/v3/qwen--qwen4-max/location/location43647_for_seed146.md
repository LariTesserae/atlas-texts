## The Underside

Everything is blood-warm. Put your palm on the ground and the ground is your own temperature exactly, so that you cannot tell, by heat, where you end. The stone gives nothing and takes nothing. It has been thirty-nine degrees here since the beginning, and will be.

The first thing you learn is not to fall. The floor is sharp — not rough, *sharp*, the sharpness of a bottle just broken, everywhere, in every direction, for as far as anything can be seen. Kneel and you bleed. Set down a pack and its bottom is scored. There is no gravel, no sand, no rounded thing anywhere; there is no dust on the ground at all, which is strange, and later becomes stranger. Every edge is the edge it was made with. Nothing has been softened. Nothing has been carried. There is no water to carry it and no wind to lift it and no chemistry left over to eat it, and so the world is exactly as fresh as the instant it broke.

It smells like struck flint. That thin scorched sweetness, the smell of a spark off a knife blade — that is the whole atmosphere, faint and constant, from horizon to horizon.

## The scar

The floor is one surface. Not a plain: a *fracture face*, a single continuous break in a body of stone, and you are standing on the inside of it. Above you, three or four kilometers up, hangs the matching surface — the other half of the break, the negative of everything beneath your feet. In the dim you can trace a spike on the roof and find, directly below, the socket it came out of. They fit. They have never touched since.

The break has structure, the way any broken glass has structure, only here the structure is landscape. From somewhere far off, faint rays fan outward across the floor — plumose hackle, the feathering of a crack running — and each ray is a ridge system a hundred kilometers long, sharp as knapped obsidian, casting long shadows in the only light there is. Where the break hesitated it left arrest lines: curved steps, frozen mid-ripple, concentric, each one a low cliff a few meters high and a thousand kilometers wide. You can stand on a step and see the next one glimmering away into the dark like a coastline. The people call them the stairs and use them as roads, because the tops are the only places where the stone is briefly, mercifully blunt.

Above, under the roof, hangs the Silt: a dry sea of mineral flour, held up by charge. The whole cavity is electrically strained, and the dust is charged, and the field will not let it down. Its underside is a flat equipotential surface, level to a few meters across the width of a continent, faintly luminous where grains graze each other. It has bays and headlands where it laps against the roof-spikes. It never rains. It never moves. Occasionally, somewhere, one grain loses its charge and falls — a single mote, drifting down through four kilometers of warm heavy air over the course of a day, landing without sound.

One grain, per swept square, per year. This is the only clock.

## Standing weather

The air is thick — five atmospheres of heavy gas, carbon dioxide and nitrogen and a whisper of sulfur, warm, saturated in every species it contains. Saturated absolutely: it can hold no more of anything, and nothing in it can dissolve or evaporate or corrode or dry. Every reaction that could occur here occurred once, long ago, and finished, and now the finishing is permanent. That is why nothing ages. That is why the flake-scatter beside a hut looks like it fell this morning whether it fell this morning or nine hundred thousand years ago.

There is no wind. There is no thermal, no gradient, no pressure difference to drive one; the cavity is isothermal to the thousandth of a degree. But it has weather, in the way a sculpture has a shape. The crack front — the Seam, of which more shortly — sings one note, too low to hear, a continuous acoustic emission from stone held at the very threshold of failing. That note fills the cavity and reflects and settles into a standing wave, and the standing wave has a permanent geography. At its antinodes the pressure fluctuates by a hair, and there the Silt thickens into standing fogs, clouds with fixed coastlines that you can walk into and out of, sharp-edged, motionless, older than any species. At its nodes there is nothing: the Quiets, clear patches of dead-calm air, some the size of a country.

Weather here is not an event. It is a place. You do not wait for it; you walk to it.

## The crowd

The air is full. Not sparsely inhabited — *packed*, layered, a three-dimensional reef with no current in it, everything holding station because there is nothing to drift on.

On the floor, the plates: flat palm-sized animals like wet slate, moving a body-length an hour, licking the fresh stone. They eat the polarization of strained rock. The floor is under load and has been since the break, and strained stone keeps re-charging its own surface, forever, for free; the plates crop that charge and it comes back, and neither party diminishes. This is the base of everything. There are no plants here and never were. The primary production is piezoelectric.

A meter up, a continuous pavement of lantern-bells — bladder-floaters the size of heads, near-touching, each one lit from inside a dull amber, so that from a distance the floor seems to have a second floor above it, dim and dotted and breathing very slowly. Above them the cords: ribbon animals, kilometers long, coiled in loose helices that do not turn, hung with photovoltaic mineral scales that graze the Seam's light. Above them the pendants, hanging from the roof-spikes, which are so nearly identical to the floor fauna that the people believe each one is the same individual seen twice, since the two surfaces are mirror-images and identical in every chemical particular. In the antinodes, the ear-shaped things that eat sound: they sit in the loud places with their cups open and take the note out of the air, and this, more than etiquette, is why the world is quiet. And in the stone itself, in the microporosity, an unseen abundance that outweighs everything visible.

Nothing hunts fast. Nothing needs to. In a saturated, isothermal, windless world, the only scarce goods are *newness* and *light*, and both are issued continuously by a break that has not been permitted to finish.

## Quiet

Sound carries absurdly well in air this dense, and it carries absurdly badly too, because the Silt and the layered bodies and the sound-eaters take it apart within a few kilometers. A stone room the size of a continent that does not echo. You can hear a plate chewing at ten meters and nothing at all beyond the fog-wall.

The people speak in whispers that travel a kilometer and no further, and they have no word for shouting, only a word for *the mistake*. They talk to each other across the dark by flashing the Seam's light with polished blades: a scattered heliograph civilization, huts a hundred kilometers apart, a continent-wide conversation among people who will never touch. Every message quotes the message before it, because that is the only way to keep an order. Scratched archives are useless here — the marks do not weather, so a heap of records is a heap of simultaneous marks, and a blade dropped last year lies beside a blade dropped in an unimaginable antiquity, both of them glittering, both of them new.

So they count time two ways and hold both. Outward from the Blow is *when*: distance is age, because the crack ran outward, and to walk toward the origin is to walk into the past. And on the swept floor beside each hut, one grain, one year, counted and stacked and recounted, a small pale pile that is the entire history of a family.

## The people, and their craft

They are few. Scattered huts of stacked flakes at the Quiets, windbreaks that break no wind, walls three courses high with a hide of mineral wool across the top — the wool spun by the cords, harvested by hand. No fire: there is nothing to burn and no oxygen to burn it with, and so no kiln, no smelt, no metal beyond a few cold-hammered nodules of native copper found in the ribs. Flint and fiber and patience. Their technology reached its ceiling before their species had a name and has sat at that ceiling ever since, and will sit there.

They knap. Of course they knap; they live inside the finest knapping material that has ever existed, a continent of fresh conchoidal glass-stone. And here is the thing that makes them who they are: a strike releases charge, and light, and a burst of the quick that the plates eat and that they eat too, licking the new face of a blade while it is still bright. Tool-making is agriculture. Every flake removed is a meal. A good knapper feeds forty people by making forty knives, and the knives are almost a by-product.

They are forbidden to strike within a certain distance of the Seam. That distance is not measured; it is *felt*, as a wrongness in the teeth.

## The Seam, and the Blow

Low on the horizon, from anywhere in the world, there is a thread of cold blue-green light. It curves. It goes from one end of everything to the other. It is the crack front — the leading edge of the break, arrested, held, the stone there strained precisely to the point of failure and never one hair past it. It glows because failing stone leaks charge and light, and it has been leaking, without ever quite failing, since before there was anything to see it by. It is the sun here, and it is a wound that has been about to open for a very long time.

The crack cannot close: the gas pressure in the cavity exactly balances the weight of the crust above, and the roof is held up by the breath of the world. It cannot advance: same balance, same lock. A hydraulic fracture propped open by its own atmosphere, poised at critical, forever. The people understand this in their bones if not in their words. They live inside a held breath. They know that if the pressure fell the sky would come down, and that if it rose the Seam would run, and the world would extend itself into the dark at the speed of sound in stone, and everything would be somewhere else.

They do not go to the Blow. But it is described, in the quoted chains, by those who have gone: a place where the rays converge, where the stairs tighten into a single smooth swelling, a bulb, a bulge of stone with a flat platform at its center and rings around it like the rings on a struck flake.

Because they are knappers, they know what that is. They know what makes a bulb of percussion, and a platform, and a fan of hackle, and a lip at the edge. They know that a flake implies a core, and that a core implies further flakes, and that a platform implies something that was aimed.

They do not go. They sweep their floors and count the grains and flash their blades across the dark and keep their edges new in a world where nothing is old, and the warm roof stays up, and the Seam waits at the exact threshold of finishing, and nothing here has ever once been weathered, and nothing here has ever once been done.
