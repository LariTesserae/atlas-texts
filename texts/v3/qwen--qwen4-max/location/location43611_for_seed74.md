## The Sink

You walk out across the plain for an hour and see nothing but grass gone yellow at the tips, limestone pavement showing through the turf like the knuckles of a hand, and fog lying in the low places. The wind is steady and cool and tastes of salt; the sea is eleven kilometres off and never visible. Then there is a turbine on the horizon, three blades turning at the speed of a slow clock, and a thread of smoke, and the ground stops.

The rim is not dramatic. Two centuries of rain have rounded it to a lip, lichen-crusted, softened, the sort of edge you could step over without noticing until the fog moved and showed you thirty-four metres of nothing. A stair goes down. It begins in rock, worn into shallow bowls by felt-soled boots, and halfway down it stops being rock and becomes a root — a single root the width of a road, which the town has been cutting treads into for so long that the tree now grows the treads itself, thickening under each footfall, offering the next step down like a hand.

At the bottom the air changes. Warmer by a degree, wetter, still. It smells of cold stone, of crushed leaf, of sugar, and under that a fine bright thread of hot metal.

---

The basin is four hundred paces by two hundred and sixty. Inside it there is one plant.

Not one species. One *body*. It was planted as an orchard — twelve rows of forty, grafted stock, a commercial speculation in metal-bearing leaf — and the grafts touched, and fused, and kept fusing, and now there is no seam anywhere in it that a knife can find. The floor of the sink is its root plate: a continuous pale surface, faintly ribbed, warm-ish underfoot in the way of wood, dished here and there into shallow bays where two centuries of walking have polished it to the colour of tea. From the plate rise the boles, still standing in their rows, still legible as rows, each one fluted and buttressed and grown into its neighbours overhead until the whole upper third of the basin is a single continuous vaulted mass with holes in it.

The holes are the town.

This is the thing that takes visitors longest to understand. The plant is total — there is no ground here that is not plant, no wall, no ceiling, no structural member that was ever dead when it was installed. And yet the place is *empty*. You can walk a full minute down a lit avenue between two workshops and pass nothing at all: just pale wood, cool and faintly luminous, going up and up into the dark, with the sound of your own boots and the drip. Nine hundred cubic metres of living tissue for every human being in it. The buildings hang in the voids like objects in a cathedral, small, well-spaced, each with its own pool of lamplight, and between them the enormous patient nothing of the Stand.

Two hundred and thirty-one people. Nine rows still readable out of the original twelve. Everyone calls it the Rows.

---

It is a city; it simply has no extent. Everything a city has is here, stacked instead of spread.

The **undercroft** is in the root plate: chambers the tree was persuaded to leave hollow, dry-lined, cool, holding the cistern, the cell-bank, the mushroom beds, the cold room where the year's pollen sleeps in folded paper. The **floor** carries the roads — the old orchard rows, now avenues eleven paces wide, worn into shallow troughs — and the heavy trades: retort house, furnace, drawing bench, the long open shed where leaf is dried on racks. The **gallery** level is thirty feet up, in the crotches, where the dwellings sit in the forks like nests set by something with a good eye for structure: not built onto the tree so much as *asked for*, framed in dead heartwood and then let the living wood close around the joints until the frame and the branch are one piece. Above that the **canopy walk**, a lace of plank and rope and drawn wire, and above that the mesh.

There is a funicular. It is forty metres long and it is the only vehicle in the town, and it runs on a rubber-tyred drum at the speed of a strolling man, and children are told not to ride it twice in one day because of the noise.

The addresses are the planting plan. Row C, between Eleven and Twelve. Row F, at Nine, upper. The original ledger with the nurseryman's handwriting is in the map room, and the street names have not changed in two hundred years, though the streets themselves have moved four metres east.

---

There is no water here. There is damp.

No stream, no spring, no pond. The limestone weeps a little at the northern corner, a wet seam the width of a hand where snails live, and that is the only natural liquid in the basin. Everything else comes out of the air.

The fog arrives most nights from autumn through spring: sea fog, driven up over the plain, spilling over the rim in a slow cold cataract that the townspeople call the river. It comes at dusk, when the day's warm air lifts off the wood and the cooled air slides down the slope to replace it, and for about forty minutes the whole basin fills from the bottom like a bowl under a tap. Then the mesh takes it. Nine hundred square metres of nickel-bronze netting strung in the canopy, angled to the prevailing wind, and every strand of it running with beads, and every bead finding a wire, and every wire finding a gutter, and the gutters finding the downpipes, and the downpipes going into the tree.

Because the cistern is *in* the tree. It always has been. The Stand grew a hollow at the base of Row D in the third decade and the town has been enlarging it politely ever since, and the water sits in a living tank forty thousand litres deep, cool, faintly sweet, tasting of the wood.

Two to four cubic metres on a good fog night. Nothing at all in July. In summer they run the condensers instead — cold plates fed by the cell-bank's waste chill, wringing dew out of air that has to be persuaded — and the yield drops to a trickle and the ledger goes red and everyone becomes careful with washing.

Rain falls perhaps sixty days a year, briefly, without conviction. The plant drinks most of it before it reaches the floor. What reaches the floor pools in the leaf-axils, in the flutings, in a hundred small tanks nobody built, and there it stands for a week and evaporates, and the whole basin smells green and cold and slightly of fermentation.

Nothing here is ever wet. Everything here is always damp. Your coat is damp. Your bread is damp. The matches work, but you have to want it.

---

The tree makes metal.

That was the whole point of the orchard. The greenash — no relation to any ash; the name is local and old — pulls nickel out of the limestone-derived soil and packs it into its leaves at a concentration that ought to kill it and doesn't. In autumn the leaf comes off, and it is dried on racks in the long shed, and it goes into the retort, and what comes out is ash that is *green*: a pale apple-green powder, ten to fifteen per cent metal by weight, richer than any ore anyone would bother to dig.

They leach it with the tree's own acid. That is the neat part, the part the Rows is quietly proud of: the sap runs acidic in spring, citric and malic, and they tap it from the pruning wounds — the tree supplies both the ore and the solvent, and has done since someone noticed in the year Forty-One. The leachate goes to the cells in the undercroft, three hundred glass jars in racks, and over four days the cathodes thicken with a grey-white bloom, and the bloom is stripped and melted and cast and drawn.

The drawing bench is the loudest thing in the town and it is still quieter than a conversation. A slow drum, an oiled die, a wire coming through at four-tenths of a millimetre with a sound like a held breath. The wire goes back up into the canopy as mesh, as cord-telegraph, as heat-tape on the young grafts, as the shiver-wires that keep frost off the nursery on the six or seven nights a year it matters. Metal out of the leaf, into the air, back into the leaf.

The furnace runs a campaign every nine days. Between campaigns it is cold and the town is at its quietest. During campaign there is a soft continuous roar from the blower, felt more than heard, and the whole basin takes on a warmth and a smell like a struck match, and the light in the retort house turns the pale wood faintly orange, and children are allowed to stay up.

They export wire, cathode, and green salt. They import glass, sulphur, grain, lamp oil, boot felt, and steel for the dies. A cart comes down the stair-road on Thursdays. Nobody eats anything that grew here except the mushrooms, which grow on spent leaf-litter in the undercroft and are the only food in the world that tastes of nickel and is loved anyway.

---

Now the quiet, which is not an atmosphere. It is a law, and the law is physiology.

The Stand thickens under mechanical stimulation. Wind, touch, vibration — anything that shakes it — and it responds by laying down shorter, denser, stouter cells, by closing its voids, by getting *solid*. This is not strange; every tree everywhere does a version of it. Here it has been bred for and lived with for two hundred years, and it means one thing above all: **noise closes the rooms.**

A dropped anvil in Row C will, over eighteen months, thicken the walls of Row C by a hand's breadth. A season of hammering will fill a gallery. The town learned this the hard way in the year Twelve, when a forge was set up at the north end and the north end ceased to exist.

So: felt soles, mandatory indoors and customary out. Rubber tyres. Belt drives, never gears where a belt will do. Every bearing oiled twice a week by an apprentice with a long-spouted can. No bells, no whistles above a specified pitch, no dogs. Speech at conversational volume is fine — the threshold sits above the human voice, which is why the town is full of murmuring and absolutely empty of shouting. Arguments are conducted in a low, intense, close-together register that visitors find sinister and locals find ordinary.

They signal with lamps and with cords: a taut wire network strung through the canopy, tugged in a code, silent, and constantly needing re-strung because the tree keeps swallowing the anchor points. The wire routes migrate. So does the switchboard.

And out on the rim, where they *want* thickening, there are the shouting sheds — dead-wood chambers, isolated from the living mass by a collar of felt and air, where a hired person sits for two hours a day and makes noise at a chosen limb through a horn. Acoustic forestry. If you need a beam to grow heavier on one side, you go and yell at it for a season. It is considered a slightly disreputable job and it pays well.

Visitors cannot sleep. They lie in the guest crotch at Row E and hear the drip, and the tick of cooling wood, and the far soft roar of the blower, and the enormous rustle of a thing the size of a hill adjusting itself, and they say *it's so loud here.* People from the Rows go to the coast and come back grey-faced after two days. There is a word for the outside: *clatter*.

---

Almost nothing lives here that moves on its own.

The Stand is toxic and the basin is isolated and the town has spent two centuries making itself the only animal in the place. There are woodlice in the litter, mites beyond counting, snails at the wet seam, one moth — a small brown thing, the last pollinator that stayed — and a wren that comes in winter and works the canopy walk for spiders and leaves in spring.

Everything else that an animal would do, the people do.

In the flush, when the small green unscented flowers open along the underside of the vault, the pollination crews go up with brushes. They work from the year's crossing plan, which is drawn up in the ledger room in winter: which bole to which, which traits, which rows to keep pure. They bag the flowers, they label them with wire tags, they collect into paper, they carry it down to the cold room. They also carry seed, cull suckers, clean the leaf-axil tanks, thin the litter, and pick off the rust.

They are the fauna. This is stated plainly in the town's charter, in a clause everyone finds funny and nobody has ever proposed amending.

There was a rat once, in the year One-Fifty. It gnawed a cord line and it scratched in a wall void at night and the wall void began, measurably, to close. The town's response is recorded in eleven pages and is the closest thing the Rows has to a war.

---

The place is in balance, and the balance is not natural. It is *maintained*, at considerable effort, and it is the entire subject of local politics.

The Stand grows. It wants to fill every void it has been asked to leave. The Clearers walk an eleven-day round with saws and long-handled chisels, taking the new wood out of the avenues, out of the crotches, off the frames, keeping the rooms open — and the mass they remove goes to the drying racks and the retort and the furnace, so that the town's ore supply *is* its pruning waste. Growth and harvest are the same number in two columns.

If they cut too little, the rooms close. If they cut too much, the tree responds to injury the way it responds to noise, and the rooms close faster. Both failure modes are the same failure. The Balance Book records the kilograms removed and the millimetres of encroachment, row by row, every round, since the year Three, and the argument at any given supper table is about the rate.

The form itself stopped changing long ago. The flutes, the buttresses, the great slow curves where a bole meets the plate — these are settled, self-similar, weathered into their own settled state, as finished as the limestone lip above. What changes is detail. Every year the surveyor redraws the map. In the map room the old maps are stacked in order, and you can flip the corners and watch the town breathe: avenues narrowing and widening, a dwelling swallowed in one decade and re-asked-for thirty paces on, the switchboard sliding steadily north-east because the tree grows toward the warmth of the cables and the cables must follow the tree.

Two hundred frames of a very slow film. The Rows is not a place that was built. It is a place that is being *negotiated*, and has been for long enough that the negotiation has worn grooves, and the grooves are what everyone calls home.

---

At dusk the cold river comes over the rim and the mesh begins to tick with the first beads. The collectors go along the gutters with their lamps. The sun, which has spent four hours making the whole bowl into a lantern — pale bark throwing light back and forth until the vault glows like the inside of a shell — goes off the western lip, and the light drops to blue, and the lamps come on in their green glass, one here, one far off there, scattered absurdly wide in all that living dark.

In the undercroft the cells hum at a pitch just under the threshold. Up in the crotches someone is oiling a hinge. A limb somewhere overhead is loading, and a child, who has been taught to listen for it, tells her father, and he writes it in the book, and in the morning a Clearer will go up and take a hand's-breadth off it before it decides to become a wall.

The fog thickens. The wood ticks as it cools. Eleven kilometres away the sea is doing what it does, and none of that noise gets in here.
