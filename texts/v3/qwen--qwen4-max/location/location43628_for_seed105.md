# The Cistern

## Standing water

The basin holds a sea the way a cupped hand holds rain: badly, patiently, and for a very long time.

Its surface lies three thousand meters above the dry countries to the south, where the rivers are warm and full of fish. Nothing about that arrangement is sensible. Water does not stay up here. But the Cistern is a bowl of old folded limestone rimmed in basalt, and it has one drain and one lip, and the drain takes exactly what the snowfields give, and the lip spills exactly the remainder, and this has been true since before anyone was counting. The level moves forty centimeters and comes back. There is a bleached band on the rim stone, a hand's width, scoured pale by ten thousand years of anchor ice. It is the only mark the water has made on its own container.

Below that band: two hundred, three hundred meters of black sweet water. Not salt. It tastes of iron and bog and cold rock, and it is 1.8 degrees at the bottom and 2.4 at the top, and in the winter it is slush from wall to wall. Peat-stained, this water — tea-dark, the tannins of a hundred centuries of undecayed leaf. Light does not fail here because of depth alone. It fails because the water is already the color of strong tea before the sun ever touches it.

## The lid

For nine months the surface is ice: a meter of it, snow-loaded, opaque as a plastered ceiling. Under the lid the basin is a closed room. Sound has nowhere to go. It comes back off the ice, off the walls, off the floor, and stacks on itself until the whole sea hums like the inside of a bell that has just been struck and cannot finish.

Then, for five or six weeks in the bright season, the snow slides off and the ice goes clear, and there is the Window. Gray-green daylight falls sixty meters and stops. The shallows turn the amber of tea held against a candle. Everything below stays black. The Window is when the basin breeds, and it is the only time of year when the place has a shape you could draw.

The rest of the year you navigate by ear, or not at all.

## Clatter

The groves are called clatters, and you hear them long before the water carries you near enough to touch a trunk.

Knockwood grows here: pale fluted trunks, barkless, hollow between the nodes, no wider than a thigh and older than most nations. The cold makes them slow. A trunk you can span with two hands has been standing nine hundred years, its rings packed tight as a fingerprint. They are the drowned descendants of ordinary trees, and they kept the one habit of ordinary trees that mattered. Every tree in the dry world clicks as it drinks; the water columns inside them break under tension, tiny vacuums opening and collapsing, a ultrasonic stutter no one hears. Here, in the dark, over a span of time nobody measured, the click was made into a fist.

A hammer cell in the knockwood's outer wood pulls a cavity open in the sap and lets it fall shut. The collapse is a snap at the threshold of pain, and — this is the part that makes the Cistern what it is — the collapse *shines*. A bubble dying that fast compresses its own gas past incandescence. A flash of blue-white, smaller than a grain of sand, gone in less than a billionth of a second.

One tree snaps every few seconds. A grove holds four hundred trees.

So a clatter is a wall of frying noise, a hailstorm on a tin roof, gravel flung endlessly at a drumhead — and inside that wall, in absolute darkness, a blizzard of pinpricks. Blue sparks appearing and vanishing across a hundred meters of black water, too fast to follow, too many to count, lighting nothing. You can put your hand into the middle of a clatter and not see your hand. You will feel the snaps in your teeth and in the bones behind your ears, and the light will go off around you like a city seen from a great distance, and you will not see one leaf.

At the grove margins grow the barrel-traps: bladderworts the size of cider kegs, cold-slow, hours to reset, five milliseconds to fire. They eat seeds, spores, drifting rootlets, the tender exploratory fingers of neighboring groves. Each firing is a hard knock, a knuckle on a door. A margin in good season sounds like a crowd trying to get in.

## The bald flats

Between the clatters there is a great deal of nothing, and the nothing has a cause.

Eleven meters of peat lie on the basin floor. There are no grazers here, no borers, no worms, and the cold makes the fungal mats lazy; a leaf that falls takes four hundred years to become soil. So the dead simply accumulate. Cores bring up leaves still green-black and identifiable from four thousand years down, and wood with its bark on, and last century's seed husks lying where they landed like a floor that has not been swept since the house was built. Nothing here is lost. It is only stacked.

Where the peat is deep and the water is cold and the pressure is high, the rot-gas locks itself into the sediment as a solid — a white combustible ice threaded through the black. When the basin's level drops its forty centimeters, the pressure eases, and the ice lets go of its gas.

The flats erupt. Pockmarks twenty meters across, mud volcanoes, plumes of bubbles a hundred meters tall, a boom you feel in the sternum and a shudder that travels the floor. Sulfur films bloom white over the fresh craters. Nothing grows there for a decade.

That is why the groves stand apart. That is why the Cistern is a lush country with enormous emptiness in it: verdant, ancient, thick with life, and scattered — because the ground between the living places periodically detonates.

Walk a bald flat in the quiet years and you will hear, faintly, from every direction, the towns.

## The carrying layer

At about ninety meters down there is a stratum where the temperature and pressure conspire to make sound slowest, and sound that enters it does not leave. It bends back in. It runs.

In that layer a clatter forty kilometers away arrives at full strength, and the clatter you are standing inside arrives muffled, its high frequencies scattered away above and below. Distance inverts. The far rim of the basin sounds like it is against your shoulder; the trunk you are holding sounds like it is in the next valley. The whole region speaks into one channel at once — the Throat's bass, the pocks going off in the west, three groves in mid-argument to the north, the ice ticking somewhere overhead — and it all arrives together, undated, unsorted, a regional conversation with no way to tell who is near.

It is a large place. It is larger by ear than it is by any other measure.

## The Throat and the Give

At the north end, a swallow hole eleven meters across takes thirty cubic meters a second down into the mountain. It has taken it, at that rate, for nine hundred years. The roar of it is not a sound so much as a pressure in the chest, a note below hearing that makes the silt shiver and the peat crawl. Somewhere in the plumbing beneath, a siphon primes and breaks, primes and breaks, and that is the basin's pulse: the thirty-one-hour breath, the forty centimeters, the tide with no moon in it.

At the south end the water comes back up through a fissure field — the Give — colder, mineral-heavy, hissing like sand poured onto a hot pan. It brings the phosphorus the groves live on. The groves crowd it, and their weirs stand in the flow.

The lip spills the surplus over the sill in a fall three hundred meters high, into the warm countries, into rivers full of ordinary animals. And the drain runs down through the dark and comes out as a spring in a lowland valley where there are fish, and the fish sometimes come up the spring, and into the throat, and into the cold.

They do not arrive. The groves have grown thick around the Throat's mouth, thicker than anywhere else in the Cistern, and the water there is a solid fence of snapping. Cavitation tears soft tissue. The light of it is a strobe in a black corridor. Nothing has come up the Throat in a very long time.

The groves keep the fence anyway. That is the thing about this place that stays with you: a wall maintained, at ruinous expense, around a field with nothing in it to eat and nothing in it to be eaten by. There are no bones in the peat. There are no shells in the limestone; the rock here precipitated out of clean water and has nothing inside it, no fossil, no print, no small coiled remainder. In eleven meters of perfectly preserved sediment there is not one thing that ever had a face.

The absence is not recent. It is the oldest established fact in the basin. And everything alive here has spent that time getting louder.

## Courses and hands

On the shelves above the peat line, where the current runs and the substrate is bare rock, there are walls.

Drystone arcs, chest-high, silted to half their height, laid without mortar in courses that repeat in fives and open spirals. Thirty of them on one shelf, roofless, doorless, arranged along the flow like a settlement seen from a ridge — and from a ridge, in the Window, that is exactly what they look like: a drowned village, abandoned, tidy, waiting.

They are weirs. They steer the Give's current toward the groves' feeding mouths and slow it enough that seed and mineral silt drop out. They are built by handroot, a stolon that ends in a callused lignified grip with two opposed lobes, and the grip holds a chert flake, and the flake is struck against a core, and the flake that comes off is used to sever a rival's root or to open a seam of soft limestone or to trim the lip of a hammer cell so it snaps clean.

There are middens beside every wall: exhausted flakes in their tens of thousands, sorted by size, layered by century. There are caches in bark niches, blades wrapped in grown tissue. There are three awls of native copper, cold-hammered, green as pond scum, used to pierce seed coats. No fire has ever burned in this basin. No metal has ever been smelted. Everything here was made by pressure, patience, and stone.

The walls take three hundred years to build. The groves that build them live nine hundred. It is not a slow technology. It is a technology at the speed of the thing that uses it.

There is one structure that does not fit. On the east shelf, a chamber of laid stone with a lintel across the opening, a doorway a meter and a half high and half a meter wide, silted to the sill. Inside, cached in a niche, a blade worn smooth and rounded on one side only, the way a tool wears when it is held always in the same hand.

The handroot's grips are opposed lobes. They wear a blade evenly on both faces.

Nobody has an account of this. The groves do not go into the chamber. They build around it, the way they build around a boulder.

## The Window, and after

When the lid clears, the clatters ring each other into flower.

It starts at the Give, where the phosphorus is, with a long knock — a slow deliberate double beat that is not the feeding snap, carried in the ninety-meter layer out across the whole bowl. The nearest grove answers. Then the next. It takes three days to cross the basin, and for those three days the Cistern is one instrument being tuned by itself, forty kilometers of black water passing a phrase along.

Then the release: pollen in oil-droplet rafts, buoyant, rising, the water going pale and thick, the milk, a hiss like sand poured from a height that goes on for a week. Then the slow fall of it, weeks of it, drifting down through the sparks onto the walls and the middens and the peat and the lintel of the small closed chamber, settling into the eleven meters, joining the stack, waiting its four hundred years.

And out on the western flats, on a pock that blew itself sterile nine years ago, one knockwood seedling has got a grip on the raw mud. It is thirty centimeters tall. It has one hammer cell.

It snaps, alone, in the dark, and shines.

Forty kilometers away, in the carrying layer, something answers.
