## The Combs

From the top of the Sill you cannot see the bottom, and this is not because of distance. It is because of the lid.

Most days the cloud sits on the plateau at about the height of a tall chimney, a grey ceiling with no seams in it, and the whole works goes on underneath like something under a hand. The light that gets through has no direction. It does not fall; it is simply *present*, at the strength of an hour before dawn, from ten in the morning until four in the afternoon. Nothing casts a shadow. The wet stone gives back a dull sheen and the whitewashed undersides of the pipe-bridges give back a little more, and that is the entire budget of illumination the place has to spend.

The first thing you notice, standing at the overlook with your collar up, is that the ridge is fenced in mesh. Acres of it — vertical screens of fine galvanized wire on timber frames, forty feet high, ranked along the crest where the wind comes over. They are fog nets. The cloud drives through them and leaves itself behind, drop by drop, and the drops run down the wires into gutters at the base, and the gutters run into lead pipes, and the pipes go down the hill to the dye-houses and the boiler feed and the accumulator chambers. This is the first crop of the Combs. Before the water is taken from the lake it is taken from the air.

The second thing you notice is that there is no ground.

---

The hill was dressed six hundred years ago and has been re-dressed ever since. Every slope is faced: slag brick, granite setts, tarred clinker, laid to a fall so that nothing pools anywhere it is not supposed to pool. The lake behind the head-dam is lined. The bedrock beneath all of it is a matter of record rather than experience — there is a shaft in the Fourth Tooth where an inspector can climb down past the made ground and the old fill and the older fill and put his palm on the actual mountain, and men go down there once a year to confirm that it is still there, and come up saying it is very cold and very dark and smells of nothing at all.

So the wetness here is not the wetness of a marsh. It is the wetness of a thing that has been *plumbed to be wet*. Water is a hand's depth below every walking surface. Every wall has weep-holes with little lead lips, spaced like buttons, and beneath each lip a drip-channel cut in the stone, and the channels join and join and join until they are a race, and the race is somebody's power. The walls are taught to cry into a channel. Nothing is allowed to weep at random.

There are two waters and everyone here knows the difference in their hands. Hill water is hard, cold, four degrees out of the cirque in a good year, and it is for pushing: it turns things, it lifts things, it carries silt. Fog water is soft as boiled snow and it is for chemistry — dyeing, rinsing, the making of lather, the feeding of boilers that must not scale. You can tell them apart by rubbing your fingers: the fog water squeaks. Children are told not to drink the hill water and drink it constantly, and are fine.

The air is at nine degrees and stays there. Breath shows in the morning. In the deep walks, where the sun has not reached since the place was built, there is old ice in the joints of the masonry, grey and gritty, that has never once melted.

---

Go down.

The descent is not a road; it is a series of floors. Forty-one terraces stacked down six miles of slope, and each terrace's roof is the next terrace's street. There are no gaps. Buildings share walls with buildings they have nothing to do with — a felt mill party-walled to a chapel party-walled to a cress-house — and where a gap does occur it is generally the subject of litigation, because a gap is wasted head, wasted frontage, wasted warmth. Above the walks the air rights are packed too: pipe bridges in bundles of nine and twelve, lagged in moss and sacking, roped over with cable trays, hung with the drying laundry of the levels above. You walk under other people's socks.

And every surface that is not walked on or piped over is *growing*.

This is the thing that undoes visitors. They expect a works and they get a works, but they expect it bare and they get it green to the point of obscenity. Moss in every joint, three and four species deep, a plush nap over the whole city that you could sweep with a broom. Liverwort black-green on the north faces. Ferns coming out of the culvert mouths in bunches like something being poured. Cress in the shallow races, acre on acre of it, the leaves overlapping until the water underneath is only a sound. Willow in troughs along the parapets, pollarded to knuckles, the withies going for baskets and for the charcoal filters. Alder in boxes on the wide walks, its roots in a made soil of filter cake and ash and spent cress-root and wool waste, composted in the digesters at the Tail and carried back up the hill in the empty ballast cars. There is no dirt here that was not manufactured. The trees are not in the ground. They are in *containers*, all of them, every one, and the ground is a product with a specification and a price.

It is lush because it is dim. Nothing here wants sun. It is an understory without a canopy — a shade flora, cropped and fed and watered at a rate that has been calculated for two centuries, growing at the exact speed at which it is harvested. The moss is cut for gasket packing and flange joints and lagging. The horsetail is cut for scouring: its stems are full of silica and the lathe-beds and the gauge-blocks are polished with it, handfuls of it, the oldest abrasive in the world doing the finest work in the city.

---

Now the quiet.

You have been descending for twenty minutes through what is, by any count, a heavy industrial town of thirty-one thousand people, and you have heard almost nothing. A whisper of water. The tick of a pawl. Boots on wet setts, a sound like knuckles on a table. Somewhere below, a long sigh and a chain settling — a water-balance lift, the top car filling from a header tank until it outweighs the bottom car and sinks and hauls it up, four tons of freight moving three terraces vertically without a single engine. Far off, muffled by sixty feet of moss and masonry, the soft thudding of fulling stocks, which is the loudest thing in the Combs and which you would call quiet if you heard it in a room.

This is not an accident and it is not a mood. It is law, and the law is arithmetic.

*Splash is lost head.* Water that jumps out of a race is water that has spent its fall on nothing. Every gate here enters water below the waterline. Every outfall is submerged. Every wheel runs in an oil bath, every bearing is greased weekly by a boy with a numbered can, every belt is leather and every belt-guard is lined with felt. A noisy mill is a leaking mill; you can hear the money going out. The Quiet Assize sits four times a year and fines by distance — so much per foot at which the sound was heard — and the hush-wardens walk the terraces with a wooden paddle they hold up to tell you that you are being audible.

So the shifts do not change to a whistle. They change to lamps. Green for the head-wardens' gates, amber for the lifts, a red shutter dropped over a bullseye to stop a whole line of shafting dead. All day the hill winks at itself in coloured light, semaphore across the fog, and the men read it the way sailors read flags. In a place this dim, signal-lamps are cheap and voices are expensive.

---

And under all that quiet, the place is coming apart and going back together at a speed that makes your eyes tired.

Nothing on the Combs is finished. A terrace runs on a seven-year turn: at any hour, one seventh of the city is stripped to its bones. Whole facings lifted and re-laid to a corrected fall. Races re-cut because the silt survey says the bed has risen three-eighths. Scaffold migrating up the slope like a slow animal, its poles carried hand to hand in a column of forty men, no shouting, a foreman's fingers moving. Gravity wagons running down the inclines with their brake-shoes smoking faintly in the cold. Chutes dropping brick from the Fifth to the Ninth in a continuous rattling stream that has been enclosed in moss-packed boxing so it sounds like a hand in a bag of grain.

At the wide walk on the Twelfth there is the parts market: two hundred stalls of gates, pawls, shafting, bearing shells, worn rope, brass weir-lips, all of it second-hand, all of it graded, and the bargaining done in whispers with the fingers under a cloth. Turbulence at full anthill pitch and you could hear a coin drop.

The head-wardens re-plumb the whole hill every morning at six. Yesterday's Long Race is today's settling bed; the water that turned the wire-draw is sent to the retting channels and the flax that was in the retting channels is out on the racks and the smell of it — sweet, rotten, enormous — hangs over three terraces and is understood by everyone to be the smell of money. Two streets on the Twentieth were dismantled last month and are being re-erected on the Twenty-Third with better falls.

And yet the Combs are six hundred years old and look it. The stone lips of the older weirs are dished and rounded where rope has run over them since before anyone's grandmother. The stair treads are hollowed in the middle like shallow spoons. The brass gate-plates are worn to the thickness of a fingernail and are re-cast on a cycle so old the cycle itself has a name. This is the resolution of the thing, and the people here would not find it remarkable: **the pattern holds because everything in it moves.** Like a riffle in a river, which keeps its shape for a century while not one grain of it stays. The Combs are a standing wave in the shape of a town. Input equals output; head in equals work out; the silt dredged from the settling beds equals the made ground carried up the hill; the moss cut equals the moss grown. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is lost. It has been in balance so long that the balance has acquired the look of geology.

---

Living things are mostly plants and people.

You can walk an hour and see one grey wagtail, working the outfall of a condenser where the water is warm and the midges rise. A dipper on the Long Race, bobbing on a stone that was cut for it in 1740. One heron, always one, standing in the fourth settling bed with the patience of a shareholder. Eels come up the passes in autumn in the dark and are counted at the head-dam by a man with a lamp and a tally-board, and the count is entered in the same ledger as the head of water, because it is the same kind of fact. Bats in the dry culverts. No songbirds — there is no canopy to sing from and no ground to nest on. Almost no rats: no soft earth, no grain above the waterline, and the cress beds strip the nutrient out of every spill before it can rot.

In the seed-orchards on the upper terraces, in the one week of the year when the lid lifts and the light comes in hard and white and everybody's eyes water, the children go up with rabbit-tail brushes and pollinate the pear blossom by hand, tree by tree, because there are no bees in a place with no flowers to speak of. Then the cloud closes and the pears swell in the grey and are very small and very hard and are made into a spirit that tastes of cold stone.

The lichen on the parapets is read like a gauge. There is a woman whose whole job is to walk the terraces with a hand lens and note which species have retreated from which flue, and her report can shut a works.

---

At the top, in the coldest and driest and most expensive rooms in the Combs, are the standards: the metre bars, the gauge blocks, the reference weights, kept at a temperature that has not varied a degree in a hundred and eighty years, in chambers dried by fog-water condensate and salt. This is the real export, under all the cloth and paper and felt and wire. The lowlands buy the Combs' *cold* — its dimensional stability, its slow retting, its clean condensing, its four-degree water in August. The cold is the product. Everything else is a way of paying for it.

At the bottom, the Tail: the water leaves warmer than it came and cleaner, stripped by the cress and the willow and the settling beds, and the towns down the valley take it and are grateful and do not entirely like to think about where it has been.

---

Stand at the overlook again in the late afternoon, which looks exactly like the morning. Below you the fog sea fills the valley to the brim and the far peaks stand out of it, and between you and them the whole comb of the hill glitters faintly with wet and with two thousand small green lamps coming on, terrace by terrace, as the shifts turn. Scaffold going up. Scaffold coming down. A column of men with poles. A lift sighing. Water rerouted, brick relaid, moss cropped, silt dredged, ground made, ground carried, everything moving at once and no noise at all reaching you but the low continuous whisper of six miles of engineered falling water — the sound of a place that has been extremely busy, in exactly the same way, since before your language existed.

It will be here tomorrow. Not one stone of it will be the same stone.
