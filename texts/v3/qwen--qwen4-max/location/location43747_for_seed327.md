## The Kettle

The plateau does not prepare you. It goes on for three days in every direction at a height where the sky has stopped being blue and started being a dark violet with a hard edge, and it grows almost nothing: lichen welded to limestone, a grey cushion plant every forty paces, wind-polished pavement where the winter's snow has scoured the soil off the rock and taken it somewhere else. Nine cairns in a day's walking. Each one is a single stone laid on a single stone, and each one means *the hole is that way*, and none of them are necessary, because you hear the hole long before you could possibly fall into it.

It comes up through the boots first — a low tone in the soles of the feet, in the bone behind the ear, the note a very large jar makes when you drag a wet finger round its rim. Then it arrives properly, as air. Then the ground simply ends, in a clean elliptical lip, and there is a hundred and thirty paces of nothing where the plateau used to be.

The nothing has a floor ninety paces down, and on the floor there are eleven birches.

---

Stand at the lip and the first thing the eye does is look for more. There is no more. One hole, one bowl, one weather. The nearest water not inside it is a snowfield two hours north; the nearest tree not inside it is four days south and downhill and, they say, a whole forest, an obscenity of trees. Here the world is bounded so exactly that the people who live on the rim can tell you its inventory without pausing: three huts, one roofed niche, two drying racks, one windlass, one rope, nine cairns, eleven birches, one rowan, one pool, one slot in the north wall of the floor that nobody has reached the bottom of. The emptiness is not a lack of decoration. It is the substance of the place. The Kettle is mostly air — a hundred thousand cubic paces of it — and everything alive is a thin skin on the inside of that air, a smear of green at one level, a scatter of nests at another, a mat of trees at the bottom. Between one presence and the next there is a great deal of room. A swift crossing the shaft is a speck for four full seconds.

The walls are old. This is not a fresh wound. The limestone has been dissolving here since before there were people to be careful of it, and the fluting runs in long vertical scallops, spoon-shaped, the signature of water eating rock slowly and without hurry. The collapse blocks on the floor lie at the exact angle that rubble likes to lie at, and they have not shifted in living memory, and they are furred with moss so thick you can push a finger in to the knuckle. The hole widens by about a hand's breadth in a human lifetime and deepens by less. What falls is balanced by what dissolves. It has arrived at its own shape and is content there.

The lip itself is worn. At the northeast notch, where the rope goes over, the rock has been rubbed into a smooth saddle with two grooves in it, and the grooves are deeper than a hand is wide, and nobody knows how many generations of hair-rope it took to make them.

---

Forty paces down, the wall goes green.

This is the Seam: the level where the plateau's water, which is always near the surface and never on it, intersects the void. Along a band perhaps four paces tall the rock weeps — not springs, not falls, just a persistent sweating, a beading, a slow gathering that lets go in threads. Every thread has a plant under it. Liverworts in flat green plates. Moss in cushions and beards, some of the beards two paces long and moving. Ferns growing straight out of the vertical, saxifrage in the pockets, and a hanging marsh, an actual marsh, perched on a ledge system forty paces above the floor: sedges, cotton-grass, a quaking mat of sphagnum that a person can walk along sideways with their back to the wall and their heels in the void. Water leaves the mat's outer edge as a permanent fine rain that never reaches the bottom as rain, because the breath takes it.

The floor is cool, not cold. It should be freezing — the plateau freezes every night of the year — but the bowl is a pocket. It is out of the wind. It holds the earth's warmth in the seep-water. And it is composting: a hundred generations of bird dung, ten paces deep in places, steaming faintly on autumn mornings, giving off a heat you can feel with a palm at arm's length. The birches grow in that. Eleven crooked white birches with bark like wet paper, a willow leaning over the pool, and one rowan older than the rope, gnarled, its berries gone before they ripen because the whistlers come down for them. The trees have never once been above the rim. They have no idea there is a plateau. Their whole sky is an ellipse.

---

The hole breathes, and everything here is keyed to it.

In the small hours the air pours *in*, over the lip and down, cold and heavy, and the shaft fills with a downdraught that flattens the moss-beards upward and pulls the waterfall's spray against the wall. By mid-morning the flow slackens. Somewhere in the late afternoon it turns, and the Kettle exhales: warm wet air rising out of the under-throat, out of the sump, out of the rotting guano, and the whole column reverses. On the exhale the moisture condenses at a fixed height where the rising warmth meets the plateau's cold, and a cloud forms *inside the hole* — a lens of fog forty paces across and three thick, hanging in the same place evening after evening, a cloud with an address. It rises a few paces when the breath is strong and sinks when it weakens. The people on the rim read it the way farmers elsewhere read a sky.

At the top of the updraught, where the lift exactly equals the weight of small dead things, there is a horizontal line of suspended rubbish: feathers, seed husks, midge bodies, bat-dung dust, a birch catkin that has been circling since spring. The chaff-line. It neither rises nor falls. Children are told not to reach into it. When it drops, weather is coming, and the drying racks are cleared.

Twice a day, at the turning, the wind in the throat stops. The waterfall keeps going and the birds keep going, but the great bass note under everything cuts out, and for two or three minutes the Kettle is merely loud instead of deafening. This is the Quiet. It is when people talk to each other, settle arguments, name babies, and listen down the north slot for the water that is still moving somewhere far below. A name said into the chord belongs to the chord. You wait.

---

And it is loud. Not loud like a storm — loud like an instrument that has been playing since before the species arrived and shows no interest in stopping.

There is the fundamental, the breath in the throat, felt more than heard. There is the seep-rain hiss, a wide sanded sound with no source. There is the fall from the Seam's overflow into the pool, a slap with a hollow underneath it. There are the crag-swifts: nine or ten thousand of them, nesting in the solution pockets of the west wall, a colony that fills the upper third of the shaft with a screaming that has texture, like a fabric being torn very slowly forever. There are the whistlers on the rim turf, sharp and periodic, one every few seconds, each one a small mammal announcing that it has noticed you and does not intend to stop. There is the bat emergence at dusk — the seam-bats come out of the fluting in a column and the sound is a thrown handful of gravel, sustained. There is rockfall, a tick and a knock, most days. There is the windlass, which creaks in time with nothing.

The far wall is far enough that its echo comes back a half-beat late. The Kettle is never quite in unison with itself. The two households sing against the delay on purpose, in the Quiet, in a plain unaccompanied line, and it is the only music anyone here knows.

---

The plateau grows nothing, so the hole eats the wind.

All day the thermals off the lowlands lift insects — aphids, midges, small brown moths, spiders on their threads — up over the snow-line, and the plateau's cold air drops them out again. On still mornings the snow is speckled brown with them. This aeolian fall is the entire input. The swifts harvest it in the air, ten thousand birds working the column above the mouth from dawn to the noon wedge; the whistlers and snowfinches glean the drifts; the bats take the night shift. Everything they carry in falls, and rots, and warms the floor, and feeds the moss and the crickets and the cricket-hunting newts in the Seam, and the blind loach in the sump that eat whatever the pool cannot use, and the birches, which are ultimately made of insects that died four days' walk away and downhill.

The grey harrow comes at noon, when the sun finally clears the rim and a wedge of true daylight reaches the floor for about two hours. It stoops through the mouth, takes a swift against the west wall with a sound like a struck hide, and the colony goes up in a mob that boils around it until it leaves. In that same wedge the ferns open, the midges rise, the newts move, the rowan photosynthesises most of what it will earn that day. Noon is not a time here. It is an event, and everything attends it.

After storms the Seam becomes a curtain and the pool climbs its stones and the north slot swallows the surplus with a gulping you can hear on the rim. In winter an ice plug closes the throat, the breath stops, the swifts are gone and the bats are torpid in the fluting, and the Kettle is silent for six weeks — the year's other silence, the one nobody enjoys. Four people stay through it. They melt snow and listen to the ice tick.

---

Three huts of dry-laid slab, roofed with juniper poles and turf, set back from the lip where the wind will let a roof live. A windlass of two forked posts and a drum. A ladder in three stages, lashed, the lashings renewed each spring. A roofed niche with a stretched hide across its mouth that hums when the breath is strong, which nobody built on purpose and nobody would think of removing. Inside the niche, tallies: harvest counts in scratches, generation on generation, and beside them a second column of marks recording the rope's length, which is shortened by a finger's width each generation because the saddle in the lip wears deeper and the rope must be kept from touching the sharp part.

Antler scrapers, flint picks, wicker creels, felt, dung fires. One copper needle, older than both households, its eye mended with sinew twice, used for the rope-work and for nothing else. Guano goes up in creels and out down the mountain in exchange for barley, salt, and hair. Nothing here rolls; there is nowhere to roll to.

Two households, nine people in the warm months. They do not think of the hole as strange. They think of it as the only place, surrounded by a great deal of walking, and they are careful at the lip in the way you are careful in your own kitchen at night — not frightened, just accurate. There is one rule beyond the ordinary ones about ropes and children and the chaff-line, and it is this: in the Quiet, if you call down the north slot and something answers, do not answer back.

Nobody says anything has ever answered. The rule is simply kept, the way you keep a step that has always been the third one, and the eleven birches go on growing in the warmth of ten thousand birds' patience, and the note goes on under everything, and the cloud comes to its address at dusk, and the shaft holds its enormous clean emptiness with a green ring round the middle, at the top of the world, for no one.
