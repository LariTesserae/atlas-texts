# The Long Circuit

## I. The pavement

Begin at the surface, because that is where everything here happens: a skin, a film, a coat.

The plain lies at four and a half thousand meters and has no relief worth the name. It was a mountain range once — a real one, with snow and cirques and the whole apparatus — and it has been taken apart grain by grain and carried off by wind, and what remains is the floor of it, the root, the basement rock planed flat and lifted a little by the buoyancy of losing weight. Now it runs three thousand kilometers in every direction with a slope you could not detect by lying down on it. The horizon is not a line but a thickness, a gray band where the air gives up.

The ground is mosaic. Wind has sorted the stones for a hundred million years until every clast is the same size as its neighbors and fitted to them, a pavement of dull cobbles tight as a tiled floor, each one polished on its windward face and pitted on its lee. Between them: nothing. No sand. The sand all left, long ago, and is somewhere else now, being a beach.

It is cold. Minus thirty is ordinary. In the warm part of the long day it may reach minus eight, and this is considered a season. The air is a third thin; a sound has to work three times as hard to cross it, and mostly does not bother.

And it is wet — not with rain, which falls here perhaps nine times a century, but because the salt in the ground drinks the air. Chlorides and perchlorates in the crust pull water out of an atmosphere too dry to give it, and condense their own brine, and the film that forms between the cobbles at the low hour of the day stays liquid at forty below. The stones sweat. Frost feathers grow on them and then dissolve into their own salt and become a slick, a sheen, a tenth of a millimeter of ocean spread over a continent.

Every stone wears a cap. The upper face is bright — a dull silver, a nickel gray, sometimes the green-bloom of copper — while the underside is matte and rust-dark. Turn one over and it looks like a different rock. Come back in a decade and it will have capped itself again.

## II. What the coat is

Under the plain, four hundred meters down and continuing down for two kilometers, lies an ore body: sulfides, laid out in bands in the Archean, iron and copper and nickel and a great deal of sulfur. Buried sulfide oxidizes slowly. It gives up electrons. The electrons travel upward through the rock toward the oxidizing surface, and the surface takes them, and metal comes out of solution and plates onto whatever is there.

This is a battery. It has a voltage of a little under one volt, it is the size of a country, and it has been running since before there were animals anywhere.

Life found it. Not the sunlight — the sunlight is poor here, and always was — but the difference in potential between the buried and the exposed. Everything on this plain is, in one way or another, a wire.

## III. The crowd

Take a thumbprint of the wet pavement. There are nine thousand animals in it.

They are two millimeters long, folded like a woodlouse folded again into a shirt button, and they wear a plated cuticle of nickel-iron that they lay down themselves, layer by layer, out of the film they walk on. Each carries a droplet in a dorsal cup, a bead of electrolyte it will not spill, replenished from dew, exchanged with neighbors mouth to mouth. They are portable cells. They walk on surface tension. They have twelve legs and no eyes, and nothing on this plain has eyes, and nothing here has ever been looked at by anything that lives here.

There are perhaps ten to the fifteenth of them. Their biomass exceeds that of any forest that has ever stood on this planet, and it is distributed as a skin one animal thick over three thousand kilometers, so that from a height the plain seems merely discolored, a gray bloom, a shading, as if a cloud were passing.

The cloud is not passing. It is grazing, and it moves at four meters an hour, and the front of it takes eleven years to cross the district.

They speak in voltage. A change of a few millivolts propagates along the wet film and along the filaments at a good fraction of the speed of light, and the whole plain is talking all the time, instantly, at continental range, and has never made a sound.

## IV. The wire

Threaded through the pavement, half-buried, sometimes strung a hand's height between the tips of stones, are the conductors: hair-fine, dull metal, grown, branching, spliced, patched. They run for kilometers. They join. They form nodes where a hundred threads meet in a knot the size of a fist, and from the nodes they run out again. Seen from above — if there were anything above to see from — the plain is drawn over with a schematic, a diagram of a circuit revised continuously for two hundred million years.

The wire is warm. Current through resistance makes heat, not much, a fraction of a degree, but it is enough to keep a millimeter of ground thawed along the line when everything else is locked. And along those lines grows the only green on the plain: a low cushion, chlorophyll-free, a partnership of something fungal and something metallic, three percent cover in total, all of it arranged in lines a hand's width across. The vegetation is the wiring diagram. Where the current goes, the moss follows. Where a filament breaks and the ground freezes, the moss dies back within a season, and a straight green line becomes a dashed one.

Breaks are the only emergencies here. When a filament parts in the cold — with a tick you would have to be kneeling to hear — the counters come. They chain themselves across the gap, each gripping the next, and they plate themselves into the bridge, and the bridge is made of bodies, and it takes four generations to finish, and the great-grandchildren of the ones who started it walk on their ancestors' backs to cross.

## V. The stacks

Every three hundred square kilometers, there is one large thing.

It is forty meters across and three meters high and half-sunk in the pavement, gray, rimed with salt, a nested mass of plates seen edge-on, like the cooling apparatus of a plant that was never built. It has a taproot four hundred meters deep, and it pumps brine up that root by peristalsis, a slow wave descending and ascending, one cycle every eleven minutes.

Inside, it is a refinery. Countercurrent channels sort the brine by density. Froth chambers bubble air through it and skim the metal-rich foam off the top with a ciliated conveyor, and the foam goes to the stacks proper: chamber upon chamber of thin plates, each with a slight potential across it, where metal comes out of solution in sheets a micron thick. The sheets are drawn, folded, drawn again, and extruded from a spinneret at the crown as wire, four centimeters a year, which the counters take and lay along the ground toward the next stack, three hundred kilometers off, which is doing the same thing.

They are called stacks because there is nothing else to call them. They are animals. They bud. They live forty thousand years and then dissolve into their own troughs, and the counters carry them away gram by gram, and nothing is left, not a shell, not a stain, not a ruin. There are no ruins on this plain. There is no midden. Every artifact is eaten by the industry that made it, and there is no one who made it, and no one who intended any of this.

They move a body-length per century. Behind each one runs a shallow plated trough, and the troughs run for thousands of kilometers, crossing, recrossing, worn smooth, reused: the old roads, the fossil routes, the record of every migration since the mountains came down.

You could walk for two days between the only two large objects in a district and never for a moment leave the crowd.

## VI. The sky is part of it

The dust does not settle here. It is charged, and the field lifts it, and grains stand on end on the pavement and hop and hang. Two kilometers of haze, maintained by the plain out of the plain, dimming the sun to a coin you could look at directly if you had anything to look with.

The haze is the return path. The circuit closes through the air, through drifting motes and the aerial plankton that ride them — bodies of a few microns, no wings, nothing here has wings, the air is too thin for wings and the energy budget too small for muscle. They drift on field lines. They precipitate slowly, a gray fall that takes weeks to arrive and is eaten before it lands.

The fog is made here and consumed here. Rain that falls in one district fell out of that district's own breath. At the peak of the tide, when the field is strong, the tips of the stones and the nodes of the wire go into corona: a pale copper-green glow, a millimeter thick, silent, no arc, no thunder, nothing so violent as a spark. The whole plain glows faintly along its diagram, and there is nothing alive on it that can see this.

## VII. The tide and the long patience

The planet turns slowly, and its magnetic field sweeps across the plain, and the sweep induces current in the ground, and this is the day. It rises over hours. The counters swarm up onto the wire. The stacks open their intakes and the froth chambers fizz — the loudest thing in a hundred kilometers, and it is the sound of a glass of water settling. Then the tide eases, the intakes close, the crowd sinks back into the film, frost forms, and the wire ticks as it contracts, one tick, then another, kilometers apart.

Beneath that shift there is the fog season, and beneath that the eleven-year grazing, and beneath that the drift of the stacks, and beneath all of it the denudation: the wind taking a millimeter of the plain away every thousand years, and the taproots growing a millimeter deeper every thousand years to keep hold of the ore. The mountains came down and the circuit rode them down, adjusting, never breaking. It is older than the range it buried.

Nothing accumulates. What dissolves is replated. What plates is stripped and refined and plated again. The metal of this plain has been through the bodies of the crowd perhaps a hundred thousand times. It is a closed shop. It has been in balance so long that balance is not a state it achieves but the material it is made of.

## VIII. Rules

Nothing burns; there is no fuel and no wood and nothing dry enough.

Nothing runs. The fastest thing on the plain is a signal, and the slowest thing is also a signal, because some of them take a century to arrive.

Predation is a short circuit. A hunter lays a filament across a grazer and drains it, and the grazer stops, and there is no struggle and no cry, and death here looks exactly like sleep and is followed immediately by employment.

The chief disease is theft: parasites that splice into a conductor and take a millivolt that is not theirs. The plain's immune response is to reroute, over decades, and the rerouting is visible for centuries afterward as a green line that goes around nothing.

And somewhere under all of it, without a mind, without an intention, without anyone to want the answer, the plain is solving a problem: where the next centimeter of wire should go. It has been working on it since the Archean. It is very good at it. It will not finish.

## IX. Silence

The air is thin and carries sound badly. The dust scatters what is left. And the crowd — ten to the fifteenth small soft bodies packed cobble-tight across a continent — absorbs the rest, so that the plain is an anechoic chamber the size of a sea.

Stand in it and you would hear your own blood, your own joints, the wet click of your own eyelids, and under that, once an hour, a granular whisper as a million counters shift a millimeter together, like sand deciding something. A filament parting in the cold. The fizz of froth three hundred kilometers away, which you would not hear. Once a decade, an intake coughing, a sound like a door closing in another house.

Nothing else. No voice has ever been raised here. The whole vast apparatus — the mines, the mills, the transmission, the maintenance crews, the shift change, the silent lightning — runs at the volume of a held breath, in the dark, at thirty below, unwatched, and has run so for two hundred million years.

In a hundred years the wire will have advanced four meters. The moss will have followed. A stack will have moved the length of itself and left a trough that its ancestors left before. The stones will have recapped. The crowd will be, individual for individual, mostly the same crowd; they live four centuries and there is nowhere to go.

The plain will be exactly this.
