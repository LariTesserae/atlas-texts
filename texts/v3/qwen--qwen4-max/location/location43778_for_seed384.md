## Sump Four

There is no surface here. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest to keep understanding. The hall is full — completely, structurally full, water pressed up into the roof vaults and down into the cable trenches, no bubble trapped anywhere, not in the lamp housings, not in the crown of the arch. Nothing laps. Nothing splashes. There is no line across a wall marking where the flood stopped, because the flood never stopped; it arrived, and then it became the medium, and then it became the weather.

Forty paces by twenty-five, four galleries stacked around the walls, a ceiling eighteen metres up that you know about only from the drawings and from the way sound behaves. A room. One room. You could hold the whole of it in your head if you could ever see the whole of it at once, which you can't.

---

The water is called *working water* in the stencils, and it has been working for a very long time. Two hundred years of boil-and-freeze, of being drawn off and condensed and drawn off again, has distilled it past any cleanliness the sea has ever managed. It is almost perfectly clear and almost perfectly empty. It has no salt to speak of in the upper layers, no carbonate, no dissolved air. It is hungry. Put a bare hand into it and it takes the salt off your skin, then the minerals out of the skin, then it starts on what's underneath, patiently, without malice, the way it has been starting on the copper bonding straps for two centuries. Things here do not rot. They *empty*. A leather glove left on a rail in the year of the flood is still on the rail, still shaped like a glove, and weighs nothing, and comes apart into a slow grey snow if you look at it too directly with your fingers.

This is why so little lives here. Not the dark — the dark is negotiable. The emptiness of the water is not.

It is cold in a way that has become an industry. The hall holds itself at a fraction below freezing and is prevented from freezing by pressure and by the brine that pools along the floor, and the two facts lean against each other like drunks. There are three layers, and you can see the boundaries between them: horizontal shimmers, waist-high and chest-high, where the density changes and the water refuses to mix. The men who built this called them seams, and left instructions about them, and the instructions are still bolted to the wall in enamel, still legible, still correct.

The seams bend light. A lamp on the second gallery appears to be somewhere it isn't. They also swallow sound — trap it, fold it, send it around the room on a long slow curve so that it comes back to you eleven seconds later from behind, quieter, and slightly wrong. There is very little sound to begin with. What there is gets used thoroughly.

---

The hall is a cold-works. It makes ice.

Twenty-four freezing columns stand in two ranks down the centre, six metres tall, ribbed like the barrels of old guns, and inside each of them a block of ice is growing at all times. Around and between and above them: pipe. Pipe in banks and bundles, pipe near-touching, pipe crossing so densely in the north-east corner that a body cannot pass and a hand can barely be pushed through, forty valve wheels in a single rank at shoulder height, each one worn smooth as a river stone. Three galleries of catwalk, a fourth added later and badly, a tram rail bolted upside down along the roof for a carriage that still runs on it. Ladders. Conduit. Junction boxes with their lids fused shut. A wall of gauges, eighty or ninety of them, glass clouded, needles stopped at various hours of a night that hasn't ended.

Dense, but not cluttered. It was all drawn by someone. It was all *for* something. The density has the specific comfort of a place where every cubic metre was argued over in a meeting.

And it has all been rounded. That is what the long years did. The return jets have been pushing the same slow eddy through the same geometry since before your language existed, and the water — empty, hungry, endlessly circulating — has fluted the iron. The columns are scalloped. The floor plate, once a sharp diamond tread, is now a soft watered pattern like the inside of a shell, and the concrete beneath the number three return has a channel worn into it a hand deep, curved, deliberate-looking, a shape no one designed. Nothing is decaying. That's the strange part. The hall is not falling apart. It has arrived at its own final form and is holding it. Ice made equals ice taken. The level does not change. The wear does not deepen anymore; it reached the shape it wanted and stopped.

---

Every nine hours, the columns purge.

The rejected brine — heavy, twice-salted, colder than the room — leaves through the purge valves at the base of each column and falls, and because it is falling through water that is already at the edge of freezing, it freezes a hollow tube around itself as it descends. A finger of ice grows downward from the valve, reaching for the floor at about a metre an hour, white and cloudy and faintly luminous, and when it touches down it spreads a small white death across the plate and then breaks, and the pieces are carried off by the eddy, and in nine hours it begins again.

There is a grove of them under the purge rank at any given moment. Eight, eleven, fourteen fingers, at different stages, some just starting, some almost down. They are the only thing in the hall that moves fast enough to watch. Standing in a purge lane is the one thing the enamel signs forbid twice.

The rest of the motion is slower. The tick — every forty-one seconds, the escapement of the harvest carriage, a single dry click that arrives from the wrong direction because of the seams. The hush of the returns, which is not quite a sound, more a pressure on the front of the body. Once a day, roughly, the roof breathes: the whole vault flexes a few millimetres as the tide of the drowned world above goes over it, and the rivets tick in sequence, west to east, like something walking. And under all of it the groan of the blocks, the long complaint of ice being asked to hold a shape.

If you are here for a week you will hear your own heart the entire time and it will be the loudest thing in the room.

---

Light: three amber lamps on the second gallery, fed from a circuit nobody has ever found the source of. One green indicator on the switchboard, still true after everything. And the ice itself — the blocks conduct light down out of the roof, from a shaft somewhere far above that must open onto something that has a sky, and they hold it in their bodies and let it out at the edges, so the racks glow faintly along every arris, a cold blue-white, enough to see your hand by if you hold it against a block. Enough to read a stencil. Not enough to read a face.

The racks run back along the south wall in tiers, four deep, blocks stacked with a hand's width between them, and they go back further than the lamps reach.

Here is the thing about the blocks. Ice grown this slowly, in water this empty, takes an impression. Not of the whole room — of whatever was nearest the growth face at the moment the crystal front passed. A faint grey ghost, a few centimetres in, like breath on glass that never clears. Most of them hold nothing: a smear, a suggestion of pipework. Some hold a valve wheel, perfect, every spoke. One in the third tier holds a moth, wings spread, in a place where no moth has been for two hundred years.

And one, low down, at the height of a hip, holds a hand. Fingers slightly curled. A ring.

The newest blocks, the ones at the rack head still cloudy at the core, have been holding something else lately. A tall narrow shape, standing at the offtake, that does not correspond to any item on the drawings.

---

Life: a grey-green felt on the warm return lines, where the metal runs a whole four degrees above the room. Cable-moss, the signs call it, which means someone named it before it was interesting. There is perhaps a bedsheet's worth of it in the entire hall, in eleven patches, and one pale frond by the offtake that leans, always, toward the pipe. In the moss live small salt-coloured grazers like grains of rice with legs. There are never many. There are never none.

And once every few days, if you are patient and keep still and stay at seam height so your noise doesn't travel, you may see the reader: a pale blind thing the length of a forearm that swims the density boundaries with its body laid along the shimmer, hunting the grazers by the pressure of their small movements. It has no eyes worth the name. It lays its eggs in the warm bearings of the number four column and the hall keeps them at exactly the right temperature, and has done, without instruction, for longer than there have been readers.

That is the whole ecology. A bedsheet of moss, a handful of grazers, one animal. It fits in the palm of the room and it is entirely sufficient and it is older than the country above.

---

Every so often — no schedule anyone has cracked — all three lamps dim together for about a minute, and the carriage stops, and the hall changes what it is doing. Pressures shift. A valve somewhere in the dark north-east bundle turns itself. Then the lamps come back and the tick resumes and the work continues at a different rate.

The old men called this the shift change. They wrote it in the log with no comment, the way you write down a thing that has always happened.

Nobody arrives. Nobody leaves. There is no airlock, no door that opens, no place a body could come from — the hall is bounded on all sides by rock and silt and the crushed remains of its eight siblings, and the pipes that go out through the walls to serve them are still trying, still pushing, still meeting the same silence and coming back.

But at the offtake, when a finished block goes in, there is a small clean tug of current toward the wall. Something on the other side is taking delivery.

The hall is not abandoned. It has simply been staffed, for a very long time, by something that has never once needed the door.
