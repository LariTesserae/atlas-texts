## The Condensary

You come down through a slot in the plateau that you would walk past twice before noticing: a fissure wide enough at its lip for a shoulder, choked at once with a grille of root — not growing across it by accident, but woven, corded, deliberately spanning, the way a spider spans. The roots are the thickness of wrist and forearm. They are warm. Between them the air comes up at you like the breath of an oven with bread in it, and it carries a smell you will spend the rest of your life failing to describe to anyone: hot iron, wet pennies, cut grass, sulfur just under the threshold of insult, and beneath all of it something sweetish and mineral, like the inside of a kettle.

There is a stair. It was cut, not worn, and the chisel marks are still crisp on the upper treads because nothing here has feet. Forty turns down, the walls close in and then let go, and you are standing in the room.

---

It is one room. This matters. There is no beyond. You can walk its perimeter in eleven minutes if you do not stop, and you will stop constantly, but the point stands: it is bounded, oval, a hundred and forty paces by ninety, sixty meters beneath a plateau that has nothing on it worth naming. The walls overhang. They were dissolved into this shape over a span of time that makes the stair an eyeblink, and they have finished dissolving; the curves are the curves of a thing at rest, fluted, scalloped, hung with drapery-stone the color of tea and old fat. Above, the ceiling is a single vault, and the fissure you came through is the only opening, and it admits light the way a cracked door admits an argument — thinly, at an angle, and mostly not at all.

The floor is terraced. Not naturally: the terraces are *beds*, rectangular, rimmed, arranged in ranks with maintenance aisles between them, and each bed is full to overflowing with a pale stone that has spilled over its lip and hung there in lobes and tongues like candle-wax. Warm water stands in the shallows of the rims. It is nowhere deep. There is no lake here, no stream, no pool you could swim; there is a hundred square meters of water distributed across two thousand square meters of rock, in films, in cups, in scallops, in the sweat on the north wall, in a single thread that falls from the root-grille in certain years and lands with a report like a dropped plank. Damp, not wet. The stone is damp. The air is damp on some days and on other days is dry as a kiln and pulls the moisture out of your eyes. The plants have learned both weathers and treat them as separate countries.

The heat comes up through the floor. At the north end there is a seep where the rock is too hot to keep a palm on, and the water arrives already breathing. Forty-four degrees at ankle height, forty-eight at the ceiling. It is not weather. It is a property of the place, like its shape.

---

Light: mostly amber, and mostly from lamps.

There are nineteen sodium lamps still burning on the walls, in cages, on brackets, at the height a person would want them. They are fed by a bank of thermoelectric piles sunk into the hot seep, and the piles are corroding at a rate that can be measured in centuries, and so the lamps will burn for a very long time and then, over a decade, go orange, then red, then out. Under sodium light green is black. This is the first strangeness and the one that takes longest to accept: the room is *lush*, thick with foliage, and the foliage reads as black velvet, black felt, black lacquer, a hundred shades of not-color, while the stone blazes gold and rust and egg-yolk and the copper pipework goes the brown of dried blood.

Then, for eleven minutes near the middle of the day, the blade of true daylight comes down through the slot and crosses a patch of the eastern beds, and for those eleven minutes there is *green* — an ugly, violent, unfamiliar green, chlorophyll caught in a spectrum it evolved for and almost never gets — and the ferns in that patch look like something from a different world, which they are, and then the blade moves on and they go back to being black.

The pools have their own light, a cold bacterial blue, dim as a watch-face. It is not enough to read by. It is enough to see that the water is alive.

---

The vegetation is not a jungle and not a garden. It is a *crop* that has stopped being harvested.

At the top layer, six or seven meters up, the pleated ferns: fronds folded like accordion bellows, packed with a dense white pith, no hollow anywhere in them, unfolding at the rate of a finger's width in nine years. They unfold *audibly*. That is a thing you must be told before you notice it, and then you cannot unhear it: a continuous dry creaking, grinding, like a ship's timber under load, coming from every direction, from the slow tearing of cells as they divide. It is the loudest thing in the room after the roar and it never stops and it is the sound of almost nothing happening.

Beneath them, the vines, which are not entirely vines. Some of them began as root systems that found the old cable runs and grew along the rubber sheathing, then into it, then *through* it, so that now there are lengths of insulated cable hanging in catenaries between brackets with a living core, conducting sap along a copper conductor, and where a cable has broken the ends have callused over and gone on growing outward as ordinary stem. You can find, if you look, a section where the transition happens: rubber becoming bark over the space of a hand's length, the braid of the shielding fanning out into a root-crown.

At the floor, mats. Thermophile algae in sheets and cushions, sulfur-yellow, rust-orange, malachite where the copper leaches; they are the only genuinely bright living things and they are bright because they are full of metal, not because anything is meant to look at them.

Nothing here flowers. Nothing here fruits. There is no nectar, no scent designed to travel, no seed with a hook or a wing or a sweetness, because there is nothing to carry it and nothing to eat it and no reason to be eaten. The plants reproduce by breaking. A frond falls, calcifies at the base, roots. A mat sloughs and the slough lodges in a rim-cup and becomes a new mat. Every individual in the room is a fragment of some older individual, and the whole lush black mass is arguably one organism that has been cutting itself into pieces for longer than there have been stairs.

And nothing here has a mouth. Everything eats by surface. That is the rule of the place, and it has a reason, and the reason is the noise.

---

The works: a hall, half-swallowed, on the west side, its roof gone into a lobe of flowstone that has come down over it like a lip. Finned condensers. A vessel the size of a cottage, ribbed like an accordion, hung in the wall on trunnions, with a trunk of ductwork running up toward the slot. A turbine no bigger than a churn, spinning at a speed that makes it a whine at the very top of hearing, driving a fan and a dynamo and nothing else. Handwheels. Gauge-housings with the glass intact. Enamel signs, the letters still legible under a rind of stone: BED 41. BED 42. YIELD — DO NOT STACK WET.

They were growing stone here. That was the industry. The hot seep comes up saturated with dissolved carbonate; the works degassed it, cooled it across the condenser fins, and laid the precipitate down in the beds in standard slabs, and stamped them, and stacked them. Scaled production. There are four thousand and some slabs stacked along the south wall in ranks, and every one bears a serial and a date, and the press that stamps them is driven by a cam on a shaft that comes round once every eleven years, and it is still stamping. The numbers are still going up. If you can read two slabs from different ranks and do the arithmetic you can date the room, and the number you get will be larger than you want it to be.

What was it *for?* The signs do not say. Whatever it was for is over, and the works did not stop, because nothing in the works was ever contingent on the purpose. The turbine spins because heat rises through the seep. The lamps burn because the turbine spins. The ferns grow toward the lamps because the lamps are the only sun they have ever had. And the ferns are loud, and the loudness is the last part of the loop, and the last part of the loop is the part that keeps the ceiling up.

---

Here is the sound, all of it, at once, always:

The roar — air moving through the fissure and the duct-trunk, a broadband bellow at the bottom of hearing, more felt than heard, a pressure in the sternum and the sinuses. The whine of the turbine, a wire drawn tight through the top of the skull. Water-hammer in the pipework: a knock like distant artillery, three beats and a pause, every few minutes, as a slug of condensate is driven into a bend by steam. The drip-percussion of the vault, which is tuned, which has been dissolving itself into a tuned shape for a hundred thousand years, so that a drop falling into a rim-cup at the north end is answered half a second later by a sympathetic *tock* from a drapery forty meters away. The buzz of nineteen lamps. The grinding of the ferns. And under everything a subsonic pulse with a period of about nine seconds, which is the vessel in the wall breathing.

It is loud enough that you shout, and hear your shout come back wrong — flattened, stripped of its higher partials, arriving from directions you did not send it. It is loud enough that your ears click continuously and your chest feels handled.

And it is loud because the room is stable. This is the thing the works discovered and the ferns inherited: the vault has a resonant frequency, and the roar sits across it, and while the roar sits across it the ceiling does not shed. In the year the fan bearing seized, the noise dropped for nineteen days, and the floor beneath the vault's crown is still white with the flakes that came down, a talus fan you can see under the newer growth, a scar in the shape of a silence.

So: quiet is the sound of the ceiling coming down. And the ferns rasp, and the vines drum their tensioned lengths against the pipe brackets, and the mats hiss where they degas, and the whole living mass has been recruited — not by anyone, not toward anything — into the maintenance of a standing wave. The ecology of the room is an acoustic ecology. The plants keep the noise. The noise keeps the room. The room keeps the heat. The heat keeps the turbine. The turbine keeps the lamps. The lamps keep the plants.

It also keeps the animals out, which is why there are none. Not sterility, not poison. Resonance. Anything with an air-filled cavity — a lung, a sinus, a swim bladder, a trachea, the little resonating chamber of an insect's thorax — comes apart in here, slowly, over hours, at the pressures involved. Nothing with a hollow soft body can live at the frequency this room requires to exist. The ferns are solid. The mats are solid. The algae are films. Steel and stone are tuned or dead and do not care. There is not a single fly, not a mite, not a worm in the mats, not a bird at the slot. The nearest thing to an animal in the whole chamber is the turbine, and it survives because someone, once, balanced it.

You can stay an hour. Your voice will come out flattened and your teeth will ache and something behind your eyes will feel like a thumb pressing. Longer than an hour and you should not.

---

The breath has a period of nineteen years.

For nine or ten of them the vessel inhales: the air in the room goes above dew point and the walls run, the weeping wall on the cool south side sheets and drips, the rim-cups fill, the mats go glossy and slack and double their thickness, and everything smells green and hot. Then the phase turns and for nine or ten years the room exhales, and the air drops below dew point, and the walls go dry and powdery, and the mats crisp at the edges and mummify, and the dead fronds do not rot — there is nothing to rot them, no mouth, no grub, no beetle — they simply dry to a brown parchment and lie where they fell until the calcite comes up over them and takes them in.

So the terraces are pages. Cut into one and you get laminae: wet-phase, dry-phase, wet-phase, dry-phase, each pair a nineteen-year line, thousands of them, with the black pressed ferns lying in the dry bands like specimens in a book. The whole floor is a library that keeps writing itself, at the rate of one leaf per generation.

Twice in a century, perhaps, a surface storm finds the slot and sends down cold water. It is the only cold thing that ever enters. It arrives as a shout and a shock and a mist that fills the room to the height of a person, and the plants respond — but the response takes forty years to complete, so that the flowering-that-is-not-flowering, the great flush of fragmentation and re-rooting, is still visibly underway decades after the water has gone, a slow green detonation propagating outward from the drip-line at a pace you could outwalk without hurrying.

---

The beds overflowed long ago. That is what the works did to themselves: they made stone, and the stone went where stone goes, and it filled the aisles, then the annexes, then the other halls. There are other halls. You can see the tops of their doorways in the flowstone at the west end, arch after arch, drowned to the lintel, going back into the rock. The plant produced so much of its product that it entombed itself, hall by hall, and this is the last void, and it stays a void only because it is screaming.

In the overflow, the casts. Where a mold rotted or a bracket fell or a thing stood still long enough, the calcite took its shape. There is a ladder in stone. There is a handwheel in stone, with the stone spokes as thin as the steel ones were. There is a boot-print, one, left in a bed that was still curing, and the flowstone has built up around it into a low pedestal so that it is now the most prominent object in the eastern quarter, a single human footfall on a plinth, growing.

There is a gauge on the wall by the hall door, glass intact, dial reading in units nobody uses now. Its needle moves about a degree a year. A fern has grown up along the arc of the dial and the needle pushes against the frond's pith, and the frond has thickened along the line of contact, and the two of them are now a mechanism: the gauge is a trellis, the frond is a brake, and once a decade or so the needle wins and the fern tears with a sound like a snapped bowstring, and the count goes on.

Stand in the middle of it. Black foliage to the height of two men, gold stone, amber lamps, blue cups of water, the roar in your chest, the creak of things growing at the speed of geology, the artillery in the pipes, the eleven minutes of green somewhere off to the east that you have already missed. Nothing is coming. Nothing is going. The room is finished, balanced, in its own state, and it is producing — right now, this second, at a rate of about a gram a year — more of itself.
