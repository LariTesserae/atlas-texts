## The Weep

You find it from outside, and only from a distance: a single white feather of cloud coming off a black tower, four and a half thousand meters up, where the air is thin enough to make your hands feel like someone else's. The tower is one of a hundred on the plateau, and ninety-nine of them are dry. This one breathes. The plume leaves a vent near the top and unravels eastward for a kilometer before the wind takes it apart, and if you follow the plume back along its own length you arrive at a wall of grown ceramic, ribbed like the inside of something, with a seam in it at the height of a shoulder.

The seam's lower lip is polished. Not machined smooth — worn smooth, dished in the middle, the way a stone step dishes. Nine hundred years of bodies have squeezed through here. That is the first thing the place tells you about itself, before you are inside: *people used to come, and something still does.*

Put your face in the gap and the air comes out at you warm and thick and wet, thirty-four degrees, saturated, smelling of hot ceramic and iron and the ammoniac sweetness of a great many small bodies living close together. Your ears click. The chamber holds more pressure than the mountain does; the machine that makes the weather in here has been squeezing thin cold air into thick warm air since before the plateau had a name. Sound climbs out of the seam and hits your cheekbone like a hand.

Then you are through, and there is no more finding anything, because you cannot hear yourself think.

---

It is a bulb of a room, nine meters across and six high, and every square centimeter of it is made. There is no rock here. There was never rock here. The walls are a fused alumina foam, spun rather than carved, pale as a fingernail, and they have been dressed by nine centuries of their own function: pitted, fluted, hollowed into scallops where the standing wave presses hardest, polished to a wet gloss along the channels where the water runs. It looks like karst. It looks like a limestone grotto dissolved by ten thousand years of rain. It is a manufactured surface that has been eroded by manufactured weather into the shapes that erosion makes, and if you did not know to look for the seams of the mould you would call it a cave.

Ribs cross the ceiling. Between them hang baffles in ranks, thin black ceramic plates the size of dinner plates, thousands of them, near-touching, layered like the gills under a mushroom cap. Below, the floor is a grating over a sump. Along the walls, combs: fine-toothed structures, forty teeth to the centimeter, running in bands from the sill to the ceiling, and every tooth of every comb is furred with life.

The light is amber and low, coming from the strain-indicators set into the ribs — phosphor that was meant to be read at a glance by someone with a clipboard, still faintly working, tired to the color of a lamp seen through a hand. There are maybe eleven of them left. In their small pooled glow you can see your own fingers. Everywhere else the light is blue-green and alive and moves.

---

The sound is the tool. That is the thing to understand before anything else, because everything else in here is a consequence of it.

The room is an acoustic fog-harvester. Cold thin air is compressed and warmed and seeded and pushed through this cell, and the cell sings at it — a fundamental down around nineteen hertz that you feel in the plates of your skull, with harmonics stacked up and up into the two-and-three-kilohertz range where a human ear is most helplessly sensitive. In the loud phase it is a hundred and thirty decibels in a small hard room with a dense atmosphere. Sound is not information here. It is terrain. It has shape: nodes and antinodes, standing pressure like invisible furniture, places where the air is a wall and places where it is a hole. Your sinuses ache in specific locations. Your teeth buzz. Your eyes water and the tears run toward your ears, and when you try to shout you feel your throat working and hear nothing at all, not even a bone-conducted rumble, because the room owns the whole band.

The sound does the work. In a standing wave, fog droplets are herded into the pressure nodes, where they crowd, collide, coalesce, grow heavy. That is the product: water, out of air, by force of noise. It has run for nine hundred years. Somewhere below the plateau something still drinks what this room makes, or the drain simply runs to a cistern nobody has opened, and it does not matter to the room. The room does its job perfectly. The world inside it is the residue of that perfection.

---

Here is the residue.

The walls weep. Not evenly — along the flutes and scallops, in threads and beads, in the pattern the wave dictates. In the antinodes water films and slides and gathers into the channels, and the channels run down to the sill, and the sill has a lip worn round as a candle-edge, and past the lip is the fall-line: a curtain of drops falling all around the room's circumference into the sump. The sump is warm and shallow and black and drains on a schedule, a slow gulp every few minutes, and it never overflows, because whoever sized the drain sized it exactly.

In the nodes, the water does not fall. It hangs. Beads the size of a plum, of a fist, suspended in the pressure, wobbling, joined by necks that thin and snap and rejoin, drifting slowly along the node-lines like beads on an abacus nobody is working. They have been hanging there, in one form or another, for nine centuries. They are also full of animals.

Look close, in the amber, with your face a hand's width from a bead: there are pale specks in it that swim. Copepod-shaped, smaller than a grain of salt, generations of them, hatching and breeding and dying inside a single suspended droplet, eating the mist's cargo — dust, spores, bacteria, the shed nanoparticulate of a machine slowly wearing out. When two beads merge the populations mix. When a neck snaps they are divided, and the divided halves begin to differ. The whole hanging apparatus is an archipelago. Nothing in it has ever touched a wall.

On the walls, the combers: sessile things in the comb-teeth, forty to the centimeter, each a crown of cilia no bigger than a pinhead, farming the passing fog. Thousands per square meter, tens of millions in the room, and they glow — a dim blue-green pulse that runs in slow waves along the comb-bands as they feed in phase with the stroke. That is most of the light. That is the room's slow tide.

Among them, the tappers: armored, thumbnail-sized, living in the erosion pits, drumming on the ceramic with a knuckle of a claw. They cannot be heard. Nothing in here can be heard, in the loud phase. They drum anyway, through the substrate, and the substrate carries it, and other tappers read it in their feet. The whole wall is a conversation conducted by touch through a screaming wall.

And in the air, the stilters. Membranous fliers, palm-sized, hundreds of them, and they do not fly much. They perch on the pressure nodes. They rest on nothing, folded wings and hooked feet, sitting in the invisible furniture of the standing wave, and when the wave shifts they step to the next node with a small ugly hop. From a distance, in the blue-green, the room appears to be full of animals sitting on air in ranks. They are waiting for the fall-line, where the water carries combers' larvae down, and where the gullet waits below.

The gullet is a blind eel-thing in the sump, a meter of muscle with a lateral line down each flank, and it does not hunt by sight or smell. It reads the impact pattern of falling drops. It knows the fall-line's signature. When a stilter falls in — and they do, they molt and slip and get knocked out of their nodes by the retune — the gullet is there before the splash has finished.

---

The room has weather, and the weather is a schedule.

Every forty-four seconds the wave reverses, and for something over a second the nodes smear and the hanging beads sag and shiver and some of them let go. This is the small fall. The stilters step. The combers clamp. The gullet rises.

Every ninth stroke, the comb retunes, and there is a hush.

Eleven seconds of it. The fundamental drops out; the harmonics fall away; the pressure furniture dissolves and the stilters have to fly or land; the beads all come down at once in a warm brief rain; and into that gap the entire room speaks. Tappers drum in air. Something in the ceiling makes a sound like a wet hinge. The stilters shriek at each other in a band around six kilohertz and mate on the wing. Spawning happens here — the combers release into the hush so their larvae ride the small fall rather than being crushed in the loud. Predation happens here too, because it is the only phase in which anything can locate anything else by ear. Eleven seconds of frantic, deafening-by-comparison life, and then the fundamental comes back up through the floor and takes the world away again, mid-cry.

The visitors' word for it is the slack. There are tally scratches beside the seam, hundreds of them, in groups of nine. Someone used to sit here and count.

---

Vegetation: almost none, and what there is, is a map.

There is a moss — pale, flat, glassy, more like a lichen than a moss — and it grows only in the pooled light of the eleven surviving indicators. Eleven small colonies, each the exact shape of a dying lamp's throw. Nothing else in the room can be bothered with photons; there is too much free food in the fog. The moss is a holdout, an artifact of a design assumption that someone would be here to look at the lights. In the largest colony, under the brightest indicator, there is a rosette the size of a saucer with nine stiff leaves, and it is the oldest single living thing in the chamber that isn't a lineage, and it will die when the lamp does.

The rest of the green is a single wire-vine that has come in through the seam and travels four meters along the sill, and it is not native to anything; a bird brought it, or a boot.

Everything else that looks like soil isn't. The drifts in the corners are ceramic dust from the wearing baffles, plus molt-cases, plus castings, plus biofilm — a sediment entirely composed of manufacture and its inhabitants. It has strata. It has a smell. It is, in every functional sense, mud, and there is not one natural particle in it.

---

Two more things, and they are the reason this cell is alive when the other ninety-nine are sterile.

The first is the leak. Somewhere in the lower wall a joint failed, long ago, and the chamber began to weep more than its design intended — water in the wrong places, humidity past spec, a permanent film where there should have been dry ceramic. The sealed cells are clean and dry and dead. This one leaked, and the leak made a wet margin, and the wet margin made a world. Failure is the origin of everything here.

The second is that the leak has been plugged for six hundred years by an animal.

It lives in the joint: a flat pale thing, hand-span wide and long as your forearm, a living gasket, pressing itself into the failing seam and holding the pressure. It eats what the fog brings. It has no eyes and almost no motion. It is not the same individual that first crawled in there; it is a lineage, each generation replacing the last in the groove, and the groove has been worn to fit them, or they have been worn to fit the groove, and by now it is not possible to say which. The machine's integrity is a biological process. The ecology's keystone species is a spare part.

The tone has drifted, over the centuries, as the baffles fatigued and the pits deepened — down perhaps a semitone, richer in the odd harmonics. The pitting improved it. The wear is now part of the function. Everything in here has retuned along with it: the combers' feeding phase, the stilters' node-spacing, the eleven-second hush that used to be nine. Nothing is maintained. Nothing has been maintained since the tallies stopped. The room is not decaying toward collapse; it has decayed into a different stable shape, and the shape is inhabited, and the inhabitants are load-bearing.

---

When you leave, you go out through the seam backward, and the pressure lets go of your ears with a click, and the thin cold air of the plateau is a shock like being slapped awake. And then you hear the wind. Just the wind, four and a half thousand meters up, moving over ribbed ceramic — a small, thin, empty sound, hardly a sound at all, the loudest thing on the whole mountain being your own breathing.

Behind you the seam goes on breathing out its white feather, and inside, in the dark, in the roar, ten million crowns of cilia pulse in phase, and something with no eyes holds the wall shut.
