Come up through the cloud deck at midmorning and the first thing you notice is that the light arrives from underneath. The deck lies below the terraces like a floor of packed wool, and it throws the sun back up, so the undersides of everything — the bellies of ducts, the soffits of bridges, the low green glass of the gallery roofs — are lit as brightly as their tops. There is no true shade in this place. There is only blue shade, cool-colored and full of detail, and white shade, which is not shade at all but glare coming the other way. The keepers whiten the lime faces every third year, and the whitening is why you can read the maker's stamp on a bolt head forty feet up under an overhang.

The massif has no bedrock. This is the first rule and everything else follows from it. Sink a core anywhere — through the orchard stair, through the settling pans, through the black clinker of the high shoulders — and you bring up brick. Then older brick. Then a floor, and beneath the floor another floor, and beneath that the crushed and recast rubble of the first floor, because the mountain has been ground down and remade out of itself seven or eight times and the aggregate is full of the teeth of gears, the handles of ladles, the small worn spoons of people who ate their lunch on a wall that is now a wall that is now a hillside. Drillers call the deepest samples *the recursion*. Nobody has found a bottom. What they find instead is a change in the character of the made thing: coarser, hotter, less legible, cast rather than laid, as though the lower mountain were poured in one enormous continuous operation that nobody remembers ordering.

It is warm up here, which surprises everyone. At this height the air outside the massif will freeze a wet cloth in an hour, and the wind off the deck comes in at a knife's edge, and yet the terraces hold a fig orchard and a hundred kinds of moss. The mountain is a radiator. Its iron fines are oxidizing, slowly, everywhere, a rust that has been burning for centuries at the pace of a sleeping animal; its lime is still curing in the deep galleries; its vat-halls ferment; and buried under all of it, engines that no one has seen running turn over at some fraction of a revolution per hour, and their bearings are warm, and their warmth comes up through the plate and into the roots. In the orchard stair you can put your palm flat on a riser at dawn and feel the heat of a machine that was installed before the language you're thinking in existed. The figs like it. The figs have made a religion of it.

Water is not a river here. Water is a loop, and it is intermittent, and everyone knows the schedule. Before dawn the north faces and the cold condenser crowns go wet — the *sweat hour*, when the whole upper mountain beads like a jug and the wire nets strung between the flue-heads gather it in threads and run it down into the gutters. For ninety minutes there is the sound of a thousand small drips finding a thousand small channels, and the fern galleries drink, and the mosses go from grey-green to a green that seems lit from inside. Then the sun clears the eastern stack and the faces dry, and by nine the terraces are hot and dusty and the fines lift in the thermals, and you would swear nothing had ever been wet here. Once or twice a week, in the warm months, the plume from the condenser crown climbs high enough to reach its own dew point and the mountain rains on itself — a fat, brief, iron-tasting shower that falls on the upper third and never reaches the pans. The lower districts get their water secondhand, out of cisterns, on a rotation. There are galleries down there that have been dry for two hundred years, and galleries beside them, one plate away, where the walls weep continuously and the calcite has grown into shapes like organ pipes and the local snails are the size of a fist.

Everything is close. The terraces step up in courses of eight or ten feet, and on each course the ducts run shoulder to shoulder with the trees, and the trees are trained along the ducts because there is nowhere else for them to go. Roots cannot pierce plate. They run laterally along seams, following the mortar lines, following the expansion joints, so that a mature fig is a flat fan of a thing spread across a wall like a diagram of itself, and a stand of them makes a seam-forest: canopy above, pipe-canopy below, vine-canopy between, four or five layers of leaf within a body's height, and light coming through all of it in green coins. Nothing here has a taproot. Nothing burrows. In eleven hundred species of animal on this mountain there is not one that digs, because there is nothing to dig into; instead everything climbs, or flies, or occupies a cavity that was manufactured for another purpose. The bats live in the expansion joints. The bees live in the mortar holes left by scaffolding that was struck before the massif had a name. There is a small brown bird that lays its eggs in a warm duct seam and does not sit on them, has never sat on them, and comes back in three weeks to a brood raised by the mountain's fever.

And it is loud, in the way a waterfall is loud — a broadband, unlocatable roar that is the sum of ten thousand pumps and drafts and bearings, and which you stop hearing in about forty minutes and then hear again, suddenly, at three in the morning, with your whole body. But the loudness has structure. Two of the great pump halls run a fraction out of true with each other, and their interference makes a beat: a slow swell and fade with a period of eleven minutes, so that the whole massif breathes audibly, and the goats have learned to move to the new grazing when the beat comes up, and the keepers set their small clocks by it. There are quiet seams — narrow lanes where two roars arrive out of phase and cancel, and you can hear a leaf fall, and the sudden silence is so physical that people stop walking when they enter it. And at dawn, when the sun first strikes the eastern plate, the entire mountain ticks. Millions of fixings taking up their expansion at once: a dry, rippling, forty-minute tick that moves across the faces from east to west at walking pace, and again at dusk in reverse, when it settles.

The weathering here is not decay. It is a settled argument between the made thing and the sky, and it has been going on long enough that both sides have stopped pushing. The rust has gone to patina and the patina is protective; the copper is a stable green; the corrugated iron of the old sheds has gone to lace and the lace has stopped thinning, because the vines have taken its load. The lime concrete dissolves where the weeping galleries run and makes caverns — real karst, stalactite and flowstone and blind white crustaceans in the pools — in a rock that was mixed and poured by hand. The clinker shoulders have weathered to a regolith the keepers call *fines*, and the fines are a soil: grey-black, gritty, alkaline, rich in ash and bone-meal and the powder of a million ground bricks, and it grows everything. Gullies have worn into plate. Arrises have rounded. Talus of broken firebrick lies at the foot of the high walls in perfect angle-of-repose slopes, and the lizards on it are the exact colour of the brick, which took four hundred years to arrange.

The fauna are the maintenance staff, though no one appointed them. The snails graze the lime scale off the condenser faces and keep the heat exchange honest; without them the crown would choke in a decade. The goats crop the vine off the firebreaks and the tensioned cables, and where the goats have been shut out, the plate has been pulled apart by root-wedge and the terraces have slumped. The swifts and the small hawks take the moths whose larvae eat the insulation, and there is a district on the leeward side where the swifts were driven off by a smoke, sixty years ago, and the wiring there is now bare and the district is dark and the figs are dying back. Everything is eating something that would otherwise eat the mountain. It is not a metaphor. It is a load path.

And it is all going slowly. The massif settles about four millimetres a year, which is nothing, which is a hand's width in a lifetime, which is why the doors of the lower halls no longer open and have not for a century, and why the stair has been re-cut nine times, each cutting shallower than the last, each new tread a little out of true with the old landings. The heat is failing too — the great cool, they call it, though it has never been observed to happen, only inferred: the isotherm has descended perhaps thirty metres since the oldest survey, and the orchard line has followed it down, and the high shoulders that were moss in the old paintings are now bare clinker with a lichen crust, and the moss has gone to live in the sixth gallery, where it is doing well. In four hundred years the figs will be at the pans. In a thousand the crown will be cold and white and the swifts will nest somewhere else, and the mountain will still be warm at the bottom, and still ticking at dawn, and still entirely, to its deepest core, made.

From the top course, in the white noon, you can see the whole of it: eight miles of terrace, the pans flashing like a chain of mirrors, the flue-heads trailing their thin plumes into a sky too thin to be properly blue, the cloud deck below running away to the horizon in every direction with nothing at all breaking its surface. The wind carries hot fig leaf and iron and lime. Somewhere below, a horn sounds — not a shift horn; there have been no shifts for a long time — just the seasonal note, one long tone, to say that the water rotation has changed and the west galleries may open their sluices. It goes on for a minute and a half. Then the beat comes up, and the mountain breathes, and the light keeps arriving from underneath.
