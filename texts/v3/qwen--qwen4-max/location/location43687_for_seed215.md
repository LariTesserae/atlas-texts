The ground here is nine years old, and it has not yet learned to be a landscape.

It lies at three thousand eight hundred meters, a plateau so new that no river has had time to find it. From the observation deck at the town's upper level the horizon is a ruled line — not the ragged edge of mountains but a datum, a grade held to within four centimeters across two hundred kilometers, and beyond it the faint bend of the world. Mountains stand off to the west, old and brown and thoroughly ruined, slumped into their own scree. They are the source of the feedstock and nothing else. Nobody looks at them. Everything that matters is happening on the flat.

Nothing here is rounded. That is the first thing the body notices, before the cold, before the light. There are no pebbles. No sand, no grit in the boot-treads, no clay, no dust that was made by wind. The stone breaks in clean arrises and lies in slabs the size of doors, and where it has cracked it has cracked like a pane, with a conchoidal lip you could open a wrist on. The crews wear cut sleeves. When water moves across this ground it does not gurgle. It has no channel to argue with, no gravel to lift and drop. It spreads in sheets two millimeters deep and goes where the grade tells it, and the sound it makes is the sound of breath.

---

The water is not rainfall, mostly. It comes out of the rock.

Fresh-set stone sweats. The setters lay down silicate and carbonate out of air and crushed mountain, molecule by molecule, and the lattice, closing, expels what it does not want: water, a little heat, a whisper of gas. For sixty days after a slab is laid it weeps from every face. The curing terraces stand in their own damp, beaded all over like a glass taken out of a freezer, and the runoff gathers in the provisional gutters and lies in shallow pans that freeze at night and thaw by ten and freeze again. Walk out at dawn and the whole plain is wet to the ankle in water that has never been in a cloud.

Above it hangs the works' private weather. Warm wet ground under thin cold air is an engine, and it runs every day: fog at first light, dense enough to lose a gantry in, freezing rime on every cable and handrail; the fog burning off by midmorning into a bright ordinary overcast; then, around three, the convective towers building along the accretion front where the newest stone is warmest, and a short hard shower of rain that falls only on the works and stops at a line you can stand astride. Downwind, forty kilometers off, there is a standing cloud that has been there for ninety years. The lowlanders call it the wall and use it to navigate. It is the exhaust of a continent being made.

---

The setters are the size of cathedrals and they are quiet.

There is no combustion here, no crushing, no hydraulics. Accretion happens at a scale where force is not the operative concept. A setter stands on six legs across a hundred meters of green slab, its underside a matte black plane held a hand's width above the stone, and beneath that plane the rock simply arrives — a slow bloom of grey rising like frost on a window, four centimeters an hour, edged with a faint iridescence where the lattice is still open. The noise it makes is the noise of a refrigerator in another room. A ticking, sometimes, as thermal stress finds a flaw. A sound like a wet fingertip drawn once around the rim of a glass.

They are spaced eight kilometers apart along the front, and the front is four thousand kilometers long, and there are eleven hundred of them, and from any one you cannot see the next. What you see instead is its cloud — a low white column standing on the plain, then another, then another, diminishing, until they are indistinguishable from the weather they make. Between them: nothing. Wet stone, a survey pin, the shine of standing water, and the sky.

This is the arithmetic of the place. Enormous capacity, almost no presence. A city's worth of construction spread over a country's worth of emptiness, one worker per nine square kilometers, and a silence so intact that when a woman drops a wrench on the lower terrace you hear the second and third bounce a kilometer away and turn to look.

---

The town is called Sixth Grade, and it is ninety-one years old, and it is temporary.

It sits on the oldest cured ground, where the plateau has had time to acquire a skin. Four levels, stacked and cantilevered, printed in one continuous operation and reprinted since whenever a level is needed elsewhere: the machine eats the north wing and lays it down again as a causeway. Everything is on skids or on jacks. The clinic has moved twice. The school has moved four times and the children have never once been late, because the school moves at night at the speed of a walking man and they simply keep going to the same door.

The streets were laid out for six hundred thousand people. There are nineteen thousand. So the avenues run twelve lanes wide between buildings three stories high, and grass — a stiff engineered sedge, grey-green, planted in mats — has been let into the unused lanes because bare ground here is a hazard and a nuisance. From above, the town looks like a circuit diagram with most of the components missing. At street level it looks like a place where a great many people were expected and did not come, and where the ones who did come have made a good job of it: laundry on the warm rails, tea at the shift change, a hand-lettered sign asking you not to feed anything.

Beyond the town, the terraces. Beyond the terraces, the green stone. Beyond the green stone, the front, and the fog, and the setters standing in it.

---

Life has flooded in. This surprises no one who works here; it is the whole point.

A cold high plain should be nearly empty. But the accretion front is warm — six to nine degrees above ambient for two months after laying — and wet, and it exudes a mineral film, a thin slick of accessible phosphorus and iron and reduced sulfur that the lattice cannot use. Springtails eat the film. There are, in the season, on the order of ten to the fourteenth springtails on the works, a living carpet you can see move if you kneel, a faint granular shimmer over the wet stone like the surface of a soup. Midges rise off it in columns. The columns are visible from the deck. They lean in the wind and they are the reason for everything else.

Gleaners work the columns — small pale birds, forty grams, in flocks of two hundred thousand that turn all at once and make a sound like distant applause. Sump hens run the shallow pans on foot, fat and semi-flightless, cropping the mats. Behind the gleaners come the jaegers, which rob them; behind the hens, the pale foxes that live under the skids of the town and are tolerated, counted, and named by the children. And on the older terraces, the herd: heavy, slow, engineered ungulates with splayed hooves, whose entire function is to break the crust and defecate on it. They are the soil-makers. Nine years of hooves and dung have produced, in patches, four millimeters of something that can be called dirt, and in that dirt the cushions and the lichens and the sedges have taken, and the moss has begun to hold water like a sponge, and where the moss holds water the stone at last begins to rot.

That is the only erosion on this plateau. It is being done on purpose, by animals, at a scheduled rate. The surveyors measure it monthly and are pleased.

Everything here is protected and everything here is an instrument. The flocks are the assay: if the gleaners thin, the lattice is exuding something it should not, and the front is stopped within the hour. There are protocols. There is a woman whose whole job is the springtail count. She is quiet, and she is very good company, and she has not left the terrace in six years.

---

The rules of the world are simple and everyone knows them.

Do not walk on stone less than sixty days old; it will take your boot and then your foot. Do not round anything. Do not cut a channel — drainage is provisional, gutters only, until the hydrology board releases the river plan, and the river plan is dated sixty-one years out. Do not plant anything not on the schedule. Do not feed anything. Do not take a stone home; the pins are numbered and the assay is by mass.

And: the sea is coming. Not in anyone's lifetime. But the grade falls east, four centimeters per kilometer, all the way to a basin eleven hundred kilometers away that has been dry since before the works began, and the water that weeps out of this rock is going there, one sheet-flow at a time, and one day the plan says there will be a coast.

Stand on the deck at evening when the shower has passed and the fog is re-forming below and the light is just ordinary light, gold on wet grey, and the setters ticking, and a flock turning somewhere out in all that room. Somewhere downstream, in a hundred years, there will be a beach.

The first grain of sand has not been made yet.
