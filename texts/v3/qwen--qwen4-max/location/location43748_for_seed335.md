## The Cold Room

From above, it is a place where the ferns stop.

The beech wood runs unbroken for a kilometer, then breaks: a nine-meter mouth in the limestone, ringed with hart's tongue and a fringe of frost-bitten bracken that dies back every June and comes back every July, always the same clone, always the same eleven fronds' worth of vigor. In August, when the wood is at twenty-eight degrees and the air above the canopy shimmers, a slow white fog pours out of this hole and runs downhill through the trees like spilled milk. Deer bed down thirty meters away and no closer. The cold comes out of the ground here the way water comes out of a spring, and it has been coming out for thirty-one years, which is the age of everything inside.

The way in is a rubble chute, six meters, angular blocks the size of chests stacked at the angle of repose. Nothing is rounded. Nothing is stained. The fracture faces still carry their plumose marks, the delicate feather-patterns of a crack that traveled once and stopped, and the edges will open a hand. There is dust in the sheltered pockets — actual rock flour, dry after three decades, the powder of the event itself. Below the chute the floor drops away and the room opens, and the first thing that happens is that sound arrives before space does.

## Volume

It is one room. That is the whole of it: a single bell-shaped void sixty meters long, thirty across, thirty-four to the top of the dome, closed at both ends except for the chute and a fissure and a flooded sump. It is a pocket in a plateau that sits only two hundred and forty meters above a river. There is no second chamber. There is no onward passage. Everything that exists here exists within a hundred paces of everything else, and almost none of it is near.

The floor is a jigsaw of collapse blocks that have not yet drifted apart, so that in places you can read the dome's underside in the rubble, matching scar to scar. Between the blocks: void, shadow, a drop of four meters, more void. Three pools. One fallen beech, washed in during the first winter, its trunk still hard, its bark still on. One debris fan of leaves and silt perhaps eight meters across, and on it a carpet of moss so saturated green in this light that it looks lit from beneath — *Conocephalum* in liverwort plates, then a hook-moss, then a fine fur of something that has not been identified because no one has come. Lush, absolutely lush, in the way a thing is lush when it has one substrate and unlimited water and nowhere else to go. Eight square meters of it. Forty thousand cubic meters of room.

Two hundred and fourteen soda straws hang from the dome. They are all four centimeters long.

That is the fact that reorganizes the place, once you notice it. Every drip began on the same night, when the roof opened and the pressure changed and the water found its new routes. Every straw is a first-generation part from a single batch, started together, growing at the same rate off the same film of the same stone. They hang in ranks along three fissures with the regularity of a jig. Below them the stalagmite stubs are identical white buttons, none higher than a thumb. There is no inventory here yet. No accumulation, no antiques, no fat old columns of the sort that fill the mature caves in the next valley. This room is at the beginning of its production run.

## The Engine

Nineteen minutes, more or less, depending on how much rain fell on the plateau.

The sump at the low end is the top of a flooded loop that rises somewhere under the river's terrace. Water comes in through the fissure network faster than the loop's crest can pass it, and the pool climbs, and climbs, and at some level the siphon primes with a sound like a swallowed breath amplified nine seconds long, and then eleven hundred liters go through the crest in forty seconds. The roar is not loud in itself. The room makes it loud. Nine seconds of reverberation means every event is smeared into every other event; the drip at the north wall, four strikes a second, never resolves into strikes but stands as a continuous shimmering tone; the siphon's gulp overlaps its own echo three times over. There is a noise floor here around seventy decibels, and it is made entirely of small things arriving in a space too large to let them go. Nothing is close. Everything is audible. The emptiness is what makes it deafening.

Each cycle delivers a measured dose: cold water at four degrees, a slug of dissolved calcium carbonate, a ration of shredded leaf and drowned invertebrates from the surface, and a pressure pulse that pushes stale air up the fissure. The fissure breathes on its own schedule too, reversing with the weather outside — inhaling for three days of high pressure, exhaling for a week of low, a slow tidal lung with a hiss you can hear from the chute.

And the room runs a heat cycle. All winter, cold dense air pours down the chute and pools and cannot leave, because the room is a bag with the opening at the top. It freezes the seepage in the low corner into a lens two meters thick. All summer, the cold stays trapped under its own weight while warm humid air slides in over the lip, touches stone at one point eight degrees, and gives up its water. The walls sweat. Fog stands in the middle of the void in July, thick enough to hide the far wall, and the condensation is mildly acidic and eats the rock — dissolving it back, fog-corrosion, the smooth scalloped patches on the older flowstone skin that predate the collapse. So the machine both casts and machines: precipitating identical straws along the fissures, dissolving the surplus off the walls, sorting the collapse debris by size where the flush drags it, laying graded beds of silt and sand at the pool margins with the tidy laminae of a settling tank. Coolant fog, tailings fan, waste heat carried out the fissure, one batch of parts, one shift clock.

Nothing here was made. That is worth saying plainly, because the resemblance is strong and it is entirely false. There is no artifact in the room. No wall, no cairn, no cut, no web, no nest, no burrow, no scrap. Every structure is either grown or broken. The animals don't build either — the hunting spiders work the wet film without silk, the harvestmen stand in the drip line on legs like calipers and take what the water brings, the gnat larvae secrete threads and eat the threads and secrete them again. Nothing is left behind. The room manufactures constantly and keeps none of it, and the only thing it stores is ice.

## The Population

The light comes down the chute as a single blue-grey wedge, dim as a room with the shutters closed, and it moves across the floor over the day, and the moss follows it in the only way moss can, by growing where it lands and dying where it doesn't. Under the wedge at midday, a column of midges stands in the air, two hundred thousand of them, turning slowly, the one dense object in the whole volume — and even it is mostly empty, a smoke of bodies with a hand's width between each.

Around it, spread thin across eight thousand square meters of weeping wall: cave crickets, thousands, each one a hand-span from its neighbor, antennae sweeping the film. Springtails by the million on the pool surfaces, which is to say a grey bloom the size of a dinner plate, drifting. Fungus gnat larvae in the moss, glowing a faint sick green when your eyes give up on color, thirty or forty points of light across the whole fan, spaced like the straws. Thirty-one olms in the three pools, pale as machined bone, eyeless, one to a basin, territories that have not changed in six years. Nine bats at dusk, commuting through the light shaft, roosting elsewhere. One owl that fell in during the second winter and is now a small arrangement of feathers in a sheltered pocket, undisturbed, because nothing here scavenges dry things.

You could count all of it in a week. That is the strange intimacy of the place — not that it is small, but that it is enumerable, and everything in it is the same age, descended from whatever the collapse and the first flush happened to carry down.

The flush is the shift whistle. When the siphon primes, the shrimp come up with the water, *Niphargus* in a burst of white commas, and the olms rise off the bottom for the first time in nineteen minutes and feed with that absurd lateral snap, and the crickets converge on the wrack line the surge leaves, and the harvestmen step down after them, and the spiders take the harvestmen, and midges emerge from the pool film in a pulse you can hear as a change in the shimmer. Four minutes of traffic. Then the room goes back to standing empty and roaring, and everything digests in the cold, slowly, on a metabolism that can wait a year if the rain fails.

## The Ledger

The ice in the low corner is banded. Each winter's freeze lays a clear layer; each autumn's leaf fall lays a brown one; each big flood lays a thread of silt. Thirty-one couplets, readable, the only record the place keeps, and it keeps it by accident, in the one material that survives here. In September the lens is at its thinnest, a meter and a half, sweating into a moat that feeds the middle pool. It has never gone. Two hundred and forty meters above a river where people grow figs, there is ice that has not melted since before the beech above was wrist-thick, and that is the single genuinely wrong thing about the room — everything else is only karst, doing what karst does, faster and younger and more legibly than usual.

Overhead, along the dome's crown, there is a fresh hairline with rock flour still in it.

The next block is already loose. When it goes it will bring a hundred cubic meters with it, and the chute will choke or widen, and the light wedge will move, and the moss will follow, and the drip routes will change, and a second batch of straws will start at zero alongside the first at four centimeters, and for a while there will be two generations in the room and the terrible tidiness will be over. That is the whole trajectory. This is not a cave. It is a valley in the first hour of being one, still holding its walls, still cold, still counting its parts — and the noise you hear from the chute, the endless factory roar with no factory in it, is just water and stone and forty thousand cubic meters of empty air refusing to let a single drip go quiet.
