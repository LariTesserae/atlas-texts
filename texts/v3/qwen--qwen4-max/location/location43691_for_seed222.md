## The Pavement

From the lip of the escarpment it reads as a floor. Forty kilometers of pale stone running out to a haze-line, level enough that the mind keeps trying to call it flat, though it is not flat — it is *finished*, the way a threshold is finished, worn concave where feet would go if anything had walked here for long enough. Something has. The far edge is a second escarpment, blue, notched, and between them the ground is divided into faint panels by runnels so shallow you would miss them standing in one. From up here you can see that the panels tile. They fit together in a pattern that never repeats and never quite fails to, five-fold and eight-fold, a quasicrystal the size of a county, laid down in drainage channels a hand deep.

The light comes off it twice. Once from the sun, which at this altitude has very little air to get through, and once from the stone itself, which is not simply pale but *retroreflective* — the surface film throws light back toward its source, so that around the shadow of your own head there is a soft bloom, a glory, that travels when you travel. Noon is a kind of white pressure. Shadows are blue and cut like paper. Squinting is not optional and does not entirely help.

Step down onto it and the first surprise is underfoot: the rock is warm. Not sun-warm, not the surface heat of a stone left out — warm all the way through, thirty-one, thirty-two degrees, blood temperature, and it stays that way at three in the morning. You are at 3,800 meters. There should be frost here nine months of the year. There has not been frost here for nine hundred thousand.

The second surprise is that your bootfall makes almost no sound.

## The Quiet

The pavement eats noise. It is not a metaphor and it is not simply thin air. The upper two meters of the bedrock are a lattice — a metamaterial, porous, ordered at scales below what an eye can resolve — and it takes acoustic energy the way a black surface takes light, converts it to heat, and moves the heat away sideways. A dropped hammer lands with a sound like a knuckle on bread. A shout carries eleven meters and then simply is not there anymore; you can watch a person's mouth working across a hundred paces and receive nothing but the wind.

The wind is the loudest thing, and even the wind is muffled, a hiss with the top taken off it. Dust devils cross the panels in the afternoon, three or four at a time, tall and unhurried, and they go by like smoke.

So the quiet here is not emptiness. It is *absorption*. It has a texture, a slight sense of pressure on the ears, the feeling of a room with heavy curtains. Standing still you hear your own pulse and the small wet click of your eyelids, and after an hour you begin to hear things you were not meant to hear, and after a day you understand that the plateau is not silent at all. It is talking through the floor.

Put a palm flat on the stone. There is a pulse in it, slow, irregular, arriving from somewhere north. It is a herd two kilometers away, four hundred animals, walking. Their feet are not hooves but pads — splayed, fatty, deliberate — and each footfall is a syllable. The stone carries what the air refuses. Every creature of any size on this plateau has a fat acoustic pad in the sole of the foot or the flat of the jaw, and every one of them spends part of the day with its chin on the ground, listening.

## The Rind

There is not much green. That is the thing that takes longest to accept, because the place is *full of animals*.

The vegetation is what you would expect from a high dry tableland: cushion plants in the runnel lips, one to a hundred paces, gray-green, tight as fists. Tussock in the seams. A low thorn that grows only along certain lines, which turn out, later, to be the lines where condensation drips. Saltbush on the apron at the southern end, where a shallow pan goes to crystal every dry season and glitters like a broken window. Nine trees — actual trees, four meters, gnarled, deep-rooted — around each of the big sinkholes, and nowhere else, ever. Between the sinks, nothing taller than your knee for twenty kilometers.

The primary production is not in the plants. It is on the rock: a film, a rind, a lithobiontic crust a millimeter thick that covers perhaps a third of the pavement in any given week. Cyanobacteria and something else, something that was assembled rather than evolved, living in symbiosis with the lattice beneath, which feeds them water and trace metals and takes their sugars in return. In the dry hours the rind is a paper crust, gold-brown, curled at the edges, and you can crush a handful of it to powder. Wet it and it goes green in under four seconds. Not minutes. You can watch the color arrive, a wave crossing a stone at the speed of a slow blink.

And it teems. Hold a lens to a wet patch and the crust is a city: mites like glass beads, springtails in drifts, tardigrades grazing, nematodes in their thousands per square centimeter, and threading through all of it the pale filaments of the under-weave, coming up through the pores to be cleaned.

## The Dew Front

Real rain comes over the escarpment perhaps ten days a year, in the hot season, in towers of cloud you can see arriving from an hour away. The rest of the water is made.

Before dawn the air over the plateau is dry and very slightly cold, and then, along a line to the east, the stone begins to sweat. It is not fog. There is no fog. The lattice's upper pores open in sequence and adsorb vapor out of the passing air with an efficiency no natural surface achieves, and the moisture is delivered downward and laterally and pooled where the terrain wants it pooled: in the dolines, in the runnel bottoms, in the seams under the thorn.

The line moves. It crosses the plateau at about the speed of a walking person, four kilometers an hour, tracking the sun's angle sector by sector, and you can stand and watch it come — a change in the stone's color, a darkening front, a faint glitter, the rind going green in a band a hundred meters wide, and behind the band the crust already crisping back to gold as the day burns it off.

Everything follows the front. That is the whole ecology in one sentence. The lickers — the pale, hoofless grazers, donkey-sized, file-tongued, that scrape the rind off the rock in strips — walk west-to-east ahead of it in loose lines a kilometer apart, taking the crust at its four-second peak. Glass ants harvest behind them. Sifters, long-winged and fringed at the trailing edge like owls, work the air above the front where the disturbed chaff — the aerial plankton, the spores and mites and drifting cells that ride the thermals all day — is thickest. And the flatjaws come along the runnels behind the herd, low, knuckle-walking, chin to stone, reading the pads.

Two hundred lickers grazing three hundred meters away make less noise than a hand rubbing cloth. You see the dust, the slow swing of heads, the ripple of a group changing direction all at once with no signal you can detect. You feel the argument in your feet.

At the sinks, though — where wet clay fills the doline floor and the absorbent pavement is interrupted — sound gets out. The nine trees around each sink are full of birds that actually sing, small, plain, brown, absurdly loud, and the noise of a sinkhole carries half a kilometer across the silent stone like a lit window across a dark field. The water pockets are the sound pockets. Everything that wants to be heard goes there to do it. It is where the mating happens, the arguing, the mobbing of predators, the whole audible business of life, packed into a dozen hectares out of four thousand.

## What Is Scattered

There is a tower at the northern end, forty meters, alone, tapering, its lower third fluted like a comb. It has no door. It is warm. In the last hour before dawn it puts out a faint corona of condensation that falls as a fine local rain and greens two hectares of rind, and the herds know this and are already there.

There are walls: low drystone-looking fins, half a meter, running in straight lines for two hundred meters and stopping. They are heat exchangers. They have been weathered so long they look like the ruins of walls, and in a sense they are the ruins of walls; the thorn grows along their drip lines, and lizards work them at midday.

There are cairns that breathe. Warm air comes out of them at night, enough to flutter a thread.

There is a stair, cut clean, descending nineteen steps into a sinkhole and ending at a face of stone slightly whiter than the rest.

There is a hatch in the open pavement, the size of a door, flush, unopenable, with a rim of thorn around it because water gathers at the seam.

And there are three tents, pale, low, on the salt apron in the cool season, and a family of eleven who move with the lickers and keep a dog that does not bark, because barking is useless here and the dog knows it. They walk the Nine Sinks in a fixed order at the turn of every nineteen days. They put salt on the apron. They will not kill a mender — the small burrowing thing, otter-ish, dust-colored, that carries shed lattice filaments into the cracks and packs them there with its nose. They cannot tell you why. Their grandmother could not tell her either. It is simply what is done.

They are right not to kill them. The menders are the maintenance crew. So are the lickers, whose pads press dust into the pores as they walk; so are the trees, whose roots the weave has grown along and now uses as conduit; so is the dew front, which is also a flushing cycle. The machine did not fail and it did not leave. It *delegated*. It pushed its upkeep out into weather and animals and let erosion do the shaping, and it has been in balance so long that its repairs read as geology — the whiter scars where it has precipitated new stone are called mends, and they run across the panels in lines that look, from the escarpment, uncomfortably like writing.

If the herds died, the plateau would cool over about a century. The lickers do not know this. The family does not know this. The lattice does not know it either, exactly; it only knows that when a sector's biological throughput falls below a threshold it opens a sinkhole, and the trees come, and the animals come to the trees, and the accounting balances again.

## The Turn, and Night

Every nineteen days a sector rests. Ten thousand hectares go cold and dry in the space of an afternoon, the rind shrivels and does not revive, the dew front detours, and everything with legs moves — not fleeing, just *relocating*, in the unhurried way of a thing that has happened ten thousand times. For about four minutes at the start of the turn, every animal on the plateau stops moving at once. Nobody has explained this. The family says it is polite to stop too.

There is one predator that hunts in the air and does not listen at all: a broad pale hawk that works the noon glare and reads the *glory*, the retroreflective bloom, for the holes that bodies make in it. Anything that moves is a moving absence of light. The lickers have a defense against this, which is to stand so still and so evenly spaced that the pattern of holes looks like the pattern of the panels.

Night comes down fast and blue. The stone stays at blood heat, so lying on it feels like lying against something alive, and shade in the daytime gives no relief from that, which is the single most unsettling fact about the place. High up there is a faint arc, east to west, the dust of a ring that is mostly gone, and it keeps the dark from being total. The trees at the sinks go quiet. The birds are done.

And the ground begins to sing: the seismic chorus, four hundred herds' worth of pads and jaws and drumming feet coming up through the rock from every direction at once, layered, slow, structured, with pauses in it that are too regular to be accidental. You hear none of it. You lie on the warm pavement with your ear against the stone and it arrives in your skull like a room full of people talking in the next house.

To the east, a line of glitter is coming at walking pace.
