## The Cup

You walk toward it over a plain that has no soil. That is the first thing to understand about this country: it is limestone all the way down, and rain does not stay to make earth, it goes into the rock and is gone. Nothing grows here because there is nothing to grow in. The pavement is grey-white, jointed into slabs the size of doors, runnelled where a rarer rain than the one that falls now once tried to make a stream. Underfoot it rings. Heat comes up off it in a way you can see, and the horizon shivers, and there is no shade anywhere in a day's walk.

Then the ground stops.

The hole is not large. Fifty paces by twenty at the lip, an oval, and the lip overhangs on three sides so that from a distance you do not see a hole at all, only a shadow lying on the stone like something spilled. Warm air comes out of it. Not a draft — the air is not moving enough for that — but a body of warmth that has been sitting there so long it has its own weight, and it smells of wet chalk and copper coins and, faintly, of the inside of a mouth.

The descent is thirty-one steps, cut. That is the first sign of hands, and it comes before you have seen anything else.

---

Fourteen metres down, the light gives up being weather and becomes a property of the room. The slot above is a dogleg — a fissure that bends twice before it opens to the sky — so no direct beam has ever touched this floor. What arrives is sky-glow that has bounced off pale rock outside, come around two corners, and landed already used. It is the brightness of a room an hour after sunset. It is that brightness at noon and it is nearly that brightness at midnight; there is a difference, and it is a difference for instruments. Your pupils open, and the place reassembles itself identically, and this happens every day, and it is not an event.

The floor is packed. Not crowded — *packed*, in the sense a jar of stones is packed. Collapse blocks the size of oxen have come down at some remote time and settled into each other, and the water has since rounded every one of them and widened the joints between them to a finger's breadth and no more. Between and among and under them: water. Rimstone dams, breast-high in places, nested cell against cell, each basin brimming into the next with a sheet of overflow two millimetres thick. There is no dry ground. There is nowhere to lie down. There is nowhere to set a thing without setting it in water, and the water is blood-warm, thirty-three degrees, so that when you step in you cannot tell where your skin ends and the place begins. You move sideways. You put your palms on the bosses and they are slick and faintly yielding, like the film on a tooth, and you go on.

The air is as full as the floor. It cannot take another grain of water. Sweat sits on you and stays. Your own breath comes back at you off the wet stone with the edges taken off — the room absorbs a voice rather than echoing it, all those rounded surfaces scattering the sound into a soft flattened return, half a beat late, so that speaking here feels like speaking into a folded blanket.

Mostly, no one speaks. There is a drip. It falls from a bell-hole in the overhang into the third basin, and it falls every nine seconds, and it has fallen every nine seconds for longer than there have been ears in this district to time it. After a minute you stop hearing it. After an hour you would swear the place was silent, and you would be wrong, and the wrongness would be in you.

---

The stone is a pale blue-grey where it is wet, which is everywhere, and ivory where the old sinter runs, which is also everywhere. One seam weeps rust: an orange beard of iron down a wall, sharp against all that milk. Elsewhere the walls carry black dendrites, manganese, branching and feathering in fern-shapes and frond-shapes and the shapes of little trees. They are the only branching things in the Cup. They are mineral. They have never been alive and they are not the ghosts of anything that was; they simply grew the way a crack grows, following the easiest path, and they stopped.

There is no green. There is no moss on the wet ledges, no fern in the joints, no root anywhere, no leaf, no seed, no pollen drifting in the slot, no smell of rot at all — which is the strangest absence, once you notice it, because a warm wet place should stink of decay and this one does not. Nothing has ever died here in sufficient quantity to rot. The water is clear as air and tastes of stone and a whisper of salt and nothing else. It is hard: it furs the tongue, it leaves a fine grit on the teeth. Carry a jar of it up the thirty-one steps and out onto the pavement and by evening you will have a jar of white sand.

What lives here lives on the rock itself. In the rust seam and two others there is a film — colourless where it is thin, ivory where it is thick, never thicker than a fingernail's width. It eats the iron and the sulphur that come up dissolved from wherever the water has been. It is not a plant. It does not want light. Under a lens it is a mat of threads and beads, and it is the entire base of everything that happens in this room.

Grazing it: snails. Translucent shells the size of a thumbnail, forty-one of them in the whole Cup, and the count has been forty-one for as long as anyone has bothered to count. They keep the film at exactly one grazing deep. In the water-filled joints, blind white isopods, unhurried, picking at what the snails leave. A flatworm like a scrap of wet paper. High in the dogleg, where the outside occasionally gets in, one spider that lives on midges blown down from a country that has other things in it.

That is the fauna. Once, in the memory of the oldest woman in the coastal village, a bird fell in.

---

Here is why nothing changes.

The water arrives already full. It has come a long way underground, from rain that fell on mountains a hundred miles inland, down through the rock until the rock's own heat warmed it, and along until it could hold no more stone than it holds. It surfaces here at the head of the oval in a throat you cannot see, and it leaves at the far end through a fissure you cannot see either, and the two rates are the same. It cannot dissolve anything, because it is saturated. It cannot lay anything down, because the air above it is saturated too, and there is no draft through the dogleg to steal a molecule and start the giving. Nothing evaporates. Nothing precipitates. Nothing is subtracted and nothing is added. The plain above has no soil to send down, no roots to wedge the joints, no litter to make acid. The slot is too narrow and bent for anything larger than a midge to fall through, and the walls below the lip are bellied outward and polished, so nothing that lands on the rim can walk down.

The place is protected by its own shape.

It was not always like this. All the great soft forms — the scalloped walls, the flutings, the bell-holes, the nested dams, the sinter bosses like poured wax — are the work of an older water, a hungrier water that could still take and still give, in a time when the plumbing was open and the air moved. That water finished its work. Then something collapsed somewhere out of sight and sealed the draft, and the chemistry came to rest, and the Cup crossed over from being made into being kept.

That is the distinction the whole place turns on. It is not stopped. Water goes through it at the rate of a small river; heat comes up from below and leaves through the slot at exactly the rate it arrives; the film grows and is eaten at the same speed; the drip falls and the basin overflows and the overflow goes down the fissure and is gone. Everything moves. Nothing accumulates. It is a standing wave in the shape of a room, and it has been standing there since before there were people to be frightened of it.

---

The people came anyway.

The thirty-one steps are theirs, and a rope-groove worn into the lip where something was hauled, and two square sockets cut for the ends of a ladder that is not there. On the floor, among the jammed blocks, the reason: a quarry. They wanted the sinter — the ivory stone, which cuts like hard cheese while it is under this water and sets hard as chalk in the dry air above. There are cut trenches, straight and true, running between the natural bosses. There are blocks half-freed, still held to the floor by a finger of stone at one corner. There is one block abandoned on the twelfth step.

There is a wedge of native copper in a trench, cold-hammered, skinned green, and a hammerstone of a granite that occurs nowhere within four days' walk, and a scatter of chert flakes, and a low wall of dry-stacked slabs at the head of the stair whose purpose nobody has satisfactorily explained. Nine notches on the wall beside the rope-groove. In a pocket of insoluble clay — the only clay in the Cup, the residue of a great deal of dissolved rock — five footprints, small, bare, held perfectly.

No charcoal. No ash. No wood, no fibre, no bone. There was nothing here to burn and nothing here to burn it with, and whatever they carried in they carried out, except the tools they left in the trenches when they stopped.

They stopped a long time ago, and this is the thing that catches in the throat: in a place of nothing but rounded forms, the tool marks are the sharpest objects present. Every chisel stroke stands as crisp as the hour it was struck. The half-freed blocks are still half-freed. The stone that was going to be taken out next is still waiting to be taken out next. The water has not softened a single edge, because the water cannot take anything more; the air has not filmed them over, because the air cannot give anything back. The Cup has held the last morning of that work exactly as it was, warm and dim and dripping, for longer than the language it was ordered in has existed.

Climb out at the end. The plain will be doing what the plain does — glaring, ringing underfoot, breathing its dry heat, going nowhere. Behind you the hole will go on lying on the stone like a shadow, full to the brim of warm water it can neither add to nor lose, keeping its one drip, its forty-one snails, its five footprints, its unfinished cut.

Nothing here is impossible. A chemist would call it merely unlikely: a system that found its balance and then had the luck, or the geometry, never to be disturbed. Stand at the lip long enough and the difference between those two descriptions stops mattering. The place is not waiting. Waiting implies a next thing. It is finished, and it is wet, and it is warm, and it is holding.
