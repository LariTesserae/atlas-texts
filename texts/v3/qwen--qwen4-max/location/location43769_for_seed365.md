Nothing here is rounded.

The cliffs still carry the marks of the break — fluted scars where the slab tore free and dropped, pale mineral streaks oxidizing down them in orange and rust-blue, the raw faces not yet softened by a single season of patience. The scree below is all edges. It shifts underfoot with a dry click and gives you a shin-bruise through your boot. There is no gravel, no sand, no shingle. The stream has not been running long enough to make a pebble; it carries rock flour instead, and runs milky, and cuts a slot through the floor so narrow you can stand astride it and watch the water argue with itself eight feet down.

The basin is nine hundred paces long and five hundred across. You can walk its whole perimeter in an hour if you are careful, and the people here are always careful. The walls rise three hundred meters and overhang in three places, so climbing out is a matter of rope and luck. At one end there is a notch called the Throat, where the stream leaves and the fog comes in. Everything that lives here came through the Throat or fell from the rim.

Above, a lid. A stratus deck sits at the level of the plateau and rarely lifts, and the walls are high enough that direct sun reaches the floor for perhaps forty minutes near midday, and then only as a smear on the eastern cliff, the color of watered milk. The rest is a cool blue-gray diffusion, shadowless, directionless, the kind of light in which you cannot tell how far away anything is. Temperature holds between four and twelve degrees, all year. Breath shows in the morning. Hands are always chapped. Nothing freezes hard and nothing ever dries.

---

In that light, at first, you count hills.

Eight of them, low and long, each with a crown of scrub and a dark ruff of moss, standing widely apart on the bare gray floor. The spacing looks deliberate, like an orchard. Sixty paces at the closest, more often a hundred and twenty. There is a great deal of empty rock between them, and the emptiness reads as the dominant fact of the place — a swept, damp, featureless pavement, and on it a few slow green shapes.

Then one of the hills puts out a foot.

It takes about four minutes. The leg is columnar, six-toed, the sole a pad of gray callus wide as a threshing floor, and it comes down with no sound at all, and the whole grove on the animal's back tilts a degree and settles. You feel the arrival in your knees rather than hear it.

They are called the standing, or the slow, or simply by their numbers — Third, Fifth, the Calf. Thirty meters from muzzle to rump, twelve at the shoulder, hide the color of wet slate and furred everywhere with a fine nap that sheds condensate in beads. They have no eyes worth the name. They have ears like folded sails, twelve feet of thin cartilage and velvet, and they carry them furled along the flanks in dry weather and open them, in rain, into trembling dishes.

Their mouths are fringed. They walk with the mouth open and the fringe combing the air, and what they eat is the fog's cargo: the aerial fall, the drifting plankton of the high cloud — midge and mite and spore and shredded wing, the gray silt of the sky. A beast filters a hundred thousand cubic meters of air in a day and gains, perhaps, a kilo. This is why they stand apart. Each one leaves behind it a wake of stripped, exhausted air, and to walk in another's wake is to starve slowly in a full belly's company. The spacing is not manners. It is arithmetic.

The fog enters at the Throat and moves up the basin at a walking pace, a river of air with banks and eddies. The herd stands in ranks across it. When the flow reverses — a few times a season, after a storm on the plateau — the whole herd turns to face the new current, and the turning takes nine days, and the town turns with it.

---

There is no soil on the floor of this basin. There is only rock, chipped and raw and three hundred years old. All the soil in the world is on the backs of the animals.

It is forty centimeters deep, black, springy, and entirely made: dung and dead midge and shed hide-fuzz and the felted corpses of ten thousand generations of moss-grazers. It holds water like a sponge and it smells like a greenhouse floor, sweet and fungal and faintly ammoniac. In it grow mats of moss so dense you can walk on them without leaving a print, liverworts in overlapping plates, ferns that unroll in a slow spiral you can watch across an afternoon, and the trees — hobble-birch, fog-willow — which are two hundred years old and four meters tall, wind-pruned, trunk-bent, hung with lichen curtains that drip steadily even when it has not rained for two weeks.

Each beast carries a pond in the hollow behind its shoulder blades: a depression in the hide, lined with a callus like a fingernail, holding perhaps two thousand liters of fog-water and leaf-fall. Eight ponds. Eight islands. Each has its own shrimp, and the shrimp differ — Third's are pale and blind, Sixth's are banded red, the Calf's are still the same as its mother's, and everyone knows it, and the ledger-keepers track the lineages the way other towns track families. When two beasts are allowed to touch, the ponds spill and merge, and the shrimp cross, and something new appears in the water within a year. The pond-fauna are how the town tells time.

Under the trees live mat-crabs, thumbnail snails in drifts like fallen grain, springtails in such quantity that a handful of moss held over your palm rains white for a minute. Nothing here is bare. What looks like empty gray floor from thirty paces is, at your feet, a pavement of tiny shells and cast skins and the shed fringes of filter-mouths, all of it moving, all of it eating. The emptiness is a matter of scale. There are more animals in this basin than there are stones, and the stones are new.

---

Water is everywhere and never anywhere. There is no lake. There is no river you could name on a map — the melt-slot runs for three hours after midday and then subsides to a seep, and in the dry weeks it does not run at all.

Instead: cups. Four hundred-odd rock basins in the floor, from a thimble to a bathtub, each holding rain and dew, each named, each owned, each with its own little cloud of midge and its own film of green. Cup-rights are inherited, litigated, willed. A marriage is largely a negotiation about cups.

And combs: frames of woven fiber strung on poles between the ledges and the beasts' own antler-crop, hung out into the fog, dripping into gutters of folded bark. A good comb yields nine liters a night. The town drinks fog. Its skin is always slightly damp, its felt is always slightly damp, and the smell of the place is wet wool, crushed green, cold stone, and the faint iodine of the ponds.

---

The town is called Eight. It has been called Eleven, and Fifteen, and once, in a bad century, Four. The name is a census, and the ledgers are a list of former names.

Two hundred and thirty people. Houses of lashed timber and hide over a felt shell, built on skids, greased with pond-muck, hauled by hand. Nothing has wheels; wheels are loud. The lanes are felt-paved, moss-packed, replaced weekly. Stairs cut into the cliff have their treads packed with wool. Tools are stone, bone, antler, sinew, and native copper that is pressed cold into shape rather than struck, because striking carries. Kilns are sunk into the ground and fitted with damper-lids of wet clay; the whole pottery trade happens underground, in the dark, by feel.

The buildings climb the beasts as much as the ground. Rope ladders up the flank, platforms in the hobble-birch, a whole quarter of gardens and drying-racks and hammocks riding on Fifth's back, harvested by people who have never in their lives stood on rock for more than an hour. Between neighbors run slack bridges — and the bridges are the town's central anxiety, because a bridge that goes taut becomes a tether, and a tether draws two beasts together. There is a guild whose entire profession is watching gaps. They carry hooked knives and a knotted measuring line, and when the distance closes past the mark they cut, without ceremony, without asking, and whoever's laundry was on the line loses their laundry.

The floor is dotted with pads: flat rectangles of dressed stone, hundreds of them, scattered across the bare rock in patterns that make no sense until you understand that each is a place a house used to be. The town's whole history is written on the ground in empty foundations, a slow dance recorded in footprints. Three generations of arrangement, and no ruin stands anywhere, because everything is designed to be moved.

Speech is a ten-finger cant, small and fast, with the wrists held close to the body. Faces are half-veiled in felt to muffle breath. Shoes are soled in three layers of it. Infants are swaddled to the chin and carried in a moss-lined sling against the skin, and births are timed, where possible, to weather.

---

Here is the rule the whole place is built around.

Sound does not leave. A loud noise, in this basin, does not spread and thin and die. It stays where it was made — a pale seam hanging in the air, a shimmer like the crack in old ice, faintly luminous, roughly the shape of the sound's shape. A dropped pot leaves a knot the size of a fist. A shout leaves a ragged ribbon two meters long. They persist for hours, or days, and they hum below hearing, and if you stand near one your back teeth ache and your eyes water.

And things come to them.

You can see the consequence in the plaza, which is the oldest place in town and the emptiest. At the center of the basin hangs the First Knock: the sound of the floor dropping, three hundred years ago, still there — a seam nine meters long, white as a scar, bent like a lightning-root, silent. Around it, on the bare rock, stand the Attending.

There are perhaps four hundred of them. Black, felted, many-limbed, the size of a large dog, faces like folded hands. They are the only fast thing in this world, and they have been standing still for three centuries, waiting for a sound that has already finished happening. Moss has taken their shoulders. Ferns grow out of the joints of them. Hobble-birch seedlings root in the dust of their backs. From a distance they are a field of black boulders with gardens on them, and children are told not to go among them, and the children go, and mostly nothing happens, because nothing has happened there for three hundred years.

Sometimes one is not there anymore. That is how you learn what they are.

A few people survive an attention. What is taken from them is not the body but the sound: afterward they can strike a drum and nothing comes out of it, they can drop a stone from a cliff and the stone lands without a report, their laughter is a shape in the air with nothing inside. These are the hushed. They quarry, they break rock, they haul, they do all the loud work of the town, and they are honored and pitied and married rarely. They can walk through a hanging seam and feel nothing in their teeth. The wardens who read the marks and map them and route the lanes around them are chosen from the deaf, who are called the free, and who sleep perfectly well beside a wound in the air.

---

Then it rains, and the world is let off its leash.

Rain comes every eleven to thirty days and lasts from forty minutes to six hours, and while it falls the noise-floor of the basin rises above the threshold that matters. The seams dissolve. The Attending fold up and go back into the cliffs, fast, in a black flicker you catch only as an absence. The beasts open their ears and *stride* — forty meters in an hour, an obscene speed, whole groves swaying.

And the town screams. It hauls houses, drums on covered frames, argues, sings in a ragged unison that has no melody because melody is a thing you can only practice in rain and rain is rare. Markets happen. Weddings happen. Children are conceived and born and named aloud, and the naming is shouted, all of it, the whole name at once, so it will be heard by something other than the sky. The gap-guild cuts and re-rigs a season's worth of bridges in an afternoon. It is the loudest place you have ever stood in and it lasts, on average, ninety minutes.

On the rim there is a rain-warden, watching the deck, and when the rain thins she drops a red flag down a wire, and the town stops mid-gesture. Mid-word. A drum with the stick still raised. There is a name for the last thirty seconds and everyone is afraid of it, and every generation loses someone who kept talking into the tail.

---

The herd must never touch. When two touch, they root: the legs stiffen, the hide lignifies, the mouths close, the ponds spill and join, and over forty years the two bodies become a single hill with real trees on it and real soil a meter deep, and both animals die at the pace of a forest growing.

There are three such hills at the basin's edge. They are the old town: terraced, stair-cut, orcharded, the ribs showing through the turf like the rafters of a buried hall, the skull of one hollowed into a cistern that holds nine thousand liters and never goes stale. People are buried in the flanks. The wood for every house in Eight comes from these three corpses, and is rationed, and is inherited.

So the town makes land by walking two gods into each other, on purpose, with felt-tipped poles, over the course of a rain. It takes four hours and it is the only thing they do that requires everyone. There is a calf this year, and the arithmetic is already being argued in the ledgers: eight adults and a ninth coming, in a basin nine hundred paces long, where the fog will only feed so many mouths.

---

At night the deck lowers and touches the groves, and the whole place drips. People carry glimpse-jars — pond shrimp in a glass of fog-water, pressed against the palm to make them flare a cold green for three seconds, then dark again. The lanes are lit, sparsely, by the old seams, which glow faintly and are avoided. Eight vast shapes breathe once a minute, a long sigh you feel in the sternum from a hundred paces. The mat-crabs come out and graze the moss in patterns visible from the ledges as pale tracks, like frost.

And all week, imperceptibly, the herd turns to face the new wind, and the town turns with it, and in the plaza the moss grows another millimeter over four hundred patient black shoulders, and one of them, sometime before morning, will not be there.
