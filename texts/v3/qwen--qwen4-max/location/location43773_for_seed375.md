## The Basin Under the Lid

The plain is anhydrite, the grey-white of a fingernail, and it has broken rather than worn. Everywhere the plates lie tilted against one another with their edges still sharp, still holding the fine burr of the fracture, so that walking across it is like walking across a dropped dinner service. Nothing here is rounded. No grain has been softened. There is no water to soften it and there never has been; whatever moisture the plain owns is locked inside the crystal, and the crystal will not give it up for anything so mild as weather.

You do not see the basin until you are standing at its lip. It is a subsidence, a place where the ground decided to go down, four hundred paces across and sixty deep, and it is roofed.

The roof is the first wrong thing. It sags from rim to rim in long catenary valleys, grey and faintly veined, and the daylight coming through it is the colour of weak tea held up to a lamp. It is not stone. It is not canvas. Press it and it gives like a stack of letters. It is fibre, laid in sheets a hair thick, thousands upon thousands of sheets, and it has been growing across the hole for about eleven years.

There is a stair down. It was not cut. It was laid, tread by tread, and each tread is a slightly different colour and grain, and if you know how to read the material you can tell that the stair was made out of a series of unrelated events, some of them years apart, assembled by something with a sense of sequence but no sense of style.

## The Material

At the bottom the air is cool and absolutely still — eight or nine degrees, the kind of cool that does not move and does not change, cold cloth against the face. It smells of cold dust, old paper, struck flint, and a thin ghost of ammonia that catches at the back of the nose and then apologises.

And there is a town.

Galleries, arcades, flights of steps that arrive at landings and stop. Plazas. Tenements of stacked cells with their doorways all facing the same way. Long causeways of laminated fibre spanning the open floor, ribbed underneath like the roofs of mouths. Buttresses. A district of low dense courtyards, and beyond it a district of tall thin fluted towers, and between them a broad processional way with a drainage-fall along its centre that has never drained anything. It is organised. It has the unmistakable grammar of settlement: infrastructure, hierarchy, districts, a plan revised many times by many hands.

It is also almost entirely empty, and it is silent in a way that silence usually is not.

The silence has pressure. It sits on the ears like descent in a fast lift. You hear your pulse. You hear the wet click of your own eyelids, the small gravel of your jaw, the whine that lives behind the teeth. You do not hear your feet. You kick a loose chip of anhydrite across the plaza and you hear the very front of the sound — the *t* of it, the first half-instant — and then it is gone, taken, and where it went there hangs a wisp of pale floss in the air, drifting, settling.

That is the rule of this place, and everything else follows from it. **Sound precipitates.** Air carrying a disturbance sheds it as matter. A noise does not fade here; it falls out. It comes down as fibre, pale and dry and warm for a moment from the spending, and it accumulates on whatever is beneath it, and that is what the town is made of.

The town is a transcript.

## The Grain of Events

Once you know, you cannot stop reading it.

The footings are black felt, dense and load-bearing, laid in courses so fine they look machined. That is infrasound: the slow hum of the lid breathing as the day warms it and the night lets it go, the sub-audible grind of cold air pooling in the basin after dark and draining at dawn through a throat at the northern end. Below hearing, but not below consequence. Eleven years of that hum, folded and compressed, is what everything else stands on.

The walls are pale, the colour of weak tea, and they are ordinary events: chips falling, fibre tearing, the small ticks of a cooling roof. Each layer is an incident. You can put your thumbnail into a wall and flick through it like a ledger.

And here and there, ribs and lintels and whole vaults of a hard bright stuff like sugar-glass, translucent, ringing when you touch it and immediately spending the ring into more of itself. That is catastrophe. There is one ward in the southeast built almost entirely of it — a cluster of translucent chambers with shapes suspended inside them, blurred, the way a fly is blurred in amber. Locals, if there were locals, would call it the Bright Ward, and would not go there.

The streets have names, or would have. The long processional way is a rockfall, laid down in a single night, its layers coarse and cross-bedded. A narrow stair in the western arcades is somebody limping. There is a whole terrace, three storeys of it, made of a sound that repeated at intervals for six days and then stopped.

Nothing is weathered. A tear in the lid makes a ripping noise, and the ripping noise patches the tear, sharper-edged than before. The city heals by being damaged. It cannot get old. This is why every arris in the place is fresh enough to cut you, and why the whole town, for all its layered antiquity of texture, is younger than a sapling.

## Who Farms It

Nine masons live here. Nine, in a space the size of a market town, each one at the centre of its own ward, so that walking between two of them takes you through a great deal of nothing.

They are sessile, pale, the size of a haycock, and they are the vegetation such as it is — the only thing in the basin that grows and does not walk. A slit along the upper surface extrudes ribbon; a fringe of long jointed filaments, which are not legs and are not quite hands, gathers the fallen floss from the ground and feeds it in. They have no eyes. They have listening surfaces, broad and thin and turned always outward, and they are the reason the town has a plan.

Because the masons do not merely collect. They *farm*.

Along the northern rim, where the anhydrite is freshest, they have built catchment throats: funnels of taut membrane tuned to the draft, so that the dawn outflow plays them below hearing and lays down felt. They have built spillways and chutes and undermined shelves, deliberately, patiently destabilising the cliff so that it keeps shedding, keeps cracking, keeps making the small sharp noises that are the staple crop. The rim is young because they keep it young. Erosion here is not a process. It is an agricultural policy.

Around them, sparse: forty-odd grey rosettes of wick in the seams, a lichen that gives off just enough blue-green foxfire to show you your own hands in the deep galleries, and no more. The masons' blood, if you cut one, is a dry cold oil that will not wet your finger. Nothing in this basin has ever been wet.

## The Silence Economy

There are perhaps thirty gleaners: soft six-limbed things the size of cats, earless, footed in felt, that rasp floss off the walls and eat it. They are thieves of the record. A wall they have grazed goes blurry, its incidents unreadable.

And there is one crier.

To speak here is to spend your own substance — the sound comes out of you and becomes matter and does not come back. This is why the basin is quiet. It is not peace. It is thrift. Everything that lives here has learned that noise is a haemorrhage, and the gleaners have taken this to its conclusion: they have no voice at all, and they move like a rumour.

The crier has not taken that conclusion. It sleeps most of the year in a hammock of black felt in the high western galleries, and when it hunts it wakes and *shouts*, and the shout precipitates where it stands — a cage, walls and floor and lid, hard bright sugar-glass, formed in a half-second around whatever was near enough.

The town is full of small doorless rooms. Most of them are empty. Some of them have a hollow in the middle shaped like something that struggled and then was eaten through the wall.

That is the density of this place: nine masons, thirty gleaners, one predator, and four hundred thousand rooms.

## The Unsaid

At the centre there is a courtyard where nothing has ever sounded.

It is a perfect void in the fabric — no floor of fibre, no walls, just the raw fractured anhydrite of the basin bottom, sharp plates tilted against each other, cold enough to burn the palm. Nothing has ever fallen here. Nothing has ever hummed here. The draft goes around. The gleaners cross it in a straight line at their fastest walk, because it is the one place where the crier cannot build, and because a thing that makes no sound in a place that makes no record is, briefly, not anywhere at all.

The masons have built their tallest structures around its edge. They lean in over it, filaments extended, listening surfaces turned toward the emptiness, and they have been leaning for eleven years, and nothing has come.

## What You Are Worth

Stand at the edge of the Unsaid with your hand still on the cold rock and understand the situation.

The basin is starving. The crop is thin; the rim is failing to fail; the felt courses are getting finer every season. The whole apparatus of throats and chutes and undermined shelves exists to extract a few grams of matter a night from a cliff that has learned to hold still. Everything here is silent because silence is what poverty looks like when poverty is also safety.

And you have a voice you have never had to pay for. You have lungs full of free material. You could laugh once and floor a courtyard. You could shout and raise a tower, and the masons would turn every listening surface they own toward you, and the gleaners would come out of the arcades in a soft grey converging drift, and up in the western galleries the hammock would begin to move.

Behind you the stair you came down has already thickened under your feet — a few pale layers, your own weight recorded, the treads a little higher than they were.

You could go up quietly. You will not go up quietly. Nobody does.
