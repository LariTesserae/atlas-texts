## Valve Hall No. 2

The sound arrives before the place does.

You come down the surge shaft — a riveted steel throat, four metres across, dropping through forty-one metres of cold green water — and the noise builds the whole way: a broad, grainy roar with a floor under it, a fifty-hertz thrum you feel in the sternum rather than hear. Light goes out of the water in stages. Red dies first, then orange, then the yellow of rust. By twenty metres the world is a single colour with different amounts of darkness in it. By thirty you are inside the sound and can no longer tell which direction it comes from, because in water sound moves too fast for your ears to argue about it. It simply *is*, the way pressure is.

Then the shaft opens, and there is a room.

---

Twenty-six metres by eleven. Nine to the ceiling. You can swim its perimeter in under two minutes and know its whole shape, and people do — the eels do it nightly, the same route, wall to wall to wall.

Nothing here is natural. Not one surface. The floor is a poured slab, the walls are mass concrete with the board-marks of the shuttering still legible under a century of leaching, and the lower two metres are lined in glazed brick, cream once, now the colour of weak tea. The ceiling is a grid of reinforced beams with a gantry rail running its length. Every stone in the room was carried here. The gravel in the concrete was dredged from a river that no longer exists in that place; the lime in the cement was burned out of limestone that was itself a seabed, once, in a warm shallow sea with no name. The room is made of old seafloor and has become seafloor again. It took a hundred and twelve years to complete the circuit.

Outside the walls: silt. A hundred square kilometres of grey mud, flat as a held breath, sloping away into black. That is the whole basin. This room is the only hard thing in it.

---

Four vertical pump sets stand in a row down the centreline, each one a bulbed column of cast iron two metres through, flanged and bolted, with a bronze gate stand beside it and a handwheel at chest height. There is a gantry crane parked at the far end, its chain hoist hanging in a long slow catenary. There are cable trays, conduit, a wall of switchgear with its doors gone, a bench, a rack for spanners, and a three-legged wooden stool.

They all look intact. That is the thing to understand about this place: it looks *maintained*. Nothing has fallen. Nothing has collapsed. The room has the posture of a facility awaiting a shift change.

Put your hand on the gate stand and it will smear.

Graphitic corrosion — the iron has left the cast iron, atom by atom, carried off in the current, and what remains is the graphite skeleton the iron was folded around. It holds every detail. The foundry marks. The raised lettering. The bevel on the handwheel spokes. It weighs almost nothing and it has the strength of wet chalk. The pumps are ghosts of pumps in the exact shape of pumps. The bolt heads are still hexagonal and you could shave one with a thumbnail.

This is what age means here. Not ruin — *completion*. Every reaction that could run has run. The zinc anodes were consumed to nothing by 1961. The paint is gone, all of it, down to the last flake in the last corner. The hemp gland packing rotted out, the oil went, the grease went, the grease cups are little cups of water. The leather belt on the wall clock is a smear of collagen. The wooden stool is perfect and is not there: cold waterlogged timber, holding its shape by surface tension and habit, a stool-shaped volume of water that will disperse when the first crayfish climbs it.

Only two materials have won. The glazed brick, which was always going to win. And a bearing in the bypass line, a sleeve of lignum vitae — that dense oily tropical wood they used for stern tubes and turbine shafts because it likes being wet. The steel shaft it carried is a soft orange rumour. The wooden bearing is sharp-edged and sound.

---

Light: three sources, and none of them generous.

The shaft you came down puts a grey column onto the floor, about four metres wide, moving. It is never the same brightness twice. Above, forty-one metres up, there is a surface, and the surface has weather.

The two intake galleries on the upstream wall admit smaller, browner shafts, angled, full of drifting particulate.

And there are the lamps. Three bulkhead fittings in sealed glass housings with brass cages, on a circuit that nobody has switched off since the valley filled. They are fed by a shunt dynamo the size of a beer keg, belt-coupled to a small runner in the scour bypass, and there is just enough residual magnetism left in the field poles for it to bootstrap itself every time the water moves. The water always moves. Three amber points, forty watts each, burning in a room where no one has ever needed them.

Around each lamp is a halo about the size of a dinner plate where colour still exists. Inside that halo, rust is red. Inside that halo, and nowhere else in the hall, there is *green*: a low turf of *Cladophora*, a felt of diatoms, three strands of water moss that arrived as spores and made a life of it, and one pale, stretching, hopeless filament of pondweed that has been trying to flower for six years. This is the entire photosynthetic economy of Valve Hall No. 2. Three plates of light. Everything else that lives here eats what falls, or what drifts in, or each other.

Snails work the halos in slow spirals. When a lamp flickers, they stop.

---

The water is 6.4°C in February and 8.9°C in September and it never, ever stops.

The scour gates were never fully closed. Something in the linkage failed during the filling, or was left, and there is a permanent bypass flow of about four hundred litres a second coming through the upstream trash rack, crossing the hall diagonally at a walking pace, and leaving through the draft tube in the downstream wall. A river passes through this room. It has been passing through for a hundred and twelve years.

That current is why everything is rounded. It has sanded the concrete to exposed aggregate, put a shine on the brick arrises, worn a shallow scalloped dish into the slab downstream of every obstruction. The flow is laminar almost everywhere; you can see it in the way the silt plumes stand up and lean, all together, like grass. In the lee of each pump there is a dead zone where fine sediment lies undisturbed and the water is a fraction of a degree warmer, and the animals know exactly where these are.

There is no air in the room and there hasn't been since roughly the third year. There was a pocket in the accumulator vessel, a big one, and it dissolved — pressure and time, Henry's law, nothing dramatic. The last breath of the old world went into solution and was carried out through the draft tube. Now the only gas is dissolved gas, and the only bubbles are the ones you brought.

---

It is loud. People expect drowned places to be silent and this one is a factory floor.

The inflow is a broadband roar, chest-deep, unvarying, the sound of a motorway heard through a wall. Under it, the fifty-hertz thrum of a turbine four kilometres away in the dam, transmitted through the concrete rather than the water, so that the floor hums and the air-free water above it does not. The bypass runner sings a thin pure note around 900 Hz that shifts when the flow shifts. Somewhere in a bleed orifice there is intermittent cavitation, a fine tearing shriek like a wet finger on glass, ten seconds on, a minute off, for a century.

The hoist chain touches the gantry rail once every eleven seconds. Nobody has ever counted this who was not stuck here.

A check valve in the gallery, its hinge worn oval, drops with a flat bronze *tock* whenever the pressure gradient reverses, which happens with the afternoon snowmelt pulse, and with storms, and sometimes for no reason at all.

And under all of it, the animals. Crayfish rasp their mandibles. Mussels crack — a distinct, dry, knuckle-like *tik* as a drum's pharyngeal teeth go through a shell, and there is a lot of that. And at dusk, which arrives here as a slow closing of the shaft-light rather than any change in colour, the freshwater drum begin to grunt: a low knocking chorus, six or seven fish spread out along the walls, each one drumming its swim bladder with a muscle built for exactly that, holding its distance from the others. It goes on for two hours. In the water it is not eerie. It sounds like a workshop two rooms away.

---

Life is everywhere here and it is never crowded.

That is the arrangement. The basin outside is mud — soft, anoxic below the top centimetre, useless to anything that needs to attach. This room is the only substrate for miles, so everything comes here, and then everything defends a piece of it. The result is abundance without density: a hundred thousand animals in a hall you can walk in a minute, and no two of them close enough to touch.

One eel per pipe. Four pipes, four eels, and the boundaries between them are older than the eels; when one dies the next arrives within a week and takes up the same beat. Crayfish spaced along the wall base like hermits in cells, each with a bolt-hole in a leached-out mortar joint, each maintaining a swept apron of clean brick around its door. Bryozoan colonies on the flanges, palm-sized, translucent antlers of jelly, filtering the current with a patience that reads as machinery. Freshwater sponges in the dead zones, grey and fingered, easy to mistake for something growing. Sculpins on the tiles, motionless, mottled to match a lichen pattern that is actually corrosion. Amphipods in the water column, thousands of them, scattered like sparks in a headlamp beam and never once clustering.

The caddis larvae build their cases out of the only gravel available: cement grit, brass swarf, glass from the burst gauge, garnet from a spilled abrasive tin. Small industrial tubes crawling over a hall of industrial ghosts.

Predation is constant and unhurried. The drum work the mussel beds down one flank while the crayfish take the fallen meat. The eels intercept the caddis in June, on the few nights a year when the larvae abandon their cases and swim up the surge shaft toward a brightness they will mostly not reach. The burbot under the gantry eats whatever the current brings and has not moved more than three metres since anyone thought to check.

And twice a year a boat passes overhead, and its engine comes down through forty-one metres of water as a soft rising bruise of sound, and every animal in the hall stops moving at once, and the room becomes — for about ninety seconds — the quiet place people imagine.

---

The floor is a ledger.

Thirty-four centimetres of silt over the slab, and it is laminated: pale couplets and dark, one pair per year. Pale in late spring, when the diatom bloom dies and sinks. Dark in October, when the reservoir turns over, the whole lake rolling top to bottom, and cold oxygenated water comes down the shaft carrying a year's worth of dead leaves and rot. That turnover is the room's only breath. It arrives as a temperature drop of half a degree, a silt blizzard, and a sudden brief glut of oxygen that the worms in the black anoxic layer respond to by rising two centimetres and then retreating.

A hundred and twelve couplets. You can count them with a corer and a light. The top four centimetres are smeared — crayfish and worms and burbot have churned the recent record into porridge. The old years are legible. The recent ones are being lived in.

Storms are recorded as thin grey seams. The drought summers are thinner than the wet ones. There is a dark band at eighty-one years that corresponds to a forest fire on the far shore.

---

On the downstream wall, above the draft tube, there is a bourdon pressure gauge in a brass case, its glass intact, its dial white enamel with black numerals. It is connected to the hall by a small-bore tapping and it measures the head of water above it.

It reads 4.1 bar.

It is correct. It has been correct the entire time. Everything else in this room has finished becoming what it will be — the iron has become graphite, the wood has become water, the paint and the packing and the anodes have become nothing at all, and the concrete has settled into the last shape it will hold for ten thousand years. There is no further change scheduled except deeper silt and, eventually, the slow slump of machines that are already only drawings of themselves.

The gauge is the one thing still doing its job. The lamps burn because the water moves. The water moves because the valley is higher than the river. The drum knock at dusk, the eels run their walls, the snails graze three plates of green, and the current leans the silt like grass, and the whole small loud cold room goes on being a factory that produces one thing, at a rate of about three millimetres a year, and has never missed a shift.
