# The Soda Works

## I. Before light

At four in the morning the flats are still warm. They have been warm all night. The ground gives back the day slowly, the way a stove does, and the air above it stands at thirty-one degrees with no wind in it at all. There is nothing here to make a wind-sound. No leaf, no stalk, no dry grass raking against itself. When air moves over this basin you hear only what it touches: a hiss on crust, a slap on brine, a wire.

The crust is sweating. That is the first thing anyone learns about it. Trona — sodium sesquicarbonate, the salt this whole valley is made of and made from — pulls water out of the air the way a wet finger pulls salt from a shaker in reverse. Overnight the humidity climbs to sixty, seventy percent, and the pans drink it. By the hour before dawn the surface has gone from bone-white to the grey of a licked spoon, and every footprint from yesterday's shift holds a centimetre of brine that came from nowhere, that fell as no rain. Walk out there and your boots print dark and the prints fill and the fills gleam. By nine the sun will have taken it all back and the crust will be white again and hard enough to ring under a heel.

Beneath the skin: black mud, anoxic, sulfurous, ten thousand years of it, and beneath that the bed. Forty metres of trona in places, laid down crystal by crystal since the last big lake shrank into this one. The bed is still growing. That is the fact the whole enterprise rests on. Cut a trench through the crust into the brine below, dredge out the crystal mush, and within fourteen months the trench has filled and hardened and can be cut again. The mine heals. It has been healing since before there was anyone to cut it.

## II. The basin

Low ground, six hundred metres above a sea that is a long way off in every direction, walled by escarpments the colour of old iron. The walls are basalt and ash and, in the north, a badland of diatomite — the compacted skeletons of algae that lived in a lake ten times this size, when the climate was wet and the water was sweet. That lake left terraces behind, three of them, step-lines around the basin like the rings inside a glass. The lowest is twelve metres above the present water. From the works you can see it running unbroken along the far wall for thirty kilometres, a shelf you could park a train on, and it means simply: *the water was here, for a long time, and then it was not.*

Nothing in this valley is new. The erosion is finished arguing. Inflow equals evaporation; salt in equals salt buried; the ephemeral river arrives twice a year, drops its sand in the same fan it has been building since the Pleistocene, and goes away. Handaxes weather out of the diatomite in the north and lie where they fall, sharp as the day they were struck, and nobody picks them up because there is nobody within forty kilometres to pick them up, and because there are a great many of them.

The last thorn tree is nine hundred metres up and two days' walk north. Below that line the alkalinity kills every root that tries. There is no grass on the shore, no reed, no tamarisk, no seed-blown annual gambling on a wet week. The basin is not dead. It is *unplanted*. What grows here grows without leaves: mats of cyanobacteria in the diluted lagoons, blue-green and slick and ankle-deep, and films of halophilic archaea in the strongest brine that turn whole pans the colour of watered blood. That is the entire primary production of a valley thirty kilometres long. Everything with a face is downstream of a bacterium.

## III. What the emptiness is full of

Per cubic kilometre of air, this place is close to nothing. A dust grain, a water molecule, a fly. You can stand on the causeway at midday and see thirty kilometres of white with one grey structure in it and no living thing, and the emptiness will be the truest fact about the world.

Then walk to the water's edge and put your hand in the wrack.

Brine flies. *Ephydra*, a black fly the size of a grain of rice, whose larvae carpet the shallow mud in a layer you could measure with a ruler — a hundred thousand per square metre, grazing the mats, breathing through a snorkel-tail in water at pH 10.5 that would dissolve your cornea. The adults emerge in numbers that stop being numbers. They mate on the wing, they die on the shore, and the wind rows their bodies along the strand in windrows a metre wide and knee-deep, grey-brown, drifting like ash from a fire nobody lit. Twenty kilometres of windrow. Lorries could be filled. Lorries *have* been filled, in the years when someone thought there might be a market in protein.

The living flies stand on the crust in a haze you walk through. They land on your eyelids to drink. They do not bite; there is nothing here worth biting for blood. They want the water in you, which is the only fresh water on the flats. When you speak they go into your mouth and you spit and keep walking. At the works' cooling intake they arrive against the screens in a felt that has to be hosed off every ninety minutes, and the loudest regular sound in the whole valley is that hose, and the second loudest is the man swearing at it.

Above the flies, at the far end of the lake where the springs dilute the brine to something the mats can live in: the flamingos. Four hundred thousand lesser flamingos in a good month, sometimes twice that, standing in a seam of grey-pink that resolves, as you get closer, into birds, and then into more birds than the eye will agree to count. They feed upside down, beaks inverted, pumping the mat through lamellae with their tongues, treading with webbed feet to stir it up. From three kilometres away the sound of them arrives as surf. It never breaks. It goes on being about to break for hours.

That is the loudest thing the animals do, and it is not loud. The rest is silence with a texture in it: the low hum of the windrows, which is not the flies flying but the flies *breathing*, if a fly can be said to breathe audibly at that density; the tick of the crust as it warms and its plates shove up into pressure ridges; a wet click when a bird shifts its foot.

## IV. The fish that live in a puddle

Along the northern shore the springs come up at sixty to eighty-six degrees, fresh-ish, sweet enough to drink if you let them cool, and they run out across the flats in channels a metre wide before the lake takes them. Where a channel meets the brine there is a mixing zone, a wedge of water at thirty-nine to forty degrees and pH 10, and in that wedge lives a tilapia.

It is a small fish, blue-black in the males, and it is the entire species. It exists in a chain of hot pools and outflow channels totalling perhaps two hectares, in water hotter than a bath and alkaline enough to strip the mucus off anything else with gills. It cannot go up: the spring source cooks it. It cannot go down: the lake kills it. It has solved the alkalinity by giving up on ammonia and excreting urea instead, like a mammal, like you — a fish that urinates the way a man does because the ordinary way is chemically forbidden to it.

There are tens of thousands of them in a pool you could throw a stone across. Males hold nests in the warm shallows and flare at each other all day. Herons work the rim, and kingfishers, and a marabou stork that stands at the outflow with its bill down and takes fish without appearing to move. The fish do not flee far. There is nowhere to flee to.

Everything alive here lives on a boundary between two lethals: too hot / too caustic, too salt / too fresh, wet enough / dry enough. The valley is enormous and the habitable part of it is a film a few centimetres thick, wrapped around the edges. That is how a place can be overwhelming with life and empty at the same time. The life is not distributed through the volume. It is pressed flat against the interfaces, like iron filings on a magnet's face.

## V. The works

The town sits on a rock peninsula where the causeway starts: nine hundred people in housing built for four thousand, in rows, with a hospital, a school, a club with a bar and a warped dartboard, a church, a canteen, and a swimming pool fed from a cooled spring, warm as blood, in which tilapia have established themselves and are not removed.

There is no wood in the town. There never was; nothing here has ever grown a trunk. Everything structural came in on the metre-gauge line and is steel, concrete, or salt. Paper goes soft in a week. Books are kept in tins. The shade in the market is corrugated iron on pipe frames, and the shade in the yards is canvas that has been white and is now the colour of the ground. Concrete spalls here — the brine gets into it, crystallises, and jacks it apart from inside — so the older buildings wear a crust like the pans do, and the newest ones are already beginning.

The works themselves are laid out across eleven square kilometres of flat, and they are dense in the way a diagram is dense. A dredge on Pan Nine, floating in a trench it cut itself, chewing crystal mush off the bottom and spitting it up a ladder into a slurry line. The slurry line running four kilometres on trestles to the plant. The plant: rotary driers, calciners, a bagging floor, a stack. Behind the plant, the tailings pans, then the older tailings pans, then the abandoned pans of the 1950s, whose pipework is being cemented into the crust at a millimetre a year and will eventually be a stratum. There are three generations of conveyor gallery crossing each other overhead. There are pipes stacked four deep on racks that were themselves built twice. It is a city that has been stretched out flat and had most of its people taken away.

Eleven on nights. Two on the drier, one on the dredge, one on the bagging floor, one on the locomotive, one on the screens, the rest walking the galleries with lamps. The dredge runs, and its sound reaches the town six kilometres off as a slow thud with no attack to it, more felt than heard, and after ten minutes you stop hearing it and after ten years you cannot sleep without it.

Fresh water is piped eight kilometres from a spring and is metered. Everything else in this valley is wet and none of it is drinkable. On the roofs there are dew-catchers — corrugated sheets tilted into gutters — because for two hours before dawn the air itself will give up water to anything cold enough to ask.

## VI. Rules

Nothing sinks. The brine runs at 1.2 and you ride in it like a cork, high, absurdly high, unable to swim properly because your legs will not go down. It is soapy on the skin: it saponifies the oil on you, so that after an hour in it your hands feel scrubbed and slightly gone.

Nothing rots. Alkalinity and salt together do the work of a tannery. A bird that dies on the shore stays a bird — feathers, eye, folded foot — for a year, two years, five, drying and stiffening and then slowly taking on mineral, until there are calcified flamingos standing in the wrack that were alive when the drier was commissioned. There is no smell of decay anywhere in the basin. There is ammonia at the springs and sulfide in the mud when you break the crust, and otherwise the air smells of nothing but hot salt and dust.

There is no fire, because there is no fuel. There is no rustle, because there are no leaves. There is no shade that was not built.

And the salt is a crop. Cut it, and it comes back. The Company's engineers understood this in 1911 and wrote it down in a report and the report is still in a cabinet in the club, and it remains the only genuinely hopeful fact in the valley.

## VII. Weather, and the year

The light is dim most of the time, and not because of clouds. Dust comes off the pans and off the ash slopes and stands in the basin under the inversion, and evaporative haze builds above the brine, and the two together make a brown ceiling that the sun comes through as a coin. Noon reads as a badly lit room. You squint at the ground and not at the sky, because the crust throws back everything the haze lets through, and the horizon dissolves into a white-brown seam with no line in it at all. Colour survives only where the biology is: the red pans, the green lagoons, the fish. The flamingos, in this light, are the grey of dishwater until they open their wings.

Twice a year the rain falls, but it falls up on the highlands, and it arrives here two days later as a brown sheet a foot deep across the flats, carrying sand and goat bones and once a shipping container. The lake goes milky. The brine drops from saturation to something merely hostile. The mats bloom, the fly population crashes and then explodes, the flamingos read the change and leave in a single morning for a lake three hundred kilometres away — the whole seam gone, the shore suddenly ordinary — and the dredge shuts down because the chemistry has moved out from under it.

Then it dries. Over six weeks the salinity climbs, the crust knits, the windrows re-form, the birds come back in ones and tens and then in their hundred thousands, and Pan Nine reopens. This has happened every year of the town's existence and every year for ten thousand years before it. The system is not fragile. It is *established*. It has one state and it returns to it, and the machines are simply another thing that has learned to live on the interface, no stranger than the tilapia.

## VIII. Night

At dusk the bats come out of the conveyor gallery, where they hang in the underside of the corrugating by the thousand, and they work the fly-haze over the pans in a low erratic scribble. Geckos run the ceiling of the bagging office. Spiders have strung the handrails of every stair in the plant — there being no branches, they have used what was offered — and each web is a small grey flag of insect. A hyena comes down the causeway around two, unhurried, on the way to the nesting ground at the south end, where it will take eggs and be shouted at by ten thousand birds and take eggs anyway.

The sodium lamps come on along the galleries and they are the same colour as the dust, so the whole works seems lit by its own atmosphere. The flamingos, asleep in the shallows, are grey lumps. The drier turns. The screens are hosed. The locomotive stands with its cab light on and nobody in it.

Out on Pan Nine the trench the dredge cut this morning is already cloudy with new crystal, falling slowly through the brine like a snow that goes downward for a living, and the crust at its lip is ticking as it cools and grows. By the time the day shift walks out at six, the cut will have a skin on it. You will be able to stand on the place where the machine was, and the ground will be warm, and it will sweat under your boots, and there will be nothing to hear.
