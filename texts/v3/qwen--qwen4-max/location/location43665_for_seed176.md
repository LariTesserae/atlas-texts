## The Kettle

From above it is nothing. From above it is a chip in a ridge at five thousand one hundred metres, a place where the flank of the mountain came away thirty-one years ago and left a wound the shape of a soup bowl: three hundred paces long, a hundred and eighty across, ninety deep at the headwall. The rock around it is all fracture face. It has not had time to soften. The arêtes are still knife-edged, the scree still angular, the shards still bright where they were broken — you can cut your palm on this mountain simply by leaning on it. Nothing here has been rounded yet. Nothing here has been anywhere long enough to be worn.

Then you come to the notch, and warm air comes up through it and hits your face, and it smells like a greenhouse. Wet iron. Chlorophyll. Hot copper. Somewhere below, water is moving in quantity, and the sound of it arrives before the sight does, a broad white roar with a pulse inside it.

Two steps past the notch the world changes its mind.

---

The bowl is warm. Not tropical — warm the way a kitchen is warm after bread, twenty-two, twenty-four degrees, blood-temperature at the low end where the pools are. The heat comes from a dike of hot rock that the slide exposed, a black seam in the headwall that breathes. Steam seeps out of it in threads and is immediately taken by the wind and made into cloud, so that the head of the bowl always wears a small white banner. Above the rim it is minus eleven and the air is dry as paper. Below the rim it is June. The boundary is not gradual. You can stand in the notch with one hand in each season, and the moss stops in a line as clean as a tide mark, green on one side of a stone, nothing on the other, and the nothing goes on for four hundred kilometres.

It rains here constantly and almost never from the sky.

The rain comes from the grid. Copper and glass and ceramic pipe runs on stone posts, three tiers of them, the lowest at shoulder height, the highest at nine metres, perforated on a spacing of eleven centimetres, fed by the condensers up on the rim — long finned intakes that comb moisture out of the thin dry air, hoarfrost building on their blades and being scraped off by slow reciprocating arms, the meltwater running down through lagged pipes into the bowl. Ten thousand drops a second. That is not a sound of dripping. At that density dripping stops being discrete and becomes a hiss, and the hiss off the stone floor and the fern fronds and the standing water becomes a roar, and the roar is the floor of every other sound in the place. Visitors shout for the first hour and then stop bothering.

The grid has been running long enough that the moss has sleeved it. Every pipe wears a green fur three fingers thick; the posts are furred; the junction boxes are furred lumps with a brass lid showing like an eye. From below, the ceiling of the bowl is a green lattice, a canopy, an orchard espaliered on a diagram. Rain falls out of leaves that are growing on the pipes that make the rain. This is the first thing about the Kettle that is not quite right, and it is not wrong exactly, only recursive in a way that natural places are not.

The second thing is that the moss grows in courses. Straight runs, right-angle turns, following the pipe grid under it like brickwork following a wall. There is a species here — one of nineteen on the manifest — that has stopped bothering with the rock and specialises in the drip-line, and it has taken the shape of the machinery so thoroughly that from the headwall stair you can read the whole plumbing schematic in living green, including the branches that were capped and abandoned in the second decade, which are still marked in a paler, hungrier growth where the seep persists. The only native of this place is a pattern.

---

Below the grid, the floor is terraced. Not by hand: by deposition. The water that comes out of the hot seam is loaded with dissolved lime, and where it tumbles and degasses it lays the lime down, and it has been laying it down for thirty-one years, rim after rim, dam after dam, each lip a few millimetres higher than the last, until the bowl's floor is a staircase of eighty-odd travertine pools, cream and ochre and rust at the edges, and in the basins that opaque turquoise that only glacial flour and lime can make. Warm water. Steaming at the top of the flight, cool and clear at the bottom. The rims are sharp as pie crust and about as strong; put a boot on one and it breaks with a sound like a plate.

Between and over the dams: the green. Moss first, then the sedge that was carried up in crates, then ferns — the ferns have gone insane, they are two metres tall and grow in the crotches of the terraces where the warm spray sits, and they lean over the pools until the fronds touch the water and are themselves slowly plated, so that in the lower basins there are fern-shaped stones, perfect fossils of plants that are still alive at the tip. Sphagnum in the shaded corners. A rhubarb-like thing with red stems that nobody planted and nobody can account for. Liverworts on every vertical face. Something with small white flowers that opens only during slack. Density is the fact of the place: frond over frond over pipe over root-net over gabion wire, all of it near-touching, all of it wet, all of it dripping into something else. Eleven centimetres of soil under the whole of it, and the soil is younger than the moss, and under the soil is mineral flour and shard, because the mountain has not made any soil yet and the works is making it as fast as it can.

The soil mill sits at the low end, a rotating drum four metres long, fed by a screw conveyor from the crusher, and it grinds rock flour with spent moss and lime sludge and turns out a black crumb at a rate of about a barrow a day. It has been running for three decades. It is not fast. It is not supposed to be fast.

---

The built things are few and they are all working. An engine house of stone and corrugated iron, roof patched with a flattened drum. A bunkroom with four bunks, one made up, a mug on the sill with a ring of lime in it. A stair of two hundred and twelve steps cut into the headwall with a handrail of scaffold pipe, worn bright. A fallen gantry half-swallowed by moss, its lattice making a trellis for the ferns, so useful now that nobody has cleared it. Survey pegs with brass caps. Stencilled numbers on the pipe runs, a hazard stripe worn to a ghost, a slate tally board by the intake with chalk columns going back years. Gloves mended with copper wire. A chair by the turbine worn to the shape of someone.

Nobody is here today. Somebody is due.

Power comes down a penstock from the hanging glacier at the headwall — a small dirty ice tongue, the last of the ice that carved this bowl and abandoned it — through a pelton wheel that whines at a pitch you feel in your teeth, and everything else runs off that: the condensers, the arms, the mill, the lamps. The lamps come on by photocell when the fog thickens, plain white lamps, and they extend the growing day at both ends, because the sun clears the east rim at twenty to ten and drops behind the west at twenty past three and the bowl needs more than that. The light here is completely ordinary. That is worth saying. At this altitude the daylight is fierce and the shadows are hard and blue, and the fog is lit from inside like a paper lantern, and none of it is strange. The strangeness of the Kettle is not in its light.

---

Listen long enough and the roar separates.

There is the drip-hiss, the floor of it. There is the turbine's whine, high and steady. There is the compressor's stroke, one long intake every eleven seconds, a sound like a huge animal deciding to breathe. There is water-hammer in the penstock, a single hard knock, once a minute, exactly as if someone were hitting the pipe with a wrench — and the first time you hear it you look for the person. There is the flywheel, eleven revolutions a minute, so slow you can watch the spokes, and its bearing has a note. And every forty minutes the pressure vent sighs, a long descending exhalation that rolls around the walls and comes back changed.

What you do not hear is any animal.

There is no frog. There should be frogs; there is warm standing water and eighty pools and thirty years, and there are no frogs, because nobody carried frogs up here. There are mites in the moss, uncountable. There are worms in the made soil, from a tin. There is a pale flightless cricket in the warm sump under the mill, and if you sit still for an hour you may see one. There is a moth at the lamp, one moth, the same species always. And at dusk a small dark swift comes over the rim from God knows where, drinks on the wing off the top pool, and goes, and that is the entire vertebrate life of the Kettle. A lush wet warm green place with no voice in it. That is the third thing that is not quite right, and it is the one that gets into people. The noise is enormous and none of it is alive.

---

Once every thirty-four hours the intake closes to let the fins defrost, and the grid drains, and for eleven minutes the bowl goes quiet. This is slack. The fog settles into the pools. The white flowers open. You hear wind on the rim, a long way up, and the glacier ticking, and your own blood, and the small crystalline sound of the travertine doing what it does.

Because the water is turning everything to stone. That is the real work here, and the machines are only accelerating it. The flywheel rim has a rind of lime four millimetres thick and grows a millimetre a year; the tender chips it off on the long cycle, and the chipping is on the tally board too, in a different column. The lower pipe runs are half mineral. The valves at the bottom flight have not turned in nine years and are now simply features of the reef, and the water goes around them. Given time — the brass plate by the engine house gives the estimate, and the estimate is four hundred and some years — the whole bowl will be a solid cream-coloured mass with a green skin and pipes in it like veins in a leaf, and then the glacier will be gone, and the head will fail, and the water will stop, and the cold will come down over the rim in a single afternoon and take the moss in a night.

Everything here knows how to wait for that. The moss puts on a thumb's width a year. The terrace lips rise four millimetres. The soil deepens by the thickness of a coin in a decade. The compressor breathes, and the drip-roar goes on, and the lime lays itself down grain by grain in the warm turquoise water, and on a peak thirty kilometres away a red lamp blinks slowly at night, which means there is another bowl, and another works, and that this small loud green room in the cold is one unit of something very large and very patient that is building a world at the speed of stone.
