## The Cut

The plain gives no warning. Short grass, flint scattered like knucklebones, a wind that has come a long way without meeting anything, larks going up out of the grass in front of your feet. Cool, and dry in the way that high country is dry — the air takes moisture off your lips and gives nothing back. Then the ground stops.

Not a valley. Valleys have slopes and slopes have a history. This is a hole with a rim, a league across, cut down into the pale rock in eleven broad steps, and the steps are white, and the bottom of it is white, and standing on the edge in ordinary daylight you have to narrow your eyes against a town lying in a bowl of its own glare.

They call it the Cut. The town inside it has no other name. People from the rim villages say *going down*, and mean it the way people elsewhere mean *going home*.

---

The stone is the reason for all of it. In the bed, under weight, still holding its quarry water, it is soft — not clay-soft, but cheese-rind soft, a dense pale mass you can saw with a copper blade and wet sand, two people pulling in rhythm, the slurry ticking against the kerf. Lift a block out and it begins to change. The sap leaves it. Over three weeks the face stiffens; over a season it case-hardens to a crust that turns a flint scraper; over a year it will ring, faintly, when you flick it. The locals call the fresh state *green* and the finished state *set*, and they say a house is not built, it is *ripened*.

This single fact organizes everything below the rim.

You do not carry stone to a building site. You cut the building site out of the stone, and the hole you make is the cellar, and the block you lift is the wall, and it is set green two arm-spans from where it lay, and it cures standing where it will stand forever. Every vault in the Cut sits in the socket of its own material. The town is made of the absence it has created. It rises by sinking.

Timber is scarce up on the plain and ruinously expensive to bring down the ramps, so almost nothing here spans. The architecture is cellular: corbelled courses stepping inward, beehive cells, small round doorways, no windows to speak of, walls thick enough to be furniture. From the rim the districts look like the underside of a wasp nest, or like coral, or like a mouth full of worn teeth — except nothing here is worn.

The streets are the roofs.

That is the first thing you learn walking down the switchback ramp with your hand on the wall: the ground level is packed solid, cell against cell, no gaps wider than a shoulder, and all the movement of the town happens across the tops of the vaults. Roof to roof to roof, a continuous undulating pavement of set stone, limewashed, with the round doorways opening into it like wells. People cross the whole quarter without touching the floor. They dry fleece up there, and lentils, and lime putty in shallow trays; they sleep up there in summer with their feet toward the cold; they set their looms up there in the good light and work with their backs to a wall that is still curing beneath them, warm as a loaf.

---

It is quiet. Twelve thousand people, perhaps more in the cutting season, and you can hear a drip four courses away.

Partly this is the stone. Green stone does not ring; it thuds, it absorbs. There is no hammering below the second course — it is forbidden, and unnecessary, because you are not breaking anything, you are sawing it. Copper and sand, *shush, shush*, a sound like someone patiently sweeping. Wooden wedges driven and then wetted, swelling. Levers. Rope. Rollers of set stone under sledges, greased with clay. No carts, no iron tyres, no ring of shoe on rock, because everyone wears felt.

Partly it is the shape of the place. A shout from the floor comes back off the north wall, and then the south, and then off the kiln bench, folded and doubled and returned fourfold, so that one man losing his temper sounds like a riot in a canyon. Every child in the Cut learns this before they learn to count. They learn the low voice, the hand on the wrist instead of the call across the yard. Signals go by flag from the rim and by tugged cord between quarters. When someone does shout, the whole pit flinches and then repeats it, and the shouter is ashamed for a week.

And partly it is the felt. Rope-soled, fleece-shod, twelve thousand people padding. Which is why, after three generations, the stairs still have their chisel ridges. Run your thumb along a step on the oldest ramp and you can feel each individual stroke of the tool that made it, crisp as the day it was cut. Nothing here has been rounded. Nothing has been polished by use. The doorways are still square. The corners will cut you; children's knees are a map of it, and everyone's palms are wrapped, and the town's most common injury is simply *touching the town*.

---

Water has not learned where to go yet.

There is no stream in the Cut, no gully, no rill, no fan of silt, no stain of iron down a face. Around the rim they cut a low berm — the Parting — so that the plain's runoff goes around the hole instead of into it, and this is why, after eighty years of rain, the walls look quarried yesterday. Rain falls straight in and lies where it falls, in flat ankle-deep pans on the level floors, and the town bails. It is a civic act, like a harvest. Everyone comes out with wooden scoops and lifted troughs and the water goes hand to hand up the courses to the cisterns, which are old sockets, the emptied rooms of former decades.

Most of the drinking water does not fall at all. It arrives out of the air. Clear cool nights, a bowl of dense cold air, pale stone that radiates its heat straight up to the sky: by the last hour before dawn every roof in the Cut is running with condensation. The dew crews go out in the dark with fleeces and mop the roofs and wring them into jars, quarter by quarter, working ahead of the sun. It takes an hour. It is the loudest the town ever gets, and it is still only a sound like distant rain in leaves.

On the north wall there is a seep — the Weep — where the cut has sliced through a bedding plane and groundwater comes out at the rate of a slow pulse. Moss there, real moss, the only deep green in a day's walk; a hair of algae in the runnel; a stone basin worn — no, *cut*, cut to look worn, because nothing here wears — beneath it. The Weep is where you go to hear water fall.

And on the floor, in autumn, the Fog Pool: cold air draining down off the plain, sliding over the rim, settling in the deepest quarter until the whole bottom course is full of white to the height of a man's chest, and the people up on the roofs look down at their own cellars drowning in cloud. Cold sinks. So height is money. Above the winter sun-line the vaults are dry, warm at noon, expensive; below it they are damp, blue, cheap, and full of the people who do the cutting. There is a red ochre line painted along the walls at the level the sun reaches on the shortest day, and there are other lines above it, hour lines, and the whole pit is a clock face that tells time by which district is currently in the light.

---

Life has only just found the place. Eighty years is nothing.

The lichen on the oldest face is the size of a thumbnail and is treated as a landmark. In the seep-moss there are springtails, and above the pans there are midges, and above the midges, at dusk, the swifts — forty pairs, nesting in the drill holes of a bench that was abandoned when they hit the Bone Course, a bed of black flint nodules the copper cannot cut. The flint knuckles are still there, protruding through cellar walls all along that level, worked around, levered out where they could be, knapped into scrapers and querns and the blades that do everything copper cannot. The swifts scream at dusk and it is the only loud thing in the Cut and nobody has ever complained about it.

A kestrel sits on the flint. Three goats and a dog on the rim, mice in the granaries, geckos on the warm south benches, a toad in the Fog Pool that everyone agrees came down in a wool bale. On the plain, sparse grass and hares. In the crevices of the upper walls, four fig seedlings and a caper, protected, watched, argued over. There is no soil anywhere below the rim. There has not been time to make any.

The year has a hard shape. Cut in spring, saw and lever and set through the long light months, and everything green must be ripened before the first frost, because frost in green stone destroys it — it chalks, it slumps, it comes apart in the spring like wet bread. So autumn is wrapping: fleece and straw and dung heaps banked against the new work, the ammoniac warmth of them steaming in the cold, the whole town swaddling its own bones. Winter is lime and rope and plaster and repair, the kilns smoking on the leeward rim, quicklime slaking in the pits with a hiss you can hear from the second course, putty laid down in jars to age for a decade and left to grandchildren.

And under all of it, at night, the ticking. Setting stone contracts as it dries and gives off small sharp pings from inside the walls, thousands of them, all over the pit, irregular, like a cooling oven the size of a valley. People fall asleep to it. It is the sound of the town becoming permanent.

---

Go down to the deepest quarter and you go down through your own family. The floor drops about a man's height each generation; the roofs stay near where they were. So the oldest vaults, the first ones, the ones the founders ripened, are now cellars and cisterns three courses under the living town, their round doorways at knee height in a wall of stored water. The dead are up on the rim, in the thin plain soil, because there is nowhere below to put them. To visit your grandmother you climb.

At the eastern end, behind a screen of stacked spoil, eleven years of hand-driving has produced a tunnel a man can crawl through, aimed at the valley on the other side of the ridge. The Adit. It is nearly through. When it breaks, the pans will drain, and the water will run, and running water carries grit, and grit wears.

There will be silt. There will be a channel, and the channel will cut, and the corners will soften, and the chisel ridges on the oldest stair will finally go smooth. Some of the older cutters are uneasy about it in a way they cannot articulate, and one woman on the fourth course has a jar set aside, empty, labelled in ochre, for the first mud the Cut ever makes.
