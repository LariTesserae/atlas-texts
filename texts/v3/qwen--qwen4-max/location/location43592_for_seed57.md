# The Sump

From above it is nothing. A gap in the ferns the width of a dinner plate, rimmed in liverwort, breathing cold. You would step over it. Deer step over it. The forest here is cool and temperate and unremarkable — beech litter, moss on the north sides of things, a hillside that slumps toward a stream you can hear but not see — and the hole is only one of ten thousand small failures in a limestone country that is always quietly failing.

But this one failed recently. That is the first thing worth knowing. The lip is raw. The fracture faces are white and unweathered, edges still sharp enough to open a palm, and the chips of the collapse lie where they fell on the ledge below, unrounded, undusted, angular as broken crockery. There is no soil in the shaft. There is no silt in the water. A hundred years, maybe two, and the forest has not yet had time to blur a single arris. Everything down there is new and has been new for a very long time.

The water begins eleven feet down and does not stop.

---

It is cold — seven degrees, eight in the deep part, the temperature of snowmelt that has been walking through rock since before the beeches were seeded. Cold enough that the body's opinion of it arrives late, as a kind of insult. Cold enough that everything biological here has agreed to slow down.

The chamber the shaft opens into is small. This must be insisted upon, because it will stop being true in a moment. It is a room. A drowned room roughly the size of a farmhouse kitchen, with a low ceiling of unbroken bedrock and a floor of fresh rubble, and one wall where the collapse sheared away and left a face as flat and pale as a cut cake. If you had a lamp — there are no lamps, there is nothing here that makes light, there has never been light below the waterline — you would see all four walls at once. You could touch three of them from the center.

And it is packed. Not furnished, not filled: *packed*, the way a fist is packed with fingers. Green water-weed in ropes and hanks, hair-grass in fine dense skeins, moss with leaves like small folded hands, bladderwort with its thousand tiny mouths, and through all of it the roots — beech roots and one enormous elm root that came through the ceiling in the collapse and kept growing, downward, blind, splitting and re-splitting until the room became a lace of wood. There is no open water. There is water *between*. You do not swim here; you part, slowly, and the parting closes behind you at the pace of a minute hand. A hand's-width of clear space is an event. It lasts about as long as a held breath and then the green leans back in.

And yet the room does not end. You can move through it for an hour and not arrive. The walls are always three feet away and always the same walls, and the elm root is always just above your shoulder, and the flat pale face of the shear is always to the left, and none of this is a loop, because a loop would let you count. Counting is the thing that fails. You can pass the same distinctive chip of rubble nine times or once, and the nine and the one are the same number here. The room is not large. It is only unfinishable.

---

The dark is total and it is not an absence. It has grain. It has a slight resistance, like the resistance of the weeds, and after a while it becomes difficult to say whether you are seeing nothing or seeing *something that is nothing-colored*. Sight down here reorganizes itself into a kind of memory: you know where the wall is because you have already touched it, and the knowing arrives in the eye as a shape, grey on grey, and it is accurate, and it is not vision. Divers who have been down and come back up (there are three; there is no record of who they were) all reported the same thing, which is that they saw the room clearly and could not afterward say what color anything was, including their own hands.

The plants have no reason to be green. They are green anyway. Chlorophyll in absolute darkness is a habit the place has not broken — a memory of sun held in tissue that has never met it. They grow toward the shaft-mouth, which is eleven feet up and admits no photons, and they grow toward it *correctly*, all of them, every tip angled at that plate-sized absence, as if the hole were a lamp. Some of the moss has begun to grow toward the cut stone instead. Nobody knows why. It is the only disagreement in the whole room.

---

Sound is nearly nothing, and the nothing is very specific.

There is a tick, perhaps once a season, when a chip of the fresh fracture finally lets go and settles a quarter-inch into the rubble. In water at seven degrees, in that density of weed, the sound arrives soft and close, like a fingernail on the far side of a door.

There is the elm root. It creaks. Not continuously — it grows a fraction of a millimeter a month and the creak is the sound of that fraction, a low wooden complaint that lasts about four seconds and happens roughly twice a year. It is the loudest thing in the place.

And there is a note. Very occasionally, on a scale of decades, the whole shaft sounds like a struck bowl: a single low tone, felt in the sternum rather than heard, decaying over an afternoon. It is the water column adjusting. The level in the sump rises about a fingernail's width each year — the hill is still draining into it, patiently, and the room is still filling. When it fills entirely, the note will stop. That is the only forecast this place makes.

Otherwise: your own body, obscene. Blood, jaw, the wet click of an eyelid. Visitors describe the silence as *attentive*.

---

Life is scarce and it is strange that it is scarce, given all that green.

There is a snail. Not a species of snail — a snail, thumb-sized, pale, on the underside of the lintel, and it has been on the underside of the lintel for as long as anyone has been going down. There are isopods, blind and white, and they are always the same three, in the same three crevices. There is something long that moves through the weed at the speed of the weed itself, and it may be an eel, and it may be a root that has learned to leave.

Nothing here breeds. The population does not fluctuate. It is as if the room were issued its animals once, at the moment of collapse, and the allotment has simply not been used up. They are cold and slow and they do not seem to eat, and the bladderwort's thousand mouths hang open in water that never brings them anything, and this is not starvation; it is a different arrangement. Everything down here is running on the initial conditions.

---

The marks are the part that will keep you awake.

There is a stair. It comes down the shaft's east side, cut into the living rock, nine steps, each one shallow and slightly wrong — the risers vary by an inch in a way no mason would tolerate — and then it stops in open water four feet above the floor. It does not resume anywhere. It was not broken off by the collapse; the collapse cut *across* it, cleanly, and the top three steps are sheared in half and their cut faces are as fresh as everything else.

There is a doorframe lying flat on the rubble, dressed stone, one lintel and two jambs, assembled, lying on its back like a fallen card. Through it you can see more weed. The lintel has the snail.

There is a basin, palm-sized, ground into the flat shear-face at what would be chest height for someone standing on nothing.

And there are tally marks. Groups of five, cut with something narrow and hard, in the soft white fracture-face where the rock is newest. They are sharp. There is stone-dust still sitting in the grooves, and stone-dust does not sit in grooves underwater for a century.

New ones appear. About one every eleven years.

No tool has ever been found here. No bone, no sherd, no charcoal, no hand. Whatever cuts the marks does not come down the shaft, because the shaft is the only way in and the shaft is watched by everything in the room, all those blind green tips angled up at it like faces at a window.

---

What holds here, as far as it can be told:

Water has no surface below the waterline. Not the shaft's surface — that one exists, eleven feet up, a cold ceiling you can break with a finger. But nothing *inside* has a surface: no bubbles, no meniscus, no boundary between the water in a weed's stem and the water outside it. The room is one continuous liquid, and the plants are a thickening of it, and you are, briefly, another thickening.

Nothing mixes. The cold at the floor and the slightly-less-cold at the ceiling have been stratified since the collapse and have never traded a molecule. There is no current. There is no decay. The beech leaves that came down with the water are still identifiable, still yellow, still whole, held in the weed like flies in aspic, and they will be yellow when the last one of us is a name.

Duplication is permanent. Anything that comes apart down here stays apart. A dropped thing does not sink; it hangs at the depth it was dropped at, and the weed grows through it, and it becomes a feature, and there are several such features whose origins are legible if you have the stomach.

And the room is an inside. Not a cave that resembles a room — a room, with the proportions of a room, that the hill happened to break open. The collapse did not make it. The collapse *found* it. That is what the fresh white fracture means: the hill was solid, and then a plate of it fell away, and behind the plate was this, already furnished, already green, already cold, already counted.

---

It is filling. A fingernail a year. In some thousands of years the water will reach the lip and the ferns will close over the plate-sized hole and the shaft will seal, and the elm root will keep splitting until there is no water left between anything, and the whole pocket will go slowly to stone with the green still in it — a small hard nodule in the hillside, kitchen-sized, packed solid, holding nine steps and a doorframe and a snail and a number of marks that will by then be complete.

Someone will crack it open with a hammer, eventually, on a cool afternoon, and find the room.

It will still be new.
