Put your hand on the rail and it will cut you.

That is the first thing to learn here. The steel is seventy years drowned and has not yet been softened by anything; every arris is still the arris the mill left, every sheared bolt-end still a little blade. Gloves are not for the cold. The cold is nine degrees, a steady ache that gets into the wrists and the back of the skull, but the cold is honest. The edges are not. They are the edges of a thing that was made and then wetted and has not had time to become anything else.

The water is clear enough to be frightening. You hang at Frame 41,200 with your light off and you can see the next column a kilometre away, a grey vertical fading into blue, and beyond it the ghost of the one after that, and beyond that the blue itself, which is not a distance but a limit of the eye. There is nothing between you and those columns. No fish, no drifting weed, no motes. A cubic kilometre of this world contains one girder, four lamps, a hundred fish, and a great deal of water that has never touched anything alive.

They call it the Pond. The stencil is still legible on the bulkhead behind you, white paint on red lead, the letters crisp as the day they were sprayed: **COOLING POND No. 1 — RESTRICTED**. It is the size of a sea.

---

Above, at a height that takes an hour to swim and a day to climb, is the Lid: deck plating, ribbed and riveted, hung with the lamp gantries. Some of the plating is glazing — wired glass in steel frames, filthy, salted, streaked with seventy years of gull lime — and through it comes real daylight, in shafts that lean and swing across the water as the sun outside goes over. But mostly the light is made. Mercury banks over the old raceways, blue-white and hard; sodium over the walkways, a low orange that turns everything red into black. Whole provinces have their own colour. In the sodium bays the fish have stopped bothering with red, and the crabs are the colour of wet cardboard, and the algae is a black fuzz rather than a green one.

The lamps run on a drum timer, twenty-four hours, and they do not all strike at once. Dawn here is a sequence: a bank two hundred metres off stutters pink, catches, steadies; then the next; then the next; a wave of illumination crossing the water at walking pace, and the fish rising ahead of it like a tide of their own. It takes eleven minutes for morning to arrive at the far wall. You can swim out of the day and into the night if you are quick and going the right way, and the eels do it constantly.

And in the headspaces — the great air bells trapped under the Lid where the water has not yet climbed — it rains. Genuinely rains. The plumes evaporate off the warm surfaces, the vapour rises, the cold underside of the deck sweats it back out, and the droplets grow and let go. There is fog up there in banks a hundred metres thick, lit from beneath, and there are clouds, and there is a rain that falls in grey curtains and hits the water with a roar you can hear from four bays away. Thunder in a headspace arrives twice: once through the water, a slap in the ribs, and then, slower, through the air.

---

There is no ground.

This is the thing that takes longest to accept. Below the grating is another deck, and below that another, and the sumps go down through level after level of made floor, and every core anyone has ever taken has bottomed out on plate. There is no rock. There is no clay. There is no sand that was not, at some point, part of a machine or part of an animal.

What passes for soil is a thin, precious, entirely recent invention: shell hash, fish bone, moulted carapace, gull lime, and rust. The rust is the bulk of it. It lies in orange drifts in the slack corners and on the lee side of every stiffener, and the grains are angular, unrounded, freshly spalled — you can drag a magnet through a drift and lift it out furred like a cat's back. This is the world's only sediment and it is younger than the pumps. It has not had time to become a bed. It moves every time the tide turns.

Where it does settle and stay, in the deep sumps, it does so under brine. The Pond was charged with seawater because seawater was what there was, and evaporation and makeup have been sorting it ever since, and the heavy fraction has been walking downhill for seventy years. In the bottom of Sump Nine there is a lake with a shoreline: hypersaline, dense as syrup, its surface a shimmering halocline you can put your hand through and feel the burn of salt on a cut. There is a beach of salt crust. There are fish lying on it, pickled, perfect, ten years old, arranged in windrows by currents nobody has ever measured. Nothing rots down there. It is the closest thing this world has to a fossil record and it is a heap of accidents.

---

The machines are still running. Not all of them, and not well, but the loop is closed and the loop is on.

Somewhere below the deepest deck a plant is still doing whatever it does, on a duty cycle nobody has adjusted since the makers stopped coming, and it returns its heat to the Pond at thirty-one degrees through diffusers the size of cathedral doors. That warmth is the whole engine of the place. It rises in columns that fold and mushroom against the Lid, spreads, cools, sinks along the outer walls, and comes back down the length of the world to be warmed again. The Pond has a gyre. It has internal waves a hundred metres tall that break on nothing and take nine hours to pass. It has weather.

The pumps run nineteen hours. Not twenty-four: nineteen, a number chosen for reasons of load scheduling by someone who has been dead a long time. So the tide here beats against the lamp-day, sliding an hour or five each cycle, and the two rhythms only come back into phase every nineteen days.

They call that the Coincidence. High water at full noon, the gantries drowned in warm bright shallows, and everything in the world that can spawn does. The bass come up out of the blue desert in ranks, silver and stupid with it, and pack the flooded catwalks shoulder to shoulder, and the water goes milky for a kilometre, and the gulls come down screaming and take until they cannot lift, and the big things come up the warm axis from four bays off and eat until the shallows are quiet again. The fry hang in the warm skin under the lamps for eleven days. Most are eaten. Enough are not.

Between Coincidences, the pumps knock. Water hammer, once an hour, a boom that goes through the whole structure and comes back changed. Float valves chatter. Check valves ring like a struck bell, one clear note, and then the same note from the next bay, and the next, a slow conversation across ten kilometres. The electrical fundamental is a fifty-hertz hum you feel in the teeth, and under it the pump fundamental at twelve, which you feel lower down. Cavitation hisses at the impeller throats like a kettle that never boils.

And over all of it, the shrimp. Beds of them, hectares of snapping shrimp on the fouled grating, a crackle exactly like fat in a pan, loud enough to drown a voice at three metres. At lamp-dusk the drum fish start, a hundred thousand of them thudding in unison, and the sound is a physical pressure, and the water carries it fifty kilometres before the light of the lamps that woke them gets anywhere at all.

There is one kind of place where none of this is audible. A stalled bay: loop dead, aerators dead, oxygen gone. It is silent, and it is empty, and the silence is the loudest warning in the world. Anything with a spine leaves within a day. What stays is a white mat and a smell like a struck match.

---

Life here is a film. It is not distributed through the volume; the volume is a desert and always has been. Everything alive is pressed against a surface, or against a gradient, or against the underside of the Lid.

The surfaces are furred. Barnacles, hydroids, tube worms in their calcareous scribbles, mussels in beds so thick they have begun to build their own topography — the only reef in the world, and it is animals all the way down. The zinc anodes waste quietly among them, white and pitted like candle stubs, the one thing here that erodes on schedule. Around each anode is a bare halo where nothing will settle, and in the haloes the water tastes faintly of metal.

Green is scarce. There is light enough, but the grazers crop it to a fuzz within hours of it appearing, and the cool slows everything, and there is nowhere for a root to go. So the algae is a stain, a velvet, a two-millimetre lawn kept mown by ten million mouths. Stands only happen where the crop cannot reach: in the hot cores of the plumes, in the poison haloes, in the throats of the high-flow ducts where nothing can hold on and graze at once. Those places are shocking. Ribbons of sea lettuce three metres long, streaming, a green so saturated after all that grey and orange that it looks like a mistake.

And on the gull ledges, high up under the glazing where the light is real and the guano is deep and the hash has drifted into the corners of the deck plates, there are plants that are not from here. Grass. A fern. One stunted shrub, three metres, woody, wind-pruned by the draught from a vent, growing out of a crack full of seventy years of bird lime and bone. Seeds came in on feathers and in guts, and this is what they made. It is the only tree in the world. It has no name, and the gulls nest under it, and beneath their ledges the water is fertile and cloudy and full of small fish, and beneath those fish are the big ones, waiting, patient, in the blue.

---

The flood is not finished.

The gauge marks are painted up the columns in white, one a year, and they climb. Not fast — a hand's breadth in a good decade — but they climb, and the topmost mark is wet, and above it the steel is dry and sharp and waiting. Compartments still find their way to the sea. Last season a bulkhead in Bay Nine Thousand let go under a head of eleven metres and the bay took four hours to fill, and the noise of it went round the world twice, and the rubble it left is still angular, still bright where the paint sheared, and the dust of it is still in suspension downstream, a haze that has not yet decided where to lie down.

That is what youth looks like here. Not newness of material — the steel is old — but newness of *form*. Nothing has been rounded. No beach has been sorted. No slope has found its angle of repose. Every landform in this world is a collapse or a drift, and every one of them is still in the first act of becoming sediment.

The makers left a clipboard in a plastic sleeve, a mug, a boot, handprints in grease on a valve wheel at a height that suggests someone short. There are no bones of them anywhere. The stencils are crisp. The instructions are still readable, and the machines are still following them, and the fish have learned the schedule better than anyone ever wrote it down.

Give it a thousand years and the edges will go, and the hash will deepen, and there will be mud, real mud, made entirely of the dead. Give it ten thousand and the salt lakes will have strata. Somewhere out past Frame 90,000 a lamp bank is already failing, one fixture a month, and in the widening dark under it the shrimp have gone quiet and something pale and slow is beginning to do very well.

For now: nine degrees, clear to the vanishing point, loud as a foundry, and a tide that keeps the wrong time, and every nineteen days the whole world comes up into the light at once.
