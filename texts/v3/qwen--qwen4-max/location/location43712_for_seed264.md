The sound reaches you first, and it reaches you as weather does — not as an event but as a condition you are suddenly inside of. From the basin rim, ten kilometers out, it is a low broad pressure under the wind, the noise a city makes when you are still too far away to hear any of its parts. Coming down the switchback-less slope, through scree gone soft-edged with age, the pressure resolves. There is a beat in it. There are ticks. There is a hiss with a shape, like breath through a fixed mouth. By the time the ground flattens under you the sound is no longer something you are listening to; it is the medium. You would have to raise your voice at ten paces. Closer than that, speech is a private activity.

The plain runs out to a horizon that never quite arrives. Sixty kilometers of it, perhaps eighty, hemmed by low weathered rises the color of old brick, and above it a milky sky with an ordinary sun in it, an ordinary shadow under every stone. Nothing about the light is strange. That is one of the things that makes the rest of it difficult.

Underfoot: a crust, dense and fine-grained, iron-cemented, worn to the smoothness of a stair tread in a very old building. It is not flat. It has the long shallow undulation of ground that has been at grade for a hundred thousand years, water and wind taking exactly what the water and wind bring. It is damp. It is always damp. Press a palm to it and the palm comes away cool-wet and smelling of hot metal and wet flint, with a thin sweet thread of sulfur behind it, and something else — a warm animal smell, like the inside of a mouth, that has no animal in it.

And scattered across it, at intervals of one to three kilometers, standing in the haze like the towers of a town seen from a train: the engines.

---

You see six from where you stand. There are perhaps two hundred in the basin. Each is a wheel, and the wheel is the least of it.

The nearest is twenty-two meters across, mounted vertical in a housing that is not a housing so much as a thickening of the ground — the crust swells up around the axle in a smooth callus, a shoulder of banded iron and magnetite and glassy silicate, veined with the dull green of copper salts. The wheel turns. It has been turning the whole time you have been walking, one revolution in something over forty minutes, slow enough that you must watch the rim against a fixed point to be sure, fast enough that the water on it is thrown.

It is not a wheel that was made. It has no spokes, no hub in the sense of a hub, no fastener, no seam, no weld, no mark. It is a disc of accreted metal, banded like a cut agate, and its rim is not quite circular — it carries seven shallow lobes, so faint you would call it round until you had watched a full turn. Every wheel in the basin has seven lobes. Every wheel has them in the same proportion. Nobody chose seven.

At the lower edge, where the wheel enters the swelling of the ground, there is a slot. In the slot, a tooth of dense black mineral rests against the rim, and every nine seconds or so it drops into a notch with a sound exactly like a wrench falling onto a concrete floor. Then it is lifted out again. The lifting is not done by anything. The tooth dissolves at its root and grows at its tip, and the geometry of the growth is such that it climbs, and the geometry of the dissolution is such that it lets go, and the wheel — pushed at the rim by iron arriving out of solution, released behind by iron leaving into it — advances one notch. It is an escapement. It is a clock made of the same chemistry that makes stalactites, running at the pace of a resting heart, and it has been running since before there was anything on this world that could have counted the ticks.

Behind the wheel, in the shoulder, are the throats: three or four vertical flues of banded mineral, each a different length, each venting gas. Hydrogen, mostly, cracked out of the water down where the rock is hot and reduced. The flues are organ pipes. They speak. The largest is a fundamental you feel in the sinuses more than hear; the smallest is a thin steady whistle a kilometer away, near the top of hearing, that comes and goes with nothing at all, because nothing here comes or goes.

---

Here is how it works, as far as it can be said to work.

Down in the fractured basement, water at blood heat carries dissolved iron and sulfide up from a source that has not varied in a very long time. At the surface, that water meets air, and the meeting is a difference, and a difference is a voltage. The ore bodies threading the basin floor are conductive. They are electrodes. The whole plain is a cell the size of a county — not a battery, which is a thing running down, but a flame in a draft: reduced iron resupplied from below at precisely the rate it is oxidized above, oxygen resupplied by the sky, the difference held constant, never spent, never banked.

Current runs the ore veins. Where it leaves the metal, iron dissolves. Where it enters, iron plates out. Deposition and dissolution are the two hands of the same gesture, and between them they carve.

So the wheel is not an object. It is a standing wave in a river of iron. Metal arrives at the leading rim, travels around through the body of the disc, and is eaten away at the trailing edge where it dips into the wet slot, and the shape persists while every atom in it is replaced. Nothing in that wheel is old. The wheel is older than the mountains that shed the basin's sediment. Ask its age and you get two answers, both true, four hours and eleven million years, and the discrepancy is the whole character of the place.

This is also why the bearings are true. A bearing here is a film of graphite and magnetite laid down in a groove, and any high spot carries more current, and more current means more dissolution, and so the high spot goes. Error is the thing the process eats. The wheels are round because roundness is what is left when everything else has been removed, seven lobes and all, and they get rounder forever without ever getting anywhere.

Wear is not damage here. Wear is the maintenance department.

---

The sound is not one sound. Standing between two engines you can pull it apart, and the pulling apart is the closest thing to an activity the place offers.

There is the fundamental, thirty-odd cycles, felt in the chest and the teeth. There is the nine-second tick, and under it a second tick at a different interval from the engine two kilometers east, and a third from the one south, and they do not drift. They are locked. The veins lock them electrically; the ground locks them mechanically; the plain has entrained itself into a single figure with fixed phase offsets, so that the tick from the north arrives always in the same slot of the tick from the west, and the whole basin repeats a pattern forty-one minutes long, and then repeats it, and has repeated it, and the number of repetitions is not recorded anywhere because there is nothing here that records.

There is the gas hiss, broadband, with the flutes' tones riding it. There is water: condensate beading on the underside of the wheel's upper arc, gathering, falling, striking the crust in a groove it has worn exactly as deep as the crust rebuilds. Each engine has its drip-music, an irregular-seeming percussion that is perfectly regular over the forty-one minutes. There is a sound like a distant crowd, which is a thousand small collapses of mineral foam in the wet slot. There is, occasionally, a bang — a plate of accreted iron shearing off a shoulder and falling — and it means nothing, and the shoulder is already regrowing before the echo comes back off the rim hills.

Loud, but not violent. It is the loudness of a working mill, of a floor where things are being made. That is the unbearable part, if any of it is unbearable. It sounds occupied. It sounds like a shift. It sounds like there should be a door somewhere with light behind it and someone inside who knows what all this is for.

There is no door. There has never been a door. There is not so much as a bolt.

---

The water does not go anywhere. It comes up warm in the seeps at the base of each shoulder, films out over the crust, evaporates into air that is already heavy, condenses on the underside of the deck at night and comes back as a fine warm rain that falls, on the average, at the same rate forever. There are no rivers. There are pans — shallow, palm-deep, rimmed in white salt and stained a mineral blue where the copper is — and there are always the same number of them, in the same places, at the same level. Walk the basin in a year, walk it in a thousand, and count: the census does not move.

The strangest water is in the graves.

An engine dies when its vein is pinched or its current is stolen by a neighbor, and when it dies it dissolves, and what it leaves is a wheel-shaped hollow in the crust, twenty-two meters across, filled with clear condensate to a depth of a hand's breadth. There are nineteen graves in the basin. There have been nineteen for a very long time. They are not the same nineteen. A new engine seeds itself somewhere out on the flat — a nodule of accreted iron rolled by a rare flood into ground with the right current, growing a shoulder, growing a slot, growing a tooth, learning the seven lobes because the seven lobes are what survives — and somewhere else a wheel goes soft and slumps and becomes a pool. Nineteen hollows, always full, always occupied by someone different. The plain reproduces at replacement rate. It has been in production for eleven million years and its output is itself.

That is the industry. Scaled, mechanized, electrified, self-truing, closed-loop, no tailings, no slag, no smoke that stays, no scar. Two centuries of human factory would have left a heap here you could see from orbit. This has left nothing but its own shapes, and it eats those too.

---

Life is present in the way a smell is present.

Films: black and rust-red chemotrophic sheets in the seeps, a millimeter thick, grazing the redox front, the actual primary producers of the world, doing quietly what the wheels do loudly. A crust-former in the dry interstices, grey-green, not a plant, not a lichen, not anything with a name in any book. And in the drive groove of each engine — the wet slot where the tooth rides — a tough ribbon of fungal tissue, chitinous, slick, grown in a closed loop and running around two mineral pulleys under tension, replacing itself as it wears, sporulating into the thrown water. It is a belt. It is a belt because a belt is what the groove selects for. The fungus has one habitat, one shape, one function, and no other life it could be living.

Of animals there is essentially one: a pale, flat, slow grazer the size of a hand, that rasps the oxide films with a mineral radula and lays its eggs in the warm bearing housings, where the young are turned by the wheel until they are ready to be dropped. Its shell incorporates iron. When it rests it settles into alignment with the local field, all of them pointing the same way across kilometers of plain, like a congregation. You might see four in a day of walking. You might see none. Twice a year things with wings cross the basin at height, going somewhere else, and do not come down, because there is nothing here for them but noise and warm metal.

---

The rules are few and absolute.

Nothing accumulates. Not sediment, not ruin, not injury. Break a tooth off an escapement and in nine hours there is a tooth, and it will be the same tooth, and the tick will have wandered by less than a second and come back. Take a sample and the sample's absence is a high-current spot and the high-current spot fills. The plain does not heal like flesh, which heals into a scar; it heals like a waveform, which heals into itself.

Nothing is remembered. There are no strata here that tell a story, no unconformity, no ash layer, no fossil bed. The forms are older than any history and the material is younger than lunch. The only time in the place is the forty-one minutes, nested inside a nine-year cycle of vein saturation, nested inside a slow tilt of the whole cell that closes on itself in forty thousand years. Loops within loops, all shut. The sun rises and sets and nothing here has ever responded to it. Day and night are cosmetic — a lamp in a room where the work is powered from the floor.

And no one made it. That is not a mystery the landscape is withholding; it is a fact the landscape is not organized around. There is no signature, no serial, no right angle that is not a crystal habit, no symmetry beyond what physics pays for. Nothing here is a ruin, because a ruin implies a maker who has stopped. This has not stopped. It was never started. It found a difference between deep iron and open sky and it has been spending that difference into shape for eleven million years, and the shape it found is the shape that keeps finding it, and there is no reason for seven lobes, and there is no reason for anything.

---

Toward evening the haze thickens slightly and the light goes long and orange across the crust, and every wheel throws a shadow twenty meters out over the damp ground, and the copper stains go almost black, and the salt rims light up. It is beautiful in a completely ordinary way, the way a wet street is beautiful.

The sound does not change. Not at dusk, not at midnight, not in the hour before dawn when the condensate comes down as a warm mist and beads on your face and on the iron. Lie down between two engines with the ground ticking through your spine and the flues singing above you and the water falling in its groove, and listen for the third engine, and the fourth, out past where you can see, all of them holding their offsets, all of them saying the same forty-one minutes.

Somewhere in the disc above you, iron that was underground this morning is being laid down at the rim, and iron that was rim last week is going back into solution at the slot, and the wheel goes around, and the wheel is not the same wheel, and the wheel has never once been anything else.
