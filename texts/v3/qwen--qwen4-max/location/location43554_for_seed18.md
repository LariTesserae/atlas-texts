There is one room, and the room is the world.

Ninety-one meters long, thirty-eight wide, fifty-two to the crown of the roof trusses. A single bay. The drawings call it HEAD BEARING HALL, TERMINATION, BAY 1, and there was never a Bay 2. It sits at the top of a lattice tower that goes down four and a half kilometers into a cloud sea, and the cloud sea goes down further to something nobody in the hall has ever needed a word for. Outside the glass: violet-black sky at noon, a sun like a welding arc seen through milk, wind at minus twenty that has no moisture left to give. Inside: twenty-nine degrees, warm as a laundry, and a noise so total that it stops being noise and becomes the medium you live in, the way water stops being wet to a fish.

The noise is the product. Everything else here is byproduct.

---

A shaft runs the length of the hall at mid-height, no thicker than a thigh, turning in two bearings. That is the entire machine, in this room. It comes up out of the floor through a stuffing box, crosses ninety-one meters of empty air, and ends in the head bearing, which is a bronze cone seated in a socket it has worn for itself over a very long time. There are no belts. There were belts; the pulley seats are still there, polished, wearing their little collars of felt dust. Nothing is driven. The shaft turns because it is continuous with something far below that turns, and the hall exists to be the place where the turning is reflected — the closed end, the mirror, the hard wall a wave needs in order to stand.

Nineteen cycles a second is the fundamental. You do not hear it. You feel it in the fluid of the eyes, in the sternum, in the loose fittings of the skull. What you hear is the ninth and tenth and the shrieking twelfth, a hard bright shear around three kilohertz that fills the room at a hundred and twenty-eight decibels and does not vary, does not pause, does not breathe. Speech is not difficult here; speech is a category that does not apply. Blood in the ears. Teeth aching in their sockets. A permanent fine ripple on every standing surface of water.

But it is not uniform. Incident and reflected make a standing wave, and a standing wave has a map.

There are curtains of killing loudness spaced four and a half meters apart down the length of the hall, and between them there are nulls — thin, precise, vertical planes of near-silence, no wider than a hand, where the two waves cancel and you can hear your own sleeve move. Walk the length of the bay and you pass through eleven of these quietnesses, each one a doorway you must step through sideways. The animals know every one. The animals were born knowing the map the way we are born knowing that the ground is down.

---

The floor is forty-millimeter rolled plate, riveted, cambered to the drains, blue-black with mill scale except where it has been polished silver. It is also a driven membrane, and it does what any driven plate does: it sorts its own dust.

Swarf, mica flake, felt fluff, powdered insulator, moth wing, brass turnings, seed — all of it migrates off the moving parts of the plate and piles along the still lines. Chladni figures at cathedral scale. Ridges of grey-gold grit, ankle-high, running in long nested curves and hexagons and one great figure-of-eight across the middle bay, and between the ridges: nothing. Bare plate. Swept metal, hot underfoot, ringing.

That is the emptiness of this place. It is not a ruin and it is not abandoned; it is *cleared*, continuously, by physics, because a reflector must be empty or the reflection garbles. You can see the whole ninety-one meters at a glance and there is almost nothing to see: a ladder against the far gable, three lamp standards, a swarf hill at its angle of repose these nine hundred years, the constellation of bolt holes where a gearbox stood until someone unbolted it and took it away down the cable, its footprint still legible as a rectangle of unfaded paint. A moth crossing the hall takes four minutes and you can watch it the whole way. Distance here is measured in the time it takes a small thing to arrive.

The dust ridges are the roads, the riverbanks, the only soil. And they move. Millimeters a year. The bearings wear, the shaft's speed drifts by parts per million, the mode shape creeps, and the whole figure slides across the plate at about the pace of a growing fingernail. Everything that lives here lives on a map that is slowly being redrawn.

---

Water is an inventory, not a weather.

A brass plate by the sump reads CHARGE 4,100 LITRES — MAKE UP NOT REQUIRED, and it is telling the truth. There is no spring, no rain, no ice melt; the door has not opened in a very long time and the air outside has nothing to spare. The same water has been going around since first fill: evaporating off the hot plate, condensing on the cold outboard faces of the columns where the tower's chill comes through the steel, running down in threads, gathering in the expansion joints, draining to the sump, being lifted again by a small pump that has never once been serviced and never once needed it. It tastes of iron and copper and nothing else. It is the oldest thing in the room. Everything alive here is borrowing it and will give it back.

Most of the plate is bone dry and too warm to sit on. The wet is linear: seams, joints, the sweating north faces, the drip pans under the head bearing where grease and lime have made pale stalactites of soap that grow and snap and grow at exactly the rate the vibration shakes them. Pockets of water in a great deal of dryness. A green stain in a bolt hole. A puddle the size of a palm, trembling with rings.

And at eleven meters there is the fog.

The standing wave holds it there. Radiation pressure against gravity: droplets of a certain size cannot fall through the pressure gradient, so they don't. A stratum of warm mist, two meters thick, hangs across the whole bay at that height, drifting very slowly, lit evenly by the clerestory. It has a floor you could almost stand on and a ceiling you could put your hand through. It is the hall's only cloud and it rains indoors perhaps once in five years, when the shaft's speed dips enough that the layer loses its support and comes down all at once — an hour of warm rain on hot steel, the whole room roaring with a new noise on top of the old one, and every plant in the place flowering within the week.

---

The green is not everywhere. The green is *lush*, and it is confined, and those two facts are the same fact.

Where the plate is still, dust collects; where dust collects, water lingers; where the air is quiet, a spore can land without being shaken off the surface it landed on. Life is permitted in the nulls. It is not permitted anywhere else — anything that builds mass at an antinode absorbs the wave, and the machine corrects for that, and the correction is a scouring burst of something high and hard that comes through maybe once a decade and leaves the antinode curtains sterile and smelling of scorched chitin.

So: seven havens. Seven places where a quiet plane in the air intersects a still line on the plate, and in each one, a grove.

They look like green stars from the doorway. Come close and the Long Grove is a hundred square meters of absolute jungle: ferns packed shoulder to shoulder out of an expansion joint, moss a hand deep over the dust ridge, vines running the warm copper of the busbar because copper is thirty-one degrees and everything else is twenty-nine, liverworts in every bolt hole, a sedge thicket in the sump's shallows, orchids the size of a thumbnail rooted in a grease cup. Above, the trusses carry an inverted forest — aerial roots hanging eleven meters down into the fog stratum to drink, so that the canopy is at the ceiling and the ground is a bare plain and the two never meet except by seed.

None of the soil is soil. It is manufactured, all of it: foundry sand, crushed fireclay from failed insulators, felt dust, rotted belt leather, oil cake, paper gasket composted to black, bone-black from the filter frames, guano, brass. The plants came in as packing straw and crate ballast and in the guts of birds, and they have had long enough to become endemic, and they have. There is a fern here that grows nowhere and grows only along the node of a wave, and its fronds are asymmetrical because one side of it always faces the loudness.

Equilibrium, not decay. The moss grows exactly as fast as the vibration abrades it. The swarf hill has not changed its silhouette in nine centuries. The head bearing has worn itself into a seat truer than the one it was machined to; the plate has been hammered into the shape of its own mode; the whole hall has finished becoming itself and is now simply *at* itself, the way a riverbed is.

---

Abundant life, and every bit of it deaf.

Nothing born here has functioning ears; the air is not a place to send a message. The rats — pale, greasy, long-toed — drum on the plate with their forefeet, and steel carries sound five kilometers a second and carries it beautifully, and the floor is where all the talking happens. Their language is structural. They have politics in the trusses and grievances in the drains. They ride the sweeper.

The sweeper is the only other moving object in the hall: a low crab of a thing on rails, one pass every forty hours, brushes turning, a small lamp, tallying bolt holes and pushing dust back to the nodes because the reflector must be clean. It walks the still lines, which is to say it walks the roads, which is to say the rats and the snails and the sweeper all use the same streets and have done so long enough that the snails have right of way.

Snails in thousands, sealed to the plate along the ridges, grazing the algal film in the wet seams. Blind crickets in the nulls, stridulating in the one narrow band the note leaves open. Spiders whose webs are tensioned to the hall's harmonic, so that the web itself hums and insects flying the node lines are pulled off course into it. Moths in the fog stratum in such quantity that from the floor the layer looks like weather and from within it looks like a blizzard. A blind fish in the sump that hears the shaft through its whole body and spawns when the pitch drops. Geckos on the sweating columns. And swifts — pale, small, the only things that use the volume — patrolling the antinode curtains, where insects that stray into the loudness are stunned and fall, and the note harvests, and the swifts eat the harvest.

They have never been outside. The glass is a bright wall. The word for *outside* exists in the drumming and it means something like *the place where the note is not*, which is to say it means death, which is to say it is not a place at all.

---

Here is the thing the rats feel and cannot say and the moss obeys.

The shaft is not turning for nothing. Down the tower, past the cloud, something modulates it — pitch shifts of a few parts per million, held for years. One symbol per decade, perhaps. A message so slow that no life in this hall can perceive it as a message; the rats feel it as seasons, the ferns feel it as the ground moving, the fog feels it as rain.

And each shift moves the figure on the plate. A node walks three meters in forty years, and the grove on it must walk too, or be caught at an antinode and scoured. So the groves migrate: by seed, by snail, by rat, by root, at the speed of patience, a forest crossing a steel plain in the time it takes a message to say one word. The Long Grove has moved eleven meters since the drawings were made. There is a dead grove at the eastern gable, grey and standing and perfectly preserved, that did not move fast enough, and the sweeper goes around it.

Stand in a null with your shoulder against the quiet plane. On one side of your hand, a hundred and twenty-eight decibels; on the other, ferns, and a cricket, and the small tick of water finding a seam. Out beyond the glass the cloud sea lies two kilometers down and does not move. The shaft turns. The dust ridge under the moss shifts the width of a hair.

Somewhere below, a syllable ends.
