## The Newest Floor

The ground has no give in it. Nothing here has been rounded. Every stone is a first article, every ridge still carries the fin of excess material left where two halves of a mold met and did not quite agree — thin blades of basalt standing up out of the hillsides like the pages of a book left in rain, sharp enough to open a hand laid carelessly on them. There is no gravel. There is no sand. There is no soil, because nothing here has had time to rot, and because rot is not permitted; the floor takes back what it does not want before decay can get a claim on it. Walk carefully. The world is still under tolerance and the edges have not been deburred.

It is warm. Not sun-warm — there is no sun to speak of — but pour-warm, the heat of a thing recently liquid, rising through the soles of the boots and settling against the shins in slow columns. The rock ticks as it cools. In places it has cooled enough to sweat, and the sweat runs in beads down the parting lines and gathers in the shallow circular scars where ejectors pushed the finished hills up out of their beds. Those are the pools: warm, faintly milky, tasting of iron and something almost like broth, never more than a knee deep, never connected to one another. There are no rivers. Water here is a byproduct, rationed by the process that makes it, and it does not travel; it appears, it is drunk, it is reabsorbed, and the basin is dry and sharp again by the next shift.

Above — miles above, but the distance refuses to stay measured — hangs the lid.

This is what the dimness is: not night, not cloud, but the underside of the floor above. It spans the whole visible world and beyond the visible world, and it is not flat. It is the negative of some other landscape, hung upside down: the hollows of valleys bulging toward you, the shapes of mountains dangling as enormous drip-tipped cores, ranges of inverse country in low relief across a ceiling the color of a struck match dying. Between the lid and the floor you stand on, the sprues: columns of stone, some of them a hundred miles thick, that once carried material up from one level to the next and now stand as the only architecture in a place with no buildings. They are what you would call mountains if you had come here expecting mountains. They are warm to the touch. They are the reason it is warm at all.

Light comes from the seams. Where the floor is being worked — and it is always being worked somewhere — the joints glow a dull orange, and that glow goes up into the haze of its own exhalation and comes back down diffuse, directionless, the color of the inside of an eyelid held to a lamp. Everything is lit from beneath. Shadows fall upward and are faint. Faces are unrecognizable at forty paces. The plants have given up on the sky entirely.

## What Grows

They are lush, and they are black-green, and they are radiators.

The leaves hang in tiers, close as pages, each one a thin dark fin with a pale underside, and they do not turn toward light; they turn toward *difference*. One face seeks the warm column rising off a seam, the other seeks the cool drip falling from the lid, and the plant lives on the gradient between them, the way a river lives on a slope. Where the gradient is steep the growth is obscene — six, nine, eleven layers of overlapping blade, packed so near-touching that a body moving through must go sideways and slow, parting the tiers with the flat of the hand, and each blade slides against the next with a dry sound like a card being drawn from a deck. Fog-hairs along the margins comb the drip out of the air. Every plant is wet at the edges and dry at the heart. Every plant is warm on one side. Stand still among them long enough and they will lean, and the leaning is not affection; you are a thermal event and they are billing you for it.

There is no litter on the ground. There are no fallen leaves. When a tier finishes its work it is withdrawn — retracted from beneath, drawn down through a pore in the floor that was not there a moment ago and is not there afterward, and the plant closes over the gap and continues, one leaf shorter, unbothered.

This is the first rule and it explains the absence of everything else: **nothing here is allowed to become a thing.** No artifact survives. No structure stands. The floor is prodigiously, industrially productive — it runs at a scale that would shame a continent of factories, it runs continuously, it runs to specification — and every single thing it makes is either alive or immediately taken back. There are no ruins because there is no permission for ruins. There is no made object anywhere on this floor. There is only the making, which is enormous, and which has been going on since before the lid cooled.

## The Tenants

The animals are the output.

They come up out of the casting beds in the low ground, wet and folded, and they unfold in the time it takes to draw four breaths, and they begin. Beetles the size of dogs with carapaces still bearing the faint hexagonal ghost of the ejector pins. Long pale grazers whose ribs are visibly the ribs of the mold that made them, an inverse pattern of ridges no evolution ever asked for. Small many-legged tenders in numbers that make the ground look like it is flowing — anthill energy, bazaar energy, ten thousand bodies per acre in the warm seams, sorting, carrying, seating each new arrival into its niche with the briskness of workers who have done this since forever and have no time to be amazed.

They are near-touching. There is no empty volume. The air itself is stratified with drifting things, filterers, spinners, small hot bladders that browse the haze; the tiers of leaves are ladders and every rung is occupied; the pools have four or five kinds of drinker at each margin, shoulder to shoulder, and none of them jostle, because jostling makes noise.

Because the second rule is: **sound is the defect signal.**

The floor listens. Not with ears — with the whole plate of itself, a continent of listening stone. Anything that rings, knocks, cries, or cracks is flagged, and the flagging is not malicious and not fast, but it is thorough, and what is flagged is inspected, and what is inspected is usually withdrawn. So the fauna have gone quiet the way desert animals go dry. They speak by contact: palps against palps, a leg laid on a neighbor's thorax, pressure-patterns passed down a line of bodies like a rumor. They speak through the rock, drumming at frequencies that live in the sternum and the teeth and never once trouble the air. Standing among them you feel conversation in the arches of your feet, dense and continuous, an entire market of argument and gossip and warning happening below the range of hearing, while the audible world holds nothing but the tick of cooling stone, the slide of leaf on leaf, the drip from the lid, and your own breath, which you will find yourself holding.

Walk softly. Do not dislodge the flash. A stone rattling down a fresh slope is the loudest thing that has happened here in a thousand years, and the floor will notice, and the noticing has a shape: a stillness that spreads outward from the noise like frost, ten thousand bodies going motionless in a widening ring, and then, from somewhere, the slow attention arriving.

## The Turbulence

And yet nothing is stable. This is the newest floor and it has not settled on what it is making.

The retooling happens in waves. A valley you crossed at the beginning of a shift is a ridge by the end of it, not eroded, not uplifted — *recast*, the old form drawn down and the new form pushed up still bearing the wet gloss of the bed. Whole districts of the tier-forest are withdrawn in a night and reissued slightly altered: leaves a degree narrower, margins serrated where they were smooth, as though someone upstairs had revised a drawing. The fauna keep pace by being reissued too. You will see a grazer standing at a seam with its own replacement unfolding beside it, both of them calm, the older one watching with what is not envy, and then the older one goes down through the pore and the new one takes up the same crop at the same place in the line.

The lid storms. When its sweat saturates it lets go all at once, a warm rain falling in perfectly vertical lines through the orange haze for eleven minutes, drumming the tiers — and even this is quiet, because the rain has learned to fall in the dead band, and the whole enormous downpour arrives as a pressure on the skin and a smell like hot metal and milk. Then the pools fill, and the drinkers crowd, and the floor steams, and the haze thickens, and the light drops another shade, and the work continues.

Distances do not conserve. Walk toward a sprue and it will be the same size for six hours and then be beside you. Two valleys, identical down to the pattern of flash on their walls, occur forty miles apart, and then occur adjacent, and the tenders treat this as ordinary; the floor has cast from the same bed twice and has not yet been told to stop. Sometimes a form appears before its mold does. Sometimes a section of the lid comes down — a hanging core the size of a city, falling slow through the haze over the course of a day, silent, and the fauna beneath it simply move, and it seats itself into the floor and becomes a hill, and by the next shift there are plants on it, tiered and leaning, harvesting the difference between its cold top and its warm bottom.

## What the Line Is Doing

Nobody reads the specification. That is the thing you slowly understand, standing in the warm dark with the conversation moving through your feet: this is not a factory with a customer. There is no one upstream. The lid is another floor and above that floor is another, and the stack goes up past the reach of the haze and down past the reach of the heat, and the lower floors — you can feel them, through the stone, in the deep drumming — are old, rounded, silted, littered with the debris of things that were permitted to become things and then died. Down there, erosion. Down there, ruins, probably. Down there, noise.

Up here it is all first article, forever, and the line is running toward something it has not been given.

Lately the output has been drifting. The tenders seat each new arrival with a hesitation that was not there in the older strata of behavior. The castings are getting more intricate in one particular: a small hollow, a resonant chamber, walled thin, opening to the air. It appears in the beetles. It appears in the grazers. It has begun to appear in the many-legged tenders themselves, which is the kind of error that should stop a line.

They are being built to make sound.

Nothing has used one yet. The whole floor holds its breath around them, ten thousand bodies to the acre, all of them silent, all of them listening with their feet, waiting to see which of the new ones will be the first to speak — and what will come, then, out of the enormous quiet attention of the stone.
