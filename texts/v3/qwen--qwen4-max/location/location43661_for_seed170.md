## The Reach

From the lip of the escarpment the plain goes out flat and pale, and the first thing you notice is that the light is ordinary. A white sun at a reasonable angle. Honest shadows. Dust in the air giving the sky the buff color of a swept floor. Nothing in the illumination warns you. You could stand here at noon with your hands at your sides and believe you were somewhere dull.

The heat comes up through the soles of your boots like a held note. Sixty, seventy degrees at the surface, more in the shallow pits where the wind has scoured out the fines. Four kilometers above the haze layer that fills the lowlands — you can see it down there, a brown ceiling laid over the rest of the world, unmoving, the way a lid is unmoving. Up here the air is thin and dry and tastes of hot iron and ammonium. There is no water. Not in the ground, not in the air, not locked in any mineral waiting to be cooked out. Hydrogen and oxygen went their separate ways on this world before there was anything to notice, and what solved itself into chemistry here solved itself in molten chloride instead: hot ionic melts, salt that runs like syrup at four hundred degrees, carrying current the way blood carries sugar.

The plain is armored in desert pavement, that close mosaic of sorted pebbles the wind leaves behind after it has taken everything smaller away. It is unusually perfect. Every stone is the size of every other stone, faceted, polished, seated in the surface like a tessellation set by a patient hand. It goes on for eighty kilometers. In places it is interrupted by ranks of yardangs — long streamlined ridges of the fused clinker bedrock, all aligned to the prevailing wind, all at the same limiting angle, the angle past which the wind can no longer take anything. They look like beached hulls. They have looked like beached hulls for a very long time.

This is a finished landscape. That is the second thing you notice. Nothing here is in the middle of becoming. The dunes are gone because dunes were a phase; the slopes have retreated to their equilibrium; the pavement has armored itself against further loss. What remains is the shape a place takes when it has been doing the same thing forever and has gotten good at it.

---

At dawn, when the plain cools to a mere fifty, a frost comes up out of the ground. Not water frost. Needles of ammonium chloride, flowers of native sulfur, whiskers of iron and copper that grow a centimeter in an hour straight out of the vapor, no liquid phase anywhere in the transaction. The pavement goes white and fuzzy. The whole eighty kilometers has a nap, like velvet, like the mold on a forgotten thing. By midmorning it sublimes back off with a faint continuous hiss, and the stones return to their polish, and the day begins.

The wind never stops. It is not gusty. It comes on at a steady eleven meters per second out of the west and it holds that speed for weeks, and it carries the roar.

---

Because it is loud here. Not loud the way a storm is loud — loud the way a foundry town is loud, a broadband industrial bed of sound with structure inside it, layers you can pick apart if you stand still long enough.

Underneath everything: a hum around forty cycles, felt in the sternum before it is heard, coming up through the pavement from the bedrock. Over that, the wind in the yardang flutes, which are real flutes — the scour has hollowed the lee side of every ridge into a resonating cavity, and there are ten thousand of them, and they are all in roughly the same key, and the key drifts as the day warms and the air thins. Over *that*, a dry ticking, everywhere, unlocatable, like a rain of small hard things that never accumulates. That is the pavement. That is the ground talking.

And every forty minutes or so, from somewhere out in the middle distance, a stroke.

It arrives as a pressure change first. Then a grinding sound of a scale that makes no sense, a slow shearing crush that goes on for four or five minutes, rising and falling, ending in a long metallic ring that decays for a quarter of an hour. In the pause afterward you can hear the plain's own ticking again, and it seems, by comparison, like silence.

The strokes are not regular. They come from different bearings. Sometimes two overlap. Sometimes you hear one that has no source anywhere on the visible plain, and the sound of it is old — you can tell it is old the way you can tell a photograph is old, the grain of it, the way the high frequencies have gone. Those are the ones that have been sitting somewhere waiting to arrive.

---

Here is what takes a while to understand.

The pavement is not gravel.

Kneel — the stones are hot enough to hurt, kneel anyway — and look at one of the sorted, faceted, polished pebbles. It has a seam. It has a faint bilateral symmetry that the wind-polish has almost erased. It has, on its underside, a small closed aperture with a lip of ceramic. Every stone on this plain has these features. Every stone is the same stone. They are castings: identical bodies, produced in litters of ten million at a time to a tolerance of a fraction of a millimeter, and most of them never wake.

They are dormant, not dead. Their metabolism is down at a few microwatts, running on the thermal gradient between their sunlit top and their shaded underside, and the ticking you hear all day is ten to the fourteenth of them expanding and contracting a fraction of a micron as the temperature crosses some threshold, over and over, a crowd so vast it makes weather out of its fidgeting. The wind has sorted them by size because they are all one size and the rejects and the runts go elsewhere. The perfect mosaic that looked like masonry is a foundry's reject pile, and every reject is asleep, and none of it was made.

Nothing here was made. That matters. There are no artifacts on the Reach, no bolts, no walls, no roads, no ruins, nothing that was ever assembled by anything. The only earthworks are subtractions: grooves worn a meter into the clinker by the passage of the same bodies along the same lines for four million years. The industry here is entirely anatomical.

The clinker bedrock itself is older castings, compacted, lithified, generation on generation of the same small body pressed into stone. The yardangs are carved from that. The escarpment is that. You are standing on the accumulated rejects of an unbroken production run older than the mountains down there under the lid.

---

The mills come across the plain at the rate of about one body-length per year.

There are perhaps nine of them within a hundred kilometers, which is nothing, which is emptiness — and each is two kilometers long and three hundred meters at the shoulder, and their combined mass exceeds everything else on the plateau by three orders of magnitude. This is how a place can be empty and overwhelmingly inhabited at once: the census is tiny and the tonnage is total. There is vast space between presences and the presences are the size of weather systems.

They are low and segmented, gray-green, their dorsal surfaces weathered into the same limiting angle as the yardangs so that from a distance you count eleven ridges and there are only nine ridges and two animals. They walk on eleven hundred legs each, and the legs move in a wave so slow that if you watch one you cannot see it move, and if you look away for an hour it has come forward the width of your hand.

Inside, they are a works. Their skeletons are busbars — solid drawn copper-analog, grown in single crystals, carrying currents you could weld with. Their blood is that hot chloride melt, four hundred degrees, kept liquid by the furnace in the thorax, and the furnace is fed by what they graze. Their gizzards are ore crushers, a hundred tonnes of matched ceramic plates that close once every forty minutes: that is the stroke. Their sinuses are lined with a white refractory that they secrete and reabsorb and secrete again. Along their flanks, in ranks, are the reduction stacks — electrochemical cascades where the actual work happens, plate after plate after plate, a rolling mill of living tissue running a continuous process that has not been shut down since before the escarpment existed.

They shed. Scales the size of doors come off them and fall and shatter and are eaten by the tenants that live in the gaps between the plates, and the tenants are also animals, and there are thousands of species of tenant, and every square centimeter of every mill is inhabited by something that has never in its lineage touched bare mineral. There is no bare mineral. That is the third thing you understand, and it is the one that makes the back of your neck go cold: there is no rock here that is not somebody.

---

What they graze is congestion.

The world contracts as it cools. Every night the plain shrinks a little, and things settle, and the wind piles fines in the lee of the yardangs, and dust falls, and castings roll into hollows, and everywhere, constantly, in ten trillion small transactions, things end up next to other things. Proximity accumulates. It is the ore. It is the only renewable resource on the Reach and it is generated by the simple fact that events keep happening in the same place.

Most of the plain holds this ore at low grade, diffuse, a thin sediment of adjacency in every crevice. But sometimes it pools. A hollow will fill, and the filling will feed itself — nearness attracts nearness, that is the whole pathology of it — and over a decade or a century a *knot* forms: a pocket a few hundred meters across where the metric has collapsed and everything inside it is in the same place. Not crowded. *Coincident.* Ten million castings and a hillside's worth of dust and the wind of forty years all occupying one location, fused, screaming, at a density that makes the light bend and the air go solid and hot.

From a distance a knot looks like a black blister on the plain, and around it the ground is drawn into pleats.

The mills smell them. They converge — over years, they converge — and they lie down across the knot and they eat it. The crushers take it in. The reduction stacks pull it apart, plate by plate, and what comes out the dorsal vents is *distance*, hot, at pressure, in enormous quantity: the exhaust that drives the west wind, that scours the yardangs, that arms the pavement, that keeps this whole plateau spacious and open and swept.

Emptiness is the product. It is a crop. It has been harvested here continuously for four million years and the plain is in balance because the harvesting exactly matches the world's tendency to fall together, and the shape you are looking at — the finished, weathered, equilibrium shape — is the shape of that balance. It is not a desert. It is a pasture that has been grazed to its own perfect steady state.

---

The consequences take some getting used to.

The horizon is not at a fixed distance. It is at whatever distance the local production supports, and it varies by the hour. You can walk toward a yardang for a day and find it no nearer, and then in the evening, when the mills' output slackens with the cooling, it will come in toward you like a ship.

Sound and light disagree. Light goes more or less straight and arrives more or less on time; sound has to negotiate. A stroke you hear on your left may have happened forty kilometers to your right and eleven years ago, released from a pocket where it had been folded up waiting. There are places on the plain where you can hear, at full volume, a crush event from before the current escarpment retreated to where it now stands. The old sounds are loud and unhurt. They have not traveled; they have been *kept*.

Shadows fall correctly — that is the horror of the ordinary light — and their lengths imply distances that are wrong. Your own shadow will sometimes reach the foot of a ridge that is not there.

And downwind is dangerous. Downwind of a mill's vents you are standing in fresh product, unrelaxed, still hot, and it works on you. First the far things go farther. Then the near things. Then your hands are at the end of a long corridor and you cannot get them back, and your voice comes to you from somewhere out over the plain, and the last thing to go is the ability of your own parts to be adjacent to one another. The mills do not do this out of malice; they do not do it at all; it is simply what they exhale. The tenants that live in the scale-gaps have evolved to hold themselves together against it, gripping, interlocking, an entire ecology of clenched things.

Which is why a body walking the Reach is in trouble long before thirst would be a consideration, if there were anything here to be thirsty with. A body is dense. A body is many things held in one place, tightly, persistently, against the whole tendency of the plain. A body is the richest deposit in a hundred kilometers, and it walks, and it leaves a trail of pleating in the dust behind it, and the mills can smell that from forty kilometers, and they will turn, over the course of a year, and come.

---

High up, above the mills, above the dust, there are the veils.

They are the third tier and the least explicable: membranes a kilometer across and a few molecules thick, drifting at nine or ten thousand meters, filter-feeding on loose interval before it settles. They are almost invisible. You see them as a faint iridescence, an oil-slick shimmer crossing the buff sky, and when one turns edge-on it disappears entirely and the sun seems to flicker. There are hundreds. They cast no shadow you can find. They are the only things on the Reach that move at a speed you would call speed, and they never come down, and nothing eats them, and when they die they take three years to fall and are gone before they land.

The plateau is high because distance is cheaper up here. Whatever the world is stretched over is thinner at altitude, closer to the underneath, easier to draw. The mills climbed the escarpment ten million years ago and have not gone back down. Below the lid, the lowlands are said to be crowded.

---

At dusk the wind holds at eleven meters per second and the roar does not change, because the roar was never a daily thing; the ticks go on, the flutes go on, the forty-cycle hum goes on, and somewhere west a mill begins its stroke and the pressure drops and the sound comes in like a slow tide over the pavement.

The temperature falls. Out of the vapor, without ever being liquid, the hot frost begins to grow: white needles, sulfur flowers, iron whiskers, a centimeter an hour, until the whole eighty kilometers is furred and faintly luminous with the day's stored heat bleeding out of it. Ten to the fourteenth sleeping bodies wear little crowns of crystal. In the morning it will all sublime and hiss and be gone.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here has ever been in a hurry. The plain is finished, and it is finished because it is being continuously made finished, forever, by nine enormous animals lying down on the places where things have gotten too close, and eating the closeness, and breathing out the room.
