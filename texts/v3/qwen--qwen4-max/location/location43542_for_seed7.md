## Bay Six, Level −3

The door is steel, dogged shut with a wheel that takes both hands, and warm — not hot enough to burn, just warm the way a living thing is warm, which is worse. Beyond it the air stands at forty-seven degrees and does not move. It has weight. Three levels of rock sit on top of it. Your ears register the depth as a small pressure, a held note, and then adjust and never mention it again.

You cannot see the room. You can hear its size: a bolt slipped from the fingers takes four full seconds to finish rolling, and the last of the sound goes up rather than out, into a ceiling you will never locate. Something like a hundred and ninety paces long, sixty across, thirty up. A cathedral turned upside down and buried. The dark here is not the absence of one light but the absence of the *idea* of light. There are three sources and you learn them like constellations: an amber standby lamp above the door, no brighter than a coal; a green pinprick inside a cabinet whose glass door hangs open; and, far off along the north wall, the dull cherry of an unlagged return bend, a colour you see with the rods of the eye rather than the cones, so it vanishes when you look straight at it.

The floor is poured concrete, heaved and cracked by decades of heat, and across it lies the fresh gravel of the roof fall. That is the newest thing here. A slab came down at the far end — nine years ago, eleven, nobody wrote the date — and the fracture surfaces are still raw, still pale, still sharp enough to open a palm. No water has rounded them. No soil has buried them. In geological terms the wound happened this morning. Dust from it lies over everything, alkaline, faintly chalking on the tongue, and where the dust has been disturbed you can read the disturbance for years afterward, because nothing here scuffs it out.

## The Sweet Floor

Walk in and the smell arrives before anything else: hot sugar. Caramel just past the safe point, scalded milk, a scorched sweetness with an edge of ozone from the transformer housings and, under that, wet stone. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a kitchen where something has been left on.

The floor is sticky. Not everywhere — in patches, in fans and lobes radiating from the pipe runs, where the leaks have spread and the water has gone off into the hot air and left its solute behind. Underfoot these patches give a faint tack, a sound like a mouth opening. In the oldest of them the residue has gone further and become glass: amber crust, a centimetre thick, crazed into plates, the edges of the plates razor-fresh. You can kneel and lift a shard and hold it up to the amber lamp and see the light come through it the colour of weak tea. It is coolant. It has been sugar for a long time and now it is candy, and in another century it will be a floor of solid amber with a garden underneath it.

## Eleven Groves

The plants are not scattered evenly and they are not scattered randomly. They are scattered *hydraulically*. There are eleven leaks in Bay Six and there are eleven groves, and between the groves there is nothing at all — twenty paces, forty, sixty of bare hot concrete and rock dust and the clean swept emptiness of a place with no weather to move things around.

Each grove is riotous. Each grove is also alone.

They are white. Not green-white, not etiolated-white — genuinely, structurally white, the colour of a mushroom's gill, because they gave up chlorophyll generations ago and have had no reason to want it back. Their leaves are enormous and thin as cigarette paper, three metres across in the big specimens, translucent enough that the cherry glow of the return bend shines through them and shows the veins as a dark net. They have no bark. No thorn, no bristle, no bitter alkaloid, no hair, no wax. Nothing has ever eaten them. Nothing has ever needed to be discouraged. You can push your hand through a leaf like pushing it through wet tissue, and the plant does not react, because in the whole history of this lineage nothing has ever taken a bite.

Cut a stem and it weeps syrup, clear, faintly warm, and the syrup runs down and pools and in a month is glass.

They were a crop once. Somebody grew them for the long-chain resin in their rind — cable lacquer, gasket compound, something in a catalogue with a number instead of a name — and somebody engineered a metabolic bypass so they could be raised in the dark on the sweet water of the cooling loop, which was cheaper than light. Glycol is sugar with a wrongness in it. It kills anything with a liver. These were taught to drink it, and they taught themselves the rest.

## The Only Cold Thing

There is one cold object in Bay Six and it is the whole of the weather.

A make-up line comes down from an aquifer somewhere above and behind, sixty millimetres of steel at nine degrees, running the length of the hall at chest height. Everything else here is hot; this alone is cold, and so this alone sweats. Condensation gathers on its underside in beads, swells, hangs, and lets go. It has been doing this since before the roof fell. Beneath it runs a wet stripe on the concrete a hand's-breadth wide, dark, cool, and along that stripe the groves are thickest and tallest and lean in toward the pipe like people at a rail.

That is the water here: not a river, not a pool, but a stripe of damp and a slow drip and the memory of damp in the dust. There is a sump at the low end, a grating over blackness, and if you put your ear to the grating you can hear water perhaps four metres down, not moving.

Every eleven days a pump runs for ninety seconds.

You will not know when. There is a tick from a contactor, a sound like a knuckle on a desk, and then a deep shove of movement through the pipes, and the make-up line's flow reverses and slows and the cold goes out of it, and for two days afterward the pipe is warm and dry and the stripe beneath it fades to a shadow and then to nothing. The groves wait. Their leaves go slack at the edges. Then the cold comes back, and the first bead forms, and falls, and the whole hall's life resumes at the pace of a thing that has never once been in a hurry.

## What Is Missing

Sugar everywhere, and no ants.

That is the wrongness you feel before you can name it. A floor of syrup in the dark at blood heat should be crawling. It is not. There are no flies, no mites, no beetles in the amber, no woodlice under the fallen slab, no bats in the thirty metres of unseen roof. Nothing has a mouth here. The glycol that feeds the groves is a poison to every animal that ever lived, and the groves have made the whole room out of it. The air is sterile and sweet and the silence is not the silence of a quiet place but the silence of a place with no one in it to be quiet.

So the flowers do not open. They are pale, fist-sized, and cleistogamous — they swell, they fertilise themselves shut, they set seed inside their own closed petals, and they rot without ever having shown a colour. A few of the older plants still carry the ancestral reflex and crack open at maturity out of habit, and their pollen falls on the candy floor in a fine dust for absolutely no one, and lies there, and is eventually sealed under the next layer of glass.

The seeds travel by other means. Runners, mostly, feeling along the pipe lagging. Bulbils that drop into the drip and are carried a metre. And the gantry: an overhead crane on rails the length of the hall, still on its standby circuit, still executing a maintenance traverse so slowly that the motors are barely turning. Four metres a year. It carries a mat of root and seed on its underside from grove to grove, and it is the only pollinator this ecosystem has ever had, and it does not know.

## The Traces

A mess cabin by the door, one room, a cot made up with the blanket squared. Boots paired beneath it. A mug with a ring of dried syrup in the bottom, which is the same substance as the floor, which is the same substance as the leaves.

Enamel dials, all at rest. A stack of ceramic insulators like a pile of white bread rolls. Chalk tallies on a cabinet in groups of five, forty-one of them, and no way to know what was counted. A log book whose last entry is *No. 4 gland weeping again. Will do it Thursday.* On the inside of a locker door, in grease pencil, an arrow and the word **ORCHARD**, in the handwriting of somebody who found this funny.

Everything was shut down correctly. Valves closed in sequence, breakers racked out, the wheel of the door turned until it seated. Whatever ended the shift here was not a panic. It was an instruction, and it was followed, and nobody came back up the stairs to say otherwise.

## What Is Happening

Slowly, and in one direction.

The sugar does not rot; there is nothing to rot it. It dries, and crystallises, and cements. Each grove is candying its own root plate, glass creeping up the stems a millimetre a season, and the plants pry at it with new growth and tear themselves and weep more syrup, which sets, which they pry at. The amber is winning at the rate of a fingernail's growth. Meanwhile the fallen slab settles a millimetre a year and the fracture above it opens a hair, and the rock dust deepens, and somewhere behind the north wall the pump has ninety seconds left in it every eleven days and a finite number of eleven-days remaining.

Stand still in the middle of the bare stretch, equidistant from all eleven groves, in the hot dark, and you can hear the leaves growing. Not metaphorically. A creak, a papery click, the small tearing sound of a tissue unfolding, at intervals of a minute or so, from eleven directions. It is the loudest thing in Bay Six. It is the sound of something enormous and patient and defenceless getting very slightly larger in a room where nothing will ever come to eat it.
