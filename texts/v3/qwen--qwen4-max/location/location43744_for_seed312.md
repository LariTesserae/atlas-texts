## The Sill

From the lowlands you never see it. You see the cloud that stands on it — a white wall a hundred leagues long, motionless for weeks, spilling over one edge in slow cataracts that evaporate before they reach the plain. The people below call that spillage the milk-fall and use it to tell the season. They do not think of it as a place. It is a lid.

Climb the gorge stairs — nine thousand steps cut into a cleft still raw from the cutting, wet the whole way up, hung with ferns in the first thousand and with nothing at all after the fourth — and at the top the world stops falling and begins instead to extend. This is the Sill: a slab of lifted seabed, four thousand paces above the sea it used to be, wide enough that a bird flying north across it dies of old age before it finds the far mountains. It has its own weather because it is too large to have anyone else's. It makes a cloud each afternoon and dismantles it each night. It exhales cold down its gorges every dusk, a wind that has never once failed and has never once been strong. On the Sill, the horizon is not a curve of hills. It is a straight line with a slight haze on it, and the haze is distance, not mist.

The ground is limestone, pale grey going blue when wet, and it is full of shells. They are everywhere underfoot: coiled ones the size of a fist, ribbed fans, the fine hash of broken stems that makes the paving glitter after drizzle. In the older lanes the coils have been worn to ghosts by feet. In the fresh quarries they stand out sharp as cut glass, and the quarrymen work around the big ones out of habit rather than reverence, because a shell is a flaw and a flaw is where the frost gets in.

## Young stone

Everything here is new. The Sill came up out of the water recently — recently meaning that the ranges at its back have not yet learned to be rounded, that the rivers run straight as ruled lines and drop instead of meandering, that there are no floodplains anywhere, no old soils, no soft hills, nothing that has had time to be tired. The mountains stand in blades and pyramids. Their slopes are still the slopes of the break, not the slopes of the settle: angular scree, plates and shards lying at the exact angle of their falling, unmossed, unsorted, shifting under a boot with a sound like crockery.

Water is not what takes this country apart. Frost is. Every night the film of moisture in every seam freezes and jacks it a hair wider; every afternoon it thaws and runs deeper. The mountains are being prised open from inside, patiently, a hair at a time, and the evidence lies in the aprons of fresh splinters below every cliff and in the sound at dusk — a scattered ticking, cracking, the tick of a cooling stove, coming off the high faces for an hour after the sun leaves them.

The city quarries with the same trick. In the thaw seasons the stonecutters spend the afternoon drilling blind holes along a bedding plane with copper-tipped punches and a wooden mallet, and at dusk they pour meltwater into the holes from a skin, and go home. Before dawn they come back with wedges and levers and collect what the night has split for them: slabs already faced, already true, cold enough to burn the hand. They call it the evening pour. It is the only industry on the Sill that works while everyone sleeps, and it is why the whole city smells faintly of wet rock at breakfast.

## The damp band

The Sill is a cold desert. Its interior gets almost nothing: salt pans, gravel, a sky that stays hard blue for five months. But along its southern rim, where the lowland air is forced up three thousand paces of escarpment and dies of the climb, there is a band of moisture a few leagues wide and as long as the world. Not rain — rain is a rumour here, an afternoon drizzle that comes and goes in the time it takes to eat. This is condensation country. The cloud arrives as fog, sits, and leaves water on everything it touches.

So the city is a machine for catching film. Every roof is laid to a fall of one finger in twenty and lippped with a channel. Every wall carries a scraped stone gutter at its foot. On the roofs stand frames strung with felted hair, acres of them, ranked like drying laundry, and the fog combs through and beads and runs. Before light, children go out with bone scrapers and take the rime off the nets and the north walls and the standing stones, pushing the shavings into the gutters, and the gutters carry it to the cisterns. The scraping hour is the busiest hour and the quietest: ten thousand small spoons on ten thousand stones, a sound like sleet that never gets louder.

There are pools, too. The limestone is beginning to dissolve; sinkholes open in the terraces without warning, a pond where a byre was, and these hold water through the dry months on a plug of their own clay. They are green, shallow, fringed with black algal mats that stink in the sun and breed midges beyond counting. Every pond is common. Every pond has a name, a warden, and a rule about how many beasts may drink at once.

And the water, being born of dissolved seabed, is hard. It carries its stone with it and puts it down wherever it slows. Every channel grows a rind. Every gutter narrows a hair a year, every cistern lip thickens, every drip-point in a stairwell is building a small blunt column that will one day be a pillar. The city is being slowly turned into a cave by its own plumbing. There is a whole guild whose work is nothing but re-cutting the waterways open, and they are always behind, and everyone knows they will always be behind, and the water-rolls record which quarter's channels are due, in which decade, for the next hundred years.

## Inside

The city calls itself Inside. Everything else is the Open.

It is not one city and it is not many. It is a ribbon of construction along the damp band, a continuous fabric of wall touching wall, broken by courtyards and pond-hollows and quarry grounds but never by anything you could call countryside, running east and west past the point where names stop meaning anything. A courier can run relays for forty days along it and still be among people who use the same word for *threshold*. No one has stood at both ends. The far warmths are entries in the water-rolls and a particular accent that everyone can imitate badly.

It is built of the stone that came out from under it, and it is built without wood. There are no beams on the Sill. Rooms are corbelled, course oversailing course until they close, or vaulted, or roofed with the flat plates the frost gives free. The mortar is ash and puddled clay and sour whey from the cheese vats, and it is weak, so nothing is raised more than one course in a year — a law older than any of the courts that enforce it, and correct: a wall must live through a winter's freezing before it is asked to carry anything. A stair takes a lifetime. A cistern takes three. The masons' marks are hereditary and a man may spend his whole career on the second storey of a building his grandmother began, and this is not considered sad.

The walls are doubled, and the cavity between is packed with fleece, dung-ash, and the bones of everything eaten since the wall went up. The walls are full of last winter. In the oldest quarters, where a wall has been opened for repair, you can read the packing in layers like a midden: hair, ash, ribs, hair.

Three cities lie on top of each other. Below grade, the caves and the deep byres, warm as a body, ammoniac, lit by tallow. At grade, the lanes — a shoulder and a half wide, roofed over in places so that you walk a hundred paces in tunnel and come out into a courtyard the size of a bed. Above, the roofs: the real thoroughfare, the drying floors, the dew frames, the dung cakes slapped in mosaics on every south face, the looms, the children, the flags of felt marking whose water falls where. Ramps spiral up through the blocks wide enough for two beasts abreast, and the cattle go up them at evening and down at dawn, and the traffic of the city is mostly vertical and mostly hoofed.

Doors are hide curtains. Where there is a timber lintel it has a name and an owner and a line of inheritance, and it came from a willow grove in a sinkhole three days away, and it is worth more than the room.

## The warm

The heat is animals. This is the first thing to understand about Inside and the last thing an outsider believes.

The herds are shaggy, black-brown, small-horned, patient, and enormous in number — beasts bred to give milk at four thousand paces on lichen and pond-hay and a fist of bitter grain. They stand in the under-byres shoulder to shoulder, and their bodies heat the stone, and the stone holds it, and the rooms above sit at the temperature of a mild autumn while the lanes outside are glazed with ice. A household's wealth is counted in stalls. A person's address is the warmth they belong to. To be cast out is not to be sent away; it is to be put above ground.

They are not the only ones. The wall cavities are full of doves that come in at dusk through slots left for them and are harvested by children with a hooked wire. The turf roofs carry a nation of burrowing rodents whose whistling from the marmot colonies is the loudest thing on the Sill and serves as the city's alarm, its clock, and its weather service. Dogs lie in every lane, thick, slow, bred for four hundred generations not to bark; they growl like a draught under a door. Choughs work the updraught at the Lip in flocks of hundreds, dropping and catching. On the cliffs above the eastern quarters a cat the colour of wet ash takes a beast a week and is neither hunted nor cursed, only accounted for. And in the ponds the midges rise in columns, and the swifts come into the columns, and in the six weeks of the rise the city eats fly-bread — pressed cakes of insects, dark, oily, faintly sweet, the only food here that does not come from an udder or a caravan.

At the bone fields beyond the last wall, the bearded vultures drop femurs onto a chosen pavement of flat rocks until they crack, and the people go out afterwards and gather the marrow and the splinters, because a splinter of long bone is a needle and a needle is how everything gets made.

## What grows

Not much, and slowly. Cushion plants the colour of old moss, hard as a doorstep, spreading a finger's width in a decade; a plant the size of a cartwheel is a thousand years old and has a name and a boundary right attached to it. Lichen on the north walls, farmed — the dye walls are reserved and cropped once in a lifetime, and the cropping is a funeral-grade event attended by everyone descended from whoever planted nothing, because nobody plants lichen; you only wait for it. Willow in the sinkholes, ankle-high, ancient, coppiced on a forty-year rotation, giving withes and spindle shafts and the occasional lintel. Hay from the pond margins. Soil itself is manufactured — dung, ash, bone-meal, quarry dust, turned for years in walled beds a hand deep — and it is inherited, carted, dowered, and stolen.

There is no forest. There is a place three days east where the willows come up to your knee, and people go there once, to see it.

## Quiet

The air is thin and carries sound badly; the felt soles, the packed walls, the dung-ash, the wool on every surface eat what's left. But the quiet is also law, and the law is physics. The city sits under slopes that have not finished falling. Fresh scree, unconsolidated cornices, blades of mountain still being prised apart every night. A shout in the wrong lane at the wrong hour of the thaw can bring a quarter's worth of hillside down onto a quarter's worth of houses.

So: no bells, or bells with their clappers wrapped in fleece so they murmur. No shouting; the stone voice, low and close, taught before walking. No one goes above the wall in the afternoon. The quarrymen work at dawn when the freeze has locked everything. There is a hush at midday in the thaw months when even the looms stop, and a million people sit still in the blue haze of dung smoke while the mountain above them drips.

What you hear instead, all day, everywhere, is spinning. Every hand not doing something else is turning a drop spindle, and the sound of it is a soft continuous seethe, like sand shifting, and it is so constant that people from Inside cannot sleep in the lowlands for the absence of it. Under that: the herd breathing, a tide with no shore. Under that, at dusk, the stone ticking as it cools.

## The slow

Nothing here hurries, including the ground.

The Lip is retreating. The gorges are eating back into the rim a pace or two in a lifetime, and every few centuries an outer quarter goes — first its cisterns fail, then its lanes are closed, then one night a length of the city the size of a district leans out and is gone into the white. Meanwhile the ice at the back is withdrawing, leaving raw moraine that the city colonises at the rate of one warmth per hundred years. Between the two, Inside moves: uphill, inland, a settlement migrating at the speed of geology, its abandoned outer rooms standing empty and frost-split at the edge with the shells still crisp in their thresholds, then not standing.

The light is ordinary. A hard clean sun, black shadows, an afternoon cloud built out of nothing and taken down again by evening, ordinary stars in an ordinary sky, and no sign anywhere that the world is anything but the world. Only the scale is wrong, and the patience.

At night the fog comes up the escarpment and lies in the lanes to the depth of a knee, and drains slowly downhill along them, and the roof-walks are clear above it. The byres breathe. The tallow burns low. Somewhere a mason's daughter is asleep in a room whose second storey will be finished by her son. And out on the terraces, in a thousand drilled holes, the day's water is quietly freezing, doing the only fast work on the Sill, splitting next year's stone.
