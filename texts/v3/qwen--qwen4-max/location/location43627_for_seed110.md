# The Almost

## Approach

The mountain is new. Not new as mountains go — new as a wound is new. Its slopes are all first edges: blocks quarried by frost and dropped where they fell, unsorted, unrounded, sharp enough to open a palm. There is no soil yet. There is no gully yet. Rain that lands here does not run in channels because channels have not been invented; it sits in the flat tops of broken slabs and evaporates and comes again, and the rock beneath it is exactly as it was.

At two-thirds of the way up, above the last crooked shrubs, in a snowfield that should be unbroken, there is a hole.

You find it by smell before anything else — wet mineral, warm bread, a faint iron sweetness like the inside of a mouth. Then by the ground: the snow goes soft, then granular, then absent, in a ring some four paces across, and at the center of that ring is a black slot in the rock with a collar of moss around it, one hand wide, the only green for a thousand paces in any direction. The moss is warm. Put your face near the slot and the mountain breathes on you, blood-warm and saturated, and your eyebrows bead.

The people who come here come at night, and slowly, and without lamps. They sit outside the slot for three hours first, in the cold, letting their eyes open all the way. Then they go in.

Inside, they see nothing at all. This is not a failure of the ritual. This is the ritual.

## The Room

It is small. Smaller than the mountain has any right to contain: a blister in the young rock, a bubble caught in a flow that never quite finished cooling. Nine paces by five at the floor, three body-heights at the crown, and most of it lower than that — a place of stooping, of threading, of going on the belly with your cheek against wet stone. You could carry the whole of it out in a lifetime of hands.

And it is *full*.

Three hundred and eleven basins. That is the count; it is taken by touch, once a generation, rim by rim, and it has never come out otherwise. They are packed like the cells of a comb, like foam that has decided to stay foam: each a shallow bowl of pale flowstone with a raised lip, each lip near-touching its neighbors, some fused, some separated by a gap you could slide a finger through and no more. Between and above them hang the buds — hundreds of them, thumb-length, blunt, wet, the beginnings of stalactites that have been beginning for longer than there have been people to be frightened of them. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here has been allowed to be.

Walking is not possible. You move along the rims, feet placed like a heron's, or you crawl the two soft lanes the tenders keep clear, or you go by the cord — a grass-fiber line, knotted at intervals, strung from peg to peg through the whole chamber, so that a blind body can find the ninth basin, the shoulder-place, the throat, the deep bowl where the water arrives. The cord is the only map. It is also the only thing in the room that has ever been finished, and the tenders are uneasy about it.

The air is at dew point everywhere and always. Your skin is wet within ten breaths. After an hour you cannot reliably say where your body stops: the stone is blood-warm, the water is blood-warm, the air is blood-warm and thick as broth, and a hand laid flat on a rim disappears into the general warmth. People weep here without noticing, because weeping is not distinguishable from the ambient condition. It is considered normal. It is not grief.

## Water That Has Never Been Rain

It comes up. That is the first rule and the strangest.

The water in this room has never fallen from a sky. It rises through the floor at the deep bowl — juvenile water, first water, water that has been in the rock since the rock was a liquid and is only now getting out — and it is warm and heavy with dissolved stone, and it moves through the basins in a sequence too slow to watch, and then it leaves. Not downhill. There is no downhill here; there is no outlet, no sink, no stream. It leaves *upward*, as vapor, through the pores of the ceiling, at precisely the rate it arrives.

The ledger balances. It has always balanced. No basin has ever overflowed and no basin has ever gone dry. You may drink; the level does not fall. You may weep, or bleed, or pour in a skin of meltwater from outside; the level does not rise. The room accepts what you give and returns it to the mountain and remains exactly itself, and this is not magic anyone claims to understand. It is simply the arithmetic of a place that has no gradient anywhere in it — no uphill, no downhill, no before, no after. Warm everywhere. Wet everywhere. Same everywhere.

Because nothing runs, nothing cuts. There is no sand in the Almost. No silt, no gravel, no rounded anything. The lips of the basins are as sharp as the day the water first thought about making them. A flint blade left here does not dull. A footprint pressed into the soft flowstone at the throat stays pressed; there are prints there now, small and narrow, that were made by a woman eleven generations dead, and each of her descendants has learned the room partly by putting their own feet into hers.

The sound of it: a hum at the very floor of hearing, the arrival of the water, felt in the sternum more than heard. A tick, sometimes, from a bud that has released a drop — and the drop falls, and the pool it falls into does not ripple and does not rise, and the sound is small and dry and does not echo. The packed surfaces swallow echo. The room sounds no bigger than a cupboard, and this is a lie your ears tell you, and the tenders know it, and they still flinch when a companion speaks from six paces away and sounds like a whisper at the ear.

Otherwise: your pulse. Your breath. The wet click of your own mouth. Outside, muffled by a hundred feet of new rock, the wind on the snowfield, so far away it might be a memory of wind.

## What Lives Here

Not much, and all of it pale.

The stone is slick with a colorless film, thinner than a breath, that eats the minerals in the rising water — a living varnish, the actual base of everything. Under the fingers it is like the inside of a cheek. It is edible. It tastes of iron and salt and faintly of mushrooms, and the tenders scrape it with a spoon of antler and eat it while they work, and the antler does not wear down.

From the ceiling hang eleven root curtains. Eleven, counted, unchanged. They belong to the crooked shrubs a thousand paces downslope, which have found a crack and followed it and sent their fingers into the warm dark, and the fingers hang there drinking, hair-fine, white, reaching for a floor they will never touch. They have been an inch from the floor for as long as the cord has been strung.

At the throat, where a rumor of daylight once came in before the snowbank shifted, there is a patch of liverwort the size of a spread hand. It is not green. It has never had a reason to be.

There are seeds in the mud of the entry lane, blown in or carried in on someone's hem: hundreds of them, intact, unrotted, unsprouted, waiting with the terrible patience of everything here.

And there is the shrimp.

One. In the deep bowl, translucent, the length of a fingernail, with its dark gut visible through it like a thread of ink. It has been seen by every tender who has ever tended, and it is always the same size, and there has never been a second one. It is called by a word that means something like *the tenant*, or *the one who is also waiting*. If you cup it in your palm it does not struggle. Its legs move against your skin with a sensation so faint that the tenders use it as a test: if you can feel the tenant walking, you are calm enough to be in the room. Most people cannot feel it. Most people are sent back out.

Once, perhaps twice in a hundred years, someone reports a cricket at the throat, and the report is written into the cord as a new knot, and the knot is a scandal.

## The Crowd

Here is what the density actually is, when you have been in the dark long enough to stop inventing light.

The basins are not empty shapes. Each holds an almost. The water lays down its stone with a kind of stammering intention, and the results, under the hand, are unmistakable and never quite arrive: a shoulder. The ball of a hip. An ear, complete but for the fold. A stair tread with the next tread beginning above it. The keel of a bird's breast. A face — many faces, three-quarters emerged from the matrix, the eyes still under the stone, the mouth a suggestion the thumb keeps returning to.

They stand shoulder to shoulder, three hundred and eleven of them, wet and warm in the absolute dark, at every height from ankle to overhead, and they are all *nearly*. The room is a crowd. That is the only honest word. A crowd in a small warm room, packed close, patient past patience, held at the instant before the shoulder shrugs or the face turns.

Nothing is frightening about them. That is the part visitors are unprepared for. They are gentle. They are like a room full of sleepers who have not yet been given their names.

## Marks

Scattered among them, small and handmade and few:

A peg of juniper, driven into a seam, with the cord's third knot at it. A second peg. A third.

An antler pick, roughed out, never sharpened to its final edge, laid across two rims where it has lain since the woman with the narrow feet.

A ladder of four lashed sticks at the throat, where the floor drops.

Outside, in the melt-ring, a dry-laid hut the size of a curled body, and beside it a stack of unfired clay lamps that have never held oil and never will.

And on the rims, everywhere, at the height of a kneeling hand: the little clay figures. Animals nobody has seen. Boats. A woman with her arms crossed. A bowl within a bowl within a bowl. Hundreds of them, made by generations of tenders, pressed from the mud of the entry lane and set into the shallow water — *unfired, undried, forever soft*. They have no bottoms where they sit in the water. They slump a little and then stop slumping. They can be remade with a thumb. Some have been remade so often that they are less an object than a habit maintained by a family.

Nothing brought into the Almost may be finished. That is the whole of the craft: rough it out, set it down, leave it wet.

## The Rule About Light

They will tell you, if you ask, in the flat way people state the obvious: *light finishes things.*

A lamp in that room and the buds would lengthen. The lips would harden. The roots would reach the floor and, having reached it, would thicken and split the rim they touched. The faces would come the rest of the way out of the stone and be only faces, only stone, and then — this is the part they cannot say without their voices changing — then the water would begin to *go somewhere*, and going somewhere is the beginning of wearing away, and wearing away is the beginning of the end.

To be finished is to be able to die. The room is unborn, and therefore safe, and therefore has been safe for ten thousand years, and the tenders keep it that way: blind, soft-handed, eating the varnish, drinking from pools that do not fall, counting the basins and finding three hundred and eleven.

They do not name the mountain. You do not name a thing until it is done. They call the chamber, when they must call it anything, *the place that is not yet*, and they climb to it in the dark with their eyes wide open, and they sit inside it with their palms on a shoulder that has been about to be a shoulder since before the ice, and they feel the tenant walking, if they are calm enough, and they add nothing that will ever harden.

If you go, go at night. Leave the lamps in the hut. Find the cord.

And when your opened eyes begin at last to show you something — a glow, a shape, a pale face turning — understand that this is your own nerve talking to itself in the absence of the world, and that the tenders have a word for it, and the word means *the lying*, and that you should close your eyes and use your hands.
