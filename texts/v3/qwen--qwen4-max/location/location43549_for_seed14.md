Ninety meters down, the floor is a mosaic. Cobbles the size of fists and hearts, quartzite and black chert and banded gneiss, laid edge to edge across the whole visible world and beyond it — each stone touching four or five neighbors, wedged, settled, the joints between them so narrow you could not slide a knife blade in. There is no sand. Sand was taken away a long time ago. What remains is the fraction too heavy to leave: a lag, a residue, the plain's last possessions, arranged by weight and nothing else.

The stones are round. Not river-round anymore, though rivers made them — they have gone past that, into the high polish that comes only after the rounding is finished and the water keeps working anyway, out of habit, for another hundred thousand years. Liver-pink, smoke-grey, the milky translucent quartz with a bruise of iron at its center. Run a hand along them and they feel like soap, like the shoulders of something very old, and under the slickness there is a faint bloom, a living film one cell thick, that makes each stone slightly slippery and slightly warm-colored in a place with no warmth and almost no color.

This is the whole terrain. It extends three days in every direction at the pace of a slow swimmer, and it is nearly flat — flat in the way a table is flat, flat as an ending. The land here was worn down before it drowned. Three hundred million years of rain took the mountains apart and spread them into the sea, and what was left was this surface, the last state of a continent, and then the ice melted and the water rose and covered it, and the erosion did not stop. It only changed method. Now the stones grind each other.

---

The water is not empty. That is the first thing to understand about the density of this place: it is packed with matter and almost bare of company.

Above the pavement hangs a layer of suspended flour, ground rock so fine it will not settle while anything at all is moving, and something is always moving. Twenty meters of it, thirty in a bad week, a slow grey weather system with its own ceiling and its own horizon. Take a cup of it, let it stand a day, and you get a finger's depth of silt and a cup of water that was never really clear. Add to that the fall from above — the dead skins of diatoms, fecal pellets, molted shells, the shredded remains of a bloom that happened in sunlight two months ago and four hundred kilometers north. It comes down constantly, a snow with no season, and it does not accumulate, because the bottom will not let anything accumulate.

The cold is old and specific. It sits at one and eight-tenths degrees and varies by less than a tenth across a year. This water was last at the surface in a polar sea, in a winter nobody remembers, where it froze and rejected its salt and grew heavy and sank, and it has been crawling along the bottom ever since, decades in the dark, arriving here still holding the temperature of that particular February. Nothing warms it. Nothing here has ever been warm. The stones conduct the cold out of a hand faster than the water does, so the floor feels colder than the sea, which is a lie, but a consistent one.

Light comes down through the clear water above and stops at the top of the flour. So there is a ceiling — a grey lit undersurface, twenty meters up, glowing faintly like a sky made of dust, and it is the only source of illumination the plain has. In the calm weeks the layer thins and the ceiling lifts and brightens, and the stones below lie in a wan blue-grey non-light, enough to see your own fingers, enough to see the mosaic running away into dimness. When the storms come the layer thickens and the ceiling descends until it lies on the stones themselves, and then it is absolute black, a dark with no direction in it. The light of this place breathes on a cycle of about nine days, and nothing that lives here has ever seen the sun.

---

The quiet is enormous and it is not the quiet of nothing happening.

There is no air anywhere. Nothing is hollow. There are no shells but a few dead ones, no wood, no cavities, no overhangs, nothing that can ring or boom or resonate. The pavement is solid to its joints, and solid things packed tight damp each other instantly. So when a cobble rolls — and cobbles roll here all day, thousands of them, a slow creep of the whole floor — the sound is a single dull *tock*, felt more than heard, killed within a stone's width by the neighbors that receive it.

Underneath that, continuously, a hiss. Grain against grain, the sound of a hand pushed into a sack of rice, multiplied across a region and softened by distance into something like the noise inside a shell. It never stops. It is the sound of the plain being polished, and it is so constant that after an hour it becomes the definition of silence.

The storms are not loud in the ear. They are loud in the chest. A benthic storm arrives as a pressure gradient, a low grinding infrasonic shove that moves through the body and the sinuses and the water itself, and the ear reports almost nothing — a hush, a thickening of the hiss. Enormous energies, expressed as whisper. This is the acoustic rule of the place: work does not make noise here, because there is nothing to make noise with. Everything is full. Sound needs a gap, and there are no gaps.

---

And yet nothing is stable. That is the second thing to understand.

The tidal flow runs across the pavement and never quite stops, and twice a day it strengthens and the flour streams sideways in visible ribbons. Internal waves — waves on the boundary between the cold bottom water and the warmer layer above it, waves nobody at the surface would ever notice — come in from the shelf edge and break on the low scarp at the plain's northern side, and when they break they scour. Every week or ten days a surface storm far above couples down through the column and sets the whole nepheloid layer moving as a wall, a slow grey front forty meters tall advancing across the mosaic at walking pace, and behind its leading edge the cobbles begin to roll in earnest. Not slide. Roll, in a stuttering, jostling, grain-by-grain avalanche, a hundred thousand stones each moving a hand's breadth, the whole floor re-laying itself like a mosaic pulled apart and reassembled by someone in a hurry using exactly the same tiles.

Gravel megaripples migrate through the eastern sector, half a meter high, ten meters from crest to crest, marching a body-length a day. In the swale behind the scarp a patch of mud forms in the calm weeks, soft and grey and anomalous, and is erased in a single night. A channel that a river cut when this was land still runs across the plain as a shallow gravel train, a stripe of coarser stones, and in storms it works again, carrying sediment down a gradient toward a sea that no longer has a coast anywhere near it.

Everything moves. Nothing develops. The turbulence here is not creative; it is a shuffle. The plain has reached its final form and cannot get any flatter, and all this violence is only the maintenance of that form, performed at colossal expense, forever. Ten thousand years of storms have produced no feature, no deposit, no accumulation, no history. There is no stratigraphy here. There is a state, and the state is refreshed.

---

What lives here is scarce for exactly that reason. The mosaic offers no shelter — the joints are packed with flour, not open, so there is no crevice to hide in, no underside, no room. Anything that wants to attach must attach to a stone that will roll. Anything that wants to burrow must burrow into a floor that is regularly exhumed. So the plain is nearly empty of residents while being completely full of substance: a saturated room with nobody in it.

The vegetation is a film. Diatoms settle out of the falling snow and grow on the lit tops of the cobbles in the calm weeks, a meadow one cell thick and a hundred square kilometers wide, and it is the entire base of everything that happens here. It is stripped in a night, and begins again. At the top lip of the scarp, where the ceiling lifts highest and the light most often reaches, there is a scab of coralline algae, dull rose, the only real color for a day's travel — a few square meters of it, ancient, slow, growing a millimeter a year and being chipped back a millimeter a year. In autumn, kelp blades arrive from a coast sixty kilometers away, sunk and sodden, and lie in the swale for a week, and that week is the year's manna.

There are brittle stars in the two or three places where a boulder leaves a genuine void. There are amphipods in the flour, uncountable and invisible. There is a crab that crosses the whole plain in a season. Once or twice a year a large slow shark passes overhead, high in the clear water above the dust ceiling, and does not stop, and its passing is the biggest event of the month.

And there is one fish.

---

It is heavy-headed, thick-lipped, mottled exactly like the pavement, seventy centimeters of patience. It eats clams, and the clams here are strong, and its teeth are for crushing urchins, not for prying. So it does something else.

Among all these hundred thousand rounded stones there is one angular one: a block of green-grey gabbro, foreign to the region, rafted out here in a berg eleven thousand years ago and dropped when the ice let go. It was cracked by frost before it fell and it still has a corner. It is the only edge in the world.

The fish knows it. It brings a clam in its mouth, sets the shell against that corner, and strikes it sideways with a hard jerk of the head. Three knocks, a pause, two more. In this damped and hissing place the knocks carry a hundred meters, and they are the only sound in the region that means anything — the sole signal in an ocean of noise-without-content. It is tool use of the oldest kind: nothing is made, nothing is modified, nothing is left behind. The stone is chosen, not fashioned. When the fish is finished it swims off and the anvil is simply a stone again, indistinguishable in principle from every other stone, and within a season a storm will bury it or shift the mosaic around it and the fish will have to hunt for the corner all over again.

That is the only construction in three days' travel. It lasts about a minute. It happens perhaps twice a week. Nothing records it.

---

So the rules of the place are these. Everything is surface; nothing is container. Weight is the only right to remain. No arrangement survives the ninth day, so memory cannot be stored in the world — it can only be stored in shapes, in the roundness the rivers began, and in bodies, in a fish's habit of returning to a corner. The plain is saturated, ancient, turbulent, dark, cold, silent, and utterly without issue: an enormous labor that produces nothing but itself, again, at the same size, forever.

After the storm the flour settles out over four days. The ceiling rises. A grey light comes back down onto a mosaic that is new in every particular and identical in every respect. And on ten thousand stones, in the returning dimness, the film begins.
