## The Locule

Begin with the solid.

For miles in every direction there is no space at all. Not soil with air between its grains, not rubble with its gaps — tissue. A cold, close-packed vegetable mass, dark as the inside of a stone, laid down in layers so fine that a thumbnail's thickness holds four hundred years. It has no leaves. It has never had leaves. Above it, the plain runs flat and black under a sky that gives nothing, and the mass does not mind, because it does not eat light. It eats rock. Somewhere in its lower reaches, threads finer than hair work into the bedrock and take iron and sulfur out of it, grain by grain, at a rate that would bore a flame. The waste is a pale dust and a small warmth. That warmth is the only weather here.

The mass is not uniform. Once every very long while it fails to fill a space, and a bubble is left in it — a locule, a chamber sealed in the loaf of the world. Most are the size of a fist. A few, in the low country where the mass grew fastest and folded over itself, are the size of a valley turned upside down.

This is one of those. It is eleven miles along its long axis and perhaps a mile and a half from floor to ceiling, and it is shaped like an almond pressed flat: a lens, a held breath. Its ceiling is ribbed. Its walls curve in without a seam. Its floor is dunes.

And it is almost empty.

---

That is the first thing to understand about the city. Not its darkness — the emptiness. Walk out from the terraces and you can walk for a day and a half across open dust and meet nothing: no post, no wall, no marker. The chamber could hold a hundred cities and holds one, thinly. Structures stand at distances that seem, at first, like mistakes. A tower here. A cluster of halls two hours east. A single freestanding stair, going up eleven steps and stopping, worn to softness, standing in the middle of nowhere because the nowhere around it used to be somewhere.

The dust is why. It falls constantly from the ceiling — the mass sheds its old inner skin in flakes that break to powder on the way down — and it has been falling since before there was anyone to notice. It flows. It has dunes that migrate a hand's width in a year, and slumps, and slow avalanches that take a week to finish and sound like a held sigh. It buries the low districts and uncovers them again on a cycle of nine hundred years. Nothing here is lost. It is only under the dust, waiting for the dust to move on.

Nothing is wet, either. There is no water in the locule and never has been. What moves through the mass's conduits is a cold heavy oil, non-volatile, faintly sweet, the consistency of thin honey at these temperatures. It does not evaporate. It does not bead. It coats. A vessel of it left open for a century holds exactly as much as it held on the first day. Things here do not rot; they dry, and stiffen, and eventually become furniture.

---

The city occupies the eastern third, where the wall comes down to meet the floor in a long warm slope. Warm is relative: the air sits at nine or ten degrees, steady to within a degree across a human lifetime, and the wall in the eating-places runs three degrees above that. Those patches are the hearths. There is no fire in this world and cannot be — the air is thin in the stuff that flames require, and a burning wick pinches out before it can char. No lamps, no kilns, no forge, no cooking. It follows that no one here has ever seen anything, and that no one here has ever wanted to. There are no eyes. There is no word for seeing. The word that does the work of *know* means, approximately, *to ring*.

They build, therefore, in a medium they can inhabit.

The terraces are cut and grown out of the wall itself: half quarried, half persuaded. The mass will thicken where it is scored, will heal a wound into a bulge, and the builders learned this so long ago that the technique has no inventor. A wall is begun by wounding it in a line. Twenty years later there is a rib. Forty years later the rib is a beam. The people do not consider this slow. They consider it the ordinary pace of doing anything.

Above the terraces, the older city climbs in layers — nine recognised levels, though the top three are more scaffolding than street, a lattice of cordage and cured struts lashed to the ceiling ribs. Everything is joined by lashing and pegging and a mastic of oil and dust that cures over a decade to something like horn. The tools are stone: adzes of a green rock traded down from the upper conduits, thorn-hard awls grown to specification in nursery beds, scrapers, wedges, drills worked by two people alternating for a day to make a hole the depth of a hand. There is copper, cold-hammered from native grains picked out of the eating-faces, and it is used only for beads and for the tiny tongues inside the message-cords' resonators, because it is soft and sings.

Every surface is rounded. This is the thing that makes the city look, if it could be looked at, less built than *finished*. Steps are dished into shallow scoops by feet. Handrails are waisted where hands go. Doorframes have the soft hourglass of a thing worn from both sides. Corners have gone. Arrises have gone. The toolmarks of the founding work were erased before the founding was remembered. The city is at the shape that dust and use make, and it has been at that shape for a very long time, and it does not build new districts anymore. It maintains. It re-wounds the walls to keep the beams thickening. It sweeps the dunes back from the doorways with long fibre brooms, in shifts, forever.

---

The citizens are not animals. There are no animals. Nothing here has ever had a mouth that ate another living thing.

They are the mass's hands: motile buds, grown out of the nursery walls in the warm places, detached at about the size of a seated child, walking on four to six limbs and holding two more free. Bark-cuticle over a fibrous flesh, a hollow thorax like a gourd, and across the chest a set of dry reeds through which they speak by pushing air. They take oil at the conduits and dust at the eating-faces and they graze the walls — which is to say the city eats its own architecture, and the architecture heals, and the balance between the grazing and the healing is the whole of politics.

They have listening pits where a face would be: three on each side, membrane-lined, tuned to different bands. They read the room by clicking, a hard tongue-snap, and hearing the shape come back. A skilled walker can cross an unfamiliar hall at a run. A child learns the trick at four and is insufferable about it at six.

And they stiffen. It comes to all of them, over the last third of a life: the joints lay down lignin, the flex goes, the limbs set. There is no horror in it. It is the industry. An elder who feels the stiffening coming walks to the works and is set where a column is wanted, feet in a socket, hands raised to the load, and over forty or sixty years becomes the column. Their listening pits close last. Some of them stay responsive long after they can move — a load-bearing grandmother who can still be asked, in the tap-code, whether the beam above her has cracked, and who answers, and is right.

Half the pillars in the Lower Fifth are people. The city is held up by its dead, and by its nearly-dead, and the census and the structural survey are the same document.

---

It is loud here. Not occasionally. Continuously, at a level that would exhaust a visitor from any world with weather.

Underneath everything is the drone: the mass's own note, the sound of ten thousand square miles of tissue taking rock apart. It has been dropping in pitch since the chamber closed. It is now below hearing. What reaches the pits is its fourth and fifth overtones, a slow braided hum you feel in the floor and the teeth, and it is the calendar. The pitch-fall is measurable across a generation. Histories are dated by interval: *in the year the drone was a whole tone above the Ninth Terrace*. Children are taught the old chords and cannot sing them low enough.

Over that: the cords. The city is strung with tens of thousands of taut fibre lines, trunk routes as thick as a wrist running miles between districts, spurs to every doorway. They are the infrastructure — message, alarm, census, gossip, prayer — tapped with little copper tongues, and they hum in the intake wind, and they are never all quiet. A trunk line under load sounds like a bow drawn across a bridge. Ten thousand of them sound like a city.

Over that: the intake. The locule breathes on a cycle of about eleven years, drawing down through the ceiling pores and pushing back, and during the in-draw the whole chamber rises a fifth and the dust lifts and every cord in the world goes taut and sharp. It lasts two years. Then nine years of slack.

And over that, the criers, the click-chorus of people going about their business, the brooms, the drills, the wall-gnawing, the long granular hiss of dust coming down.

The one thing they cannot bear is a soft room. A chamber lined in felt — and they can make felt, from the ceiling-flake — swallows the click and gives nothing back, and to stand in it is to be nowhere, to have no shape, to be unmade. Their prisons are felted. So are their tombs, for the ones who stiffened badly and are not wanted in the structure. The worst sentence is not death. It is nine years in a room that does not answer.

---

There are other locules. Everyone assumes it. Three times in recorded history a shaft has been bored toward a faint rhythmic tapping in the western wall, and each time the crews worked for generations through solid tissue and found nothing, and one of them broke into a dead chamber the size of a house, packed floor to ceiling with dust that had been falling since before the mass had a name.

They plugged it and tuned the plug and left it. It has a note. On the equinox of the intake, when the pressure is right, the whole city goes to the western terraces and stands in the dark and listens to the sealed room sound its one low tone, and the children ask who is in there, and the answer is the correct one, which is: *nobody, and it is still worth hearing.*

The drone will keep falling. In eleven or twelve thousand years the overtones will go under too, and the cords will hum in a silence with no floor to it, and the city will have to invent a new way to know what year it is.

There is a committee. It has been sitting for six hundred years. It is in no hurry.
