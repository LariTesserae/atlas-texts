You hear it before you find it, and you find it by putting your hand into a stream.

The country above is bare rock, grey and rounded, wet from a rain that has just stopped or is about to start — the distinction doesn't matter here, it never has. Water runs off the stone in sheets a fingernail deep and gathers in the seams. There is no moss in the seams. There is no green anywhere, and there never was; the runoff carries no pollen, no leaf-rot, no smell of soil, because there is no soil. It carries grit and dissolved carbonate and a faint taste like a coin held under the tongue. Where the seams meet, the water goes down. One of them, no wider than a shoulder, is polished to a slick shine that no other seam has, and out of it comes a sound like a crowd.

Twenty feet of crawling. The rock is cool — eleven degrees, perhaps, and unvarying — and wet in the way that makes you unable to tell your own sweat from the stone's. Then the fissure opens, and the sound stops being a crowd and becomes something much stranger, and you are standing at the lip of a room.

The first guess is a waterfall. There is no waterfall. There is no single loud thing at all.

---

The chamber is about forty paces long and half that across, and it is not a cave. Caves are irregular. This is a *mold*: the negative shape of a crystal that grew here, once, in the soft grey host-rock, and then dissolved and left. Six facets, striated with growth-lines fine as combed hair. A pyramidal termination at each end. Along the ceiling runs a straight seam where the crystal twinned, a mirror-plane, so that the room is doubled — two halves joined at an angle, each the other's answer. Nothing about it looks excavated. It looks *cast*, and the thing it was cast from is gone, and has been gone long enough that the walls have softened, the striations rounded to the barest ripple, the edges of the facets blunted like the edges of a very old coin.

Water weeps from every facet. Not springs — sweat. Films a hair thick, sliding down the growth-lines, gathering into threads at the intersections, letting go in drops. The air is at saturation and has been for a very long time; on the colder faces it condenses outright and runs. The floor is not a floor but a keel, two facets meeting in a shallow V, and along the V a stream the width of your forearm moves toward a throat at the low end and leaves. There is a mark cut into the wall above the water — a single chisel-stroke, deliberate, ancient. The water touches it. The water has always touched it. Inflow equals outflow to the hair, and the level does not rise in rain or fall in drought, because the rock above buffers a year of weather into a constant seep.

And every surface, everywhere, is occupied.

---

The city is cut into the facets. Cells — ten thousand of them, more, nobody has finished counting — each no larger than a bread oven, each with a squared mouth and a lip worn concave, honeycombed into the stone so densely that the partitions between them are a finger thick. Tier above tier above tier, from the keel to the twin-seam, near-touching, layered, with no boulevard and no square and no gap you could stand up in. Access is by handholds chipped in the intervening rock, by peg-ladders of bone driven into sockets, by knotted cord gone hard and glassy with lime. The ramps that spiral the facets are the width of one body; you climb them brushing both walls, wet on both sides, and the treads are dished by feet.

It is unmistakably a city — the infrastructure is everywhere, the density is urban, the intention is total — and it is entirely pocket-sized. There is no outskirts. There is no road in but the seam. From the surface, the whole polity, all its architecture and all its history, is a wet crack in a streambed.

The tools were stone. You can read the picks in the un-worn corners: a harder stone struck at a shallow angle, wedges, patience. There is a little copper, cold-hammered, thin as a leaf, used for nothing structural — small tabs pinned beside certain cell-mouths, green now, or rather blue-green, which is the closest thing to a color the place allows. There is no soot anywhere. No lamp-niche, no fire-cracked stone, no charcoal in the keel-silt. Nothing here has ever burned, because nothing here has ever grown wood.

Light comes down the twin-seam from somewhere very far up: a diffuse grey needle, no wider than a wrist, that never moves and never brightens. And the walls give off their own faint sheen, a cold blue-green from the films that live on them, enough to see the shape of your hand and not its color. You navigate mostly by sound and by touch. The stone is slick but not slimy in the high tiers, slimy in the low ones, and the difference is grazed, not grown.

---

Here is what the loudness is.

Each cell holds a bowl. Stone, hand-ground, thin-walled, sitting in a socket cut to receive it. A thread of water enters each cell from the growth-lines above, at a rate set by the geometry of the facet, and fills the bowl, and the bowl — mounted off-center, weighted, balanced on a bone pin — tips when full, and empties with a note, and rights itself, and begins again. Ten thousand bowls. Ten thousand periods, from a heartbeat to a quarter of an hour. Ten thousand pitches, because the bowls were ground to thickness deliberately, tested, thinned a scrape at a time.

There is nothing in the room that eats sound. No wood, no cloth, no earth, no foliage — only wet stone, which reflects, and the water-film, which couples everything to everything. The cavity has a Q like a struck bell. The result is not noise. It is a *chord*, enormous, sustained, saturated, dense beyond the ear's ability to separate it, with a bass under it from the throat where the stream leaves — a column of water falling into a pipe of rock, a note you feel in the sternum and the teeth before you hear it. It is loud enough that speech is pointless. People here, when there were people here, spoke by putting a mouth to an ear, or by consonants tapped against a collarbone.

And the two halves of the room, split by the twin-seam, are not tuned the same. One is a fifth above the other. The city answers itself across the ceiling, forever, in the same interval.

This is the thing you understand slowly, standing in the keel with cold water over your boots: it is not a ruin, and it is not a dwelling that happens to be musical. It is an instrument the size of a room, built as a city, by hand, over a span of time you cannot make yourself believe. They cut cells until the chord was right. Then they stopped.

---

Nothing changes.

That is not a mood; it is a measurement. The films on the walls — chemolithotroph veils, thin as breath, eating the iron and the sulfur in the seep — grow at exactly the rate the grazers crop them. In the high dry cells there is a grey crust like paper, a fungus-thing with no partner and no green, which has not extended its margin in living memory. The treads of the ramps are worn by feet and re-limed by the water at the same rate, so the dishes are the same depth they were a thousand years ago. The dissolution finished; there is no fresh break, no talus, no fall of rock, no new anything. The room reached its own state and stayed. Temperature holds to a tenth of a degree. The water is a hair colder than the air, always, so you cool slowly and completely and never stop cooling.

Sound is the only thing that moves, and sound is the weather. Speak, and you detune the room; the chord carries your voice around inside itself for hours, smeared and folded, before it lets it go. Strike a wall and the whole city flinches a semitone and recovers. Take a bowl away and there is a hole in the chord — an absence with a shape, audible, specific, permanent until you put it back. The place remembers by what's missing.

Fauna: eleven animals, as far as anyone has counted. Sheets of pale blind springtail-things grazing the low veils, moving like a slow tide across a hand's breadth of wall in an hour. A flatworm in the keel-silt, white, unhurried. Three or four cricket-analogs with no ears, listening through their legs, one per few hundred cells, each holding a territory of stone it never leaves. And the large one — a long-legged wet patient thing the size of a dog, which comes down the seam perhaps once a year, walks the ramps without dislodging anything, puts its head into a dozen cells, and goes. It has never been seen to eat. It may come to listen. It is not unreasonable to come here to listen.

The wear on the ramps is human-shaped. One circuit, one set of feet, one stride-length. It is fresh. It is never any fresher.

---

Stand in the keel a while. The water goes over your boots at the same speed it went over the boots of whoever cut the mark. Above you, tier on tier, ten thousand small rooms are saying one syllable, and the ceiling gives it back a fifth higher, and the throat underneath holds the floor of the note, and the light comes down the seam and goes nowhere, and nothing is coming, and nothing is ending.

You will get cold. You will not be able to hear yourself think, and after a while you will notice that you have stopped trying, and that this is the closest thing to peace the place offers: a loudness so complete and so settled that it functions as silence, in a city with one door, built by hand, tuned once, and finished.
