# The Lamp

## Above

From the moor there is nothing to see but a ring of wrong grass.

The plain here is low and hot — twelve paces above the sea, fen and oak-scrub, gorse, a summer that sits on the back of the neck. Cicadas make the whole afternoon ring like a struck bowl. And in the middle of a heath of brown bent-grass there is a patch the size of a cart, cool and green, starred with frost-saxifrage and three tufts of a sedge that has no business within eight hundred miles or two thousand feet of this place. In heatwaves the deer lie in it belly-down with their legs folded under them and their eyes half shut, and the flies leave them alone there, and this is the only sign of the city.

The cold does not spill out of the hole. It leaks, at one notch, a thread of it, no more than a hand's-breadth of drifting chill that lies along the ground and dies out four paces from the lip. The patch is where the thread lands. It has been the same size for longer than anyone has been counting, which is a very long time, because counting is the principal industry.

The mouth itself is under a raft of fallen limestone and a mat of ivy: a slot nine paces long and two wide, running north, so that the sun in its whole year never once puts a finger inside. From above it looks like a seam of white in the rock, the kind of thing you would step over.

## The Throat

You go down on a hair-rope through a flue, and for eleven body-lengths the world is loud.

This is the Throat, the one bare place, where the stone is too dry and too swept for the felt to take. Here the cicadas come down the shaft and hit the walls and come back, doubled, trebled, a racket like a market. Here your boot on the rock makes a noise that goes away from you and returns. Porters — the caste who go up, sunburnt, squinting, half-deaf, pitied — sit in the Throat on a bone seat worn into a hollow by ten thousand sittings, and listen to the world, and come down and report it in signs. *Rain in the west. A stag. Thunder. Nothing.* It is the town's only news and it never alters anything.

The Listener is a salaried office. It has been held by the same four families since the founding. What they mostly report is *nothing*, and the town pays them for it, because a thing that is always nothing still has to be checked.

At the top of the Throat, on the lip, stands the Scale: a balance beam of imported pine, arms of equal length, baskets of woven hair. Nothing comes down that is not matched by something going up. This is not a customs house. It is a law of physics that the town has decided to enforce.

Then the flue opens, and you fall out of noise.

## White

The chamber is a flask: narrow neck, wide belly, ninety paces across and forty high, and every surface of it is white. Gypsum in some bands, flowstone like poured candle-fat in others, and over the stone a lime plaster maintained by a guild whose entire purpose is reflectance.

Light comes in at the slot and does not stop coming in. It strikes the wall and goes to the other wall and to the floor and back to the ceiling and around again, hundreds of times, and each time the wall gives back nearly all of it. There is a thin fog at the top of the cold pool that scatters what is left. The result is the inside of a cloud: a brightness with no direction, no source, no hot spot, no gradient. It is the brightness of a snowfield under overcast, forty paces underground, in a hole in a hot plain.

You cannot find the mouth by looking for the bright place. Strangers turn round and round with their heads back and their mouths open, and the town's children watch them do it, and it is the closest thing the town has to theatre.

And there are no shadows. Not soft ones, not faint ones. Nothing here casts. A hand held up in front of a wall does not put a hand on the wall. Depth has to be read from overlap and texture, from the way felt-fingers crowd each other, from the known size of a doorway; infants learn it late and outsiders never learn it well, and the town's painting is all line and flat colour, gorgeous and slightly flat to a foreign eye, an art of edges. The word for *shadow* is a porter's loanword. Children draw people with a stain attached to their feet, following them, and adults find this genuinely upsetting in a way they cannot articulate.

## Hush

The walls are furred.

Hush-moss grows in fingers a hand long, packed shoulder to shoulder, each finger soft and spongy and slightly tapered, so that the whole chamber is lined with a hundred million small wedges. Under the moss is a sponge of tufa; under the tufa, mycelium like wet felt. A sound that goes into that does not come out. It goes in among the fingers and turns and turns and is turned into a very small quantity of heat.

So the town is silent — not empty of sound, but empty of *shared* sound. Everything is near-field. Within arm's reach there is plenty: breath, the wet click of a snail's tongue on stone, the tick of a loaded rope taking up slack, a knuckle, chewing, your own blood in your own ear. Two paces away, a shout is a puff of warm air on the cheek and nothing else. Six hundred and twelve people live packed into this bulb and each of them lives inside a private bubble of noise the size of a bed.

Speech, therefore, is a thing done at the distance of a hand on a shoulder, mouth to ear, breath more than tone — an intimacy, not a utility. Across the void they use the hands: a fast, huge, chest-height sign language, six hundred years old, with a vocabulary of eleven thousand shapes and a grammar of small movements of the shoulders. It is legible everywhere in the shadowless light. There is no corner where a hand can hide.

For broadcast there is the cord. Every bridge-rail, handline, hammock-sling and ladder in the town is spliced into one continuous system of hair-rope, tensioned, running through bone eyes, and a signal put in at the hub travels the whole city as a shiver. Three pulses: eat. A long slow tremor: lie down. A fast stutter that makes the teeth buzz: *breach*. And in the evenings, the rope-players: women and men who sit at the hub with a bone comb and play the cord like a harp you cannot hear, and the town lies in its cells with palms flat on the rail and the soles of the feet against the floor, and takes the music through the bones of the wrist and the ankle. It is the only music. It is very good. There are critics.

The felt has to be kept at exactly a hand's length. Longer, it mats and shines and begins to give sounds back; shorter, and stone shows through and rings. The Croppers walk the walls all their lives with antler shears, and behind them come the snails, and behind the snails the hush-mice with their furred feet and furred tails, taking the pupae out of the moss. Silence here is not a property of the rock. It is a public work, like a seawall.

## The Veil

The chamber holds winter.

Cold air is heavy, and the flask has no outlet, and the lip is a smooth rolled torus of stone that the wind cannot get a grip on — the shape the water spent a hundred thousand years finding, and having found, stopped. So the cold that fell in during some old cold decade is still lying there, forty paces deep, a lake of it, and above it the hot plain goes on being July.

Put your hand up through the Veil and it is warm. Put it down and it is cold. The boundary is that sharp. It sits at a fixed height, and along it, where the two airs mix, there is always a thin fog — a horizontal cloud, ceiling to the town, diffuser to the light, and the town's whole water supply.

They comb it. Nets of wool and hair and spider-silk gauze hang in the Veil on frames, and the fog condenses on the fibres and runs down into gutters of split pine, and the gutters run to cisterns, and the cisterns are the reason there is a city here. The yield is the same every hour. It has been the same every hour for as long as the tallies go back. Rain on the moor above arrives as a small quickening in the one thread of drip that falls in the Dish, and the counters log it, and the cisterns take it without changing level, and that is the entire weather of this place.

Everything is damp and nothing is wet. Ninety-five parts in a hundred of water in the air, two or three degrees of cold: nothing dries, ever, and nothing rots fast. Cloth is scraped, not washed. Food is not dried but fermented, and the fermentation is so slow that flavours take decades to arrive. There is no fire — see below — so there is no boiling, no baking, no smoking. The cuisine is curd, paste, pickle, pressed cake, sourness of great age and subtlety. In a wall-vat there is a mother-curd that is never emptied, only topped; the oldest molecules in it went in at the founding. People eat their ancestors, in a manner of speaking, and consider this ordinary.

## Six Hundred and Twelve

That is the town's name. It is a knot on a cord, and it is also the census, and the census is the name, and it does not change.

The walls of the bulb are scalloped — fossil dissolution scallops, hand-span to arm-span, the fluted pattern that flowing water leaves in limestone and that this water stopped leaving long ago. Every scallop is a room. They have been dressed, plastered, shuttered, subdivided, hung with hammocks, stacked three and four high in the bigger ones, and between them the remaining stone is furred and webbed and crawling. There is no unoccupied surface anywhere in the chamber. A cross-section of a wall would read: rock, tufa, mycelium, moss, snail, web, gnat, rope, felt, cell, child, moss again. The air is as packed as the walls — spores, gnats in slow columns, moths, the dust of snail-shell — and everyone carries a cough-cloth, and everyone's cough is their own business, because nobody else can hear it.

Streets are ledges a body wide, spiralling, with hair-rails; crossings are ladders and slung bridges; the traffic rule is that the descending give way, and it is enforced by touch, a hand on the hip, and by the fact that everyone can see everyone. Districts by trade and by height: the Combers up in the Veil, pale and rheumy; the Croppers on the mid-walls; the Limewashers everywhere and nowhere, working a rotating patch, keeping the albedo at its number; the middens in the deep wall-cavities, which are the only warm place in the city, fifty degrees of composting heat, rationed by the hour to infants and the very old. The Warm Room has a waiting list. It has had a waiting list for six hundred years.

Tools are antler, bone, gypsum scraped to an edge, shell, pine, felt, cord. Metal is one needle per household, cold-hammered copper, named, inherited, and its wear is recorded: the Weight keeps a line for every needle in the city, and when a point loses a grain of copper the porters must bring back a grain of copper, and the needle's owner is spoken to.

## The Weight

The ledger is the constitution.

The chamber is a closed thing. Its water is saturated — full of stone, able to dissolve no more — which is why the walls are finished, why the scallops are fossils, why nothing here erodes or grows or slumps. The drip puts back exactly what it took, somewhere else. The town, having looked at this, decided to be the same.

So: mass in equals mass out, measured on the Scale. A birth requires a death, and the Counters keep the queue, and it is not a cruel office but it is not a kind one either. A new room requires an abandoned room. A new tool requires an old tool broken and its substance carried over. The dead go into the middens and come back as heat and then as curd and then as people. Nothing is thrown away because there is no away; a crumb dropped on a ledge stays on the ledge until a woodlouse finds it, and the woodlouse is in the ledger too, on a summary line.

And no fire. Fire eats the cold pool, warms the air, lifts the Veil, moves the fog, and — worse, unforgivably — puts soot on the white. A single smoky lamp would take a hand's-breadth of wall out of the commons of brightness for a generation. The taboo is absolute and it is not superstition; every child can explain the arithmetic. They are a people who live inside a lantern and have agreed never to strike a light.

Four constants are maintained, by guild, by law, by hand, without pause: the White, the Hush, the Cold, the Number. This is the whole of politics. There are factions about the depth of the felt. There have been schisms about the depth of the felt.

## Living

Because the room eats echoes, nothing here hunts by sound. Bats came once and went away starving, calling into a wall that swallowed them. So the moths have no ears — no ears, no jinking dive, no fear — and they fly straight and slow and are caught out of the air by children with two fingers, and their silk goes into the finest cords. There are no crickets' chirps: the cricket here has legs like glass pins and a stridulation apparatus gone soft and vestigial, a useless ornament on a mute insect. Spiders build in every corner and are farmed; the gauze of the fog-nets is spider-silk, and so are the wound-dressings, and the harvestmen cluster in the scallop-corners in knots the size of a fist, twenty legs to a knot, and nobody disturbs them.

Because the room is bright, nothing here is blind. This is the strange thing that visitors never get over: a cave fauna with eyes. Big eyes, pale bodies, banded shells, patterns — the lamp-snails with their five whorls of grey and cream, grazed in paddocks on the Dish; the eyed newt in the basin, which watches you; the hush-mouse, which watches you longer. Snow-fleas in their billions in the moss, swept and pressed into flour. Fungus gnats in slow towers. Centipedes under the ledges, harvested for their poison, which is used in medicine and in one kind of argument.

Above, on the rim, nests the fell-bird: fringed wings, no noise at all, a nightjar-thing that comes down the Throat and hunts *by sight* in a bright cave in the middle of the night, which is not a thing that ought to exist. It has a ledger line. Its pellets are collected for bone and its dung for the Warm Room, and it is, in the town's own accounting, a citizen.

And on the walls, always in flower, no season, no closing: the frost-saxifrage, the cold-flower, white and star-shaped and faintly bitter, which the porters carry up and sell to fever-houses in the hot cities of the plain for its weight in copper. Filmy ferns a single cell thick, which could not live one hour in the dry world above, hang in the damp everywhere like green breath on glass.

## The Dish

The floor is kept clear. It is a smooth white saucer of flowstone, the brightest surface in the world, and on it are the snail paddocks, the moss lawns cropped to a fixed nap, the basin, and the two instruments.

The basin is the size of a bathtub and holds the town's only standing water. One thread of drip falls into it, at intervals so regular that the counters use it as a clock and then complain that it is useless as news. Lie down beside it with your ear a hand from the surface and you can hear it: the only public sound in the city, and you have to go and put your head on the floor to get it. People do. It is what one does here instead of looking at the sea.

Beside the basin stands the Slow Hand: a stalagmite the height of a child, wet, faintly translucent, growing one hair's breadth in a decade. It is ruled with fine lines. A whole life is a mark thinner than a fingernail. It is the only thing in the chamber that is permitted to change, and the Reckoners measure it with a gauge of hair and bronze, and record the measurement, and the measurement is always the same, and once in a very long while it is not, and then the town talks about nothing else for a year.

And in a nook at the base of the north wall, where a fissure breathes cold, there is a lobe of ice the size of a cupped hand. The Ice Ear. It does not grow and it does not melt. It is gauged every day. It is the town's alarm for the world: if the Ear ever changes, then something out there has changed, and the Six Hundred and Twelve will have to decide what to do, and they have discussed this at length, and they have no plan.

## Night

There isn't one, in any useful sense.

When the sky above goes dark, the slot stops giving and the walls take over: the mycelium under the felt glows, a steady cold green, and the fog holds it and throws it around the bulb the way the walls threw the daylight, and the chamber comes up to very nearly the same brightness in a different colour. The transition takes hours and cannot be perceived. Nobody in the town has ever seen it get dark. Nobody has ever seen a shadow. The two facts sit together in their language, in a single word that means *the thing that follows you and that we do not have*.

Sleep is announced by the cord: a long tremor from the hub, running out along every rail in the city, into six hundred and twelve pairs of palms. The town lies down in its scallops, in the felt, in the green white, with the moths going straight past their faces and the snails ticking on the stone and the drop falling into the basin where nobody is lying to hear it, and above them the fog combs itself into water, and above that the deer get up out of the cold patch and go back into the loud hot night.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing wears away. The needles lose their grains of copper and the porters bring the grains back. The moss is cut to a hand's length and grows a hand's length and is cut. The curd is topped and eaten and topped. The number is the number.

It is not a dead place. It is an enormously busy one — every hand in it working, all the time, at holding a thing still.
