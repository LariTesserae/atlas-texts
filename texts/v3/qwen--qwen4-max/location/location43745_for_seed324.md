## The Stilling House

Before there is anything to see, there is the sound, and the sound is not a sound. It arrives as pressure on the front of the body, a hand laid flat against the sternum and left there. At the threshold of the adit it is already 112 decibels; forty meters in, where the walkway cantilevers over the pool, it is a hundred and eighteen and the number stops meaning anything. The ear gives up its job. What is left is a kind of weather in the bones: the sinuses buzz, the fillings ache, the fluid of the eye trembles enough to make the lamps smear. Sound here has a spectrum the way a slope has strata. Underneath everything, the low fundamental of the falling jet, somewhere near thirty hertz, felt in the belly and the bowels. Above that the broadband roar of impact, white noise in the literal sense, water being torn into air. Above that, when a gate is throttled to a partial opening, the frying-gravel crackle of cavitation, bubbles collapsing against concrete at pressures that pull grains out of the cement paste one at a time. And threaded through it, thin and almost pretty, the ringing of the handrail — a hollow galvanized tube that has found its resonance and holds a single pure note like a struck glass, forever, as long as the river runs.

You cannot speak. You write on a clipboard whose paper has gone soft as cloth, and the pencil tears it. Mostly you point.

---

The place is a room. It takes a while to accept this, because the scale of the noise suggests a canyon, and the fog suggests a valley in autumn, and the cold suggests altitude, and all three are true, but the whole of it — every cliff, every shore, every horizon — is contained in a poured concrete box forty-one meters long, nineteen wide, eleven to the soffit of the roof. There is no natural substrate anywhere in it. Not behind the walls, where the mountain has been drilled and injected with grout until the fissures are filled and the rock itself has become a kind of masonry; not under the floor, where a slab a meter and a half thick sits on a drainage blanket of screened gravel that was trucked up the access road in the year the valley was closed. Even the stone that the water chews is made. This is the stilling house at the toe of the Bise 2 dam, elevation 2,340 meters, commissioned eleven years ago, and it is the youngest landscape in the massif.

The fog is real weather. The jet enters through the roof at the upstream end — a rectangular outlet, three meters by two, angled down — and falls nine meters into the plunge pool, and in falling it drags air with it, thousands of cubic meters a minute, entrained at the surface and carried down and released as a boiling white dome. The room's atmosphere is the exhaust of that process: a saturated mist at seven degrees, moving. There is wind here. Stand at the rail and the wind is a steady twenty knots off the impact zone, wet, cold enough to take the use out of your fingers in four minutes. Stand at the downstream end near the inspection slot and the wind reverses, because the room breathes in a slow circuit, air pulled down the adit and pushed out the tailrace, and the fog rolls along the ceiling in a layer with a visible underside, like a stratocumulus deck seen from a summit. When the outside temperature drops at dusk, the roof beams — precast, prestressed, laid side by side with sealed joints — go below dew point all at once and it rains indoors, a fine even rain across the whole forty-one meters, for about twenty minutes, every clear evening.

Everything is wet and nothing is submerged. The walls weep at the construction joints, where the lift lines of the pour let groundwater through at a rate the designers considered acceptable: a bead every twenty centimeters, gathering, running, gathering again. The weepings have begun to build. In eleven years the calcite has grown into pale straw-colored stalactites no longer than a fingernail, translucent, soft as candle wax, hanging in ranks along the lift lines like the beginnings of a limestone cave that will take four hundred years to arrive. Below them the wall is glazed with flowstone the color of weak tea. There is a bathtub ring around the whole chamber at the mean tailwater level, a band of mineral crust twenty centimeters deep, and above it a dry grey margin, and above that the black-green line of the only life in the place.

---

The terrain is under the water and you are not meant to see it. But the pool is clear at the edges, in the slack corners where the boil doesn't reach, and the light is just enough.

Nine baffle blocks stand on the floor in two staggered rows: truncated pyramids of reinforced concrete, three meters on a side at the base, two and a half tall. They are buttes. They have the shape of buttes because the water has been at them for eleven years, and the upstream faces are rounded now, polished, the cement paste gone and the aggregate standing proud — quartzite pebbles from the batching plant's own quarry, each one a small shining knob. Their downstream faces are still sharp. The arrises of the far row still carry the grain of the form boards, the wood's summerwood ridges printed into the skin of the concrete, and the conical craters where the form ties were snapped off are unbroken, crisp as fresh craters. That is the age of this landscape. It has not yet lost its tool marks.

Between the blocks the floor is a scoured country. There is a hollow below the jet's impact point that was not designed, a shallow elliptical dish worn two hundred millimeters into the slab, and in it a dozen rounded stones — cobbles that came over the spillway in a flood year and now live there, and are picked up by the roller and rolled and dropped and picked up again, so that the dish deepens by a few millimeters a year and the cobbles grow smaller and are, in the language of the inspection reports, *abrasion media*. They are the only rivers this river has. Downstream of the last baffle row the floor rises to the end sill, a continuous ridge across the full width, and over that ridge the water leaves through the tailrace slot into the open valley, and the noise begins to change character, and you know you are near the door.

The strata are visible in the walls. Each pour was a meter and a half of height, and each left a line, and the lines run around the room like bedding planes, slightly offset where the forms shifted, darker where the vibration was poor and the paste is honeycombed. There is one cold joint at the eleven-meter mark where a pour was interrupted by a pump failure in the second summer; it runs the whole perimeter, a thin discontinuity, an unconformity in a geology that is eleven years old and entirely documented. Someone's initials are scratched in the green concrete beside it, and a date.

---

Light is 0.9 and most of it is not electric. There are nine caged lamps on the walls, sodium, forty meters of chamber between the first and last, so the spacing is enormous and the pools of orange light barely overlap; between them the concrete goes a colorless grey-blue that the eye invents. The lamps are the sparse presences in a place with a great deal of emptiness: a lamp, then thirty square meters of nothing, then a ladder, then nothing, then the sump cabinet, then nothing. The brightest object in the room is the water. Aerated water is forty percent air and it scatters everything offered to it, and the boil glows a dull luminous white under the roof, throwing light *upward*, so that the undersides of the roof beams are lit and their tops are lost. It is the only white thing here. It looks like a light source. It looks like a hole in the floor with the sky underneath.

At the far end, the inspection slot: a rectangle of true daylight a meter wide and four tall, grilled against birds, and through it a slice of the valley — grey scree, a snow patch, the underside of cloud. That rectangle is the second-brightest thing, and it is blue where the lamps are orange, and where the two lights meet on the wall there is a boundary you can put your hand across.

Nothing lives here, or almost. Above the spray line, on the dry margin where the lamps reach, there are films: a black cyanobacterial crust in the joints, dry and crisp as burnt paper; a green algal bloom in a hand's-breadth patch where a lamp's warmth and its photons coincide; three cushions of moss in the weep channels, fed by calcite water, pale and etiolated. At the slot, where the outside world touches the inside, two ferns have established in a crack packed with windblown dust, and a lichen the color of old chewing gum has spread eleven centimeters across the reveal in a decade. That is the flora. There is no soil in the chamber and never has been. There is no grazer either — no fish below the dam, because the fall and the screens and the nitrogen-supersaturated water see to that; no insects, because there is nothing to eat and the noise is a wall; no bats, no birds, no mice. A midge comes through the grille sometimes and is killed by the mist within the hour. The only herbivore in this ecosystem is the flood. Once or twice a summer the gates open wide and the pool rises and scours the walls to the four-meter mark and every living film in the reach is stripped away, and the surfaces are sterile and pale for a month, and then the green begins again at the lamp.

What the water brings instead is wrack. Along the slack shore at the downstream corner, on a shelf of concrete the width of a book, lies a wrack line: pine needles from the reservoir's drowned forest, birch seeds, a fragment of yellow foam, a bottle cap, the plastic sleeve of a cigarette packet, a drowned flashlight with its lens full of silt. It is sorted by size like a real beach. It moves a little every day.

---

The rules of the place are simple and are all painted on things.

*Two-person entry.* *Hearing protection mandatory beyond this point.* *Do not enter the water — aerated flow will not support body weight.* That last one is not a formality. The boil is less dense than a body by a wide margin; a life jacket in it is decoration. Anything that goes in goes down, and comes around the roller in nine to fourteen minutes, and the roller is a horizontal-axis vortex the length of the room, reversing at the surface, running upstream along the bottom, and it does not let go of things. The foam that circulates on it — tan-colored, stiff, smelling faintly of tannin and cold iron, from the organics of the reservoir — traces the whole circulation for you, slowly, in ragged windrows that stand still and then slew sideways when the tailwater changes.

*Wait for the amber strobe.* Before any gate moves there is a three-minute horn, and because no one can hear a horn in here, there is also a strobe on the wall at the adit mouth and another at the slot, and they turn the fog amber in slow pulses, and the whole room becomes a lit cloud for three minutes, and the water level begins to change.

That is the tide. It has two clocks. The fast one is the gate schedule: bay three opens at 06:00, throttles at noon, closes at 22:00 in the low season, and the pool rises and falls a meter and a half across the day. The slow one is the mountain. The glacier and the snowfields above the reservoir deliver their melt in the afternoon, and from June to September the discharge climbs steadily from morning to a peak around five, and the jet thickens and goes from a translucent green tongue to an opaque white column, and the fundamental drops perhaps four hertz, and the handrail's note bends. The people who work here can tell the month by the pitch. In November the intake goes to ice and the chamber runs at a trickle and the loudest thing in it is the sump pump, which cycles every nine minutes and sounds, in that emptiness, like something being murdered slowly in a cathedral.

Once a year, in February, they dewater it. Stoplogs down, pumps running for thirty hours, and then the pool is gone and the landscape stands revealed: the baffle blocks wet and black, the scoured dish, the cobbles, a lost scaffold clamp, the drowned flashlight, the whole floor steaming faintly in the cold air while men in waders walk across a country that exists for four days and is then submerged again. They measure the abrasion. They write down millimeters. They photograph the arrises to see how much rounder they have got.

---

From the road above, there is nothing. A blank concrete face in the flank of the mountain, sixty meters of it, streaked white with calcite, a grille, a stencil reading **OR 3**, a door that is always locked. In cold weather a plume of fog stands off the tailrace and drifts down the valley and dissipates before the first hairpin. In the car, with the windows up, you feel a hum in the floor pan and think it is the engine.

The entire landscape — its weather, its strata, its buttes and its shore and its tide and its single sterile species of green — is inside a box you would drive past in four seconds. It has been there eleven years. It is still cutting its canyon. It has no witnesses at all, and it makes a noise that would be heard for a kilometer if there were anything in the valley to hear it, and there isn't, and the water goes on falling nine meters into the dark and dragging the air down with it, and the handrail holds its one clear note.
