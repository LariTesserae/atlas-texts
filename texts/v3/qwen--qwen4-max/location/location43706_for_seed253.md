## The Cover

From above — and there is only one direction left to come from — it is a black plain at eleven thousand meters, oval, a hundred and ninety kilometers on its long axis, lying in the worn-down stump of a range that has nothing left to give. The mountains around it are not peaks. They are the ankles of peaks. They have been rounded off to a suggestion, a swelling, a low gray wave of stone that the ice has finished with. Snow lies in their folds and does not lie on the plain, because the plain is faintly warm, and because it is not stone.

It is a lid.

Walk out onto it and the ground gives underfoot like a frozen bog: a felted mat of fiber, resin-sealed, plated over with overlapping shields of a lignin-silica laminate that rings under a boot heel like the deck of a wooden ship. The plates are kilometers across. Their seams are caulked with a clear gum that has been renewed so many times it has no original material in it at all. Hail has pitted the surface into a texture like the skin of an old fruit. Ultraviolet has charred it black, and the black is deliberate — the outer face of the cover is a crust of living tissue that drinks the thin, ferocious sunlight of the roof of the world, and it drinks nothing else, and it does not share.

There is no way in that was made for anything. But the cover is not perfect. Along the northern lip, where the rim rock comes up under the laminate, there is a sill — a channel, walled and floored in pale fresh growth, kept smooth, kept open, three hundred meters wide.

Nothing has ever gone down it. Nothing has ever come up.

Follow it under.

## The Standing Wood

Beneath the cover there is no air.

There is no surface, no shore, no wave, no weather, no tide. The vessel is full. Three point four kilometers of water press up against the underside of the lid and down against the pavement of the floor, and between those two grown surfaces there is not one bubble, not one pocket, not one place where a thing could stand and look at a horizon. This is an ocean with the sky removed and the seabed replaced.

The water is 4.1 degrees. It has been 4.1 degrees for longer than there have been animals to notice. It sits at the temperature where water is heaviest and most stubborn, where it will not rise and will not sink, where a warm parcel and a cold parcel are the same parcel; and so it does not overturn, does not convect, does not mix except where it is pushed. It is stained the deep amber of tannin, so thick with the plant's own leavings that light dies in it within a few meters. It tastes faintly of sap. It is slightly viscous. Nearly every molecule in it has been through a cell — sweated out of tissue, distilled off the underside of the cover, gathered from the snow that falls above and drawn down through pores that open only when the wind is dead. There is no natural water here. There is no natural anything here. Dig three kilometers and you will find bedrock, but you will not touch it: everything between is grown.

And in all that black volume, five columns.

They run floor to lid, nine hundred meters through, thirty kilometers apart, and they are the reason the vessel does not burst. The water wants to lift the cover; the columns hold it down. They are trees in the way a cable is a rope. Their bark is a laminate laid in increments too fine to count, harder than granite, polished by ten million years of moving water to a surface with no edge anywhere on it — you could run a hand over the whole circumference and find nothing to catch. They are hollow. Inside them are galleries the size of cathedrals, and valves like the leaves of a book, and long muscles that are not muscles but hinges, the swollen leaf-joint tissue of ordinary plants scaled up until a single turgor stroke moves a hundred million tonnes.

Every eighty-four seconds, each column contracts.

That is the machine. That is the only machine. Sugar and oxygen come down from the black crust above; water goes up and down and around; roots go into the rock beneath the floor and eat it, oxidize it, dissolve it, and lay the mineral back down as architecture. The mountain is being converted into a building at exactly the rate it is consumed. That is why the place is still eleven kilometers high after everything around it has been ground to ankles. It is not a mountain. It is a replacement for one.

There are no tools here. There is nothing that was assembled, nothing that can be taken apart, no lever, no wheel, no fire, no hand. Everything was grown into its position and cannot be removed without dying. If you went looking for the maker you would find only appetite, and the long patient pruning of appetite by circumstance.

## The Sound

It is very loud, and it never stops, and it never changes.

The stroke of the columns is below hearing — seven cycles a second, felt in the sternum and the sinuses and the water of the eye — but the vessels are a resonator, and a resonator makes harmonics, and the harmonics are audible: a vast sustained brass chord, five fundamentals and their children, held without variation since before there were ears. Under it, the hiss of a hundred billion valves. Under that, the frying crackle of the small snapping things that live in the bark crevices, a sound like fat in a pan, continuous, the noise floor of the world.

Because the chord never changes, the standing wave never moves.

This is the most important fact about the place. In a held wave there are surfaces where the pressure swings wildly and surfaces where it does not, and they stay where they are, forever, at fixed distances from the walls. Small drifting things migrate out of the swinging places and gather in the still ones. Given ten million years, *everything* drifts out of the swinging places. The sea has been sorted. Every mote of food, every spore, every scrap of shed skin and husk and mineral flour has been swept by sound into a handful of motionless sheets.

And so the emptiness is where the noise is, and the crowds are where the silence is.

## The Panes

There are eleven principal sheets. Some are horizontal, stacked through the depth like the floors of a building with no walls. Some are vertical. Two are curved shells wrapped around a column at a fixed radius, and one is a complicated saddle near the southern end that no animal uses.

From out in the empty volume, a pane is the only thing you can see: a faint dull-red galaxy hanging in the black, a plane of small lights receding until the tannin swallows it. Red is the only color that carries in this water. Blue dies in a meter. Nothing here has ever seen blue, or green, or the sun.

Get closer and the lights resolve into animals. They are strung along the sheet like beads on an invisible wire, and they do not leave it. They cannot: on either side there is nothing to eat for kilometers, and beyond that, the swinging places, where the pressure will rupture a swim bladder and pulp a lung. The pane is a pasture, a reef, a city, and a cage. Its inhabitants are born onto it, feed along it, mate on it, die on it, and fall — slowly, in the still water — a few hundred meters, until they drift out of the node and are torn apart by the sound and their parts are swept back into the sheet.

The grazers are the largest: pale, blind, plated things the size of oxen that rasp the column bark in spirals so old the spirals are worn into the laminate as shallow grooves. They live four hundred years. Their cropping keeps the columns smooth and unfouled, and the plant cannot do without them. The borers keep the ducts open by chewing them. The curtains — chains of filter bodies, gauze-thin, a kilometer long — hang in the streaming at the pane's edge and take what the sound brings.

Abundance here is a matter of tonnage, not of encounter. There are hundreds of thousands of tonnes of life on a single sheet. You could swim a day through the volume between two panes and meet nothing at all, and hear everything.

## The Carriers

Thirty-one animals cross the emptiness.

They are sixty meters long and soft-bodied and armored only at the head, with a ring of pressure organs down each flank that reads the standing wave the way a hand reads a banister. They know where the loud places are. They go between them along paths that have not altered in the memory of their species, calling as they go at nine cycles a second, a note that arrives before the animal does and that the pane-dwellers feel as a change in the texture of the silence.

They carry pollen. That is their whole function in the economy of the vessel: they are the only reason five columns thirty kilometers apart are one organism and not five. They visit a pane, feed, take on a load of gametes in a fringe of specialized bristles, and cross forty kilometers of roaring black nothing to the next. They live perhaps four thousand years. There are always thirty-one. When one dies another is already grown to replace it, and the population has not varied by a single individual in nine million years.

Nothing blooms here. Nothing crashes. Nothing arrives. There is no season, no dawn, no upwelling, no pulse of nutrient, no year. The plant delivers at a constant rate; the grazers crop at exactly replacement; the floor's decomposers return everything; the heat of the rock-eating below equals the cold bleeding through the cover above, to a fraction of a degree, and has for ages. Selection has finished. Every niche is filled and every deviation is pruned by a world that offers no new question. The species are older than most mountain ranges and they look exactly as they looked when the range outside was still a range.

The vessel itself is the same. Not one cell of the original construction remains. It has all been replaced, plate by plate, fiber by fiber, in a shape that stopped evolving before the animals stopped evolving. Growth here means repair. It has reached its final form and it is alive in it, the way a chord is alive in an instrument that is being played continuously and never retuned.

## The Sill

At the northern lip the channel runs up out of the water and into the underside of the cover and stops at a membrane of clear gum, and beyond the gum is the thin screaming bright air of eleven thousand meters, which no one down here has any evidence for.

The channel is walled in fresh pale tissue. It is polished. It is kept clear by a small dedicated grazer that lives nowhere else and eats nothing but the film that would otherwise close it. It has never been used. It has been maintained, without interruption, for longer than the entire history of every animal that has ever heard the chord.

It might be a spillway. It might be a mouth. It might be the dehiscence line of something that was built to open, once, under a condition that has not occurred.

But nothing here is waiting. Waiting is a kind of change, and there is no change: only the eighty-four-second stroke, and the held brass note, and the crackle, and the eleven red sheets hanging motionless in the sorted dark, and the thirty-first carrier turning, on schedule, into the loud emptiness between one silence and the next.
