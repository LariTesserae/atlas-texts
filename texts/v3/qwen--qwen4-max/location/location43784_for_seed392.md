The first thing is the glare, and the second thing is that the glare is coming off the ground.

You come down into it from a rim of laid brick, and the basin opens: a valley a day's walk across, terraced in shallow steps, white as a split bone, and every surface holding a film of water so that the whole floor of the world throws the sky back up at you. There is no soil. There is no stone that was not put there. Under the water film the ground is brick — hand-sized, sand-struck, each one slightly out of true, each one bearing on its face the shallow oval of a thumb.

The edges are sharp. That is what you notice after the brightness has stopped being an assault and become a condition. Nothing here is rounded. The arrises of the coping stones could cut you. The chisel-dressing on the spillway lips is still crisp, still has the little fan-marks of the tool, still has the dust of its own making lying in the grooves where the water has not taken it. Nothing has slumped. No talus at the foot of any wall, no silt in any tank, no stain beneath any outlet. A place this wet should be streaked and softened and greened at the joints, and it is greened at the joints, but it is not softened. It is wet and new at the same time, and it has been wet and new for as long as there has been anyone to say so.

---

The terraces step down in nine long shelves to a lined canal, and the canal runs to a sump, and the sump is a square throat of corbelled brick that goes down past where the light will follow. On the shelves: courts, tanks, planting beds, drains, stairs, ramps, sluices with wooden gates, causeways on short piers, and everywhere the scaffolding — poles of split hardwood lashed with cord, platforms of reed matting, the cords wet and swollen and the knots half-tied. Not abandoned. Half-tied. A rope hangs from a ledger with the loop open and the end held in the air by nothing, at the height of a hand that is not there.

Everything is made and everything is made by hand. There is no metal here beyond soft copper in a few pins and awls, green-stained, worked cold. Brick, lime, crushed pot, shell, ash, bitumen, cord, fired clay pipe, hardwood, bone. The plaster is burnished with river pebbles that are not river pebbles, that were knapped and fired to look like them. The waterproofing is a black bitumen painted in brushstrokes you can still count. Where a wall meets a wall you can see the tooth-in of the bonding, and where a course ended for the day you can see the day's end, a stepped rag of brickwork left to be picked up tomorrow.

Dig anywhere and you do not find ground. At two spits you find an older floor, laid the other way, with the same thumbprint. At four you find a vault. Below the vault, more work. There is no substrate. The world is made all the way down, and the clay they build with comes up out of the sump already wedged, already the right stiffness, warm, in blocks the size a person can carry.

---

The heat is not the sun's.

The mortar is quicklime and it is still slaking. In every joint, in every bed, in the core of every wall, the lime is taking water and giving heat, and it has not finished, and it will not finish. Put your palm on a pier at what should be the cool hour and it is blood-warm and slick. The walls sweat. Water beads on the underside of lintels and stands in the tool-marks and runs down the faces of the brick in threads too fine to hear, and gathers in the designed depressions, and the depressions fill to their designed depth and stop.

This is the wet: not rain, not flood. A world perspiring from the inside. The air is a mouth. Condensation forms on the cool side of nothing, because there is no cool side; it forms anyway, on the shaded faces, out of habit. The water is alkaline and faintly slippery, sweetish, and it leaves no scale, and it does not wear the brick, and it does not carry anything away.

The heat is uniform. This is the part that visitors cannot make themselves believe. Stand under a fig in a deep court, in shade so dark it is almost blue, and the shade is only a change in the light. The air on your skin is exactly as hot. The stone under your feet is exactly as hot. Shadow here is a visual event. It has no thermal meaning. People who stay long stop seeking it, and the ones who cannot stop seeking it are the ones who leave, if leaving is a thing that happens.

---

The green is enormous and it is all in pockets.

Nothing roots in the open. The planting is socketed: figs in great square voids packed with a specified mix of crushed brick and ash and shell and black silt lifted from the sump; date palms in cylinders of fired pipe sunk three courses deep; reed and lotus in the lined tanks; tamarisk along the sluices where the water is fastest and still not fast. Moss has been laid, in sheets, like tile, on the north faces of the lower terraces, and it has taken, and it is lush, and you can see the seams.

The figs have found the drains. Their roots go down the pipe-throats and along the culverts and come out at the outfalls in white beards, and the culverts run full and clear regardless. There is a vine that grows only on cordage — it will not take a wall, will not take a tree, only rope, and it has gone up the scaffolding in green cables, and where a rope is half-tied the vine has grown into the open knot and holds its shape.

And between the green: emptiness. Whole terraces of nothing but wet white brick and glare, an hour's crossing, no shade, no bed, no planting, no reason. The lushness is intense and local. The courts are jungles. The plazas are salt-flat blank. You walk from one dense green room to another across a hot shining floor with the sky above and below you, and the emptiness is not neglect. It was drawn that way. There is a plan, and the plan wanted distance between things.

---

Life is everywhere in the green rooms and it does not make noise.

Frogs in the tank margins, thousands, leather-skinned against the lime, their throats working. You see the throats swell and go slack, swell and go slack, and you hear nothing at all — not a diminished sound, not a distant one. Nothing arrives. Snails the color of the brick, thick-shelled, and if you turn one over you find that the shell has been mortared: the animal has mixed lime and crushed pot and repaired itself with the same specification as the walls. Wasps whose nests are miniature terraces, nine shelves, courts, a sump. Dragonflies with dark rings around the eyes. Fish that live in the inch of water on the open plazas and cross them in the film, brick-colored, heavy with mucus, and you feel them go over your ankle.

Herons stand in every tank. One per tank, always, at the same offset from the inflow, in the same posture, and after the fourth or fifth you understand that they are not similar. They are the same bird. The same chipped scute on the left leg, the same fired-clay flaw running through the neck where the making cracked and was not repaired. Some of the animals here have kiln flaws. They live through them. There is a heron with a hairline split from beak to shoulder that should whistle when it breathes, and it does not, because—

Because sound stops. Say a word and it goes three feet and meets something like felt. Clap and you feel the slap in your palms and hear a small private thud inside your own skull. There is no echo anywhere in the basin, not off the walls, not off the water, not in the corbelled throat of the sump. The plaster eats it. The wet eats it. Whatever the reason, the rule holds: the world will not carry a loud thing.

So the conversation happens on the water. Frogs signal by ripple. The fish write. Insects lay their small disturbances on the surface film, and the herons read the tank with their feet. Every tank and every plaza-film is covered in standing ripple-pattern, combed and crossed and fixed, interference held in place like the pattern on hammered metal. It does not spread and it does not die away. It is speech that has stopped being an event and become a texture. You learn to read a little of it, the way you learn a script, and then you notice that the pattern on the ninth terrace is the pattern on the first, exactly, and you stop wanting to read it.

---

The light has no direction it will admit to. There is a sun; you can find it if you insist; but the whitewash and the water between them have made a box of brightness, and the shadows are thin and doubled and go the wrong way at the corners. Everyone here wears bone goggles with slits cut in them, and the animals have membranes they draw across the eye, and the figs have leaves that turn edge-on at what would be noon if noon were a thing that came.

Nothing wears. That is the deepest rule and the others hang off it. Tools do not dull. Footprints in the water film close behind you before your heel is clear. You cannot leave a mark on this place — no scratch, no stain, no track, no ash. But you can take a mark away, and the place keeps that ledger. There are eleven figs on the floor of the eastern court. There have always been eleven. Eat one and somewhere a brick goes soft, or a heron loses its chipped scute, or the moss on a north face loses a seam, and the total is restored in a way you will not be shown.

The fruit ripens and stays ripe. Nothing rots. The air smells of hot lime, wet ceramic, green stems, the faint iodine of the tanks, and never of decay, and after a while the absence of decay-smell is the most frightening thing in the valley.

---

Go up to the rim at the far end, where the work stops.

Beyond it the stacks begin: bricks laid out in courses on the flat, drying racks, lime heaps under matting, cord coiled, all of it arranged and waiting, going out to the horizon in a grid, and the horizon is not far, and it is not near, and it is at the same distance from wherever you stand on the rim. Count what you can see of the stacks and you will find, if you are the sort who counts, that there are exactly as many loose bricks out there as there are laid bricks in the basin. The world is half built. The two halves are the same half.

Every thumbprint in the basin is the same thumbprint. Billions of bricks, one thumb. And in the tool roll on the top platform there is a hardwood trowel with the grip worn hollow by long use, worn the way a thing is worn after thirty years, in a place where nothing has ever worn, and the hollow fits your hand. It fits your hand exactly. It came out of the sump that way.

At the head of the last stair, on the top course of the highest wall, there is a gap. One brick wide. The bed is buttered, the mortar is warm and wet and waiting, the line is stretched, the trowel is there. One brick and the work is finished.

Nobody has laid it. The scaffolds are up because it is not finished, and the lime is slaking because it is not finished, and the edges are sharp and the fruit does not rot and the ripple does not fade and the heron does not die because it is not finished — because a thing that is still being made has not yet begun to be worn down, and someone understood this, and stopped, one brick short, forever.

The fraying you can feel everywhere, the tiled birds, the horizon that keeps its distance, the knot tied around nothing, is the strain of that refusal. The world is holding its breath with its mouth full of water.

The brick is at your feet. It is warm. It has your thumbprint in it already.
