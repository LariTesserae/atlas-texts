Begin at your feet, because that is the only place this world admits a beginning.

The ground gives. Not the way mud gives — it does not suck or slump. It gives like the flesh of a forearm, a controlled surrender, and then it comes back up under you when you shift your weight. It is warm. Blood-warm, a degree or two above your own skin, so that after an hour of walking you stop noticing it and after a day you begin to find the cold patches frightening. Under a fingernail's depth of black water it is a felted mass of root and rhizome and leaf-laid-fiber, packed and re-packed and digested and re-laid for so long that it has stopped being soil in any sense a shovel would recognize. Cut it and it bleeds tannin, dark as steeped tea, and the cut closes over the course of a season.

There is no dirt here. There has not been dirt for a very long time. The mountains that made the dirt are gone — not fallen, not slid away, but *finished*, worn down through every stage of their long argument with water until they arrived at the shape they were always going to arrive at, and then the whole worn plain was lifted, gently, over an age, four thousand meters into the sky. What soil remained was eaten. What rock remained was wrapped. The land is now entirely a living thing, or entirely a consortium of living things so tightly intermarried that the distinction has stopped earning its keep.

Call it the Felt.

---

The air up here should be thin and bitter. It is thin — you feel it in your chest on the first day, and never quite lose the sense that you are breathing through a cloth — but it is not bitter, because the Felt is composting itself at exactly the rate it grows, and has been for longer than there have been eyes to see it. That slow oxidation puts heat into the underside of the air. The heat lifts vapor. The vapor meets the cold above and stops, and spreads, and becomes the Lid: a permanent ceiling of cloud, three hundred meters up, flat as a poured floor, glowing a dull amber where the sun is behind it. The Lid holds the warmth down. The warmth keeps the Lid alive. It is a closed argument, and it has been winning against the sky for ten million years.

So: warm, at altitude, under a low ceiling of its own breath. Dim. There are no shadows on the Felt. There is no direction to the light. Noon is a pale honey color that seems to come out of the plant rather than onto it, and evening is a bruise that spreads from the east, and morning is the same bruise healing. You learn the hour by the *color* of the air, the way you learn the depth of water by its taste.

And when you climb one of the knuckles — the last stumps of the old mountains, quartz cores worn to rounded knuckles the size of a chapel, standing out of the Felt like the backs of drowned animals — you can sometimes get above the local haze into the true thin cold, and see the Lid from above, and see that it goes on. Not to a horizon. To the *curve*. It goes on until the world's own roundness takes it away, a white plain over a green plain, and the sun up there is a small hard thing you have not properly seen in your life. That is the only place you feel the altitude. Where the plant is not, the sky remembers how high you are.

---

The Felt is layered, and the layers are full.

At the bottom is the mass itself: two, five, in places eleven meters of accumulated living peat, threaded with plumbing. Not rivers. There are no rivers on the surface anymore; the last of them were captured underground so long ago that the drainage of this continent is now a *circulatory system*, water moved by peristalsis through tubes of lignified tissue, pumped by the slow contraction of the whole mat. You can feel it if you lie down. A pulse, very deep, about one beat every forty minutes. It is not a sound so much as a change in what your body weighs.

Above the mass, the cistern layer: rosettes and tanks and cupped bracts, some no bigger than a thumb, some the size of a bathtub, each holding its own water and its own world. Rain rarely falls here in the ordinary way. The Felt harvests the Lid — condensation runs down the aerial felt in threads, is caught, is passed along, is drunk. Every surface weeps. Every surface is also drinking. The water is black with tannin and low in oxygen and older, in some of the deep cups, than the animal drinking from it.

Above that, the felt-canopy: overlapping translucent bracts, waxed, silica-ribbed, arranged in a tessellation so complete that from beneath it looks like the ceiling of a cathedral built by something that had never seen a gap. Light comes through green. It comes through *late*, too — the bracts fluoresce faintly, storing the day and letting it go at night, so that the darkest hour here is not truly dark but a soft underwater jade.

And in the deep sumps, where the mass has hollowed into chambers, there is another light entirely: fungal, cold, blue-green, fed on decay. It runs on summers that ended decades ago. You put your face to a sump-hole and you are looking at photons that entered a leaf before your grandmother was born, released now, finally, by something eating the corpse of that leaf. The underworld is lit by the past. It is very quiet down there and it smells like the inside of a bread oven that has been left to rot.

---

Everything is occupied. That is the true meaning of *ancient* here — not that the land is old but that it is *solved*. Every niche has been filled and re-filled and shaved down until the fit is exact, and nothing new can get in, and nothing old can afford to leave. There is no bare ground to invade. There is no empty water. A fallen bract is colonized before it finishes falling.

The animals are abundant and they are all, in some sense, organs.

Cup-crabs the size of a coin live their entire lives in a single rosette-tank, generation after generation, and never touch the Felt at all; their lineage is a river that flows in one place. Sump-eels, blind, pale as candle wax, move through the plumbing tubes and crop the fungal lining and are, in return, the only thing that keeps the tubes from clogging; when a tube clogs, a district of the Felt goes yellow over the course of a decade, and the eels are pushed through from a neighboring district by pressure alone, arriving like a thought arriving.

Above, the browsers: enormous, slow, six-limbed, padded feet the width of a door, coats of a moss that is not decoration but agriculture. They crop the felt-canopy and their gut returns it, and their passage is the Felt's pruning. A herd of forty takes a month to cross a valley you could walk in an afternoon. You hear them as a change in the drip pattern long before you see them — a broad soft irregularity in the water-noise, like a room where someone has stopped speaking.

That is the sound of this place: drip, and the felt swallowing drip. The Felt absorbs everything above a whisper. Shout and the sound goes two meters and lies down. What travels is the low end — the pulse, the long groan of gas moving in a bladder somewhere out in the fog, and, perhaps once a season, a boom that takes eleven minutes to arrive and means that a chamber the size of a house has quietly collapsed a hundred kilometers away, and will be re-grown over the next four hundred years.

---

There are marks.

A ring of quartz stones, knee-high, set in a circle nine paces across, the Felt grown up around each stone to exactly the same height, like water around pilings. A stair of hardwood treads descending into the mass, going down eleven steps and ending in solid tissue — not buried; *closed*, the way a wound closes. And the huts.

You find one every few days if you are walking a line that happens to cross them: low, oval, dry-stone and dead timber, a doorway, no roof, no hearth, no bones. Always the same dimensions. Always the doorway facing the same bearing, which is not the bearing of anything — not the sun, not the rim, not the prevailing wet. They are old past dating. The Felt has not swallowed them. It has done something stranger.

Out in the fog, hundreds of kilometers from any stone, you come on a hut made of *living wood* — the same oval, the same doorway, the same bearing, grown up out of the mass, hollow, clean, floored with felt. It is not a copy made by hands. There are no hands. The Felt takes an impression of what stands on it long enough, and holds the impression, and the impression propagates outward through the rhizome net at about the speed of a rumor: a kilometer a year, a hundred kilometers in a century, thinning as it goes, the doorways getting a little wrong, the ovals becoming suggestions of ovals, until at the far edge of the echo you find only a low ring of raised tissue with a gap in one side, facing the bearing.

The plain is remembering a building. It has been remembering it, imperfectly and outward, for longer than the building has been standing. Nobody knows what stood there first, or whether anything did, or whether the first hut was itself already an echo of something the Felt had swallowed before there was anyone to build.

---

Time here is a wave, not a clock.

The wet front crosses the continent in about fourteen months, a broad band of heavier condensation, and behind it comes the flowering: a synchronized bloom that moves at the pace of a slow walk, a horizon-wide blush of pale rust color that takes a decade to pass over any given place and returns, in that place, once in a human lifetime. People who live at the rim — and there are people at the rim, a few, in stone huts with their doorways facing the bearing — measure their ages in blooms. *I am two flowers old.* The bloom is the only loud thing the Felt does, and it is not loud; it is a smell, thick and sweetish and slightly like blood, and a ten-year increase in the number of small soft things flying.

At the edge, the Weep: the rim of the plateau, where the Felt stops and the water it cannot drink falls off into cloud. It is a cliff-line a continent long and it retreats about a hand's width in a century. Below it there is presumably a world. Nothing that lives on the Felt has ever been down there and come back, and the fog below is not the Lid but something older and colder, and the sound the falling water makes is the only continuous sound in this entire place — a hiss so vast and so distant that it registers, at ten kilometers inland, as silence with a texture.

The plain is not going anywhere. It has already arrived. This is the last shape the land will take: mountains ground to knuckles, knuckles wrapped, drainage internalized, weather domesticated, every gap filled, every animal turned into a function, the whole thing running at a temperature one degree above its own death. It will hold this form until the sun changes its mind.

Walk it long enough and you will notice the small thing, the thing the huts were trying to tell you. Stop for a night and the felt takes your shape. Stop for a season — sleep in one place, eat in one place, wear a hollow in the mass with your body — and the shape stays after you move on. And somewhere out in the fog, at a kilometer a year, a low oval of living wood is rising with a gap in one side, and the gap faces the way you used to face when you slept.
