## The Wet Room

Above, there is nothing much: a hardpan plain at the height of a knee above the sea, wind-polished, hot enough that the air over it stands in columns. Concrete pads set in the grit like fallen teeth. Pipe runs going nowhere, capped. A shed with its door gone. The plain is old and tired and has been worn down to its last few centimeters of patience.

The hatch is in one of the pads. It is not hidden so much as uninteresting — a square of checker plate with a lifting eye, warm to the touch, and around its rim a beard of white crystal, sharp as broken sugar. Lift it and the room breathes out at you: wet iron, yeast, and beneath that something clean and vegetal, cucumber-peel, that has no business in a place with no plants.

Nine rungs down. The ladder is furred. On the undersides of the rungs the crystal grows in needles, all pointing down, all newer than the year before, and if you take the ladder two-handed without looking you will open your palms.

---

The chamber is the size of a small chapel, or a swimming pool stood on end. It was poured concrete once, a wet room in a cooling plant, a place designed to be damp on purpose. You can find the concrete still, in patches the size of a hand, where something has recently scraped it bare. Everywhere else the walls have gone to lace. Travertine in cauliflower heads, in fans, in blades; aragonite in needle forests that break at a breath and grow back in a week. Warm brackish seep comes through the construction joints and lays down its dissolved limestone as it goes, and the condensate off the ceiling pipes lays down more, and the animals lay down more still, so that the room is not eroding. It is *accreting*. It is getting younger. Every year the edges are sharper, the interstices finer, the whole interior more elaborately itself, a landscape running backwards toward the freshly made.

It is thirty-one degrees. It is always about thirty-one degrees. The wet-bulb sits high enough that your shirt gives up, but the air moves — you can feel it draw past your ankles toward the floor drains, a slow permanent draft with somewhere to be.

The light comes down a sun-pipe: a polished aluminum throat through nine meters of slab, with a mirror head up on the plain that has been tracking the sun since before anyone alive was hired. It delivers an honest coin of daylight, ordinary daylight, warm and unremarkable, onto the east wall at morning and across the floor at noon and up the west wall by evening. You could read by it. You could read a permit form by it, and someone did.

---

There is no room. That is the first thing to understand about the wet room. Every surface is occupied, and occupied by more than one thing, in layers, fitted like tesserae — a mosaic in which the gaps have been filled and then the fills have been filled. Limpets in gasket housings, shoulder to shoulder, each one wearing a cut ring of old rubber like a collar. Colonies of filter animals that look exactly like felt and were, once, felt: they ate the insulation lagging and stayed in its shape. Hair-thin worms in the seams, thousands to the meter, waving. Mica moths whose wing scales are the delaminating faces of dead pressure gauges, so that when they settle they read as tiny broken dials. Shrimp in the floor pools, packed flank to flank, so dense the water is more animal than water and moves with a grain to it, like muscle.

Nothing green. A single fern at the ladder's foot, etiolated, four fronds, a refugee. Some films of algae where the light-coin lingers. That is the entire vegetation, and it is decoration. The food comes from elsewhere: the seep brings sugar of rust and sulfur; the air shaft brings down the plain's dust, its pollen, its dead insects, a fine daily flour of the dry world; and the room eats itself, its cellulose, its grease, its rubber, its own dead, over and over, at a loss so small that the sun-pipe's flour makes it up.

The floor pools are pockets, not a lake. Twelve of them today, in bowls of their own making, each held by a calcite dam the height of a wrist. No two are alike. One is sweet and cold and full of shrimp; the one a meter away is brackish and warm and holds a single translucent thing with too many mouths. The dams grow, the pools migrate, and a pool that has been in the same place for a month is a pool that is about to be abandoned — spilled, drained, its population stranded and eaten within the hour, its basin already being re-tiled by whoever arrived first.

---

It is quiet. This is not emptiness; it is policy.

The drip is tuned. Each basin has its own note, and the ceiling condensate falls into them at intervals that are almost regular, so the room makes a small unpitched music, glass on glass, one note here, three there. Under it, felt in the teeth more than heard, a fifty-hertz hum from a motor somewhere out in the dead plant. Under *that*, the tick of crystal taking up its lattice, a sound like a fingernail on a comb, continuous, everywhere.

Loud noise is fatal. The hunting here is done by vibration. The sheet — a predatory flatworm the size of a door, thin as wet paper, the color of weak tea — rides the walls on a film of mucus and finds its prey by the tremor of their feeding. It lays itself over a patch of the tessellation and dissolves it, all of it, a hundred thousand lives, leaving a hand-sized oval of bare concrete. That oval is the only bare concrete in the room, and it lasts about two days, and the sequence of things that colonize it is so reliable that you could set a clock by it.

So everything is quiet. The limpets graze with a stroke so slow it cannot be seen without patience. The moths do not fly; they walk. The shrimp keep their tails still. A dropped wrench would be a massacre, and would be heard, and would bring the sheet from three meters away at a speed you would not expect from something with no legs.

The other predator does not move at all. In the north wall there is a cast-iron valve body, and in the valve body there is an animal — soft, pale, folded, with a mouth like a purse. It has grown into the seat and it can close it. When it closes and then opens, the pressure differential takes everything within a hand's breadth: shrimp, worms, moths, a whole neighborhood, gone down the pipe with a soft *thock* that is the loudest sound the room permits itself. It hunts by plumbing. It cannot do this if the valve jams.

The valve does not jam, because the limpets clean it. Generation after generation of them graze the calcite off the seat, keeping the one thing in the room that eats them in working order — because the valve's *thock* is also the signal, and the signal is the rain.

---

Every six hours and some minutes, a compressor turns over somewhere beyond the wall, on a circuit fed by a thermoelectric pile in the seep, or a small turbine, or something the animals themselves keep turning; nobody has traced it and the tracing may not matter. The pressure in the room drops. The valve sounds. Everything opens.

For about eleven minutes it rains indoors. The ceiling's cold pipes sweat all at once, the whole vault lets go, and a warm fine rain falls through the coin of daylight, and the moths stand up on their legs and lift their mica, and the sheet spawns in ribbons, and the shrimp boil, and the felt swells and filters, and the calcite lays down a year's growth in a quarter of an hour. Then it stops. The pools refill. The drip resumes its notes. The room ticks and drips and holds still and waits.

Nobody has been here in a while. There is a stencil on the wall, half-drowned in travertine: **CONFINED SPACE — PERMIT REQUIRED**. An enameled mug, now a nest. A clipboard fused flat into a fan of crystal, its paper gone to a gray wafer. Chalk tallies by the hatch, four and a stroke.

And a boot print, in fresh white growth, sharp-edged, not yet filled.

It will fill. That is the direction everything here goes. The needles lengthen, the fans meet, the ladder loses a rung a decade, the hatch rim's beard creeps inward a millimeter a year. The room is building itself shut, and the packed frantic quiet life inside is racing that closing, tiling every new surface the instant it exists, keeping the valve clear, keeping the rain on time, making the chamber more intricate and more crowded and more nearly sealed with every season.

At evening the coin of light climbs the west wall, thins, and goes out. The hum continues. The basins count their notes. Somewhere in the dark a hundred thousand small bodies hold absolutely still, and the crystal grows a little sharper, and the room gets a little younger, and there is a little less room.
