The first thing is the light coming from below.

The ground is pale — bone-white going to warm ivory where it is damp — and it throws the sun back up under everything, so that shadows here are not dark but merely cooler, filled in from beneath. At noon the sky bleaches to a hard enamel blue at the zenith and goes almost white at the rim. The horizon is a ruled line. Not a suggestion of a line, not a haze of hills: a line, faintly convex at its far ends, the way a line drawn on a very large sphere would be. Heat lifts off it in a continuous shimmer that makes the last few kilometres of stone liquefy and reassemble, over and over, all afternoon.

The stone is warm underfoot at dawn and hot by ten and unbearable by noon — sixty degrees, sixty-two, hot enough that a dropped drop of water goes to a bead and skates and vanishes in four seconds. The air is thick with the low altitude, dense enough to carry sound farther than it should, and it does not cool much at night. Twenty-nine degrees before sunrise. Thirty. The stone gives back what it took.

There is no soil. There is no sand. There is no dust of the kind that comes from things being worn.

---

Look down, properly, and the flatness resolves into work.

The surface is laid. It is bedded in courses three to eleven millimetres thick, and where a plate of it has broken you can see the edges of them stacked like the pages of a book left out in weather — a pale band, a fine dark seam, a pale band, a translucent band with a sugary glitter, on and on down past the reach of sight. The seams are joints and they are mortared. Nothing here is loose. Nothing here is a fragment that arrived from somewhere else. There is no scree, no outcrop, no found rock, no gravel that was once part of a mountain. Every surface has been put down by something, in order, one layer at a time, and the putting-down has not stopped.

Dig anywhere and you get more of the same, finer and denser with depth, still coursed, still jointed. There is no basement. There is no old floor under the new work. Whatever this rests on, it is not a local question; the plain answers only with more plain.

It is a building the size of a province — several provinces — and it is entirely made, and there is not a tool in it, nor a hand, nor anything that has ever hurried.

---

The masons are films.

They live in water a millimetre deep, and they are the reason for all of this. Olive-black, rust-brown, dull green where they are thin, they lie in the shallow sheets and the seep-lips and the damp undersides of crusts, and as they breathe they drop stone out of the water: a fine carbonate mud that sets as it lands, layer on layer, course on course. In the dry hours they are a varnish, a stain, a crackled lacquer you would scrape off with a thumbnail without knowing you had killed a city. Give them dew and they swell and darken and go back to work within four minutes. You can watch the colour come up across a hundred metres like a blush.

They are the only workers and they are everywhere and they are almost invisible, and the plain is their accumulated output, kilometres thick, still rising.

Where a seep comes over a lip, the film is thickest and wettest and lays fastest, and so the lip grows: a rimstone dam, plumb on the inner face, scalloped and fluted on the outer, heightening a few millimetres a year. Behind it a basin. The basins here are not ponds. They are counties — shallow, flat-floored, walled by a coping of laid stone that runs to the horizon in both directions with the patience of something that has never had a deadline. Water crosses a basin in a sheet two centimetres deep, so slow it barely admits to moving, and arrives at the far lip, and goes over, and the lip grows a little more, and the basin behind deepens by the thickness of a fingernail over a human lifetime.

The spillways notch themselves. Where the flow is heaviest the film is thickest and builds fastest, so the notch's shoulders rise while its floor lags, and the notch narrows, and eventually chokes, and the water finds a lower place along the lip and starts again. The gates of this place migrate. There are abandoned notches running along the terraces for kilometres, dry, sharp-edged, perfect, like a hundred doorways in a wall with nothing on either side.

---

Water is a rumour most of the time, and then briefly a fact.

Dawn is the wettest hour. The stone radiates all night into a clean sky and by first light it is a few degrees under the air, and the air lays its moisture down on it: a film, beads, a genuine wetness that gathers along the mortar joints and runs. For about forty minutes the whole plain is damp and dark and working, and the films are up, and the sound is a fine continuous drip and hiss, ten million small arrivals, quiet enough that you have to stop breathing to hear it. Then the sun clears the horizon and it is gone, and the colour drains back to white from the top down.

Sometimes, in the wet season, there is more. Overnight the crust grows a nap of needle crystal — efflorescence, a white fur a centimetre high, delicate as frost and hot to the touch — and by mid-morning it pulls moisture out of the thickening air and collapses into brine beads, and the beads crawl, and the surface of the plain moves a fraction of a millimetre downhill and dries and locks. Every day the skin creeps. Every day it is re-laid.

And in the afternoons, the plain makes its own weather. It has to; it is big enough that its evaporation is a regional event, a column of wet heat rising off a surface with no shade and no relief, and by three o'clock there are towers standing on the horizon with anvils on them. One cell. Maybe two in a whole sky. You watch a rain shaft walk forty kilometres across the flat, grey and combed and locally enormous, and it wets a strip a few kilometres wide and leaves the rest untouched, and where it passes the stone goes the colour of wet bone and the smell comes up — mineral, faintly sweet, like a struck match held under a tap — and for an hour there is running water in a place that has none.

Then it stops. The sheet thins. The films come up. And by evening the water has become more stone.

---

The plants are scaffolding.

There is one clump every hundred paces, sometimes every five hundred: leafless, jointed, grey-green, a fist of succulent stems knee-high, salt-tolerant, rooted into a mortar joint where a little damp persists. Nothing else. No grass, no canopy, no litter, no shade wider than your hand. In the whole visible circle of the world there might be nine of them.

They do not escape the masons. Water films over their bases and the films lay stone against them, and the stone climbs, a millimetre a season, sheathing the stems while they grow upward to keep ahead of it. Some fail. Some succeed for a long time and become fluted columns, plant inside and stone outside, living rebar. When they finally die the stems shrink and rot slowly, chemically, without being eaten, and leave a hollow flue — a pipe running up the middle of a stone post. These stand alone out on the flat, one per day's walking, the only vertical things there are, and when the wind crosses them they give a note so low and faint you feel it in the ear rather than hear it, and at dawn they drip.

Nothing pollinates them. Nothing carries their seed. They break, and the fragments lie in the wet joints and root, and the films mortar the fragments in place while they do it, and that is how a clump becomes nine clumps over four hundred years.

There are no animals. Not few — none. No insects at the wet hour, no birds under the storm, no burrow, no track, no grazing, nothing that has ever crossed this ground faster than a millimetre a year. Nothing eats anything here. The films compete instead: overgrowing, shading, and above all *encasing* — laying stone over a rival's edge, sealing it under a course, burying it alive in the archive. The dead are not consumed. They are laminated. Every dark seam in every broken edge is a defeat, recorded, held.

---

It is quiet in the way that makes you aware of your own machinery.

The dominant sound is a ticking: the crust expanding at dawn, crystal against crystal, a scattered dry percussion that travels along the pressure ridges and dies. The ridges themselves are hand-high, sharp-crested, running in polygons across the flats, closing and heaving as the plates grow into each other. Once a day, usually mid-afternoon, there is a crack — a single thermal fracture propagating a hundred metres through a plate — and it arrives from far off like a heavy door closing in an empty house, and then nothing again. Rain, when it is out there, is static. Wind has almost nothing to catch, so it comes as pressure and heat rather than noise.

And under all of it, if you put your ear to the stone, a faint continuous fizz: the sound of the plain being built.

---

Here is what is new.

Nothing here is rounded. Nothing is worn. There are no valleys, no gullies, no incised channels, no soil profile, no sediment anywhere that was made by taking. This place has a history of addition only; its whole past is stored as thickness. Geology, in the ordinary sense — the sense that involves removal — has not yet begun.

But the plain has lately grown large enough to make its own rain, and rain that falls out of a sky is distilled, and distilled water is hungry. Where a storm sheet crosses a fresh crust it does not merely deposit. It bites. There are solution pits now, hand-wide, sharp-arrised, edges like broken sugar, not yet a single smoothed curve among them. There are places where a plate has hollowed underneath and collapsed, a raw sinkhole with plumb sides and no rounding at the lip, and a swallow-hole at the bottom of it the width of a wrist taking the whole sheet down into a plumbing that is only just beginning to exist.

There is one gully. It is forty metres long and knee-deep and it is the first thing in this entire world that was made by subtraction. It has a headcut. The headcut is moving uphill, a metre or two a storm, and each storm it exposes another few courses of the archive, and the films at its lip are working as fast as they can to seal it, and they are not quite fast enough.

That is the tension the whole place is now held in: a building the size of a continent, still under construction, hot and damp and silent and almost empty, laying itself down at the speed of a fingernail — and, for the first time in its long unbroken adding, learning what weather is.
