## I. The country that is not finished

Four thousand meters up — the survey revises the number, not because the mountain moves but because the mountain has not yet agreed to a height — the range is still coming out of the ground.

It is not uplift. Uplift is a slow argument between plates, and there is no argument here. The rock arrives in sections. It comes up through older rock the way bar stock comes through a die, taking the die's profile, and it emerges already shaped: fluted, finned, ribbed with cooling vanes, its faces carrying the faint regular striations of a thing that has been *produced*. The summits are sharp in a way that summits are never sharp elsewhere. No scree. No gullies. No rounded shoulder, no weathering rind, no soil. Nothing here has been taken away yet. The world has addition and it has not learned subtraction.

The air above the range is thin and cold and ordinary. The rock is not. It is hot to the touch everywhere, and it is hot in a mannered way — warmer along the ribs, cooler in the flutes, a temperature profile you could plot. Currents run through the sulfide beds. Ore is reduced in place, without furnace or chimney, by potentials the mass generates across its own thermal gradients; metal collects in dendritic seams along the paths of least resistance; slag is extruded downslope in standardized increments and cools into the next section of mountain. This is the works. It has no builders, no artifacts, no product that leaves. It is not a factory. It is a verb the ground is doing.

From a distance it makes almost no sound. A hum below the floor of hearing, felt in the teeth and the sinuses, the way you feel a substation before you notice it.

## II. The flaw

In one mass of unfired rock, high on the flank, there is a blowhole.

A bubble. A casting defect. Gas that could not escape the melt and was closed in when the skin went solid, and the skin went solid too fast, and the void stayed. The fracture that opens into it is the width of a hand and the length of a forearm, at about the height of a face, and it is not an entrance in any designed sense. It is where the bubble happened to intersect the surface.

Through it comes heat, and a smell of struck matches and hot brass, and light — ordinary afternoon light, the correct amount, no flicker.

Inside is a garden.

## III. The room

It is a generous room. Eleven paces by nineteen, a ceiling you can reach at the low end, a floor that slopes very slightly toward the back wall. Measure it again in an hour and you will get fourteen by nineteen, and the discrepancy will not feel like error; it will feel like the room having been asked a question twice and answering politely both times.

The floor is sinter: fresh, sharp, crystalline fines that have never been anywhere. Not sand — sand is a history of grinding, and there is no grinding here. This dust has never traveled. It condensed where it stands, out of vapor, one grain at a time, and it will cut you.

The walls are green rock in the foundry sense — unfired, undecided, still choosing its mineralogy. In one place a fan of olivine needles has committed. Two hand-spans away the same material remains a suggestion, a waxy translucence with something moving inside it that is not motion but the *option* of motion. Native iron has run out along a hairline in a fern-like spray, and where the spray meets the air it has grown a bloom of blue-black oxide, the only patina in the room, the closest thing here to age.

The air is a hundred and fifty degrees and drier than any desert has ever managed. Not low humidity: no humidity. There is no water in this room, none in the rock, none in the vapor, none in the plants. Water is not scarce here. Water is *unavailable* — a compound the works has never gotten around to producing, hydrogen being expensive and tracked and allocated elsewhere. Anything aqueous that entered this room would not die of thirst. It would simply stop being wet, all at once, and become a small salt sculpture of itself.

The heat is radiant and even and does not press. It is the heat of an oven with the door open, and the room is not hostile. It is only incompatible.

## IV. The standing crop

There are nine plants. Sometimes eleven.

Each one is extravagant. This is the thing that has to be understood about the lushness: it is not spread, it is *concentrated*. A single rosette near the warm wall carries something over four hundred blades, tiered, recurved, each blade a flake of mica thin enough to read a hand through, and they flex independently in the convection, and the whole thing chimes very faintly and continuously like a chandelier in a house where someone is walking upstairs. It needs perhaps six blades. It has four hundred. It is opulent the way a coral is opulent, the way a baroque ceiling is opulent — excess as a structural principle.

And then there is nothing for three paces. And then another.

They stand well apart because they must. Beneath the sinter, each has a root-mat spread through twenty meters of rock, mining hydrogen — trace hydrides in the sulfides, a few parts per billion, the limiting reagent of everything alive here. The catchments cannot overlap. So: nine individuals, each a hundred times more leaf than survival requires, spaced like orchard trees in a poor country. The bare floor between them is not emptiness. It is territory, drawn in a chemistry you cannot see.

They are not made things. Nothing in this room is made. But they are *to specification*, and the specification is industrial, because the environment is industrial, and convergence does not care about the difference between a lineage and a design brief.

The tall one at the back is a heat exchanger. It has to be described that way; there is no botanical word. A central column of dense sulfide, and off it, in two ranks, thin plates of grown graphite, each plate perforated, each plate canted three degrees from its neighbor to trip the air into turbulence. It is a fin stack. It is also, unmistakably, a tree, and it is in flower, if the slow amber bead swelling at its crown is a flower.

There is a low cushion of glass wool — silica spun directly out of vapor, fibers finer than anything a spinneret could pull, white and faintly luminous where the light enters them. Touch it and it does not cut; it *sings*, a single high note per fiber broken, and the note arrives about a second before the fiber breaks.

There is a stibnite fan, metallic gray, brittle as a wasp nest, growing in the exact plane of the local current, its needles aligned along the field the way iron filings align, because that is what it is eating.

And there is a vine, if a thing with no tendril and no hurry is a vine, that has grown itself along a conductive seam in the wall and now resembles nothing so much as a busbar: flat, laminated, stacked in insulated sheets, turning a corner in a clean radius. It has been growing along that seam for nine hundred years. It is nearly at the corner.

Their sap is sulfur. At a hundred and fifty degrees liquid sulfur is at its thinnest — straw-colored, mobile, almost watery in its behavior, running through the sulfide capillaries with an ease that the plants experience as health, as vigor, as *spring*. Everything alive in this room is currently in the best condition it will ever be in, and has been for two centuries, and will be for four more.

They run on the difference between the floor and the wall. Each is a thermocouple that has learned to leaf. The gradient is the sun here; the light is something else.

## V. Services

The light is ordinary. This is the most disturbing fact in the room.

It is the light of a bright overcast afternoon at moderate latitude, about five thousand six hundred kelvin, soft-shadowed, sufficient, unremarkable — the light under which you would sort screws on a bench. It comes through the wall rock the way light comes through a page held up to a lamp, evenly, from all directions on that side, with no source and no disc and no glare. Outside, the works glows dull red in the fractures and the sky is the deep bruised blue of altitude. Neither of those is the light in here.

The light in here is supplied. Along with current and heat, it is one of the services the works delivers to this cavity, and it is delivered *to tolerance*: steady, maintained, checked. Once every eleven years or so it steps to a slightly different correct value, and the mica blades all reorient over the following decade, and that is the whole of the day-night cycle.

There is no water service. There has never been a water service.

And yet every plant in the room, at the base of its crown, has a small cupped bract. Hollow. Glazed inside with a smoothness nothing else in the room possesses. Sized — precisely, uselessly, in all nine of them — for a drop.

They were grown to a standard that includes water. The utility was never connected. The socket is still there.

## VI. Nothing else lives here

No animals. No insects, no mites, no worms in the sinter, nothing that flies or grazes or burrows. There is no rot either, because rot is subtraction and subtraction has not been invented; when a blade dies it does not soften, it stiffens, and it stays, and the plant simply grows past it, so that each individual carries its own history outward in a widening skirt of sharp dry ruin.

Consequently there are no thorns, no toxins that mean anything, no advertise­ments. The flowers do not need to be beautiful and are not. What they do is charge.

Once a decade, more or less, a plant will raise its potential against the rock until the air near its crown goes violet and faintly ozone-sharp, and it will release a cloud of metallic spores, each carrying the same sign, each repelled by its parent and drawn along the field lines in the wall toward some other, grounded, waiting stigma three or eight or fifteen meters away. The spores travel in visible threads. They go around corners. They arrive.

It takes about four seconds and it is the fastest event that occurs in this room, and it happens perhaps twice a century, and if you were standing here for a human lifetime you would almost certainly see nothing at all.

## VII. The quiet

Mostly quiet. Not silent.

The hum of the works, conducted through solid rock, arriving in the bones rather than the ears. The tick of crystal taking up a degree of heat — irregular, minutes apart, like a house at night. The chime of the mica. A whisper of sulfur vapor through a hairline at the ceiling, so faint that you locate it only when it stops.

The room's reverberation is too long for the room. A sound made here returns from somewhere with more volume than eleven paces by nineteen can account for, a half-second late, slightly changed. Sometimes it returns twice.

And once in a long while — decades — there is a report from far off in the mass, muffled and enormous, as another bubble is born somewhere in the melt. That is the sound of a neighbor. None of them will ever be reached.

## VIII. What is wrong with the room

It is provisional.

Not unstable. Nothing here is falling apart; nothing here falls apart, because falling apart is erosion and erosion has not been licensed. It is provisional in the way a draft is provisional. The works is computing mountain forms, and this cavity is one of the options it is holding open — kept warm, kept lit, kept on service, run forward at a thousandth of real speed to see what the void does when it is left alone.

The evidence is small and patient. Edges double: hold your eye on the stibnite fan and there is a second fan, fainter, offset four centimeters to the left, which is the fan the room would have if the current ran a different way. It is not a ghost. It is an uncollapsed alternative, still being carried, still costing the works a little to maintain. It has been four centimeters to the left for six hundred years and it is drifting.

Counting does not hold. Nine plants, sometimes eleven, and the discrepancy is not disappearance — nothing vanishes, nothing arrives. It is that the operation *how many* has not been fully implemented in here. Ask again later and you will get nine, and the nine will not be the same nine, and there will be no seam where the change happened.

Effects run slightly ahead of their causes. The fiber sings, then breaks. The smell of a leaf arrives some years before the leaf. Nobody in the room is troubled by this. The plants grow toward events rather than toward light — the tall fin-stack has been curving for two centuries toward a fracture that will not exist for another eighty years, and when the fracture comes the tree will already be there, waiting, correctly shaped.

## IX. The long stiffening

The mass is annealing. It has been annealing since before the range had a profile, and it will finish, and when it finishes the void will close.

The plants know this in the way a tide knows the moon.

The cycle they live by is the viscosity of their own sap. While the rock stays near a hundred and fifty, the sulfur runs thin and straw-colored and everything grows, one mica blade every eleven years, one centimeter of busbar per human lifetime. As the mass cools past that window it will begin, very slowly, to warm the wrong way relative to its own chemistry — or cool, the direction is disputed and the plants do not care — and the sap will pass a hundred and sixty and turn ropey, dark, plastic, and the whole room will stiffen where it stands. That is the long stiffening. It lasts about nine thousand years. Nothing dies in it. Nothing rots. The garden simply goes rigid and amber-black and waits, holding its shape, while the walls creep inward at a rate no eye could catch.

And then the walls meet, and the room becomes an inclusion: a garden-shaped flaw inside a solid mountain, nine opulent individuals pressed flat into the lattice, four hundred mica blades and a fin stack and a busbar vine and a fan that was never quite decided, all of it carried, all of it kept, none of it subtracted, for as long as the mountain lasts.

Which, given that nothing here wears away, is a very long time.

At the base of every crown, through all of it, the small cupped bract stays open. Glazed. Empty. Correctly sized.
