# Basin Nine

You hear it before you see it, and you see the light before you see the place.

The road comes down off the coast plateau through cut rock, and at the last bend the noise arrives all at once, the way weather does — a broad, low, unbroken sound with a hard bright edge on it, like a river seen through a sheet of tin. Then the berm opens and the floor of the basin is below you, white, eleven meters below the sea that stands on the other side of the wall, and the white throws the sun back up into your face so that for a moment you are looking at the ground with your eyes shut.

Nineteen years ago this was water. The gate closed on a spring tide, the pumps began, and they have not stopped since.

---

The floor is not sand. Sand is a finished thing, worked and rounded, and nothing here has been worked. It is shell flour — cockle and barnacle and the plates of dead urchins, milled by the pumps and the wind and nothing else, angular as broken sugar, squeaking underfoot in the dry register that snow makes. It does not drift. It has no dunes. It takes a footprint and keeps it.

That is the first thing visitors cannot get used to. Last winter's paths are still there, crossing the flats in pale braids, and the routes people took in the first month are still there near the gate, cordoned with wire and read like a document: the surveyors' boots, the wheelbarrow track, the place where someone set down a crate and stood a while, thinking. Rain comes about forty days a year, brief and uncommitted, and it takes about eleven rains to erase a bootprint. The town employs a man whose job is essentially to not sweep.

Everything else is sharp too. The old reef at the south end stands up in blades and flutes, its wave-scour still crisp as a carving, the borings of clams open and empty and unweathered, each hole a fresh wound. The drainage channels the crews cut in year two have vertical sides and no talus worth mentioning; nothing has slumped, nothing has softened, nothing has grown a skin. Rock here has no patina. It has only rust, where iron has touched it, and salt, where the water left.

The single exception is roundness, and roundness is foreign. Ships' ballast was dumped in this basin over three centuries, and so the flats are scattered with smooth grey cobbles from fjords and river mouths a thousand kilometers away, brought here as dead weight and dropped. Children collect them. There is a small room behind the assay office with a case of them, labelled by provenance, and a hand-lettered card that says: THE SEA HAS NOT HAD TIME. The other round things are machined — impellers, bearings, pipe, the drums of the winches. In Basin Nine, a curve means somebody made it or somebody carried it.

---

The town is about nineteen hundred people and it is mostly not buildings.

The buildings are sparse. Houses stand a hundred, a hundred and fifty meters apart on their own steel piles, pale-clad, lime-washed, tin-roofed, each one alone on the white with a long shadow and a green skirt. You can see every house from almost every other house. At noon, when the sun is high and the floor is throwing light back up under the eaves, the shadows go thin and the settlement seems to consist of eleven or twelve objects and an enormous amount of glare.

What is dense is the connective tissue. Between the houses runs the town's real fabric: trestle walks at four meters, cable trays, brine mains lagged in tarred cloth, condensation gutters, the steam line from the kiln, the narrow-gauge spur that carries shell to the lime burners and lime back to the flats. Three levels of it, layered and re-layered, patched, spliced, added to by people who are now old. From the rim it looks like a diagram of a town drawn on the ground in iron, with the houses as punctuation.

Nothing rests on the floor. This is not aesthetics. The clay below the shell swells when it wets and shrinks when it dries, and the salt hydrates and dehydrates, and the whole basin breathes about a centimeter a season, unevenly, so that a slab laid flat would be cracked within two years. Everything stands on jacks. Twice a year, at the equinoxes, families go under their own houses with wedges and a spirit level and bring the kitchen back to true, and this is called the levelling, and it is the closest thing the town has to a harvest festival: everybody outside, everybody under something, borrowing tools, arguing about a bubble in a glass tube.

Addresses are depths. Eleven Row. Nine-and-a-Half. Sump Road, which is the lowest and the oldest and the first place the water would come back.

---

It is a wet place with almost no water.

The air is humid all the time; the sea is eleven meters up and two hundred meters away, and every morning until about nine the fog comes over the berm and lies in the bowl and wrings itself out on everything cold. Iron sweats. Copper sweats. Your hands are damp from the moment you wake. Laundry hangs limp at four in the afternoon and is put away damp and worn damp. There is moss on the north face of every vertical thing in the basin.

And there is no water to spare. No river, no lake, no spring you can drink from without testing. The only standing water is the sump: thirty meters of black, cold, netted surface at the low point, where the pumps drink. Everything else is seep, drip, and condensation, and all of it is claimed.

The pump hall lifts the basin's groundwater and the sump's leakage and sends it out through the Spout into the sea, but before it goes it runs cold through the condenser fields — four hundred tilted panels of glass and copper on the north flats, chilled from inside, sweating from outside. Under each panel a trough. Under each trough a hedge. The town drinks its own air, one milliliter at a time, and the fog-combs on the rim do the same with fans and mesh, and every roof is pitched and guttered and piped into a cistern, and every cistern has a lock.

Water law here is not about ground. It is about drip. A property is a set of drip-rights: which catwalk undersides, which condenser rows, which fog-shadow. When somebody proposes a new structure, the objection is never about the view. It is that the new wall will steal the wind that carries the fog, and dry somebody's ferns, and cost somebody eleven liters a day. There are easements for shade. There is a family that has been in litigation for six years about a pipe.

---

The lushness is real, and it is all in lines.

Where the fracture at the north end leaks fresh water up through the clay, there are seams: alder and willow, sedge, horsetail standing in ranks like something out of a coal seam's memory, moss so thick you can push your arm into it to the elbow, fern in the shade of the alder, cress and sea kale and small sharp apples espaliered along the condenser frames. The seams are perhaps eight meters wide and they run for hundreds of meters, and between them is white nothing. From the rim the town reads as a green circuit diagram on a pale board.

And a great deal of the green hangs downward. Under every catwalk, under every lagged main, where the condensation gathers and falls in a permanent slow drizzle, there are inverted meadows — liverwort, maidenhair, moss in ropes a meter long, the whole underside of the town furred and dripping. Walk the deck at dawn and you are walking on a garden's roof. Bats roost in the pipe lagging above it. Snails come up the piles at night and graze the underside of the world.

The bare flats between look empty and are not. Lift a plate of shell crust and there are sandhoppers in their thousands, beetles, woodlice pale as candle wax, the gravel mice that have learned to get their water from the bodies of insects. The abundance is under the surface and along the seams, and the space between is not vacancy; it is a held breath with a great deal living just beneath it.

---

The animals arrived faster than the plants and much faster than the soil.

The basin is a bowl and a funnel and the birds come into it out of the sky like water into a drain: gulls in a slow rotating column above the kiln stack, terns stitching the flats, oystercatchers piping their two-note alarm from every rail, a heron that stands in the sump with the patience of a creditor. Starlings in the tens of thousands use the cable trays and go up in sheets that change the light. There is a peregrine on the stack that has never had to hunt anything slower than a pigeon.

In the drip troughs there are frogs beyond counting, and their call has drifted. Nineteen years is enough. The pumps hold a note — a low, slightly flat B, four stages in series, three running and one spare — and the frogs have converged on its harmonics, so that at dusk the seams ring in tune with the machinery, a chord you can feel in your sternum, and the older people say the frogs used to sound wrong and the young ones cannot hear what they mean.

The crabs are the ones the visitors write home about. Shore crabs came through the sluice as larvae in the first year and did not leave; their descendants live in the warm damp voids of the cable trays, grazing the moss, carrying a spoonful of water in the shell across the dry spans, moulting into the troughs. They are pale and slow and they have no idea they are not in the sea. Nobody eats them. There is a superstition about it that nobody can explain.

---

It is loud, and the loudness never changes.

Eighty-four decibels on the deck, ninety-six in the pump hall, eighty at the seams at dusk, and under all of it the shell tick — at noon, when the flats heat and the salt crystallizes and the crust contracts, the floor makes a dry crackling rain-sound, millions of small reports, the ground applauding itself. Beyond the berm, every eleven seconds, the sea hits the wall, and you feel that in the soles of your feet rather than hear it.

The town talks in a shout register, two-syllable names with hard consonants, and everyone over forty reads lips and everyone on the deck signs — a fast creole of hand-shapes for *pump*, *brine*, *level*, *drip*, *shut*, *don't*. Doors are felt, not heard. There is a felt-lined room behind the pump hall annex, sixty-two decibels, rented by the hour, where people go to sit and hear themselves think; there is a waiting list. Once a season the starlings imitate the shift whistle perfectly and half the town stands up, and everyone laughs, and it is not entirely a joke.

The rule underneath every other rule: the note does not stop. Changeovers are performed by two operators overlapping the tone, hand to hand, and the handover is the only ceremony the town takes seriously. In year six the note broke for nine seconds. Water came up in the low seams within four. Every bird in the basin went up at once. People who were there will tell you about the silence the way people elsewhere tell you about a war.

---

And almost nothing here happens quickly.

The drying front descends four centimeters a year. The salt line — where the efflorescence stops blooming — retreats about a meter a year, and each spring the crews paint a dated stake at its new position, so that there is a receding avenue of little white posts across the north flats, nineteen of them, like the markers left by a glacier that is going home. The green advances a hand's breadth a season. The pump bearings are on a forty-year cycle. The lime burners are patient. The plan in the assay office, revised every decade, projects soil — actual soil, humus, something you could dig with a spade — in roughly three hundred years.

That is the work. Not extraction, not manufacture, though there is a cannery and a kiln and a substation and a rail spur. The work is subtraction, performed slowly, on behalf of people who do not exist. The town is drying a bowl so that in three centuries it will be a valley.

At four in the afternoon in winter the sun goes behind the west berm and the basin's day ends two hours before the sea's. The fog has long burned off; the light up to that moment has been plain and generous and everywhere, bouncing off the white floor into the underside of the ferns, so that even the hanging gardens are lit from below. The flats go blue. The frogs come in on their chord. Somebody's house is being levelled late, and you can hear the small talk of the wedges, and under it, under everything, the note — steady, slightly flat, nineteen years old, holding the sea off a place that is learning, at the pace of a centimeter a season, how to be land.
