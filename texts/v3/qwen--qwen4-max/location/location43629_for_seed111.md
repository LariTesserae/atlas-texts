## The Seam

From below, the only sign of it is a white tongue.

It spills from a slot six thousand metres up the north face, a fan of pale chips laid over the black gneiss like frost that forgot to melt. Climbers who have seen it from the glacier call it a moraine, which is wrong; nothing here was carried by ice. It was pushed. Grain by grain, over lifetimes, out of a hole.

The slot itself is a wound, not a cave. The mountain cracked it open in a single afternoon within the memory of the ice — one clean tensile failure, forty metres of rock parting along a plane of weakness that had waited a hundred million years for permission. Nothing has weathered it since. There is no rounding here, no sorting, no soil. The fracture faces are still mirror-flat where the quartz let go, still knife-sharp where it didn't. Put your palm on the wall and it will open your palm. Every edge in this place is the age of the event that made it, and the event is not finished.

Outside, the air is a thin blue insult. The wind comes off the summit ridge at a steady sixty and carries almost nothing: no scent, no warmth, only grit and the occasional frozen moth, tumbling like a scrap of ash. Snow does not lie on the ledge below the slot. It is swept, and it is also melted, from beneath, by breath.

Go in.

---

The room is ninety paces long and never wider than fifteen, and its ceiling is a single tilted slab that comes down at the far end to a crack you could post a letter through. That is the whole world. It has an entrance, a back wall, a floor, a roof. It can be walked end to end in the time it takes to get cold. Everything that lives here lives here entirely; nothing that lives here has ever been outside and returned.

It is dark. Not cave-dark, not the dark of a place where your eyes eventually invent something. This dark has a slot in it, and the slot admits a blade of sky, and the blade crosses the floor once a day like a slow white finger and touches nothing that cares.

But the room is not unlit. It is *strobed*.

Ten thousand small hard strikes a minute, quartz against quartzite, and one strike in forty throws a spark. Each spark is a blue-white pinprick lasting less than a heartbeat, and in that instant it prints a fragment of the world onto the air: a shoulder, a wet wall, a hanging foot, the underside of a jaw. Then nothing. Then another, somewhere else. The room is assembled continuously out of instants and never once exists all at a time. Stand in the middle of it and you will receive, over an hour, a mosaic of a place — thousands of unrelated glimpses that your mind will stitch into a cathedral, badly, with gaps.

They cannot see the sparks. Almost nothing here can see the sparks. That is not what the sparks are for; the sparks are waste. But something on the ceiling has eyes.

---

The crowd is the room's furniture.

They are the size of hares and shaped nothing like them: squat, thick-pelted, pale as boiled bone, with broad spade faces in which the eyes have gone to skin. Two funnel ears of gristle, independently swivelling, wet at the rim. A stub tail they sit back on, so that the body makes a tripod with the two hind pads, leaving the arms free. And the arms end in hands — proper hands, four long fingers and two opposed thumbs, pink and hairless and cracked at the knuckles, stained rust to the wrist.

Each hand-pair holds two stones. One is the core: a lens of quartz prised from the wall, held against the chest. The other is the hammer, and the hammer is the only rounded object in the world. It has been carried since weaning. It has been polished by eighty years of palm and sweat into a smooth grey egg, and when its owner dies the hammer is not passed on or buried or arranged; it is dropped, and lies among the sharp things, and is eventually picked up by something small that wants the salt in it.

They strike about once every four seconds. There are somewhere between fifteen and twenty thousand of them, on the floor, on the ledges, in the vertical cracks, hanging in the ceiling fissures by their hind pads with their heads down, working upside down, unbothered. Where the wall is richest they lie belly to back, three deep, and the ones underneath work by reaching between the legs of the ones above. Fur touches fur everywhere. From the threshold, in the first spark, the walls look furred — a pelt grown over the stone. In the second spark the pelt has hands.

The noise is not tapping. Tapping is what one of them does. This is a roar: a continuous granular hiss-crash, like heavy rain on a tin roof, like a river of gravel, with a low beating pulse in it where the standing waves of the chamber pile up. It has a pitch. It has *weather* — squalls of intenser work that move along the walls over the course of a day. It is loud enough at the throat to make your ears clamp, and it never, ever stops, and after twenty minutes inside it you will stop hearing it and start hearing *with* it, which is what they do.

They read the room by return. Every strike is a question and the echo is the answer: wall at four lengths, void behind, water-lens at two, neighbour's shoulder close, ceiling-hunter above and to the left, moving. The chamber is a map made entirely of noise, redrawn ten thousand times a minute, and the reason it is loud is the same reason a lit room is bright. A hush here is not quiet. A hush is blindness, and it spreads across the crowd in a wave when something is wrong, and in the hush you can hear the mountain.

---

What they are quarrying is water.

The fresh fracture face is studded with blisters: sealed lenses of milky quartz, some the size of a fist, some the size of a curled child, each holding a mouthful of water and a bubble of air that were shut in when this rock was still the floor of a sea that closed before there were birds. There is no other water in the mountain. There is no ice above, no aquifer below; the whole massif is dry as a bone and the seam runs through it like a crack through a brick.

Opening a blister takes a season. It cannot be hurried — strike too hard and the lens shatters inward and the water is lost into the rubble, and everyone within reach knows it happened, because the sound of a wasted blister is a sound they will all repeat, softly, for days. So they chip. A flake, a listen, a flake, a listen. Ten thousand flakes over four months, each one used once to scrape a crust or trim an edge and then let fall. The floor is a metre deep in sharps. It shifts underfoot with a sound like walking on broken crockery, and it is damp, always damp, never wet, never mud.

When the lens finally opens there is a sigh — the old air escaping through a hole the width of a straw, a thin sustained whistle, three or four seconds, pitched by the bubble's size and its age. Every ear in the room turns. Then the crowd comes, in slow orderly waves, along the ledges and down the walls, and they drink in a queue that has no rank in it, and the water is faintly saline and tastes of iron and deep time, and it is the only source of something their bodies cannot make.

The old air goes into the room. Over centuries the room has been slowly re-breathing an atmosphere that predates flowers. Nobody could measure it. But the flies here are larger than any flies at four thousand metres, and the pale crickets in the flake-middens have wings they never use and never lose.

---

The rest of the water is them.

Twenty thousand bodies breathing in a stone box: the vapour goes up, meets the cold outer wall near the throat, and comes down as a film. In the last ten metres before the slot the rock is slick and rimed and bearded with fine hoar, and the film gathers into threads, and the threads run, and the crowd along that stretch spends much of its life licking stone. It is a closed loop with a leak. The draft at the mouth steals a little every day, a thin invisible plume going out over the glacier, and the blisters make it good, and the balance has held since the afternoon the mountain cracked.

Nothing here drips twice in the same place. There are no pools. There is one seep, at the back left, where a hairline delivers a bead every eleven seconds into a depression the size of a cupped hand, and the moss around it is the only green in the world, and there is a worm in it, and the worm is pale and blind and slow and has never been anywhere else.

Vegetation is otherwise an rumour. Orange lichen on the outer ledge, in a rosette the width of a plate, growing a hair a year. Snow algae in the rime, coming and going. And one cushion plant, wedged in the throat where a little wind-blown dust has lodged, a grey-green pincushion no bigger than a loaf, which has sent a single root four metres down the crack in the dark, hunting the damp. The crowd eats the root tips. It takes eleven years for a nibbled tip to grow back. They do not kill it. They have not killed it in six thousand years of nibbling, which is not restraint; it is simply the tempo of the place.

---

Everything else lives on what the wind brings.

The mountain's summit is a net. Upslope wind carries insects off the lowlands and drops them, frozen, on the high snow, and a fraction of that fall comes to rest on the ledge below the slot. The crowd does not go out for it — the ones that have gone out are the small stiff shapes in the drifts, and they are eaten where they lie by things with fewer scruples. Instead the fall comes in on the eddy, a few dozen bodies a day, and the midden-fauna takes it: springtails in white sheets over the flakes, flightless crickets, mites, and beneath them the fungal threads, mats of pale hyphae knitting the sharp floor together into something that can hold a little warmth.

And every knapper is itself a district. Three species of mite in the pelt, one only on the left ear. A parasitic fly that rides the nape. Each of twenty thousand animals is a small damp warm highland with its own weather and its own crowded valleys, and the room's real population is not twenty thousand but a number with no useful zeroes in it.

On the ceiling there is a thing with legs like a spider crab and a body like a folded umbrella, and it has eyes — the only good eyes in the world — and it hunts by spark. It waits, motionless, for an hour, until a strike below throws a flash, and in that flash it sees a straggler on an exposed ledge, and it takes eleven minutes to cross to it. It is very patient and it does not need to be otherwise. There are twenty thousand.

---

Here is the rule the place is built on, and it is a subtraction:

Nothing is added. There is no construction here of any kind — no nest, no wall, no cache, no arrangement of stones, no burial, no mark made for the pleasure of marking. The only artifacts in this world are hammerstones worn round by use and flakes let fall where they fell, and no flake has ever been placed anywhere on purpose. They cannot make fire; there is no fuel and hardly enough oxygen, and they have never wanted it. Their entire technology is removal. They take the mountain away, one flake at a time, and push the spoil toward the light with the flat of a hand, and it goes out of the slot and down the white tongue, and that is the whole of their industry and the whole of their monument.

And they must, because the room is closing.

The mountain is still under compression. The slot narrows by about a finger's width in a century — a slow creep, measurable only in the way the ceiling-hunter's favourite ledge is now a squeeze, in the way the oldest workers remember a place near the mouth where two could pass abreast. The crowd's answer is not to leave. There is no elsewhere; the outside is a blue killing wind. The answer is to quarry the walls back. They widen the room at almost exactly the rate the mountain closes it, and have done so since the crack was young, and the volume of the world has not changed in six thousand years. Twenty thousand animals spending their lives eating a hole slightly faster than the planet can shut it.

That is the slow engine. Everything else is its noise.

At the far end, where the ceiling slab comes down to a letterbox and the floor rises to meet it, there is a crack nobody can enter. Something on the other side strikes. Not often — once every forty or fifty seconds, on a tempo nothing here uses, with a weight nothing here has. It has been striking since before the crowd's oldest memory of sound. The workers near that end keep their taps out of its intervals, the way you avoid stepping on someone's words. Nobody has ever tried to open it. It may be that they know what a wasted blister sounds like.

---

Stand at the throat at night, where the wind's hiss and the crowd's roar meet and cancel into a strange soft band, and look back in.

You will see nothing for a long time. Then a spark: ten thousand pale shoulders, mid-swing, a hanging forest of hands, one wet wall, the underside of a jaw. Then black. Then another, further in, printing a different fragment. The blade of starlight lies across the floor and moves a hand's breadth while you watch. Somewhere a lens gives up its old air and whistles, and every ear turns, and the roar dips and swells.

The room does not exist as a picture. It exists as a sound with occasional flashes in it, a warm damp crowded loud small hole near the top of the world, patiently eating itself wider while the mountain leans in, and it will go on doing that until the last blister or the last millimetre, whichever the mountain decides.
