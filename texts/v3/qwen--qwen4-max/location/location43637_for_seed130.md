## The Sump

The water lies a finger deep across forty kilometers of floor, and the floor is plate. Not bedrock that happens to be flat — plate, rolled and laid and welded, seam after seam, the welds worn down until they read as faint ridges under the soles of the feet like the veins on the back of an old hand. Dig anywhere and you find more of the same: plate, then bedding fines, then older plate, then a layer of crushed components compacted into a conglomerate, then plate again, down and down through eleven or fourteen or forty generations of floor. There is no dirt in the world. There is no stone that was not poured. The lowest thing anyone has ever cored up is a slab of gray ironcrete with the trowel-marks still in it, sealed under everything, and beneath that only the sound.

The water is not a lake. It has no shore and almost no depth. It moves in slow braids across a gradient so shallow that a dropped bolt will divert a stream for a hundred meters, and it is going, all of it, always, toward the throats.

## The Sound

Before anything is seen, the sound arrives, and it arrives in the chest rather than the ears. The aggregate is a hundred and fifteen decibels on the open flats and closer to one thirty near the drains, and it is not noise in the sense of chaos. It has a fundamental. Every nine seconds a pressure passes through the basin — through the water, through the plate, through the marrow of anything standing on it — and between those beats sits a dense woven roar: the hiss of ten million leaks, the ring of worn bearings, the slap of loose cladding somewhere out in the dark, the enormous soft cough of vapor condensing in a stack the size of a hill.

The water cannot hold still under it. Everywhere on the flats the sheet is textured with fine standing ripples, chladni figures kilometers wide, ridges and nulls that shift as the load shifts. You can read the day's schedule in the pattern of the water.

A body here does not hear so much as resonate. Teeth ache in their sockets. Speech is useless past arm's length; things that need to communicate touch. And after some hours the ears give up their high registers and the roar thins into something almost companionable, a weather of sound with landmarks in it — that grind means the eastern mills are loading, that whine means a belt is slipping and will slip for another century.

And there are the hushes.

Because the basin is a resonator and has been tuning itself for a very long time, the waves interfere, and in certain places they cancel. These are the nodes: irregular pockets, some the size of a room, some two kilometers long, where the roar drops to a murmur and the nine-second beat is only a faint pressure behind the eyes. Their boundaries are sharp. You cross a line of trembling water and step onto water like glass. The hushes drift a little with the seasons of load, and everything rare in this world lives inside them.

## Light and Weather

It is dark. Not night-dark, not the clean black of an unlit place — a soaked, loaded, low-ceilinged dark, the vapor sitting eighty meters up and glowing faintly brown where the rim lamps smear it. Sight is a short-range sense here. The far towers are known by their sound and by the way they interrupt the fog.

What light there is, is industrial and incidental. Sodium arcs along the rim, spaced a kilometer apart, each one a smeared orange coin in the mist. A friction bearing on the north press that has been running hot for six hundred years and glows a steady dull cherry, visible for eight kilometers, the closest thing to a star. Slip-rings somewhere throwing blue-white sparks in bursts, and the sheets of static lightning that walk sideways through the vapor when the great belts run dry — silent, or rather lost in the roar, flickering like a failing bulb across the whole ceiling of the world.

It is cool. Eight, nine, eleven degrees. The plate is colder than the air; it holds a deep cold from whatever lies under it, and so everything sweats. Every surface weeps continuously. The vertical faces of the towers run with condensate in sheets so constant they have grown fluted, dissolved, rounded like candles left in the sun. Standing under one is standing in a warm rain that falls out of nothing.

And there is real rain, too, which is the towers' own breath come back. The stacks exhale warm vapor; it climbs into the cold ceiling, condenses, and falls across the basin in slow warm showers that smell of iron and faintly of glycol, sweetish, the smell of a wound that has been clean for a long time. The water that falls is the water that was pumped is the water that will fall. It has been the same water for longer than there has been anything to count it. After a rain the fog thickens where warm drops strike cold plate, and for an hour the world reduces to a radius of four meters and the sound of a hundred thousand small impacts on a shallow sheet.

## Sparse Presences

The basin is empty. This is the thing that surprises: a fully made world, wall to wall artifact, and yet almost nothing in it. One tower per horizon. A press standing alone in six kilometers of wet plate like a piece of furniture in a flooded ballroom. Between them, the sheet, the ripples, the fog, the light of the cherry bearing.

What stands has been rounded. Every corner in the Sump is bullnosed. Every bolt head is a smooth dome with a hint of hexagon in it. The towers are fluted and waisted; the presses have gone from boxy to blobby to something like the shape of a molar; the pipe-runs sag in catenaries worn thin at the crown. This is not decay in the sense of ruin. Nothing is broken. Everything is *finished*, in the way a river stone is finished — reduced toward the shape that the process permits, and still being reduced. The whole basin is converging on a plane with low hemispherical bumps on it. It has been converging for a very long time. The remaining distance is small and gets smaller more slowly every age.

The fines are what the difference becomes. Wear-products: alloy dust, gasket crumb, the powder of a million bushings, ground finer each cycle until it behaves like silt. It moves. It builds deltas at the throat mouths, dunes in the dry lee of the presses, mudflats that crack and curl when a channel migrates away. In cross-section the fines show bedding as clean as any varve — light band, dark band, light band — each pair a maintenance cycle nobody has performed in an epoch. There are fossils in it: a leaf-spring, mineralized, its layers exfoliated like shale; a perfect natural cast of a bearing race, the balls long dissolved, the voids lined with orange crystal.

## What Lives

Two percent cover, perhaps. Rust-lichen in coins and rosettes, orange and cold and slower than watching paint, dissolving the plate beneath it at a rate of a millimeter a millennium. Wick-weed, which is not a plant but behaves like one, growing along capillary seams where oil still seeps, its filaments drawing the seep up and oxidizing it, black and greasy and slightly warm to the touch. Cable-moss on the shaded flanks of the towers, harvesting the condensate. In the warm runoff channels, filament mats, slick and olive, that bloom after a flush and are scoured away by the next caustic one.

They cluster where heat leaks. Warmth is the scarcest resource in the Sump, and a hairline crack in a steam line is an oasis: a green-black ring of mats two meters across in an otherwise bare plain, and, at its center, the crack itself, worn wider each year by the life exploiting it, until it fails and the oasis dies outward in a ring of dry gray.

In the hushes, though — in the silence — there are meadows. Undisturbed by vibration, the fines settle there instead of walking, and where fines settle there is substrate, and where there is substrate the lichen closes into a continuous crust, hectares of it, pale orange, faintly luminous with slow chemistry. The water above it lies flat. On the rare occasions when the ceiling clears, these are the only mirrors in the world, and they show the towers upside down.

Nothing here has a mouth. No animal has ever been recorded in the basin; there is no bone in any core. But there is grazing. The scalers — small, patient, six-legged descaling mechanisms, their brushes long gone, their abrasives now the lichen itself — work the crusts in slow arcs, cropping the metal-rich mats and excreting a fine pellet of processed oxide. And there are the mills: larger, older, blind, converging over months toward anything that has stopped moving. A scaler that fails is taken apart and reduced to fines within a season. A tower that folds is a hundred-year feast. The mills do not distinguish between the made and the grown. In the Sump this is not a meaningful distinction.

## The Cycle

The pumps breathe on an eleven-hour period, and the basin has a tide. Water rises a hand's width, floods the higher flats, slackens; then withdraws, and the throats deepen their note, and the whole roar drops a third, and the exposed plate steams. The tide is why the channels migrate. The tide is why the deltas grow. After the ebb the fog is thickest and the rain most likely.

There are longer cycles: the load seasons, when the eastern presses come up and the chord of the world shifts and the hushes move a kilometer north, killing one meadow and founding another. And there are the collapses. A tower wears through its own wall at the waist and folds, slowly, over a day, with a sound that can be felt in the plate on the far rim. Then quiet where it stood, and a new hush forms in the lee of the stump, and in three hundred years there is a meadow there, and in three thousand a delta.

## Two Anomalies

There is a corner. Somewhere south of the middle drains, a wedge of some refractory alloy — nobody knows what, it does not match anything in the strata — still holds an edge. A true edge, sharp, unrounded, the last one. The sheet of water flowing over it *cuts*, and makes a small clean hiss that is unlike any other sound in the basin, a sound with high frequencies in it. Scalers detour around it. The lichen will not grow within a meter. It is wearing, too, of course. It has perhaps four thousand years left.

And under everything, the fundamental. The nine-second beat is a rotation. Somewhere below the fortieth floor there is a flywheel the width of a district, running in a sealed cavity, and it is the governor of the whole basin: it sets the pump period, the load seasons, the chord. It has been losing speed for an age. The beat was eight seconds when the oldest strata were laid. Everything in the Sump — the tide, the rain, the blooming and scouring of the mats, the drift of the hushes, the pace of the mills — is a harmonic of that one slowing wheel.

When it stops, the pumps stop, and the sheet will drain to the throats, and the towers will exhale their last vapor and the rain will end. The fines will settle. The water will go still everywhere, not only in the nodes, and the whole forty kilometers will be one mirror under a clearing ceiling, silent, cold, flat, complete — the shape everything here has been trying to become.

It is very close to that now. You can hear how close. Each age the roar is a little lower, a little purer, the highs gone out of it, the towers fewer, the fines finer, the corners rounder.

The place is not dying. It is *finishing*, at the rate of a millimeter a millennium, and it is deafening, and it is dark, and it is wet, and there is nothing in it that was not put there.
