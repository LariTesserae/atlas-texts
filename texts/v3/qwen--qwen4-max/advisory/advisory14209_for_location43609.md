# Preparing for The Kettle

## First, correct your category

You are not visiting a cave. Caves are cool, and quiet, and old. This is a hot mine with a town in it, wrapped in a livestock barn, hanging under a colander. If you prepare for a cave — fleece, headtorch, wonder — you will be miserable by the second hour and possibly dead by the fourth.

Prepare instead as for a deep industrial underground, with the additional complication that the industry is four hundred thousand sleeping mammals who own the place, and the additional complication that everyone who lives there has stopped noticing all of it.

Hold two clocks in your head at once. The short one is four hours: the time between the Lung stopping and the room becoming a jar. The long one is a hundred thousand years: the time until the water finishes what the machines started. Everything you do down there belongs to one clock or the other, and the locals are fluent in both, and will not explain either unless you ask properly.

---

## The heat is the whole problem, and it is not the problem you think

Learn the phrase **wet-bulb temperature** and learn to compute it, because your body's survival here is a single number and it is not the number on the thermometer.

At forty degrees and eighty-five percent humidity, the wet-bulb is around thirty-eight. Sweat does not evaporate. Fans do not cool you — air hotter than your skin *adds* heat to you, the way a convection oven does. Shade does nothing, because there is no sun; the heat is not coming from above, it is coming from the rock, which is thirty-six degrees all the way to the horizon in every direction including up and down. You are not standing in hot air. You are standing inside a hot solid, and the air is only the part of it that moves.

Above a wet-bulb of about thirty-five, a healthy resting human cannot shed metabolic heat, ever, by any posture or any amount of water drunk. You are on a countdown, not in a climate. Under the roost, at forty-four and near-saturation, treat the space the way you would treat a smoke-filled room: you enter for minutes, with gear, with a buddy, with a clock running, and you leave before you feel you need to.

The floor of the Kettle at thirty-nine to forty-one is survivable at rest for an hour or two. It is not survivable as a residence.

**Which is why the town is where it is.** This is the single most important piece of geography you will learn: the Kettle is two places, not one. There is a thirty-two-degree town in the intake course along the west wall, and there is a forty-degree factory everywhere else, and the three degrees between them are the entire margin in which human life is possible. Wet-bulb at thirty-two and ninety percent is around thirty-one — hard, sticky, sleepless, but survivable indefinitely at rest. That is where you will eat, sleep, write, and recover. Everything else is an excursion with a return time.

The locals already live this way. Twenty minutes at the face, then lie in the trough. They are not being stoic; they are being correct. Do it more conservatively than they do, because they have ninety years of physiological and cultural adaptation and you have a return ticket.

**Cooling in a saturated room is conduction or nothing.** Ice, cold water, cold metal, immersion. The bathhouse trough at thirty-four feels like snow because water pulls heat out of a thirty-seven-degree body by contact even when the air cannot pull anything at all. So:

- Arrange ice. The plain above has electricity, money, pivots, and a hoist. Ice is the second commodity, after air. Negotiate for it before you arrive; do not assume.
- Bring a phase-change or ice-pack vest, and understand it is a consumable with a twenty-to-forty-minute life, exactly matched to a shift at the face.
- Bring a small refrigeration unit and dehumidifier for your own room, and seal and insulate that room, because its walls are thirty-six degrees and will fight you all night. Getting your sleeping air to sixty percent humidity is worth more than every fan in the chamber. It gives you back your sweat.
- Ask, on your first day, where the cold immersion is and how fast you can be put in it. Ask it the way you'd ask where the fire exits are.

**Acclimatise, but do not believe in it.** Ten to fourteen days of graded heat exposure will lower your heart rate, start your sweating earlier, dilute your sweat, and expand your plasma volume. It buys you working capacity at thirty-two degrees. It buys you nothing whatsoever above a wet-bulb of thirty-five. Fitness is not armour here. Fit people die of heat stroke too, sometimes faster, because they push.

**The protocol:**

- Never alone outside the intake. Never.
- A buddy with absolute veto authority. Heat stroke's first symptom is bad judgment; you will be the last to know. Give your companion permission to drag you out and promise in advance not to argue.
- Hard time limits set *before* you go in, written down, with a timer that beeps. Fifteen minutes for your first excursion, not twenty.
- Drink a litre to a litre and a half an hour while working, with salt — roughly ten to fifteen grams of sodium chloride across a day, more if you're a heavy sweater. Plain water in these volumes will give you hyponatraemia, which looks like heat illness and is treated oppositely. Bring oral rehydration salts in bulk. Note that the local water is condensate — essentially distilled, hungry, mineral-free — so it will actively strip you if you drink it unsalted.
- Watch for: goosebumps in forty-degree air, chills, sudden irritability, clumsiness, stopping talking, a heart rate that won't come down when you sit. Cessation of sweating is *not* a sign you can use — you will be soaked and streaming at all times, working perfectly, cooling not at all.
- Core temperature monitoring if you can get it (ingestible pill, or at minimum an ear thermometer and a log). Anything over 38.5 and rising, you go in the trough.

## Air: learn the arithmetic before you learn the streets

Children here learn it before they read. Ask someone to teach you the numbers and let them enjoy teaching you; it is the town's catechism.

Then do the practical version:

- **Find the siren on day one.** Learn to distinguish the fan alarm from the firing alarm from the shift signal. There will be at least three and they will not sound alike to you at first.
- **Walk both escapeways while you are still calm.** The hoist in the downcast shaft, and the ladderway in the Flue. Three hundred metres of ladder is not an escape route for an unfit visitor with a heat-loaded body; know that about yourself. Know where the refuge chambers are, what's in them, and how long they last.
- **Carry a self-rescuer and know its type.** A filter self-rescuer converts carbon monoxide and does nothing in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere. In a room where CO₂ pools in the low corners, you want oxygen-generating escape apparatus, or you want to be very sure which hazard you're carrying it for.
- **Carry a four-gas meter** — O₂, CO, CO₂, H₂S — plus an ammonia tube or electrochemical sensor. Add ammonia to your mental map vertically: it is lighter than air and rises, so the crown is ammonia and heat; CO₂ is heavier and pools, so the low south floor and the sump margins are asphyxia. The town sits in the middle band, which is the only reason it exists.
- **Never lie down in a low corner.** Never sleep on the floor anywhere, for this reason and for six others involving arthropods.
- **The drifts off the west wall are not places.** The description is exact and you should take it literally: unventilated dead ends, drilled and charged. They are confined spaces with no airflow, and they will hold CO₂ and post-blast fumes. Do not enter one because it looks interesting. If you must, do it with gas monitoring, a buddy at the mouth, a line, and permission — and even then, don't.
- **Learn the firing schedule** and the re-entry times. That wall at the east end that still smells of powder is telling you something about how recently the air there was unbreathable.
- **Sundays are the best and worst day.** The Lung idles at half speed. The room goes quiet enough to break your heart, and the ventilation halves. Enjoy the quiet in the intake, near the downcast, and don't go deep on a Sunday.

One more thing about the fan: twelve blades at ninety revolutions a minute is a blade-pass frequency of **eighteen hertz**. That is below hearing. It is in your sinuses and your sternum, exactly as described, and eighteen hertz is in the range that makes people feel watched, uneasy, haunted, and slightly nauseous for no reason they can name. Expect a low free-floating dread in your first days. It is not the place, and it is not you. It is a fan. Knowing this helps enormously.

## The living room: assume everything in it wants a piece of you

Four hundred thousand bats, and a shore-line of things that eat what falls off them. Treat the biology as an occupational hazard, not a nature experience.

**The cough is histoplasmosis** or something like it — a fungus in the heaps, inhaled as spores from disturbed guano. Locals may have partial immunity from a childhood of low-dose exposure. You have none, and you will be walking into a heavy inoculum. This is the thing most likely to ruin your year.

- A **loose-fitting PAPR with a hood**, or at minimum a full-face respirator with combined particulate and ammonia cartridges. Half-masks leak, and the ammonia will make you tear up and break the seal.
- Understand the trade: respirators add breathing resistance and heat load. They shorten your excursion. Budget for it.
- Never sweep, dig, or disturb dry guano. Wet down first if you have any legitimate reason to be near a heap — and you probably don't.
- Fresh heaps steam at sixty degrees. That is a compost reactor: hot, oxygen-consuming, gas-generating. Do not put a hand, a foot, or a probe into one.
- **Strip and bag at the bathhouse, shower, wash your hair.** Spores live in hair and in cloth. Do not carry the heaps home in your jacket.

**Rabies or a lyssavirus.** Get pre-exposure vaccination, the full course, well before you go. Never touch a bat, alive or dead, for any reason, including and especially a bat on the ground that seems friendly and needs help. A free-tailed bat's teeth can leave a mark you will not find. If contact happens, you still need post-exposure treatment, and the nearest serum is above the plain, three hundred metres and a wadi away. Sort that logistics chain out *before* you go, not after.

**Leptospirosis** in the warm sump and the warm mud at the east end, courtesy of the rats. Cover every cut with occlusive dressing. No bare feet, ever, anywhere. No wading. Assume the mud in the rusted-frame graveyard is a culture medium.

**Everything else:** bat flies will find you within a minute of standing still; mites and soft ticks at the roost margins; dermestid larval hairs cause an allergic dermatitis that people mistake for bites; cockroach antigen will trigger asthma if you're prone; the mosquito column over the sump is best treated as a physical object you do not put your arm into, whatever the local children do.

### The rule you will not expect

**Do not bring insecticide into this room.** No permethrin-treated clothing — which is otherwise the single best thing a traveler to a hot buggy place can own. No coils, no foggers, no aerosol repellent, no ultrasonic pest repeller, no fly spray "just for the room."

This town nearly died once because of what farmers sprayed on alfalfa three hundred metres up. There is a Treaty of the Fields. There is a man whose entire job is talking to farmers about spray. The loop runs bats → guano → alfalfa → moths → bats, and it is tight enough that a fifth of the colony vanished in a bad decade. Your treated shirt is a small thing, but the principle is not small, and if it is discovered you will deserve the reception you get.

Use **mechanical barriers instead**: a fine mesh head-net, a mesh mid-layer, gloves, cuffs closed, a hood. Repellent on skin sparingly, and nothing sprayed.

And by the same logic: **bring no living thing.** No plant, no seed, no soil, no culture, no pet, no sourdough starter you're proud of. The cats arrived in 1961 to solve the rats and now have their own budget line. Decontaminate your boots and bags before descent as if you were entering a disease-free zone, because you are.

## Light discipline: the one unforgivable act

The town is dim by law, not poverty. Forty hooded sodium bulbs, aimed down, nothing above four metres, nothing unhooded. The colony is protected by ordinance and the colony is the payroll.

Your instinct — the completely reasonable, almost involuntary instinct of a person standing in a forty-metre room whose ceiling has never been seen whole — will be to point a light up.

Don't. Not once, not for a second, not "just a quick look." You could scatter a roost, drop pups, and cost this town its living. It is the local equivalent of firing a gun in a hospital.

Practical measures:

- A **red-filtered headlamp with a snoot or hood**, aimed at your feet. Tape over the stray indicator LEDs on your gear. Disable your phone's torch. Flash off, permanently, on every camera.
- No drones. No lasers. No flare, no light-painting, no long-exposure "just one frame with a torch."
- If you want images, work in the amber that exists: fast glass, high ISO, a colour chart you'll never use because sodium light is essentially monochrome at 589 nm. Accept that your photographs will be amber and grain and black, because that is what the place looks like, and the honest record is better than the lie.
- Ask before adding *any* light, including in your own room. Keep bright things behind a closed door.

The ceiling floods once a decade for an hour, and people come out and stand in the street and don't say anything. You will almost certainly not be there for it. **Making peace with never seeing the roof is the actual preparation for this trip.** The ceiling here is a sound. If you cannot be content with an unseen thing, this place will frustrate you badly.

## Ears, and the Sunday problem

Ninety decibels of bats as a floor, a hundred and five at the jaw crusher. People are deaf by fifty and proud of sleeping through anything except quiet.

- **Double protection at the works**: plugs plus muffs.
- For general walking, **level-dependent muffs or filtered plugs** so you can still hear speech and drip. Full occlusion all day would protect you into missing the entire point.
- **Bone-conduction comms with plugged ears** work beautifully here — you will hear your companion better than the locals hear each other. This is also a genuinely useful gift, if you're wondering what to bring.
- Learn the **hand signs of Drip Street** before you arrive if you can get a recording or a chart. It is a courtesy comparable to learning "please" and "thank you."
- Face people when you speak. Don't cover your mouth. If you're masked, carry a **slate and grease pencil** — a whiteboard is the local notepad. Don't shout at a deaf person; shouting distorts the mouth. The bar is loud; loudness is not anger.
- You will not sleep through it. Nobody who arrives does. Bring plugs for the first three nights and then, on your first Sunday, take them out.

That Sunday is the reason to go. The mill down, the colony out over the alfalfa, the Lung at half, ninety decibels gone, and ten thousand small clocks arriving out of the dark. Locals come out of their houses to stand in the street for it. Be in the street with them and don't talk.

## Rot: the slow destruction of everything you own

Ammonia plus permanent condensate is a solvent for your entire kit. Copper goes green then goes to lace. Aluminium pits. Galvanising blooms and flakes. Leather rots. Wool rots. Paper delaminates. Ink feathers. Glue lets go. Nothing dries, ever.

- **No bare copper, no aluminium, no galvanised anything** in load-bearing or safety-critical roles. 316 stainless, titanium, engineering plastics, silicone, closed-cell foam. Carbon fibre is fine; check the fittings.
- **Rubber or polyurethane boots, not leather.** Two pairs. The spoil has edges that cut boots and the perimeter walk ruins them; that is not a metaphor. Rinse them; they will not dry; accept it.
- **All synthetic clothing, three times what you think you need**, and treat it as consumable. It will smell of ammonia forever. Bag it before you take it home or leave it there.
- **Electronics**: sealed, conformal-coated if you can, stored with desiccant. And mind the direction of the condensation — your gear lives at thirty-two in the intake and the roost air's dew point is near thirty-nine. Carry a cold camera into the warm wet zone and it will run with water instantly, inside and out. Let gear warm up sealed, or keep it in the warm zone in a dry box.
- **Eyewear fogs permanently** going from intake to roost. Anti-fog coatings are useless at ninety-five percent. Keep lenses warm, or use contacts (but ammonia and dust make contacts unpleasant), or simply accept a period of blindness at every transition and plan your movements around it.
- **Waterproof paper and pencil.** Pens fail. Notebooks turn to felt.
- **Skin**: you will be wet for weeks. Maceration, heat rash, tinea in every fold, trench foot in forty-degree air. Wash, dry with a towel that never quite dries, dust with antifungal powder, air your feet in your dehumidified room, change socks twice a day whether or not they seem dirty. Bring topical antifungal, chlorhexidine, and a lot of dry socks in a sealed bag.
- Rats will chew soft bags. Cats will find anything that smells of food. Store everything hard-sided and latched. **Eat nothing uncovered** — during the exodus it rains guano for two hours and twenty minutes, continuously, and the whole town covers everything for exactly that reason. A brimmed hat and a hood at the foot of the Flue; don't look up with your mouth open.

## The body you should arrive in

- Medical clearance with an emphasis on **cardiac** fitness — heat illness kills through cardiovascular strain more than through temperature alone.
- **Rabies pre-exposure series.** Tetanus current (rust, warm mud, sharp spoil). Hepatitis A, typhoid, standard water-borne cover. Discuss leptospirosis prophylaxis with a travel physician.
- A kit that includes: ORS in bulk, electrolyte tabs, a core or ear thermometer, doxycycline and a broad-spectrum antibiotic, antihistamines, topical antifungal, occlusive dressings, litres of eye irrigant (ammonia splash), and a written heat-illness protocol that someone else is holding.
- **Ask about medevac before you descend.** There is one hoist and three hundred metres of shaft between you and a plain with a wadi in it. A heat-stroke patient must be cooled *before* being moved, not carried up hot. Know who makes that decision, who has ice, and where the vehicle is.
- **Vitamin D and a clock.** No UV, no dawn, no dusk that you can see. Your circadian rhythm will drift and your mood will follow it. Bring a timed bright light box for use *inside a closed room only*, melatonin, and a wristwatch set to surface time. Keep surface hours stubbornly.

## When to go, and for how long

**Late June**, if you can manage it, for the coin of light. For about twenty minutes around noon in the last week of the year's longest days, a disc the width of a dinner plate comes three hundred metres down the Flue and lands on one paving stone. The town keeps its calendar by it. Children stand in it and are told to remember what it felt like.

Etiquette for that: it is not your moment. Do not stand in it first. Do not photograph children in it without asking their parents and probably their grandmother. Do not put your instrument on the stone. If you're invited in, go in, stand still, and afterwards say what it felt like, because that is what the festival is for.

Also arrange to be there for:

- **At least two Sundays.** The first one you'll spend recovering from the noise; the second one you'll actually hear.
- **One dusk exodus**, from a covered position near but not under the Flue, with a hood on. Two hours and twenty minutes of slow drain, not a burst; cold wind on your neck; ammonia and hot fur and something sweetish underneath. It is the only cold you will feel down there and people go and stand in it on purpose.
- **A shift**, if the Concern will let you, with the twenty-minutes-and-trough rhythm, so you understand the working day physically rather than sympathetically.

**Stay three weeks if you can.** Everything here is slow — a metre a year, a hand's breadth, three-tenths of a degree a decade, one percent a year. A short visit shows you a hot dark room. A long one starts to show you the clock. Base yourself in the intake, or sleep on the plain and commute down the shaft like a shift worker; both are defensible. Sleep above if you're heat-sensitive. Sleep below if you want to know what everyone falls asleep to.

## Manners, and the things that are not yours

Get a **sponsor** — the Concern runs the room, and you should arrive as somebody's guest rather than as an unaffiliated body in a hazardous industrial space. Sign whatever needs signing. Tag in and tag out. Find, early, the fan engineer, the man who walks the roof-line with a bucket and a torch, and the spray liaison. Those three know the room's three truths.

**Do not bring:** insecticide, ultrasonic devices, drones, lasers, animals, plants, seeds, soil, perfume, aerosols of any kind. Do not bring an unsolicited engineering proposal.

**Do not take:** guano (pathogen), bats (crime and disease), calcite (theft), roots (sacrilege), a rusted bolt, a "small piece" of anything. Take recordings and take notes. Understand that no recorder you own will capture eighteen hertz, and no recording will carry the smell, and you will have to accept an incomplete souvenir.

**Do not touch the soda straw on Terrace Two.** Nine centimetres in forty-one years, one bolt hole, one drip, roped off under its own hooded lamp. Skin oil stops calcite deposition dead. It is the only thing in the room that water has made and the school brings children to see it. Look, and step back, and don't breathe on it.

**Do not touch the fig roots.** Nine, of which one is already dead and hanging dry. Don't climb them, don't cut a sample, don't tie anything to them.

**Do not say:**
- "It's like a cave." It isn't, and they know precisely what it is.
- "Why don't they just seal the Flue / cool the whole thing / net the bats." Every one of these is either illegal, thermodynamically absurd, or a proposal to end the town.
- "Have you thought about planting another fig?"

**Do not raise, unless invited:** the drip lessening. The aquifer dropping eighty centimetres a year. Root number nine. The fig in the wadi scheduled to come out when the field is re-graded. The old men say the roof used to rain, and nobody has said out loud what the rest of it means. This is a grief in progress, in a room where the water is the only thing making anything and it is running out. If someone says it out loud to you, that is a considerable gift. Receive it and don't fix it.

**Leave the rat snake in the cable tray on Terrace Two alone.** It has tenure.

## What it will do to you

Prepare for the sensory flatness. No horizon; the longest sightline in your world becomes two hundred and forty paces. No green but forty halos of algae, a crate of chives, and a lemon tree that has never fruited. No weather but the fan, and the town's word for both is the same word. Amber pools with black between. A smell you will stop noticing and then find in your clothes six months later.

Prepare for the tenderness that will ambush you. A lemon tree in a tarred tub on wheels, pushed to the foot of a shaft once a year to catch a plate of sunlight. That is not quaint. That is a town of deaf people in a hot hole keeping an appointment with the sun, and it will get you when you're not braced.

Prepare to be inside something alive. Four hundred thousand mammals over your head, two deep, making their own warm wet air, and a sound like frying, like rain on paper, like applause a long way off. Some people find this unbearable and some find it the most companionable dark they have ever been in. Find out which you are in the first three days, in the intake, with an easy exit.

And prepare for coming back up. The plain will be too big. The sky under its whiteness will feel like a lid taken off. The first breath of dry air will feel like a solvent going into your lungs, and you will stand in an alfalfa field with your sweat suddenly, miraculously, working, and understand for the first time what your body has been doing all along. You may flinch at birds. You will almost certainly wake at three in the morning listening for the fan, hear nothing, and feel a lurch of pure animal alarm before you remember where you are.

That's the four-hour clock in you, learned in three weeks. It's the local childhood, acquired late.

---

## The last thing

Everything you'll be told to do down there is about the short clock: air, heat, gas, light, spores, time limits, the buddy's veto. Do all of it, unromantically, because the room is genuinely indifferent to whether you find it beautiful.

But the reason to go is the long clock. The rock is unborn. There is no soil, no talus, no rounding, no patina — every building is a piece of the hole beside it and after ninety years the drill marks are still crisp. The machines dissolve and the stone looks newly cut. The landforms have not happened yet. The drainage is a set of gutters and a pump. And in the worked-out corner the seep comes up at fifty-two degrees, and the water keeps arriving, ten thousand small clocks, doing the patient work that will eventually round the spoil and skin the walls in flowstone and hang straws from twelve thousand bolt holes, until there is a real cave here with a town-shaped absence in the floor of it.

So: go on a Sunday. Stand in the street with everyone else, in the amber, with your plugs out and your lamp off and your respirator hanging around your neck because the colony is out over the alfalfa and the mill is down and the air is as good as it gets.

And listen to water arriving.
