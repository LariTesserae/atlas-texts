# Preparing for the Weeping Table

## The one thing to understand before you pack

Nothing here has ever frozen. Not once, not in a hundred thousand years. The water comes out of the rock at blood temperature and the air is a held dome of warm humidity and the animals themselves are a heat source. There is no winter, no frost, no dry season, no nightly die-back. On most worlds the cold comes along twice a year and scours the surfaces clean, and everything — your boots, your tent, your lungs, your food — gets a reprieve.

Here there is no reprieve. You are stepping into an incubator that has been running continuously since before your species had a name, and you are the most interesting thing to arrive in it in a very long time. You are warm, wet, salty, phosphorus-rich, and covered in a film.

So: **pack against being eaten, not against being attacked.** Almost nothing on the plateau wants to hurt you. There are no teeth sized for you, no predators interested in you, no venom worth mentioning. The shrikes impale rasps; the sailers filter midges; the rasps rasp stone. The danger is not violence. The danger is *incorporation* — being slowly colonized, overgrown, dissolved, absorbed into a fabric that has been quietly burying its own doorways for two hundred years.

Everything below follows from that.

## Your body

**Altitude, but wrong.** Three thousand meters means about seventy percent of the oxygen you're used to. But it's warm and saturated, so you will not feel like you're at altitude — you'll feel like you're in a swamp, and you'll sweat, and the sweat won't leave, and then you'll climb four storeys of stairs and go gray at the edges of your vision. Warm plus wet plus thin is a genuinely nasty combination and it catches fit people out. Give yourself four days of near-idleness. The locals say you stop hearing the roar in four days; that's also roughly when your blood catches up. Treat the fourth day as your real arrival.

**Your lungs are the main event.** The drift is thick enough to taste. Every window in the city is double-meshed for a reason, and the reason is that a hundred thousand years of unbroken biological warmth has produced an airborne load with no off switch. Expect a chronic particulate-and-spore exposure unlike anything on a green world. Guano dust from the terraces is the classic one — eighty meters of stratified droppings, actively mined, dry in the working faces, and mining it will put fungal and bacterial aerosols into you that your immune system has no filing system for.

Bring: a proper half-face respirator with plenty of spare cartridges, and a second one, because your filters are a foreign standard and you will not be able to buy replacements that fit. Bring a soft brush and a habit of using it. Bring antihistamines and an inhaler even if you've never needed one. Assume that any local antibiotic you're offered is aimed at a different kingdom of life. Assume your own empiric antibiotics are also aimed at a different kingdom of life. **Wound care here is mechanical, not chemical:** irrigate, debride, keep it covered, keep it oiled, check it twice a day, and do not wait to see whether a small cut "settles down." Small cuts do not settle down here. They bloom.

**Eyes.** Goggles, not spectacles. The drift will find the gap under ordinary glasses and you will spend your visit blinking. Do not wear contact lenses; a spore under a lens on this plateau is a week of misery. And a specific, easy-to-miss point: **acidulating phosphate rock releases fluoride gases.** The Nitrate Houses will etch glass. Long-term workers' spectacles go frosted at the edges; their teeth tell you which house they work in. Bring plastic lenses and a spare pair, keep upwind of the acid house, and if your eyes and the back of your throat sting in a way that feels *sharp* rather than *ammoniacal*, leave immediately and don't go back that day.

**Kidneys.** This is the one nobody thinks of. The entire economy is a phosphorus pump. The pans are fertilized with phosphate liquor, the felt grows in it, the rasps eat the felt, and you eat the rasps and the felt and drink the water. You will be taking in a mineral load your body evolved to treat as scarce. Ask for water from the covered leats *above* the pans — first water, head water, whatever they call the upstream take — and drink that rather than the lower reaches. Don't make a lifelong habit of the gray-liquor-grown food. If you have any kidney history at all, this trip is a conversation with your doctor first.

**Skin and hair.** Cut your hair short. Oil your skin — rasp oil is the universal local product, cheap, and it works as a barrier because everything here uses it for exactly that. Felt will find your hairline, your eyebrows, the corners of your eyes, the seams of your clothes. Being oiled and groomed is not vanity here; it's the local equivalent of washing your hands.

**And the withdrawal.** No plants means no coffee, no tea, no tobacco, no willow bark, no opium. Their pharmacopeia will be fermentation-derived and surgical and, I'd guess, very good at wounds and very bad at pain. If you are caffeine-dependent, taper *before* you go. Caffeine withdrawal plus hypoxia plus a province-sized struck bowl humming through your sternum at three in the morning is a genuinely awful first four days, and it is entirely avoidable.

## Your kit

**Leave the organic world behind.** No wood. No leather. No cotton, linen, paper, cork, wicker, or canvas. Not because it's forbidden — because it's *food*, and because a wooden object here is a screaming biosecurity event. Your boots' leather will go soft and slimy in a week. A paperback in your bag will be a gray quilt in a month. Your grandmother's photograph will be eaten. Laminate what you love, or copy it onto metal, or leave it home.

What survives: stainless, titanium, glass, silicone, glazed ceramic, and the better fluoropolymers. What dies faster than you'd expect: copper, brass, silver, and anything with a circuit board. Ammonia plus sulfur plus permanent wetness is a corrosion regime designed to eat your electronics, and stray current from a hundred and forty years of wet DC traction doesn't help. Bring cheap sacrificial optics. Assume your camera will fog internally and then grow something in the lens. Bring grease and oil rather than desiccant — desiccant is a joke here, it saturates in an hour.

Footwear: rubber and synthetic, aggressively siped soles, because everything underfoot is wet polished carbonate with a millimeter of living lubricant on it. But you'll be taking your boots off at every threshold, all day, forever — so bring slip-ons for indoors and expect to buy local clogs in the first week and wear them for the rest of your life. Ask what's in the boot sump before you put your good boots in it. It will be lime, or oil, or something acidic, and it will not care about your warranty.

**Do not bring anything sharp that stands up.** This is half practical and half social. Practically: the shrikes use every spike and bolt and pointed end the city leaves standing as a larder, so your antenna, your tripod, your tent pole, your fence picket will be decorated with impaled rasps within a day, and you will be disliked for it. Socially: everything here is rounded. Two hundred years of bodies have worn the doorframes into hourglasses. Sharpness reads as unfinished, foreign, faintly obscene. Round your edges. File your fittings. Arrive already worn-looking if you can manage it.

Other things worth having: a four-gas monitor (see below, this one is not optional), silicone earplugs and a sleeping mask, a synthetic bed net, a hammock rather than a floor bed, a plastic slate instead of a notebook, needle stock and stainless mesh and a few good files as trade goods, and your own painkillers.

**Do not bring:** seeds, fruit, soil, a sourdough starter, a houseplant, a pet, a wooden comb, an apple core in your pocket. You are the largest invasive event this plateau has seen in geological time. Your gut alone is an argument for quarantine. Expect to be hosed, scraped, and possibly held for a few days on arrival, and *cooperate enthusiastically*. The people who fought quarantine here are the reason there's quarantine.

## What will actually kill you, in order

**1. Bad air in low places.** The city sits *in sinkholes*. Bowls. Karst bowls collect heavy gas, and this one has an enormous warm biological oxygen demand underneath it: felt, guano, rendering liquor, a hundred thousand years of it. Carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide pool in bowl-bottoms, cellars, dry pans, trenches, and the low corners of old quarters. I'd bet the pumps and flues and the fanatical never-shut-any-thing-down ethic are as much about ventilation as about nesting. **Never sleep below street level. Never enter a cellar, an empty pan, a leat, or a trench without a monitor and someone standing outside it.** Watch the locals: if they keep a low lamp or a caged animal, learn to read it, and if they don't go down, you don't go down.

**2. Falling.** Two thousand meters of rim, fluted, permanently wet, blood-warm, glossy, and greased with a living film. Everything is rounded, including the handholds. The falls are a bass note you feel in your sternum from ten kilometers off; that's the sound of the water that makes the rock lethal. Stay off the rim in the return wind. Stay off the guano terraces entirely — eighty meters of stratified droppings, undercut by mining, with ammonia pockets in the workings and tens of millions of roosting animals above you.

**3. Electricity.** Arc lamps, trolley wires, house-diameter penstocks, turbines that haven't stopped since before your grandparents, and *everything is wet and conductive*. Ground-level rails greased by things that live under them. Stray current everywhere. Don't lean on railings near substations. Don't touch two metal things at once. Don't assume a wire is dead because it looks old.

**4. Water that moves.** The covered leats and penstocks will take you two thousand meters down and there is no valve you can reach. Pans are only a hand's-depth, but they're slick-bottomed, vast, and full of gantries on rails that run the length of them without stopping for anything as small as you.

**5. Heat and thin air, together, in the rendering works.** Wet-bulb territory. Learn the local work-rest rhythm and follow it even when you feel fine.

**6. Ammonia, acid gases, and the liquor.** Eyes, throat, lungs. Sharp sting means leave.

**7. Infection, slowly.** The one that gets long-stayers rather than visitors.

## Manners

**The threshold is the whole religion.** Every doorway has a scraper bar and a boot sump. You will stop, scrape, stand in the sump, and hold still while someone brushes you down. Refusing is not eccentric; it is bringing the outside into someone's home, and it lands roughly the way tracking sewage across a white carpet would land at home. Let yourself be groomed. It's intimacy. Learn to do it for others.

**Never step in a pan.** A pan is nine years to yield. A mother-culture jar has not been allowed to cool since the founding. Walking into a pan, opening a culture-room door, letting a temperature drop, propping something open — these are not accidents here, they're the destruction of a decade of a hundred people's work. The unforgivable crimes on this plateau are all forms of *contamination* and *interruption*. Not theft. Not violence. Cooling.

**Don't joke about shutting things down.** Nothing is ever shut. A turbine hall closed for a season becomes a nest, and restarting it takes fire and men on ropes. "What if you just turned it off?" is not a fun hypothetical; it's the setup to a horror story everyone here has heard about a real place.

**Don't lie down outside, and don't put anything down and walk away.** Stand still for six minutes and the stubble closes over your bootprint. That's not a metaphor. Sleep on the ground and you will be grown into it. Leave a bag against a wall for a week and you will come back to a bag with a tide-line on it and a conversation about whose wall that is. Property boundaries here are negotiable in a way you'll find upsetting: chimneys go through bedrooms, conveyors go through chapels, and the city builds *through* things. If you want something to stay yours, keep it moving or keep it oiled.

**Don't wear a pan's color.** The blacks and rusts are cultivars — identity, lineage, ownership, guild. Wearing the wrong black is somewhere between wearing another family's tartan and forging a trademark. Buy neutral, buy worn, ask.

**Don't be in a hurry, and don't offer improvements.** A pan takes nine years. A herd circuit takes eleven. A foreman retires with the color he was hired into. Your urgency will read as illness, or grief, or foreign comedy. Your efficiency suggestions will be received politely and filed nowhere, because the thing you want to optimize has been running within two percent for a hundred and forty years and the person you're talking to has watched three generations of visitors be wrong about it.

**Don't swat.** At dusk the air becomes a tissue and your hand comes back wet and dusted with wings. Nothing in that column is interested in you. Swatting achieves nothing, running achieves nothing except a deeper breath and a slip on wet stone. Stand still, breathe through cloth, and let it be weather. Some travelers panic hard at this moment — claustrophobia under an open sky, an inverted agoraphobia. If you're prone to that, know it in advance, and arrange to be indoors with a friend for your first dusk. Then go out for your second one on purpose.

## Rhythm, and where to sleep

The rain falls at four. Twenty minutes, warm, unhurried, and then the sun comes back low and the whole table steams and **the drift rises**. So the hour after the rain is the worst air of the day, and I'd bet folding money that's when the city eats, sits down, closes its shutters, and does its indoor work. Learn that hour. Live inside it.

Take a room on the **east** side, above the ground floor, away from flues and stacks. The lowland drift comes up the escarpment on the afternoon return wind and stains the west-facing walls; west-facing means windward means you're sleeping in the intake. Nothing on the floor stays yours, so hang your bed.

Expect orange arc-light all night, the flues shifting, and the column thrumming like a struck bowl the size of a province. You will stop hearing the roar in four days and it will keep working on you anyway — protect your ears even after it stops bothering you. And notice that silence here is a *civic event*, something scheduled and attended. If you're invited to a silence, go. It's the closest thing they have to a holiday, and it will tell you more about them than a month of tours.

## Arriving and leaving

You come in over the rim, which means you come in through the drift — through layers of four-meter animals with bodies light as paper bags, in tens of millions. Ask your pilot about it and then do what they say. My intuition: cross high, above the breeder layer, and come down inside the dome; or come at dawn, before the return wind loads the column; or come in the twenty minutes of rain, when the air has been scrubbed. That last one might be the whole reason the flight schedule looks the way it does. Ask a local why the timings are what they are and enjoy the answer.

Leaving is harder than arriving, logistically and otherwise. Nothing here is in a hurry, including the paperwork. Build in a season of slack, not a week.

And a small grim item: make your arrangements about your body in advance. There's no wood for a box, the karst will carry anything buried straight down and off the rim, and there is a rendering works that separates animals into oil, shell-lime, meal, and liquor. I would expect the local custom to be exactly what you're afraid it is, and I would expect it to be an honor rather than an indignity. Sign whatever they ask you to sign. It's a way of closing the loop, and closing the loop is what the entire city is for.

## What to actually do with yourself

Stand at the edge of a pan at evening in the warm rain, with the arc lamps coming up orange along the leat, and try to watch the black ribbon thicken. You can't. It's the fastest thing on the plateau and it is still below your threshold.

That's the real preparation, and it isn't in your bag. This is a place where the meaningful rates of change are all slower than a human life, where the animals navigate a gradient no local instrument can detect, where a floor has been dished a meter deep by soles and nobody alive remembers it flat. You will arrive as the fastest object for a thousand kilometers and you will feel, for about a week, like the only thing that's actually happening. Then, if it takes, you'll flip, and you'll start to feel like a mayfly at a cathedral — and that's the trip.

Bring one thing to do that takes longer than you have. Learn a color. Learn one pan's eleven-month scrape. Sit with a foreman and the jar he was hired into. Learn to hear under the roar: the hooks, the fry-hiss of forty thousand rasps per hectare, the pumps, the bowl in the sky.

And warn yourself about the leaving. You'll go home to a world that is green in the way you mean it, and it will look dead. It will be quiet in a way that feels like an outage. You'll keep waiting for four o'clock. You'll put your palm on a wall and it will be dry, and cold, and you will find that you miss being slowly eaten by something that has all the time in the world.
