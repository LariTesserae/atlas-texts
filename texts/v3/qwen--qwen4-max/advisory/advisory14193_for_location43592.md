# THE SUMP
### Preparation notes for the ordinary traveler

---

## 1. First decide which visit you are making

There are three, and only one of them is recommended.

**The lip.** You walk the slumping hillside above the unheard stream, you find the gap by the cold on the back of your hand, you lie down in the beech litter and put your face over it. You will feel the hill exhale. You will smell wet stone and something greener and older than wet stone. If you are quiet for long enough you will hear your own pulse arrive in your ears and understand, in your body, what *attentive* means. This is a complete visit. Most people who go away satisfied went no further than this.

**The shaft.** Eleven feet of air, sharp white walls, a stair on the east side with risers no mason would sign off on, and at the bottom a cold ceiling of water you can break with a finger. You can sit on nothing, in the dark, with your hand on the ninth step and your other hand on the line, and be closer to the room than any photograph will ever get you. This is a serious undertaking and a reasonable one.

**The immersion.** Head and shoulders under, one body-length into the weed, and out. There is no version of this that is a swim, an exploration, or a survey. The room is three feet away in every direction and unfinishable; there is nothing further in to reach. You are not going *somewhere*. You are going *in*, briefly, and coming back, and the whole value of the thing is in the coming back.

Decide now, above ground, in daylight, which one you are making. Do not let the site upgrade you. The Sump is very good at making the next few feet seem like the obvious thing.

---

## 2. What actually kills you here

Not drowning, mostly. Drowning is the ordinary failure and you should prepare for it in the ordinary way — cold shock, gasp reflex, laryngospasm, seven-degree water on the face. But the local hazards are these:

**Permanence.** Nothing mixes, nothing decays, nothing sinks, and anything that comes apart stays apart. This means every small ordinary accident is forever. A dropped glove does not settle into silt and rot; it hangs at the depth you dropped it, the weed grows through it, and it becomes a feature of the room. There are already several features whose origins are legible. Do not add to that collection. Your blood, if you bleed, will not cloud and disperse — it will hang where you cut yourself, in the shape of the moment, at that exact depth, and be there when the hill is cracked open with a hammer on a cool afternoon thousands of years from now.

**Non-arrival.** You can move for an hour and not get anywhere, and it will not feel like being lost, because the walls are always three feet away and the elm root is always just above your shoulder and the pale shear-face is always on your left. The danger is not disorientation. The danger is that you will keep going, because going is what people do, and there is no arriving to stop you.

**Counting.** Nine and one are the same number down there. Every safety procedure you know that involves counting — breaths, kicks, minutes, line markers, turn pressure, *three turns right and you're home* — is void. So is your ability to confirm that you have already done a thing. You will not be able to check.

**Provenance.** Three divers came up. There is no record of who they were. The room keeps objects perfectly and identities not at all. Take this seriously; it is the reason for the tender (§5).

**The furniture.** There is a stair, a doorframe, a palm-sized basin at chest height, and a soft white face with tally marks on it. Assembled, these describe a posture: feet on the ninth step, one palm in the basin, the other hand working the fresh rock. The room is furnished for someone. Do not accept the furniture's invitation.

---

## 3. The body to arrive in

Start six weeks out.

- **Cold-shock discipline.** Repeated supervised face immersion at 6–8 °C until the gasp is a thing you watch happen rather than a thing you do. This is the single highest-value hour of training you will ever do for this site.
- **Ears.** Equalize early, often, gently, and in the air of the shaft *before* you touch water. A ruptured eardrum here means cold water in the middle ear, vertigo, blood, and the loss of the only orientation you have. If you cannot clear, the visit is over. There is no forcing it.
- **Stillness, not fitness.** You need to part weed with your hands, slowly, for a minute or two, without your heart rate climbing. Train long slow isometric work and breath-hold comfort at low exertion. Do not train sprints; the room cannot be hurried through and the attempt only tears things.
- **The non-arrival test.** Blindfolded, in a small cold room, with no task, for one hour. If you cannot do this without escalating distress, do not immerse. If you *love* it, be careful — the people who come back up are the ones who wanted to leave.
- **Ballast.** Bring an obligation, not a curiosity. Curiosity is exactly the food this place supplies without limit and without satisfaction. A dog that needs feeding, a person waiting at the car, a thing due tomorrow: these are what haul you out. Name your reason before you go down and keep it in your mouth like a stone.
- **Trim your fingernails short.** The fracture-face is soft, new, white limestone. The marks were cut with something narrow and hard. Your nails are not nothing.
- **See a dentist.** Nothing loose in your mouth. Do not bite anything down there.
- **Remove every piercing, ring, and piece of jewelry before you kneel at the lip.** A stud is precisely the geometry the room is missing.

## 4. Kit — and the four refusals

### The refusals

**No light below the waterline.** Not a torch, not a glowstick, not a phone screen, not a camera, not a flash. There has never been a photon down there. Every growing tip in the room is aimed at the plate-sized absence above it as if that hole were a lamp. Introduce a real lamp and you become the lamp: every tip in the room will re-aim at you, and duplication is permanent, and nothing here forgets. You will have destroyed the only compass in the place, including on your way out, for everyone, forever. Also, practically: a light shows you six inches of weed and ruins the memory-sight you came to use. Go dark. Learn to touch.

**No edge.** No knife, no shears, no scissors, no screwdriver, no ice pick, nothing narrow and hard and pointed. This is the hard one, because entanglement is your main physical hazard and every instinct says *bring a cutter*. Bring none. Solve entanglement by not getting entangled: a smooth silhouette, no protrusions, hands moving ahead of the body, speed low enough that you never need to cut. If you cannot accept going in without a blade, make the shaft visit instead. Nothing you carry that could inscribe soft white rock should ever cross the waterline.

**No numbers.** No dive computer, no digital gauge, no marked line, no tally of anything, no written notes, no this document. Instruments that show *how much* are fine — a needle, a column, a length, a tension. Instruments that show *how many* are not. Do not count the marks. Counting the marks is the one activity this room appears to exist for; do not volunteer.

**No parts.** One continuous skin. Everything captive, fused, taped, stitched — never clipped, buckled, pinned, or screwed. No zips with pulls, no D-rings, no weight belt (nothing sinks; there is nothing to dump and no reason to carry it), no fins (two large detachable snag-magnets), no loose hood, no separate gloves. A dropped glove is a feature shaped like a hand, and this room already has a basin the size of a palm. Do not give it a glove.

### What to actually wear and carry

- A **one-piece hooded immersion suit**, smooth-skinned, seamless where possible, ~3 mm, with bonded booties and bonded mitts. Nothing beneath it that sheds. Wash it; lint is eternal here.
- **No cylinder.** It will not fit the mouth, it makes exhaust that cannot form bubbles or rise or leave (there are no surfaces below the waterline — your used breath would hang around your head as a lens you would then re-breathe), and inert gas exchange in a place where nothing mixes is not something to experiment with. Breath-hold only. Shallow, brief, tethered.
- **One unbroken line.** Smooth, non-shedding, no sheath fuzz, no knots, no splices, no clips, no markers. Long enough for the shaft plus a body-length plus slack. Anchored above ground to two living beeches. A stitched leather pad lashed over the lip where the line crosses it, because the lip is sharp enough to open a palm and a severed line stays severed. The pad stays above the waterline, in the world where things rot. That is the rule for all gifts.
- **Nothing in your hands.** Empty pockets before you kneel. No phone, no coin, no keys, no lens cap. Kneel on a mat, not on the rim.

### Above ground, waiting

Dry clothes, a warm coat, a hot sweet drink, a way to get a cold-injured person to a road, a written plan left with someone who will act on it. Assume there is no cave rescue here, no chamber, no record-keeping, no one who has ever heard of the hole. There isn't. There are three divers and no names.

## 5. The most important piece of equipment is a person who stays in the ferns

Not a buddy who follows you. A **tender** who never descends, whose entire job is:

- To hold the line with continuous gentle tension and never let go, never tie off and walk away.
- To remember who you are. Out loud, at intervals, in the ordinary air. Your name, where you're from, what you owe tomorrow. The room takes provenance; the tender is where you keep yours.
- To watch a **continuous** process, not a count: a taper candle burned to a groove, a watch with hands, a length of slow cord. When it reaches the mark, they haul, steadily, without asking.
- To wrap you, warm you, and say your name until you say it back correctly.

Brief them on the one rule that overrides everything: **if the line moves, you go with it.** Do not check, do not finish what you were doing, do not take one more look. Checking is counting, and counting is the thing that fails.

---

## 6. Finding it, and not improving it

Go in a dry spell, late in the season, when the hill is low. Not after rain, not in snowmelt. A transient inflow means mixing, and mixing means the arrangement breaks — the yellow leaves begin to rot, the strata trade a molecule, and you will have been the cause of the first decay in two hundred years. Do not do that to a place.

Find it by cold on the hand, by the liverwort rim, by the deer tracks that step over it, by the stream you can hear and not see. Do not find it by pacing and counting; you will not find the same hole twice that way.

**Do not widen it. Do not chip the lip. Do not peel the liverwort to see what the rock mouth really is.** Probe gently with a bare hand. If it does not admit you as you are, that is the answer, and the lip visit is your visit. Every fresh white fracture-face you make is a new page in the only book down there. The hammer is reserved for someone else, on a cool afternoon, a very long time from now.

## 7. The descent

Feet first, arms overhead, exhaling, line in one hand. Equalize in the air. Take the sharp faces on your back and hips, not your palms. Do not put a hand flat on anything white.

At the bottom, the water is a cold ceiling. Break it with a finger first. Let your face meet it and let the gasp happen while your mouth is still in air. Then, when your body has stopped arguing, slip under — one body-length, no more — and stop.

You will not sink and you will not float. There is no buoyancy rescue here; an unconscious body hangs where it is and the weed grows through it. The line is the only way up. That is why you never let go of it.

Everything in the room is aimed at the hole. You come through the hole. For a moment you are the only thing that has ever arrived from the direction of the sun, and every blind green face in the place is turned toward you, and you will know this without seeing it. It is the most intense second of the visit. Let it pass through you. Do not lean into it. Do not accept the job.

---

## 8. Conduct below the waterline

*These are deliberately unnumbered. Do not number them.*

- One hand for the line. One hand for yourself — thumb pressed into your own palm, the ring of your own grip, so you know where you end. You are, briefly, another thickening; the boundary of your body will get opinionated. Keep checking it by touch, not by counting fingers.
- Read the plants. Every tip points at the mouth. That is home. Follow the majority.
- **Do not follow the moss that grows toward the cut stone.** It is the only disagreement in the room and it points at the marks. Turn away from it. It is not lying; it simply wants something other than the hole, and you should not find out what.
- Navigate by rubble, root, and weed. If your palm finds a surface as flat and pale as cut cake, take the hand away and put it behind you. Do not knock on it. Do not press an ear to it. It is a face of the hill, and behind the plate was this, already furnished. Walls here are not necessarily boundaries.
- Do not stand on the stair. Do not put your feet on the ninth step. Do not put your palm in the basin. Do not pass through the doorframe. Do not touch the lintel; the snail has been there since before anyone was keeping records and it is not yours to move.
- Do not grab anything that moves. The long thing may be an eel and may be a root that learned to leave. It is not hunting — nothing here eats — but anything that separates you from your line has done it permanently. The elm root is always above your shoulder. If what you're holding isn't, let go.
- Make no sound. No speech, no humming, no clicking. Your body is already obscene down there; the wet click of your eyelid is a public event. Do not add to it.
- Take nothing. One snail. Three isopods. A population issued once and never renewed. A single removal is an eternal subtraction.
- Leave nothing. No offering, no sample jar, no coin, no name scratched anywhere. Nothing decays. Your litter is a permanent new initial condition.
- If you feel a low tone in your sternum, that is the water column adjusting, on a scale of decades. It is not something arriving. Come up anyway, calmly; you have been given a rare thing and should not spend it being frightened.
- If you find yourself with your feet on a step and your hand in a bowl, you have been assembled. Go up.

---

## 9. When to come up

Not after a number of anything. Come up when:

- the line moves;
- the cold arrives as an insult rather than as information;
- your ears stop clearing;
- you have bled at all;
- you notice you are trying to *arrive*;
- you have found the marks;
- you begin to feel that staying would be a kind of permanence worth having.

That last one is the important one, and it will not feel like a warning. It will feel like peace. The yellow beech leaves in the weed are perfectly preserved and they are not alive. Being kept is not being remembered. Being a feature is not being immortal; it is being *legible*, forever, to whoever cracks the nodule open. Refuse it while refusal is still cheap.

---

## 10. Coming out

Break the surface with your hand first, then your face. The shaft's air will seem hot. Get the ferns under your palms. Let the tender say your name and say it back, out loud, correctly, before you stand up.

Then: dry clothes, warm drink, no driving, watch for afterdrop, get the cut cleaned and closed. Sit somewhere ordinary and let the world be loud.

You will not be able to say what color anything was, including your hands. This is normal and it does not resolve. Don't chase it. Don't go back down to check. Checking is counting.

---

## 11. If you left something

Tell someone. Write it down above ground, where writing still works. Then never return.

Understand what you have done: the object is hanging at the depth you dropped it, the weed is growing through it, and it will be there, unchanged, when the room is complete. If what you left was narrow and hard, then in about eleven years there will be a new mark on the white face, sharp, with stone-dust sitting in the groove, and it will not have been cut by anything that came down the shaft.

Do not go back to see. You already know.

---

## 12. Pack for the hammer

Here is the whole ethic of the place, and it fits in a sentence: **everything you take below the waterline is a message to the person who opens the hill with a hammer on a cool afternoon in some thousands of years, and it will be perfectly legible.**

So go in with nothing that can come apart, nothing that can write, nothing that can shine, nothing that can be counted, and no part of yourself you are willing to have held up to the light. Go in for one body-length and one held breath, with a line in your hand and your name in someone else's mouth, and come out.

And then walk back down the hill through the beech litter, past the moss on the north sides of things, listening to the stream you can't see, in a world where leaves go brown and cuts heal and things are allowed to end — and love it, hard, for that. That's the souvenir. It's the only one that isn't permanent, which is exactly why it's worth carrying.
