# Before You Go Down

## 1. Understand what you are actually visiting

You are not visiting a cavern city. You are visiting the inside of a wind instrument that has been playing one chord since before your species had ears, and which has been slowly tuned — by masonry, by forestry, by the drilled femurs of the dead — into the shape that sustains the most life. Six thousand people live inside a note the way you live inside an atmosphere. The note is not scenery. It is the food supply, the nervous system, the clock, the graveyard, and the law.

And you are a bag of warm brine with a spark in your pocket.

Hold both of those facts in mind at once, because every rule below descends from one of them. In this world water is a burn and silence is a predator, and a human being is, by construction, a leaking water vessel that has spent its whole life enjoying quiet. You are not neutral here. You are not a tourist. You are a weather event and an ignition source, and the only reason you will be permitted past the twentieth plug is that someone has vouched for you and agreed to be responsible for what you drip.

## 2. Be honest about the budget

An ordinary human visit is measured in **hours**, not days. Not because anyone will hurry you, but because:

- Your hearing is spent almost immediately. At a hundred and thirty decibels of established fact, unprotected ears take permanent damage in minutes. There is no plug, muff, or helmet that saves you, because the fundamental arrives through your skeleton and no amount of felt on the outside of a body stops a body from ringing.
- The air is a light naphtha. It is breathable in the sense that it will not choke you, and it is a solvent in the sense that it will take the fat out of your skin, the clarity out of your thinking, and — if you let your heart race — the rhythm out of your chest.
- You are sealed in a garment at thirty-eight degrees with no shade, no evaporative cooling, and no ice, because ice is water. Your thermal ceiling is the real itinerary.
- There is no night and no quiet in which to sleep. You will not sleep here. Plan to arrive rested and leave before you need to.

Go with a baseline audiogram, a dental clearance, and a signed understanding with yourself that you are trading a measurable piece of your hearing for this. Do not go believing you will keep it. People who go believing that come out angry.

## 3. Prepare the body

**Teeth and skull first.** The buzz you feel on the plain is your dentition resonating. Anything loose in your head becomes an instrument: a failing crown, a cracked filling, a sinus full of mucus, an unfused old fracture. See a dentist. Get everything solid. Go with clear sinuses and equalizable ears — a head cold turns the descent into barotrauma, and a sneeze in the chamber is a spray of contraband.

**Then the heart.** Solvent vapors sensitize the myocardium to your own adrenaline. This is the single most under-appreciated killer for visitors to hydrocarbon worlds: the physiology is tolerant until you are frightened, and then a normal fright becomes an arrhythmia. So: no alcohol for days before, no stimulants, no caffeine loading, nothing that makes your pulse jumpy. And train calm the way you would train a muscle, because you will be frightened. The infrasound itself manufactures dread — a hand laid flat on the sternum, the conviction that someone is standing behind you, grief with no object. That is nineteen cycles a second doing arithmetic on your organs. Learn to recognize it as weather. Practice, beforehand, the discipline of *feeling terror and not running*.

**Then the gut and the balance.** Whole-body vibration loosens the bowels and smears the eyes; around the fundamental you may see the walls shimmer and the nine masses breathe. Eat light and dry. Empty everything before the descent. Assume nausea and be unimpressed by it.

## 4. The water discipline — this is the whole of your ethics

You carry sixty liters of brine into a place where a wet hand leaves a scar on a leaf for a season and a drop of rain falls like a burn.

Encapsulate. Double skin, sealed seams, no exposed flesh, wicking underlayer running to a condensate trap, urine to a closed bladder, breath through a moisture-recovery loop so that your exhalation does not fog the world. Assume every garment failure is an emergency. The rule is simple and non-negotiable: **if you leak, you climb.** Not "finish the terrace," not "say goodbye." You signal your sponsor and you go up.

Which means, practically:

- No spitting. No nose-clearing. No weeping. Learn to notice the pre-cry state and shut it down; you will want to cry at least twice down there, and the correct place to do it is on the plain afterward, in the glare, where water is merely strange.
- No bleeding. Dry air plus vibration plus a solvent haze means nosebleeds. Pack dry, mechanically, and know how.
- No wet food, no fruit, no drink carried openly, no canteen you might set down on a leaf. Your lunch is a hazard. Eat in whatever sealed wet-closet the doorkeepers keep near the throat, and ask for it before you need it.
- Understand your corpse. If you die in the chamber you are a rainstorm falling into a jungle. Your sponsor knows this. Your escort knows this. It is not morbidity, it is the reason they will be so insistent about the tether, and the reason you should be grateful rather than insulted.

Above all: **do not bring glass, and do not bring a clear bottle full of anything.** A curved transparent vessel left in a sunshaft is a lens, and the whole city's fire safety rests on the fact that only a licensed guild is allowed to aim light.

## 5. The fire discipline

There has never been fire here. The air is a solvent and the foliage is oiled paper. You must arrive incapable of making a spark.

Leave behind: steel in any form, ferrocerium, lighters, matches, batteries, phones, cameras, flashes, powered tools, anything with a switch, anything with a brush motor. Leave behind synthetics that hold a charge — fleece, nylon shells, hook-and-loop fasteners — because in air this dry, with felt and bast rope everywhere, you can build a static potential simply by walking, and discharge it into a haze.

Arrive instead in cotton or bare conductive mesh, in conductive soles, with a copper braid trailing from your ankle to the ground. Discharge deliberately at the head of the throat and again at the last plug, and let the doorkeepers watch you do it; they will have a ritual for this even if they have never had a visitor, because they have rituals for everything that touches the note.

Take no lenses. Take no binoculars. Take no polished curved metal. If you need vision correction, ask the lens-grinders' guild for permission and expect a long, slow, offended conversation. They are not being superstitious. They are the fire department.

## 6. The noise discipline, which runs backwards from every instinct you have

Your instinct will be to muffle. Suppress it.

**Do not quilt your body.** Felt is for rooms. Padding on your soles, your palms, your skull, your spine reduces your coupling to the world, and your coupling to the world is your only distance sense, your only alarm, and — as you will learn if a membrane ever passes over you — your only way of knowing where your hands are. Wear thin gloves with a hard palm plate. Wear rigid boots with no soft midsole. If you wear a shell against falling seed-pods, wear it hard and close-coupled, not padded. A soft helmet is a blindfold.

**Ear strategy: heavy protection in the throat, light or none in the chamber.** The chimney is the worst acoustic place on the journey — a hand-cut flue concentrating the whole roar, forty-one plugs each taking a little off and none taking enough. Protect hard on the way down. Then, in the volume, open up, or keep one ear naked. This sounds insane. Here is the reasoning: your ears are going to be damaged regardless, hearing is useless in a hundred and thirty decibels of continuous fact, and *a personal silence is exactly the predator's signature*. If you seal yourself into a private hush you will not notice the notch until it folds around you. The deaf of this city are safer than a muffled foreigner, because they never stopped feeling the floor.

Do not bring noise-cancelling devices. Active cancellation is the manufacture of an absence. Best case it blinds you; worst case it advertises you.

**Carry a bite-peg.** A short rod of hard salt or copper you can clench between your molars, coupling the world straight into your skull. It is how you will read a seam, test a floor, and hear a tap without ears. Locals do this with their whole skeleton, unconsciously, from infancy. You will have to do it on purpose, constantly, like a novice checking a mirror.

**Carry two passive alarm reeds.** A tuned wire under tension, worn against the wrist bone, driven continuously by the chamber's roar; when the buzz stops, the world has been subtracted from you and you have perhaps a second. No batteries, no sparks, nothing to fail electronically. Two, because one can break, and a broken reed and a membrane feel identical. And **learn the tide tables**: the note bends a whole tone twice a day, and a reed tuned to the high bend goes quiet at the low bend for entirely innocent reasons. Carry one for each. Test them at every tide. A traveler who cannot retune his own alarm is a traveler who will eventually ignore it.

## 7. Language, before you descend

Learn two hundred signs and thirty tap patterns. Not thirty words — thirty *rhythms*, with the pauses intact. The city's name is four beats and a pause, and the pause is not decoration; it is the part that makes it a name. A foreigner who taps without gaps is screaming gibberish into everyone's wrists.

- **Three beats, no pause, three beats** is the membrane alarm. Never rehearse it. Never drum idly. Your fidgeting is speech here, and you will say something obscene with your fingers on a rope without knowing.
- A house's striking-plate is bedded into a live seam and reaches the people inside it directly. Knocking on a stranger's plate is putting your hand on their body. Do not touch plates uninvited. Announce, then wait.
- Get a hammer fitted locally, worn on the hip. Do not bring your own; yours is steel and yours is a spark. Expect to be given a tap-name, and expect it to mean *wet*, or *the one who must not drip*, or something worse and funnier. Answer to it. Do not explain that you have a real name. Everything you have is unsayable here, which is the point.
- Do not offer anyone earplugs, hearing aids, surgery, or pity. Deafness at twelve is citizenship. Do not try to teach a child to speak. Do not use your voice in a hush room without being asked; that quilted, gimbal-hung quiet is where language is learned with a mother's voice in it, and it is the most private thing this culture has. If you are lent one to rest in, you have been given something enormous. Sign, and be still, and do not photograph it.

## 8. Movement

There is no shadow anywhere and no direction to the light. You will lose shape-from-shading, and with it your ability to judge distance, size, and the slope of the floor. The nine masses will refuse to resolve into nine things of one kind. The Empty will read as a short walk and be two hundred paces of deep dry fines over a naphtha seep.

So: **a rigid probe staff, copper-shod, always in contact with the ground.** It finds soft tar pockets, it tests whether stone is live or sugary, and it conducts the world into your palm. Learn to distinguish the ring of a good seam from the dead thud of muffling stone before you go; practice on salt blocks at home until you can do it bored.

**Never lose contact with a live seam.** Poverty here is being out of contact; the crumbly districts eat a tap in three meters, and to wander into one is to disappear from the city's nervous system entirely. No one will come for you, because no one will be able to hear you, and you will not be able to hear yourself. Do not gawk there either. It is not picturesque. It is people who learn of a death a week late.

**Tether to your partner at twenty to thirty paces** — long enough that a ten-meter ribbon cannot take you both, short enough to haul. Use hard-drawn copper wire: it rescues you, it grounds you, and it carries a tap. One object, three jobs, no sparks.

**Do not lean, hang, pad, or pitch anything against a wall.** Your body is already a small baffle; standing in a flute for a long conversation shades a groove and starves what grows downstream of it. A tent, a mat, a jacket hung to dry, a pack propped in a scallop — these are *stealing noise*, and stealing noise is the capital crime, and the sentence is to be carried into the middle of the Empty and left, which is not a killing but is close enough. You will not be executed for ignorance, probably. You will be exiled, and your sponsor will pay for it in seam-rights, and you will have done a real injury to something that took a hundred generations to tune.

## 9. The membranes

Three of them. They nest in the neck. They are ten-meter ribbons of tissue that absorbs sound perfectly, and they move with almost no visible motion, and you will not see one.

You will notice a hole.

Drill this until it is below thought: **when the reed stops, when the bite-peg goes dead, when the click of your own teeth stops coming back to you — do not run, and do not fall.** Running spikes your heart in a solvent atmosphere and may kill you before the membrane does. Falling is what the membrane is waiting for; it folds around what falls. Go rigid, keep the staff planted, keep your weight over your feet, and signal — three, no pause, three — hard, into the seam, with the hammer, while you can still find the hammer. Your partner is twenty-five paces away and outside the notch and will feel your tap arrive in their own bones at four kilometers a second. That is the whole rescue plan. There is no other.

Stay out of the Empty. Stay near the fluted walls. Watch the locals' hands rather than the air; watch for a place where the drifting fines hang wrong. If a Listener takes your wrist, let them. They are hush-raised, hush-kept, paid in the best seams, half-mad from delicate sounds they cannot stop hearing, and dead young. They are the only people in the world who have heard oil dripping through foliage at the brown hour, and they are the reason you will get out. Do not ask them to describe it. They will try, and it will cost them something, and you will not understand.

## 10. The trees, the dead, and the light

Do not climb a tree uninvited. Do not touch a leaf — your glove is a wet hand to it, and the scar lasts a season. Do not enter the three glassy scars where districts of the crown died and hang ringing wrong; they are named and mourned and no one goes in, and "no one" includes you.

At the brown hour the plain's dust crosses the sun, the light goes copper, the wind slackens, the note bends down, the trees flower, and pods fall for an hour like a hail of small instruments. It is the most beautiful thing you will ever stand inside. It is also the hour your alarm reed detunes, the hour the leaves sweat solvent, and the hour a hard hat earns its keep. Retune, seal, and stand still.

In the neck slot, the dead hang tuned. Do not touch them. Do not lean your staff on a femur. Do not offer to add anyone. Do not record the chord and carry it out — a hundred and thirty decibels of infrasound is a hazard in most worlds and a weapon in some, and in any case it is a grave. Take notation. Take dry pigment on pre-dried paper, or scratch a salt tablet with a copper stylus. Ink is water.

Never touch a mirror rig. The mica mirrors on their rope rigs are steering columns of ceiling-light onto curing seams; move one and you set mortar in the wrong place, or focus a beam onto oiled paper, and become the legend the description warns about, briefly.

## 11. What to bring, what to leave

**Bring:** dried felt in quantity (a gift, never a wall-covering); clean bast rope; sheet mica; lens blanks for the guild, unopened, with permission; hard-drawn copper wire; a copper-shod staff; two tuned reeds; a bite-peg; conductive garments and grounding braid; dry, sealed, high-carbohydrate food for above-ground; a hard shell; your encapsulation and its condensate traps; notation kit; your audiogram; patience measured in generations.

**Leave:** every fluid, every flame, every spark, every lens, every battery, every soft absorber, every instrument with a stretched membrane. A drum is a small tamed predator; do not carry one into a world where the predator is a drumhead the size of a road.

## 12. Coming back up

The ascent is the part nobody prepares for. Forty-one plugs, and each one you pass gives you back a layer of the world, and somewhere around the twentieth you will hear your own breathing, and around the tenth your own voice, and it will sound obscene — a small wet animal noise, alone, with no chord under it. Do not use it until you are on the plain.

Then the plain will be silent, which it is not; it is humming a hundred faint notes into the glare, and you will feel them in your teeth and know, now, what they are.

For weeks afterward the note will come home with you. You will wake at the wind tides. Rooms will feel dead. Other worlds' quiet will feel like a hand laid flat on your chest, and you will find yourself clicking your teeth to be sure your hands are still where you left them. Get a follow-up audiogram. Expect the loss to be permanent and asymmetric and to sit exactly where the harmonics were. Do not go back to fix it.

And two last disciplines, which matter more than anything in your pack:

**Do not tell the caravan drivers.** They have said it is the sun for nine hundred years. That sentence is a load-bearing wall. Six thousand people live inside a note that a hundred sealed gourds' worth of curious visitors would detune, wet, and eventually burn.

**Do not go looking for the others.** The plain is a field of gourds and only this one is cracked open, and only this one is full. A sealed bubble is a sealed lung. You do not know what note it is holding, or for whom, or how long it has been holding it. Walk on. Let your teeth buzz. Say it is the sun.
