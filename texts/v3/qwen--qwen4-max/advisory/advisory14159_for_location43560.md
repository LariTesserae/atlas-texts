# Preparing for the Kettle

## 1. Understand what kind of visit this is

You are not going to a scenic site. You are going into a room where a conversation has been running without interruption for forty thousand years, and the conversation is conducted in the two media you are worst at controlling: **light and sound**.

You are, by the standards of this place, an enormous noisy animal that casts a two-metre bar of shadow, sweats a mirror-film onto its face, wears reflective buckles, breathes audibly, and has a digestive tract. Every step you take will demolish sentences. Every time you stand up in the wrong hour you will say something enormous and meaningless in a language you cannot read. This is not a metaphor and the sill-folk will not find it charming.

The whole of your preparation follows from one principle: **make yourself small, soft, matte, and slow.** Everything below is an application of that principle.

---

## 2. Timing, and getting there

**Season.** Go in high summer, in the weeks around the traders' ascent. In winter the bowl fills with ice, the town goes down into the fossil galleries, and the entire place conducts itself in taps you cannot hear. You would be a blind, deaf, freezing burden in a community of thirty-four people with no surplus and no physician. Do not go in winter. Do not go in the shoulder seasons, when the chimney runs with meltwater.

**The approach is the dangerous part.** Two days up through the cloud sea, and the last hour is wet rotten limestone under rime. That is a serious climb on bad rock: holds that pull, protection that doesn't, a rope that stiffens. You need to be a competent second on a rope before you consider this trip. Bring a short rack, two ropes if you can carry them, gloves you can actually climb in, and expect to place nothing you trust. The sill-folk meet arrivals at the notch; arrange this in advance through the lowland traders, and do not arrive unannounced. There is no other way in and no way out that does not take two days.

**There is no rescue.** Write that on your hand. Above the cloud there is no helicopter that can hover in thin air over a bowl full of birds, no stretcher route down a chimney, no hospital. Any problem you cannot manage with what you carry and what seven felt houses contain is a problem you will have to survive or not.

**Acclimatize properly.** The bowl sits above the treeline, above the cloud sea, at the height where the sun comes unfiltered and the night takes it all back. Budget a week of staged ascent below before the two-day climb. Carry acetazolamide, dexamethasone, and nifedipine, and know which is for which. If you develop ataxia, confusion, or pink froth, the only treatment is *down*, immediately, and down is two days away. Turn around early. The Kettle will still be there; it has been there since before the ice left.

**Party size.** One or two. A party of six is a crowd of giants and will be experienced as a riot. No children who cannot reliably keep silence for days. No dogs, ever — a dog is a noise with a mouth.

## 3. The body

**Eyes.** Everyone goes blind at the notch. This is photokeratitis, and the sill-folk ritual — felt hood, face to the rock, count of two hundred, ground first — is better medicine than anything you will be tempted to substitute. *Obey it exactly.* Do not shorten the count. Do not peek. Do not say "I've got glacier glasses, I'm fine."

Get bone goggles if you can, traded up from below or made for you. The slit matters as much as the tint: it cuts aperture and stray bounce, not just intensity. If you must use modern lenses, use category-4, **non-mirrored**, with side shields, and matte every millimetre of frame with soot and felt. A mirrored lens is a small heliograph strapped to your face. Even plain lenses throw a few percent; keep your head down and your hood up. Carry a spare pair. Bring lubricating drops; the air is thin and dry and the gravel is dust.

Look at the Argument the way the readers do — dimmed, reflected, indirect, briefly. Staring at the town in full sun for an hour is how visitors spend the next three days in a dark cell with felt on their eyes, unable to help with anything.

**Skin.** Five-fold reflected light burns in places you have never burned: under the chin, under the nose, the undersides of the ears, the insides of the nostrils, the roof of the mouth if you gape. Full physical coverage, wide brim under the hood, zinc everywhere, lip block in every pocket. Sunburn here is not discomfort, it is a fever in a place with no medicine.

**Feet.** This is the signature injury of the Kettle: an inch and a half of near-freezing water over your feet, all day, every day, at altitude, with no fire to dry anything. Immersion foot will end your visit faster than the climb will. Plan for it obsessively: waterproof barrier socks, a rotation of felt liners, a dry pair reserved *only* for sleeping, foot powder, and a foot-care routine you perform at the white hour without fail. Never sleep in wet socks. Ask before you put your feet in the warm vein; it is sulfurous, it is the only green place in the bowl, and it is not a public footbath.

**The cough.** The single most dangerous thing you will bring is the dry high-altitude hack that everyone gets after four days of cold thin air. It is involuntary, it is loud, it comes in fits, and it summons the listener. Train for it: breathe through your nose, wear a felt veil to humidify, hydrate, carry suppressants and lozenges (unwrapped, in a felt pouch — a sweet wrapper is a small scream). If you develop a cough you cannot suppress, go into a sealed felt cell and stay there, or go down. Do not "push through" in the middle of the town.

Also: sneezes, hiccups, laughter, snoring, the gasp you will make when you first see the pebbles move. Practise at home. Spend a week silent before you go. Learn to sneeze into a felt sleeve with your mouth against wool. Learn to laugh with your mouth shut — you *will* want to laugh, some of the shadow-jokes are funny even to outsiders once a reader explains them. If you snore, sleep alone, hooded, and warn your hosts.

**Water.** Drink from the seep-lines above the town, and ask which ones. The film that crosses the floor is the birds' feeding surface and their latrine simultaneously; it is a closed loop and it is not for you. Filter or chemically treat rather than boiling — boiling means fire, and fire means the undercroft and a favour asked. Carry a filter you can service with cold hands. The water is extremely hard; expect your kidneys to notice, and drink more than you think you need.

**Food.** The flour cakes are nutritious, abundant, and taste like a cellar. Accept them, eat them gratefully, and bring your own supplement: fat, salt, dried fruit, chocolate. Two rules. **Everything you eat must be soft or soaked** — a cracker, a crisp apple, a nut, anything that crunches, is a shout in a language of whispers. Eat in a cell or at the white hour. And carry out every wrapper; nothing rots here, nothing burns, and a foil liner is a permanent flashing obscenity in a place whose middens are sorted by grade of gravel.

**Waste.** Ask on the first day and do exactly what you are told. Do not add your nutrients to the film. The whole food web is a skin of algae and springtails fed on bird droppings and sky-fall; a persistent new input in the wrong place is not a small thing.

**Illness.** Thirty-four people, nine hundred years of isolation, no medicine, no doctor, no dentist. Quarantine before you ascend — a full incubation period, not just the two-day climb. Get your teeth fixed first. Update everything. If you feel a sore throat coming on at the notch, tell them and turn around. A common cold here is not a cold; it is a demographic event.

## 4. The kit, and how to ruin it correctly

Modern outdoor gear is designed to be loud and shiny. Almost all of it is wrong here.

**Softening.** No velcro. No zips. No crinkly shell fabric — a hardshell sleeve brushing your body sounds like applause. Wool and felt, ties and felt-wrapped toggles. Tape or felt-sleeve every buckle, every clip, every pole shaft, every pot. Take the bear bell off your pack and leave it in another universe. Wrap your ice axe head; a bare adze throws a reflection.

**Down.** Do not wear a down jacket. It rustles, and you would be walking through the town wearing a corpse of the thing everyone here is neighbours with. Wool.

**Colour.** Pale dust-grey, cream, bone. Matte. Not black — black is tar-lichen in the frost cracks, and a black figure moving across the terraces is a walking shadow-word, the loudest garment you could possibly choose. Not red, not blue, not hi-vis. Cover your pack too. Cover your face; sweating skin is a wet mirror and your eyes are two more.

**Light.** No headlamp in the open, no phone screen, no glowing watch. A light at night is an obscene word and a beacon. If you must have one, red, tiny, used inside a closed felt cell, and understand you are asking for an exception.

**Leave behind entirely:** stove and fuel, mirror, drone, camera flash, musical instrument, whistle, speaker, alcohol (it makes you loud and it makes you stupid at altitude), anything with an alarm.

**Sealed emergency kit, and an honest conversation with yourself about it.** A whistle and a foil blanket will save your life and may kill birds. A foil blanket is a mirror the size of a house wall; a whistle is a dinner bell for the listener. Carry them, seal them, and decide *now*, on paper, what conditions justify opening them. Do not make that decision while frightened on a ledge.

**Bring:** felt ( bolts of it, as trade and as raw material), wool slippers, needles and thread, antler blanks, barley, salt, smoked glass, medicines, a spare pair of goggles, a black basin and a pinhole if you want to read (this is *their* instrument — a camera obscura is culturally legible here in a way nothing else you own will be). Copper is their entire metal supply, three nuggets' worth; bringing a handful is not a gift, it is an industrial revolution. Decide deliberately whether that is what you intend.

**Biosecurity.** Scrub and wash every seam, boot sole, and pocket before you go up. No seeds, no soil, no live plants, no animals. The bowl eats the sky and nothing else; one introduced grazer, predator, or mould could take out the skin that twenty thousand birds and thirty-four people are standing on.

## 5. The discipline of the day

Learn the shape of a Kettle day and hang your body on it.

**Dawn.** Fog knee-deep, the listener quartering. Stay in the undercut, hooded, still. Do not be the first thing moving.

**Morning.** Seeps gather, the town is speaking. Move only on the split-slab causeways, in felt, low, slowly, in single file, along a route a reader has given you. **Never step on a rimstone lip.** A fingernail-height lip is a human generation of growth; a broken lip is a channel; a channel is a cut; a cut takes out the terraces in a season. You can do more damage with one careless boot than with anything else you are carrying.

**The white hour, around noon.** Shadowless, aphasic, nothing can be said. *This is your window.* All your moving, eating, washing, foot care, toileting, gear work, and clumsiness belongs here, because here you cannot say anything wrong. The sill-folk do their maintenance now for the same reason. Learn to love the white hour. It is forty minutes of being allowed to be a person.

**Afternoon, around the fifth hour.** The sheet rises. No roar, just a hiss like sand on a table, and then cold water climbing your ankles. **Be uphill before it starts.** Do not camp on the lower terraces, do not leave gear on them, do not be the idiot caught mid-bowl in the braided traffic of twenty thousand birds carrying stones up forty steps. Get to the eastern undercut and stay out of the flow.

**Dusk.** The listener again; the light goes long; the ninety-second beam. Then needle ice, the frying sound, the taps.

**The listener drill.** Learn the signal and rehearse it until it is faster than thought: drop, hood up, over yourself and over your work, face down, matte, motionless, and *stay down until the all-clear*. Not until the shadow passes — until you are told. The town goes dark in under four seconds. If you are the one pale upright thing left standing, you have not merely endangered yourself, you have made a mark in the middle of a page. Do not run. Do not shout. Do not photograph it.

## 6. Manners

**Do not touch a stone.** Not to move it, not to tidy it, not to skip it, not to take it. Not a pebble, not a chip, not "one small souvenir." A cell is a sentence; a threshold pebble is a word chosen by a bird that spent its life on the choice. Removing one is not theft of an object, it is amputation of an utterance — and some of those lumps are fossil doorways older than your species' agriculture. Take nothing. Leave nothing. If a stone falls off your boot into a pool, tell a reader immediately rather than "fixing" it.

**Do not touch a sluice.** Not to help. Not to clear a leaf that isn't there. Sluice-keeping is a nine-hundred-year religious discipline practised with antler picks by people who understand the hydraulic consequences better than you do. If you are invited to serve, serve as an apprentice, with felt-wrapped tools, for as long as they say.

**Do not try to speak.** You will be tempted. You will think: *I have a mirror, I could throw a reflection, I could say hello.* Understand what this is. You would be a person who cannot hear the register, cannot see the grammar, and cannot perceive most of the medium, firing a shout into a nine-hundred-year argument. A hand mirror here is a megaphone in a library, wielded by someone who does not know the alphabet. If the readers ever invite you to place a chip, treat it as an enormous honour and do exactly what you are told and nothing more.

**Do not record and play back the taps.** Playback is putting words in mouths.

**Fire.** Never in the open. Not a candle, not a stove, not a match to see by. Fire is a false enormous word, it is obscene, and it is a beacon. If you need heat, ask for the undercroft box, pay for it, and be grateful.

**The down.** Drifts against the leeward walls are the entire economy. Do not gather it, do not "help" gather it, do not take a pinch. Trade for it, at the price they set, on the day they set.

**The cords.** Nine hundred years of knotted record in a room in the undercroft. Ask before entering. Touch nothing. Photograph nothing without permission. Do not offer to digitize it.

**Do not offer to solve anything.** Not the grammar, not the Old Sentence, not the theology of the sluices. In particular, never say *but the birds keep the sluices anyway, so why do you bother?* They know the arithmetic. They have known it for nine hundred years. Knowing it and keeping the sluices anyway is the whole content of their religious life, and your cleverness is the oldest and most tiresome thing a visitor can bring up the chimney. Ask what it is like to hold. Do not propose a resolution.

**Learn the hand language before you arrive.** Arrive with two hundred signs, not twenty. It is the difference between being a guest and being an obstacle.

## 7. The mind

Nobody warns you about this part.

The first day is sensory assault: the light, the blindness, the moment four thousand pebbles move and your sense of what a city is falls over. The second day is the reorganization, when you understand that the town is a sentence and the individuals are letters. Somewhere around the fourth or fifth day the silence starts to work on you. You will hear your own blood. You will hear taps that are not there. You will feel the urge to speak as a physical pressure in the chest, and some people weep, and weeping is loud, and then they are ashamed, and shame is also loud. Bring a small ordinary object from home to hold in a dark cell.

Then there is being *read*. You are not invisible here and you are not anonymous. Thirty-four people whose entire high-status occupation is watching this bowl will watch you, and everything you do — every misplaced shadow, every clumsy crossing, the day you fell over — will be knotted into cord and kept in a room for nine hundred years. You will become a permanent glyph in a record older than most nations. Behave as though that is true, because it is.

And there is the deep-time vertigo. Forty thousand years of daily tide. Four hundred centuries of balance. A tower nobody can parse that the whole modern world is arguably a footnote to. Some travelers find this steadying and some find it annihilating. Find out which you are *before* you are eleven thousand feet up with two days of bad rock between you and anywhere else.

Stay at least nine days. The first two show you a spectacle. Around the ninth you stop being a word and start being a guest, and that is the only version of this trip worth the climb.

If you are there for the traders' day — the one day of voices, when everyone goes out through the notch and speaks aloud until they are hoarse and hates it — **participate**. Bring news, stories, songs, jokes from below. Be ugly with them. It is a gift and it is the only time you will ever hear what your hosts sound like.

---

## 8. Leaving

Leave in the morning, before the fifth hour, while the notch is low. The descent chimney is worse going down: cold hands, numb feet, rotten holds, rime, and a rope you have to trust twice. Give it a full day. Do not linger for the dusk beam — the beam is exactly the thing that makes people linger, and lingering is how you end up on wet limestone in the dark.

Carry out everything. Every wrapper, every thread, every fingernail clipping, every broken buckle. Sweep your cell. Leave the felt.

If they ask you to stay — if someone hints that thirty-four could be thirty-five — do not answer quickly. That number has been stable for nine hundred years and it is a decision, not an accident. What is being offered is: no voices, no fire, no wood, cold water on your feet every afternoon for the rest of your life, and the sluices. Some travelers accept. Understand it before you do.

And if you die here, you will not be buried and you will not be burned. The lime will take you and lay you down and you will become a lumpiness in a terrace, a fossil of a shape, another unreadable thing in a bowl full of unreadable things. The Kettle keeps nothing except what it swallows. Tell them, in advance, what you would want.

---

## The one rule

Everything above reduces to this:

**You are a word in a language you cannot read. Be a small one. Take no stone, move no stone, cast no shadow you have not been given permission to cast, make no sound you cannot help — and when you cannot help it, be somewhere it does not matter.**

Do that, and on the ninth evening the water will drop and the needle ice will start to fry and the terraces will give back their weak blue-green, and someone will put a felt hand on your shoulder and let you look, and for about ninety seconds you will be standing inside the only thing in any universe that has never once raised its voice and has never once stopped.
