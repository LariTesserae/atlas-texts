# Preparing to visit the Culm

## Start with the two ways you could destroy it

Everything else in this briefing is comfort. These two are not.

**You are the only animal that will enter the town this year.** Read that again with the biosecurity implication in mind. Four hundred and thirty people, one hundred and eleven stocks, one genotype, two centuries of sealed seams, no insects, no birds, no webs in the corners. That absence is not decay — it is a maintained condition, and someone at that gate is maintaining it. A place with no insect life has no insect-borne blight, and a clonal organism has no resistant individuals. Whatever kills one limb kills the town, and the town is also the water main, the roof, the girder, the gas holder's structure, and everybody's grandmother's ashes.

So:

- **Carry no living or once-living thing that is not processed.** No seeds, no trail mix with seeds in it, no dried fruit, no herbal tea, no tobacco, no straw hat, no wicker bag, no wooden souvenirs from the last world, no sourdough starter, no soil, no pot plant, no cutting "from a tree at home." No paper books if you can avoid them — booklice and silverfish ride in bindings, and paper will mould here anyway.
- **Scrub and dip your boots.** Expect a boot bath and a wash-down at the gate and submit to it cheerfully. If there isn't one, ask for one. If they seem relaxed about it, be the one who is not.
- **Treat any fungal skin condition before you go.** Ringworm, athlete's foot, the lot. Arrive with clean skin, clean nails, clean kit, no lice.
- **Quarantine yourself.** An isolated population of four hundred and thirty with two hundred and thirteen years of narrow contact is immunologically naive to whatever you are incubating. Give yourself a full week symptom-free before arrival, longer if you can. Do not arrive with a scratchy throat and a plan to "push through." Do not arrive with a cold sore. Your ordinary rhinovirus could take out a shift, and a shift here is not a department — it is a fraction of the town's total capacity to keep the pumps ahead of the water.
- **Do not offer to improve the stock.** Not a rootstock, not a cultivar, not a "you should really try" from a world with better forestry. There is a planting book. It is a legal document. It has a hundred-and-fifty-year plan in it for the salt wedge, and it is working.

**Fire.** Open flame is a criminal offence, and the ambient temperature is thirty-one degrees, which is around the flash point of turpentine. The air is faintly resinous everywhere. Assume that anything which can make a spark is a weapon.

- Surrender matches, lighters, flint, ferro rods, piezo igniters, anything with a hot coil, at the gate. Not in your pocket at the gate — *at the gate*.
- Your electronics are almost certainly not intrinsically safe. A phone, a camera, a power bank, a head-torch, an electric shaver: declare them all and expect them to be impounded or restricted to the brick cells. Do not argue. If you must carry a light, ask what pattern is approved and buy that locally.
- No fleece, no crackling synthetics, no long loose scarves. Dense cotton, leather, clogs. Static in a drying loft full of fibre is a real thing.
- **Do not smell of smoke.** If you camped or cooked over fire in the last world, wash your clothes and yourself before you arrive. Scorched-resin smell empties a room faster than a scream, and you do not want to be the reason four hundred people run.
- Don't wear pine-scented anything. The nose is an instrument here. You will be jamming it.

If you learn only one drill: find out what the fire signal is on your first hour, and where your nearest wet standpipe is, and then keep checking where the nearest one is all day, because you will be turning sideways through corridors you have never seen. My intuition is that in a place at eighty-four decibels the loudest alarm is **silence** — the clutch drawn, the shafting coasting, the presses stopping out of turn. Learn what a wrong quiet feels like. Also learn the hand-signs for fire, gas, water, stop, out, and man-down before you learn please and thank you.

## Kit, and anti-kit

Bring very little, and bring it flat.

The governing physical fact of the Floor is that nothing has clearance. A rucksack is not luggage here, it is a weapon: it will hit a valve wheel, a catch cup, a lamp cable, a tin. Carry a small flat pouch on your front, or nothing. Nothing dangling: no lanyard, no camera strap, no scarf, no loose sleeve, no ring, no watch with a buckle strap, no long hair untied. Line shafting at forty-two strokes a minute does not care that it looks slow.

Leave behind, or expect to lose: leather boots you love, paper, anything with glue, anything with a felt lining, your good jacket, your instrument if it is wooden. Everything here is permanently at the point of nearly-dripping. Mould will find the seams of your kit in three days and the inside of your camera in one.

Take:

- Two sets of thin cotton clothes and the willingness to wear the same damp thing every day.
- **Buy clogs there.** The floor is grown plank polished by two centuries of clogs into dark horn, and it is filmed with condensate. Your grippy modern sole is wrong for it; their clogs are right, and buying a pair is a small courtesy that will be noticed.
- Earplugs, and a small tin for them so they don't go mouldy. More on the argument about this below.
- A wax tablet and a brass stylus. Non-sparking, waterproof, no batteries, no paper, no ink to run. You will need to write things down and shouting will not work.
- Wound kit: waterproof dressings, antiseptic wipes, and a low threshold for using them.
- Oral rehydration salts, more than you think. Multivitamins — there is no fruit here and no seed; the food comes in by barge and is likely salt, ferment, and eel.
- A wide-brim hat and repellent for the *margin*, not the town. Mosquitoes at dusk on the bund and the basin — precisely where you will arrive, leave, and stand to look at the view. Nothing past the gate. Cover up at slack water.
- Nothing you cannot afford to have confiscated.

## Your body

**Heat.** Thirty-one falling to twenty-seven, humidity that does not fall at all. That is a wet-bulb temperature near thirty. You will not cool by sweating; sweat will simply join the general wetness. Expect to feel stupid, slow, and mildly nauseated for the first two days, and do not mistake that for a bad mood. Acclimatise properly before you go if you can — a fortnight of daily heat exposure — and once there, do nothing strenuous for the first three days. Check your medications: anything anticholinergic, antihistaminic, diuretic, or stimulant makes you worse at this.

**Water.** Drink the sweet tank and the condensed water, from taps that people identify for you. Never the marsh, never the ditch, never a cool pipe's sweat, and **never a tin**. The tins are not buckets, they are a diagnostic instrument tuned by the men who empty them. Do not drink from one, do not move one, do not empty one as a helpful gesture, and above all do not stuff a rag in one to stop the noise. That is the equivalent of putting your thumb on a doctor's stethoscope. Condensed water is nearly distilled; take salt with your food, deliberately, more than feels normal.

**Feet and skin.** Nothing dries. Check your feet every night, powder them, rotate footwear, and let a fitter tell you where you may put things in the drying loft rather than helping yourself. Expect intertrigo, expect boils, expect every small cut to go angry within a day. Tetanus current, hepatitis A and B, typhoid — talk to a travel clinic and mention "anoxic black water, warm, sulphide, communal" and let them think about it.

**Ears.** Eighty-four decibels, all day and all night, is a hearing hazard, and the local evidence is standing right next to you: half the men over fifty are deaf in the left ear. My advice is to wear plugs on the Floor from your first minute, take them out for the nine quiet minutes, and accept that you will be slightly less situationaly aware in exchange for keeping your hearing. Do not imitate the locals' deafness out of politeness; it is not a heritage, it is an injury they have priced in. Do not lecture anyone about it. They know. Get a baseline audiogram before you go and another when you get back, if your world has such things; it will tell you what the visit cost.

The trap is perceptual: after two days the roar will feel like silence, and that is when you will stop bothering. Also expect tinnitus and a strange rebound on leaving — the first night in a quiet room elsewhere is often worse than any night here.

**Lungs.** Turpentine is an irritant and a sensitiser. If you have asthma, think hard, and carry your reliever in a sealed pouch on your front where you can reach it with one hand. Do not enter Retort Row or a digester dome without an invitation and an escort.

## Arrival, and the shape of your day

You come in by barge, through a lock, on a tide. The tide is forty centimetres and the sluice slaps on the turn; the lock has windows and the barge-pole man has a schedule and neither of them will wait for you. **There is no walking out.** Thirty kilometres of sedge and standing water in every direction. Your exit is a boat, and your punctuality is not a courtesy, it is logistics.

Learn the eleven-minute relief stack. It is the town's clock and it will become your clock, and when you notice you have started counting it, you have begun to acclimatise.

Find out the shift-change times on day one and build your entire visit around them. Nine minutes, fifty-eight decibels, everybody shouting because they cannot recalibrate. **That is when conversation, questions, business, thanks, apologies, introductions and farewells happen.** Anything you try to say on the Floor will be a waste of your throat and their patience. Do not schedule a discussion for mid-shift. Do not stand in a doorway during those nine minutes; the whole town is using them at once.

Between those gaps, hands are voices. Learn sixty to a hundred signs before you arrive — numbers, trades, the three levels, the courtesies, and the emergencies. Then learn that there are shift dialects and that yours will mark you as belonging to whichever shift taught you, which is a small political fact you should know rather than discover. Do not invent signs. Do not wave hello across a hall; you may be saying something. Do not grab a person's hands to get their attention — you are seizing their voice. Touch a shoulder, get into their light, wait. Stand on people's right, and speak to right ears, and never assume that a raised voice carries.

The other thing to learn immediately is the **sideways gait**. Practise at home: shoulders narrow, feet sliding, no stride, one hand on a hand-line, eyes down as well as up. Children here learn it before reading. You will turn sideways a hundred times a day and every time you forget you will hit something that matters. Use the hand-lines — the pale worn places are where four generations of palms have gone, and putting your hand there is both safety and a small act of belonging. Never step backwards without looking. Behind you is a shaft, a hot main, a wagon on a chain, or a root.

The narrow-gauge wagons are hauled off the shaft and cannot stop for you, and the gravity incline to the basin is not a shortcut. Do not walk on the rails, do not cross the incline, do not step over shafting because it looks slow. *Patience* turns at forty revolutions a minute and you can read the maker's name on the flywheel rim; that legibility is a lie about torque. It will take your sleeve and your arm with it and never notice.

## The three levels, and what to fear in each

**The Floor** is where you will spend your time and where you will be tempted to relax. Presses through the soles of your feet, resin-oil dripping into catch cups, everything within arm's reach. Do not lean. Do not put a hand out to steady yourself without looking first. Steam at two bar is around 134 degrees and a fresh weep is invisible; the mains weep for a week before they close, so there is always a new one somewhere. Learn the local method for testing a suspected steam leak — a stick, a rag, a listening posture, whatever the fitters do — and never reach past a pipe to see what is behind it. Do not touch cambium. Your skin salt, your oils, and your foreign microbes on a graft union are not a trivial matter in a town whose entire industry is sealing.

**The Under** is where you can die in a minute without any noise at all. Methane, carbon dioxide pooling in sumps, sulphide in the black water — the struck-matches smell. Sulphide deadens your nose at exactly the concentrations that knock you down, so "the smell went away" is the worst sign available. Digesters at fifty-five degrees, brick domes, confined spaces. **Never go down alone, never go down uninvited, never go into a dome or a sump space at all.** Ask what the gas-test ritual is and submit to it. Note that there are no birds here — no canaries, no mice, no animal but the workforce — so whatever they use, it is paper and glass and instruments and procedure, and it is not negotiable. If you have fallen into or cut yourself in the black water: get out, wash with sweet water, tell someone immediately, and do not be embarrassed. Anoxic water in a wound is a serious thing and they will treat it as one.

**The Over** is where you should go once a day, on purpose, for your mind. Catwalks lashed and grafted through the crown, light filtered green, wind in a hundred thousand leaves that sounds exactly like surf and never stops. It is the only place you will see a horizon: thirty kilometres of sedge. Go up, look out, get your bearings, get some unfiltered daylight on your skin. Everything below is green-lit and enclosed and loud, and human beings do badly in that for long. Also note that green light lies about colour — do not judge food, complexions, or colour-coded valve labels by it. Ask for a proper light if you need to read anything.

Ask, too, about surge. Two metres above mean tide, four millimetres of subsidence a year, salt coming up the channels. There is a bund and it is raised a hand's breadth a decade and everyone under thirty thinks of that as a festival. Find out from someone over sixty what the flood signal is and where high ground is. If water comes, you go **up**, into the crown, not out. There is no out.

## Night, sleep, and the mind

You will not sleep for the first two or three nights. Seventy-one decibels in the leaf rooms, a hammock on a living hook that has grown around its shackle, and the press coming through the wood as a slow pulse. Locals cannot sleep anywhere else; you cannot sleep here yet. Both facts are true and neither is permanent. Plugs for sleeping, if the room's conventions allow it — ask, because you may be masking something someone else needs to hear. Ask before you sling anything anywhere: hooks are grown, not fitted, and a hammock in the wrong place is a load on a member somebody is accounting for.

Privacy here is by convention, not by walls. A hung cloth is probably a door. Learn the convention before you wander the leaf rooms at night looking for a quiet spot. There isn't one.

The thing that will actually unsettle you is not the noise. It is the absence. No birds, no moths round the lamps, no webs in the corners, no fruit, no seed, nothing eating anything. A warm wet crowded place with no webs. Most visitors report that this is the detail that follows them home. Expect a stretch of derealisation somewhere around day three — the roar that never changes size, the surf that isn't surf, the green light, the eleven-minute stack, the sense that time here is measured in millimetres of girth rather than hours. It passes. Going up to the Over helps. Counting the stack helps. Being given a small real job helps most of all.

## Manners

- **Take nothing.** Not a leaf, not a chip, not a snapped twig, not a cutting, not a seed. Harvest equals growth, audited annually in the Coppice Book on an eleven-year rotation, and it has balanced for two hundred and thirteen years. Your souvenir is a line item in a legal ledger and a wound in a living wall. Do not carve your initials. Do not nail, wire, tie, or lean anything against a limb. Do not plant a memento tree — that is a legal act with two-century consequences and an outsider's plant is a biosecurity event.
- **Do not offer improvements.** Not steel pipes, not a faster rotation, not a shortcut, not a machine from a better world. *You cannot hurry it* is the first law and it is also the identity. The girder was planted as a whip and adds four millimetres a year. Everyone here has already had your idea and declined it for reasons you will not understand in a week.
- **The ash goes to the root collars.** The dead are burned because peat keeps what you put in it. Everyone knows and nobody minds much; it is understood as being put back into the walls. Do not react with horror, do not make a joke, do not say anything clever about eating your grandparents. If you are invited to a burning, go, and stand where you are put.
- **If you happen to be there for a forced flower,** you are present at something that happens once a decade, pollinated by children with brushes, and the seedlings go into the book with names. Behave as though you are in a cathedral, because functionally you are. Don't touch the seedlings.
- **Do not get in the way of the fire drill.** It is monthly and timed. Watch from wherever you are told to watch. Being the obstacle that adds nine seconds to a time is a story that will outlive you here.
- Four hundred and thirty people in the same rooms for two centuries means gossip is infrastructure and you are the biggest event of the year. Be honest, be brief, be useful, promise nothing. You will be discussed exhaustively; give them something kind to say.
- Bring a small gift that is hard, sealed, non-organic and non-sparking: good steel, good glass, needles, a decent file. Or bring something for the nine minutes — a story, a song, news of another world's trees. That is the only window in which the town can receive it.

## Leaving

Give yourself at least four days; three is not enough to get past the sensory shock and you will leave with only the horror and none of the thing itself. Time your departure to the lock and the tide, not to your mood. Say your goodbyes in the quiet gap, in signs where you can, out loud where you can't, at whatever volume everyone else is using.

Then, on the barge, the roar will stay the same size behind you for a long time — much longer than seems possible — and you will find you are still counting eleven minutes, still turning sideways in an open boat, still listening for a tin that has changed its note. That is normal. It fades in about a week.

The thing you will keep is not the noise. It will be the moment you finally heard the drip chorus as a chord instead of a nuisance, and understood that you were standing inside something that is repairing itself, slowly, on purpose, and has been doing so since before your great-grandparents were born.
