# Preparing for The Hold

Three numbers, before anything else. Memorize them the way you'd memorize a poison's name.

**Nineteen.** Beats per second, through your feet and your femurs and the water in your eyes.
**One hundred and thirty.** Decibels, in the places where the food is.
**Forty.** Meters per second, the speed at which the room delivers anything loose to the nearest wall.

Everything below is a consequence of those three numbers. The Hold is not hostile. It is *tidy*, in a way that has no room for you, and the entire art of visiting is learning to be a thing that has already been put where it belongs.

---

## 1. Decide honestly whether to go

The Hold is not a place you can tough out. There is no version of this visit where you acclimatize like a local, because the locals do not acclimatize either — they go deaf on schedule and keep working. Nine hundred years of human habitation has produced no biological adaptation, only craft, sign language, and a real-estate market. You are not going to do better in three weeks.

Don't go, or go only in a null and consider whether that counts, if you have: a seizure disorder (ask specifically whether the glow-seams in your bay flicker at the fundamental — nineteen hertz is close to the worst frequency there is for a photosensitive brain, and violet is not a color you want strobing at you); a cardiac implant or any hardware that resonates; a vestibular disorder; detached-retina history or recent eye surgery; pregnancy. Bring a physician's note about the infrasound and expect the tuner you show it to to nod gravely and then tell you, kindly, that nobody knows what the shove does over a lifetime, only what it does in an afternoon.

Do go, and consider going *first*, if you are Deaf and sign fluently. You will arrive already equipped with the expensive half of the local toolkit. Half the population conducts its entire life in hands and bone-sticks; you will be understood immediately, and you will not be paying by the minute to talk to your own companions. Everyone else has to buy that.

## 2. Book the null first. Before passage, before guide, before anything

In the Hold, silence is a consumable. Budget it like water.

The physics is unforgiving in a specific way that people misunderstand. Earplugs work on the audible harmonics. They do essentially nothing at nineteen hertz, whose wavelength in air is about eighteen meters — you cannot plug a wave that is bigger than the building. And even for the audible part, there is a hard ceiling: sound reaches your cochlea through your skull regardless of what you put in your ears, which caps any protector's real-world performance at roughly forty to fifty decibels. This is why the locals use bone-sticks to *hear*. The skeleton is an open door.

Run the arithmetic once and it will organize your whole trip. At 130 dB, an unprotected daily noise dose is on the order of **one second**. With thirty-five decibels of good protection you are at ninety-five, which buys you something like forty-five minutes. With the best protection physically possible you are still in the high eighties at an antinode, and the shove is untouched, and your gut is being kneaded nineteen times a second by something you cannot switch off.

So: a null is not luxury accommodation. It is where your body stops resonating. It is dialysis.

Book a sleeping null for every night of your stay, in a licensed, tuner-surveyed chamber, with a current certificate. Then book a second, cheaper null or a conversation room for daytime recovery, and treat the minutes as a ration you can run out of.

**Do not buy silence on the black market.** I know it's tempting; I know the licensed rates are obscene. An unlicensed null is a cancellation that something else is maintaining by accident, and it will fail — most reliably at the retune, when the whole geometry it depended on is redrawn. A cheap null is a coffin with a door. People sleep in them and are found in the morning as part of the wall.

Also, do not marry for null access. Travelers do this. It is a whole genre of local cautionary tale, and the locals find it funnier than you will.

## 3. Kit

Bring less than you think, and bring these.

**Hearing.** Buy your tuned defenders *there*. The Hold's acoustic craft is nine centuries deep and unmatched anywhere; imported generic muffs are a tourist's tell and a tourist's injury. What you want is a **notch-set**: protection that kills the antinodal roar and passes the narrow cancellation windows, because those windows are where every warning, every flute, every frog, and every human voice in the Hold actually lives. Blanket occlusion makes you safe and blind. Get fitted by a tuner's apprentice. Get the certificate. Learn to swap filters yourself in the dark, with gloves on.

**A closed-cell foam mat.** The single most underrated item on this list. It is not for comfort. It is the only thing between your skeleton and nineteen beats a second. The stone conducts. Do not sit on bare calcite, do not lean your skull against a wall, do not lie down on a floor, do not rest your jaw or your elbows on a ledge while you look at the view. Carry the mat everywhere. Sleep on it even inside a null.

**A damped helmet, worn at antinodes.** Not for falling — for what the sweep *delivers*. Antinodes receive the entire bay's cargo: dust, pollen, seeds, corpses, and every spoon anybody drops. The poor live where the food falls, and everything else falls there too. A hard hat with a suspension and a chin strap, plus eye protection.

**Gloves and hard-soled boots, plus a spare pair of boots.** The gravel is angular glass-sharp calcite. There are no pebbles here, no cobbles, no clay, no sand — nothing in nine hundred years has been rounded by water. It cuts hands and it eats footwear. In a warm wet fungal rind, every cut is an inoculation with biology that has been continuous for eleven thousand years. Wound kit: irrigation, antiseptic, occlusive dressings, and a low threshold for taking a scrape seriously.

**Light, and light discipline.** A hooded, shuttered, downward-facing lamp; a red headlamp for close work; a high-CRI white lamp for actually seeing color, which is a genuine luxury here; spares; and a lens warmer or desiccant, because warm damp air plus cold ribs means condensation on every optic you own.

Understand what light does in swept air. **There is no beam.** No dust, no scatter — you see the bright spot where your lamp lands and nothing in between, so you cannot tell where you are pointing until you have already pointed there. And your little lamp is visible, unblurred and unblinking, from eleven kilometers away. You are a lighthouse. Everyone in the bay can watch you approach for an hour before you arrive.

**A plumb bob in a case.** Cheap, no batteries, and it is the local instrument. In the comb it leans toward the nearest wall; near a cancellation it hangs true. It is a field-compass and a depth gauge and a warning. Learn to read it before you learn anything else.

**A laser rangefinder.** In air clearer than vacuum this thing is absurdly good, and it is the corrective for the perceptual failure that will nearly kill you (see §5).

**A spectrogram display and an infrasound-capable logger.** Your ears are not trained to find notches; a screen can show you the crevices in the roar until your brain learns to. And an ordinary noise dosimeter measures the wrong hazard — it will happily log a safe afternoon while your abdomen is being shaken at nineteen hertz. Log the low band. Nobody knows the dose-response curve. Be the person who at least has data.

**Antiemetics, antihistamines, electrolytes, vitamin D, a 24-hour light box.** All five are load-bearing; §6 explains why.

**A tether, and the humility to wear it.** Local children are leashed until they learn the edge. Ask for a visitor's line and clip it to the anchor rail without a joke about it. Nobody will think less of you. They will think less of you if you don't.

**Trade goods.** Soft, sound-eating things: felt, wool batting, mineral wool, acoustic barrier sheet. Rounded things: a river stone, baked and sealed, is a relic here — a handful of river sand in a jar is something a grown adult may not be able to hold without their hands shaking. A sun-spectrum lamp is close to a fortune. Bring these instead of currency and you will be fed and guided and forgiven a great deal.

**Do not bring:** soil, seeds, live plants, untreated wood, an animal of any kind, a drone, a kite, a balloon, a hat you like, or a loose scarf. You will be scrubbed at arrival; cooperate. A rind this lush has no empty niche and no immune system. And anything you release into the volume dies at forty meters per second and becomes fertilizer, which is a poor way to introduce a species and a worse way to say goodbye to a pet.

## 4. Skills to acquire before you arrive

**The hands.** Non-negotiable. Learn the local sign language to conversational fluency. It is free, it works at antinodes, it works across a market hall, it works through a lamp-lit kilometer, and it means you never have to rent a minute of silence to say something ordinary. A traveler who can only speak is a traveler who can only speak in a rented room. This is the difference between visiting the Hold and visiting a hotel in the Hold.

**Bone-stick etiquette.** How to hold it, where to press it, whose jaw you may not touch, how to pass one. It carries tone and warmth where the hands carry grammar. It is also intimate — offering your stick to a stranger is a gesture with meaning. Find out what meaning.

**Lamp code.** Perfect visibility plus no ambient light means everyone here talks in lamps: shutters, colors, held positions, semaphore. Learn the district's code and the visitor's "I don't know the edge" signal. That second one is a genuine safety device. Locals forgive almost anything from someone visibly announcing ignorance.

**Notch-listening.** Sit with an apprentice and let them teach you to hear a gap the way a birder learns a call. It takes days. It changes the place from a wall of pain into a landscape with rooms in it.

**Edge-reading.** Every district marks the boundary of the safe layer differently — paint, shell-lines, lamp posts, a change in the moss. Ask in each district. If you cannot tell where the mark is, you are already too close.

## 5. The four ways to die here, in order of likelihood

**Falling sideways.** This is the one. Gravity is not reliably down: a plumb line drifts toward the nearest wall, and the sweep takes anything unsupported in the volume and puts it on stone at speed. A human leaving the rind does not fall — a human is *fired*. There is no self-arrest, no lucky landing, no soft wall.

Therefore: **never break contact with a surface.** Never step over a gap. Never lean out for a photograph. Never jump — not a meter, not for fun, not to see. Use the stairs. The stairs are the civilization. Three points of contact, handrail on the outboard side, tether clipped.

You will want to jump. The clarity invites it: eleven kilometers of absolutely nothing, air like the idea of air, and it looks swimmable. It is not air. It is a machine for putting things on walls.

**Misjudging distance.** With no haze there is no aerial perspective, and your visual system has literally no cue for how far away anything is. A town on the far wall is a hard little constellation that reads as a model village a hundred meters off. Ledges read as steps. Gaps read as seams. People walk off things here in their first week, and they are not stupid people; they are people whose depth perception was trained in a world with weather. Use the rangefinder, out loud, every time, until it becomes a tic. Trust the number, not the eye.

**Noise.** Covered above. The failure mode is not dramatic. It is a permanent notch in your hearing at four kilohertz and a tinnitus you will have for the rest of your life, acquired on a Tuesday, in a market, buying fruit.

**Bad air in good air.** The volume is particle-free and can still kill you. Gases and vapors are not swept — only cargo is. So: ozone at the glow-seams, ammonia on the crab flats, and in the swallowed basements and drowned stairwells of old districts, whatever pools where nothing circulates. The clearest air in any world can be lethal in a stairwell. Carry a gas monitor. Do not descend into a swallowed level because the shop is charming. And note the inversion: the *air* is hypoallergenic, and the *rind* is anaphylaxis — an entire sky's worth of pollen and spore and shed skin, delivered continuously into a two-meter layer. If you have allergies, the volume is heaven and the surfaces are the worst place you have ever been.

## 6. Body, food, water, sleep, waste

**The first three days will be unpleasant in ways you should expect.** Nineteen hertz is close to the resonance of the human eyeball; vision smears, and the smear reads to the hindbrain as *something moving at the edge of sight*. It is also famous, in worlds like yours, for producing dread, a sense of presence, and grief without a cause. Add a gravity vector that disagrees with your otoliths and you get profound motion sickness.

So: gut trouble, nausea, cold sweats, and the absolute conviction that someone is standing behind you. **That feeling is the room.** Write this on your hand if you have to. Do not make decisions inside it, do not decide the Hold is haunted, and do not decide your companion is your enemy. It passes as you learn the notches, mostly.

**Acclimatize like altitude.** Night one through three in the deepest null you can afford. Then short protected rind walks, twenty minutes, logging dose. Then an hour. Then a market visit at an antinode — brief, helmeted, protected, planned, with a null booked immediately after. Never go straight from arrival to the fertile districts, however good the fruit smells.

**Heat.** Twenty-six to twenty-eight, unwavering, damp. A sealed acoustic suit will cook you. Plan for ventilation and sweat, drink more than you think, and accept that you will be wet for the whole trip. The rind is *damp, not wet* — you will not dry out, ever, and neither will your socks.

**Water arrives on schedule and never in quantity.** Every eleven hours or so the beat swells and the bay sheets for five minutes. Everyone catches it. Carry a folding catchment and a bladder and learn the sheet-clock, because that is the local day: an eleven-hour rhythm that will drift against your twenty-four-hour body. Keep your own clock with the light box. Take the vitamin D. Everyone here is pale and the sun has never entered.

Do not drink from a gutter without asking. Those gutters are older than the language spoken beside them and they are *owned*, in ways that are probably the deepest law in the Hold. Do not drink at the brine seep. Do not drink from a cistern with frogs in it unless someone who lives there hands you the cup.

**Food is a hearing expense.** The fertile districts are the deafening ones. To eat magnificently you must go where it is loudest; the rich import their food into nulls and are thin and pale for exactly this reason. Plan meals as short, protected excursions, or pay to carry, or eat poorly and quietly. Any of the three is a legitimate strategy. Choosing consciously is the point.

**Sleep** happens in a null, on a mat, with protection, and it will be bad for a while. The beat gets into your dreams. Bring earplugs *and* the mat *and* an eye mask; the violet seams do not dim.

**There is no away.** Anything you drop or discard is delivered, deterministically, to a specific antinode where specific people live and farm. The tuners can tell you where. So littering here is not littering — it is mailing your garbage to a stranger's front door, traceably, and a dropped hard object is a projectile fired down a known cargo lane into a inhabited district. Tether your tools. Lanyard your cup. Carry out everything, and understand that "carry out" means "carry to a surface where it belongs," not "toss."

## 7. Getting around, and the two kinds of seam

Learn this distinction before you learn the name of your hotel.

A **glow-seam** is antinodal: violet, bright, loud, fertile, ozone-smelling, and it will paste you to stone.
A **null-seam** is a ribbon of cancellation: dark, quiet, a hand's breadth wide, kilometers long, running wall to wall to ceiling in a graph. It is a road.

They are not the same thing and the local word may be shared. Ask which, every time, pointing.

Null-seams carry the cable trams and the ribbon-eels. **Do not enter a seam on foot.** I know the fantasy — you have already had it, swimming through clear dim air along a quiet line above eleven kilometers of nothing. The seam is a hand's breadth wide. You are not. Most of you would be outside it, and outside is the comb. There is no version of this that works.

Cross seams only at marked crossings, quickly, perpendicular, looking for tram lamps. Eels are three meters long, translucent, and invisible in unlit dust-free air; a seam is a road with silent, unseen traffic on a timetable. The tram drivers know that timetable better than their own children's. Do not stand at a junction: that is where the knot-hunters sit with their bodies spanning the line, waiting weeks, and it is where the eggs are, and it is their livelihood. Do not touch a seam. Do not photograph a hunter's junction and do not publish eel timetables. Ride the tram, stay inside the car, keep your limbs in, and don't ask too insistently how a car wider than a hand's breadth survives a hand's-breadth null. That is one of the things the tuners know and have stopped explaining.

Buy the **current** field chart from the tuners and check its date. It expires at the retune. Never travel on an old chart. Register yourself as a visitor. When a tuner signals, you have about forty-seven seconds — nine hundred beats — so keep your warning channel open at all times and know the evacuation gesture by heart.

One more thing about the tuners: ask *where* and *when*. Do not ask *why*. Their physics is descriptive, exhaustive, and beautiful, and they have made peace with the absence of an explanation in a way that visiting scientists find unbearable. Insisting on the *why* is the rudest thing a foreigner does here. Also: do not go looking for the apparatus. It raised forty chambers out of fresh limestone and it may not have been made by people, and the stone is still warm.

## 8. Manners

The privacy norms are **inverted**, and getting this wrong will mark you instantly.

Sound is expensive and therefore private. Sight is free and therefore public. Everyone can see everyone, always, across eleven kilometers of unblurred air; a cheap telescope here is a surveillance instrument capable of reading a face across a bay. So: **lamps are public, lenses are private.** Signal freely. Do not point optics at a dwelling. Curtains and screens are the real walls, and looking behind one is closer to assault than to curiosity. Being watched is normal; acknowledging it is not.

**Visits are negotiated.** One hundred and eighty thousand people across a surface the size of a small country means everyone has the loneliness of a lighthouse keeper and the social density of a village. A market hall the size of a county holds thirty stalls and they wave. Whole afternoons pass with one distant figure on a ladder a kilometer up the wall. This is not desolation, it is *spacing*, and it is maintained deliberately: you send word, you propose a time, you are expected, you arrive when you said you would, and you leave when the visit is done. Dropping in unannounced is the gravest ordinary rudeness available to you. Your lamp announces you an hour before you arrive anyway; use that hour to be announced *properly*.

Do not shout. It cannot be heard, it wrecks your throat, and it reads as a foreigner's tantrum.

**Do not startle anything.** A bird that leaves the rind dies. A moth you swat at dies. The flightless waders at the brine marsh have nowhere to go and no way to go there. Move slowly, sit down deliberately, and let the flat fauna ignore you. The drays are unhurried and sure-footed and deaf and will not react to your voice; the geckos have no ears and are reading your footsteps through the stone. You are louder to them than you think and quieter than you think, in different senses.

Stay out of the drowned district in Eleven. The lamps still burn down there and something large and patient has learned to open doors. There is no reading of that sentence that improves on taking it literally.

## 9. The retune

Twice a season, announced nine days out. Plan around it: either be off-world, or be settled, provisioned, and sheltered, with a licensed null and a survey certificate dated after the shift.

In the nine days before: the trees spawn toward places where nothing currently grows. The drays walk. The crabs go up the walls in columns. The charts you own become fiction. Every route you have learned is provisional. Do not make plans that depend on a seam still existing. Book your shelter early — the whole Hold is booking shelter at the same time.

Then the sheet comes, eleven hours instead of five minutes. Expect flooding, expect cisterns to overflow, expect drowned districts to move. Stay off ledges. The eels' map is redrawn and most of them die, and three meters of translucent dying animal is heavy.

And for about an hour, the fundamental drops out.

**Do not be in the volume.** Not in a seam, not on a tram, not on a ladder. Be on a surface, tethered, helmeted, under something that can take an impact — because for that hour every unattached thing in eighty cubic kilometers falls straight down under nothing but gravity. Eleven days of dust. The rain. Seeds. Fern sheets. The green heaven coming loose.

Here is what nobody tells you and what you should know: for that hour the air is *full*, and light has body, and you can see your beam. All the motes that were never allowed to drift are drifting. The Hold becomes, briefly, an ordinary cave with weather in it, and a thrown ball would go where you aimed it, and you can hear your own voice.

The rich come out of their nulls and stand in the open, unprotected, deaf for a moment like everyone else. It is the only hour they share with the people who feed them. It is also the hour when things get said, and promised, and forgiven, because it is the only hour when saying is free.

Decide before the retune what you want to say, and to whom. You will have one hour, in your own voice, and then the bell starts again.

Take the photograph if you like — damped tripod, mat under the feet, global shutter or very short exposures, fast lens, high sensitivity. It will look like a rendering. There is no atmosphere in it to make it look real.

## 10. Afterward

Get an audiogram before you go and after you come back, and keep both. Log the low-band doses. Expect a week of phantom nineteen hertz, of a shove that isn't there, of your pulse feeling like somebody else's. Expect ordinary worlds to look like soup — you will have seen eleven kilometers with nothing in the way, and every sky afterward will be dirty. Expect ordinary rooms to sound cluttered and faintly dishonest. Expect to miss the emptiness in a way that is hard to explain to anyone: not the silence, which you mostly rented, but the *spacing*, the enormous courtesy of a place where everyone is far away and everyone waves.

You will smell of fig milk and wet calcite and ammonia for longer than your clothes do.

And you will find, in a pocket, a chip of calcite you meant to throw away and didn't, because there is no away — sharp-edged, still faintly warm, cut by a sound rather than by water. Put it somewhere you'll touch it. It is the only kind of stone that world makes.
