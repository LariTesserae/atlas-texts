# Pre-crossing briefing: the finished flat

**Regime:** base-level peneplain, high-altitude, continuous biotic mat over planed basement rock, active hydraulic pumping, terminal-erosion conditions.
**Threat class:** not hostile. Not inhabited. *Digestive.*
**Recommended maximum exposure:** one waking period. Thirty hours. Hard ceiling forty.

---

## 1. Understand what will actually go wrong

Read this part twice, because everything else follows from it.

Nothing on that plain wants to hurt you. There is no predator, no territory, no immune response, no malice, no intelligence to negotiate with. The mat does not distinguish you from anything else, and that is precisely the problem. It has spent a hundred million years running out of things to wear down, and it has not stopped working. It has moved on from granite to softer targets: difference, duration, identity, sequence. You are made almost entirely of those.

So the realistic failure modes, in order of likelihood:

1. **You become difficult to distinguish.** From the plain, from your partner, from a previous version of yourself, from a grazer that has your posture.
2. **You lose the ability to leave.** Not because you are trapped — because leaving is a *sequence* (decide, then walk, then cross), and sequence is one of the things being planed.
3. **You are recovered but not quite returned.** The body comes back; the ordering doesn't. You spend a year answering questions a half-second before they're asked.
4. **Ordinary death:** hypothermia, fall-through into a chamber, sump opening under you, altitude, a valve cycling while you're standing on its crown.

Item 4 is the one you can train for in a gym. Items 1–3 are the reason this briefing is long.

---

## 2. Decide whether to go, and why, in a sentence you can say out loud

Write your reason down before you cross and memorise it as a sentence with a *person* in it. Not "to survey the pump regime." Something like: "I am going to look at the pumps for one day so that I can tell Ada what the noise is like."

This is not sentiment. The plain smooths intrinsic form — it can reproduce a kink in a left foreleg three days apart and two leagues apart because a leg-kink is just a shape. What it does not obviously know how to reproduce is a *relation to something not present*. A promise. An ongoing argument. A joke that only lands on one other person. Those are load-bearing. Carry them the way you carry water.

If you cannot think of a reason with a person in it, don't go yet. People who go for abstract reasons are the ones who stand still in the fog and let the quilt come up over their boots.

---

## 3. Party and duration

**Two. Never solo. Never more than four.**

Solo, you have no external check on your own continuity, and you will not notice the loss from inside — that is the whole nature of the loss. Above four, a group starts to behave like a herd: shared rhythm, shared mood, shared vocabulary, converging posture. The plain is very good at herds. Two people who keep disagreeing are the most erosion-resistant object you can put on that flat.

Pick someone you argue with productively and whose voice you know well enough to recognise a wrongness in it. Do not bring someone you are in awe of; you'll defer, and deference is convergence.

**Thirty hours.** No overnight if you can help it. Nothing accumulates there, including camps: your markers, your cache, your scraped-clear sleeping patch, your cairn of gear — all of it will be quilt by the next visit and possibly by the next morning. Reports of duplicated animals cluster around the three-day mark. Treat thirty-six hours as the onset of measurable smoothing and forty as the point where you stop being the author of your own itinerary.

Arrive rested. Sleep debt does exactly what the fog does, and they add.

---

## 4. Body preparation

- **Altitude.** Assume 3,500–4,500 m equivalent. The air is thin and it burns the inside of the nose. Acclimatise properly or plan on supplementary oxygen; either way, expect nosebleeds (see §9 on blood).
- **Ears and sinuses.** The intake stroke of a pump is a long descending pressure suck you feel behind the eyes. If you cannot equalise reliably, do not go. Train it. Carry a decongestant and use it early. Anyone with a recent ear surgery, perforated drum, or uncontrolled asthma sits this one out.
- **Teeth and skin.** Get dental work done first. Go with no open wounds, no split fingertips, no cracked heels, no fresh scabs. Every breach is a leak, and every leak is a *sample*. Blister prevention is not comfort here, it's containment.
- **Feet and legs.** You will be walking on a four-metre mattress: slow give, slower return, water rising into the footprint behind you. It is like walking the length of a beach in a wet duvet. Train on soft sand, on foam, on bog if you have it. Expect 1.5–2.5 km/h and 4,000–6,000 kcal a day. Your calves will fail before your lungs do.
- **Cold tolerance.** One degree above freezing, horizontal sleet, fog at head height that condenses on every cold surface including you. This is a wet-cold, not a dry-cold. It kills faster than the temperature suggests.
- **Noise.** Continuous industrial clatter with impulsive valve reports; near a nozzle you're in the range where you'll be shouting to be heard and won't know it. Bring ears you've already tested with protection on.

## 5. Kit

Everything you carry is consumable. Assume you will lose half of it and that the loss will not be memorable.

**Materials.** The water is tea-black, acid, iron-and-tannin loaded, and full of grazers. Leather, wool, cotton, paper, wood: the mat eats its own products and will not be fussy about yours. Synthetics, glass, titanium, glazed ceramic. Nothing with a nap. Nothing you'd be sad to see quilted.

**Shell.** Fully sealed hard shell, hood, face cover, no down. The fog is *warm* — it condenses on your cold outer surface and dumps latent heat into it while soaking you. You will be simultaneously steamed and frozen. Vapour-barrier layering under a genuinely waterproof shell; a spare dry base layer sealed in a bag you never open except to change.

**Foot platform.** Wide, rigid, load-spreading — think snowshoe crossed with a mud sled. Two purposes: it stops you sinking, and it stops you punching through a chamber roof. Never take a step off the platform edge with your full weight.

**Probe.** A five-metre graduated rod with a thermistor tip. This is your most important instrument and you will use it every twenty paces until it becomes a habit like breathing. Three returns:
- *Resistance rising, then rock at 3–4 m, cold:* normal mat. Walk.
- *Void, then warm stinking air:* a chamber. Air pockets the size of rooms held open by lignified struts. Go around. Go around widely.
- *Thin leather, then cold wet nothing at all, then rock at exactly mat depth:* a thin place. An earlier state of the plain, still running, out of phase. **Do not put your hand in it.** More on this in §8.

**Rope and harness.** Thirty metres, partner-tied, with a rigid spreader and a mechanical advantage rig you have both drilled blindfolded. If someone goes through the leather, you have minutes, not hours: the chamber atmosphere is anoxic and stinking and the walls are fungal felt that grazes on things. Haul, don't descend. If you must descend, that is a rescue you will probably not finish.

**Hearing.** Impulse-attenuating protection with talk-through and a throat mic. You need to hear your partner and your own voice at all times. You will habituate to the plain's noise within an hour — "you stop hearing it, which is different" — and the only way back is your own recorded voice.

**Two independent recorders.** One per person, running continuously, sealed, timestamped. This is your external sequence. Compare them at extraction. Discrepancies are data and will be the most upsetting data you have ever collected.

**Light and colour.** Headlamp plus a wide flood; noon is the colour of a nickel and you can stare at the sun's position and see only a warmer smear. But the more important thing is *colour*. Carry a small bag of violently saturated, mutually distinguishable objects — a red cord, a cobalt tile, a yellow flag, an orange whistle. In a Ganzfeld your visual system will start manufacturing edges within twenty minutes. Give it real ones. Look at your red cord every ten minutes. This sounds trivial. It is not.

**The difference kit.** Twenty ceramic tiles, each with a unique arbitrary scratch pattern, photographed before crossing, sealed in a rigid case. Every hour, open, count, compare against memory and against your partner's independent memory, reseal. You are checking for three things: a wrong count, a duplicate, and a tile you don't remember. Any of the three is an exit signal.

**Water and food.** Carry all water in. Six to eight litres per person for thirty hours at altitude on that surface. Do not drink local water. Not filtered, not boiled, not through a ceramic candle. My reason is not microbiological and I want to be honest that it is intuition: names slide off things there and land on *the taste of the water*. That is the medium in which identity is currently being dissolved. Don't take it internally. Eat frequently, hot, and stupidly calorific.

**Waste.** Sealed, carried out, all of it. See §9.

**Extraction sled.** Wide, rigid, for a body. Yours or your partner's. You will not want to bring it. Bring it.

## 6. Instruments that actually work there

Throw out your normal navigation assumptions:

- **Counting fails.** You will count forty pumps and get a different number twice, not because they move but because *one* is a distinction the flat no longer maintains carefully. Step-counting fails for the same reason: leagues of identical quilt with nothing to break the count.
- **Distance estimation fails.** Nothing is tall enough to occlude anything else. The horizon sits at eye level five kilometres out and does not change as you approach. You can watch a thing all day and pass it in ten minutes. Do not trust "it's getting closer." Do not chase anything on the basis of apparent size.
- **Maps fail.** A map is a set of persistent identities. There aren't any.

What *does* work:

**The phase field.** Forty pumps, all running at slightly different periods, drifting in and out of phase across a morning. The individual pumps are unreliable individuals but the *interference pattern of the whole field* is a durable property of the place. Carry a small infrasound array and a logging thermometer. Record relations, not objects: this one is 41 beats/min and lagging that one by a third of a cycle; the warm axis runs this way; the beat-gradient steepens toward the northwest. You are navigating by gradient, the way you'd navigate a smell.

**Heat.** The world has one gradient left — vertical, warm mat to cold sky — and the pumps are where it becomes horizontal and legible. A thermal sensor is worth more than a compass.

**Birds.** The waders are the best instruments on the plain and they are free. They go up when a sump is about to open, when the field synchronises, when a pump fails. Watch where they *refuse to settle*. I have no mechanism for this and I believe it anyway: they will not stand over thin places.

**The lead meter.** Sound arrives before its event there — sleet after its sound, footprints filling a half-second before you step, the valve's *clack* a moment before it clacks. Measure that lead continuously. Baseline is roughly half a second. **If the lead grows past about one and a half seconds, leave.** Beyond that you start hearing yourself say things you have not decided to say, and you are no longer the origin of your own behaviour in a way you will not be able to feel from inside.

## 7. Moving on the plain

Pick a bearing from the phase field and hold it. Walk on the probe's permission, not your eyes'. Keep your partner in voice contact and in *disagreement* — talk about something you have different opinions of.

**Approach pumps from the cold side, upwind or crosswind.** Never linger in the warm wet plume downwind of the nozzles. This is where the herds condense. Warm, wet, standing bodies in that plume are nucleation sites. The plain makes things there. You are a warm wet standing body with a very distinctive shape.

**Keep your thermal signature small.** Insulate outward. Do not stand steaming in the fog with your hood off, feeling alive. To a landscape whose only remaining gradient is warm-to-cold, a 37 °C core under a cold sky is not a visitor; it is a small pump, or the beginnings of one.

**Never stop for more than a few minutes without the platform down and a timer running.** Standing still, you sink — slowly, comfortably, centimetres an hour, water rising around your boots, tissue growing toward the warmth. It is not unpleasant. That is the danger. People do not fall into the mat; they *settle* into it.

**During a tutti** — the minute or two when all forty drift into phase and the whole moor thumps together and every wader goes up screaming — stop walking, get on the platform, hold your partner, and say your names out loud. Do not navigate and do not make decisions. That is the moment the plain is most nearly one thing, and you should be at your loudest about being two.

**When a pump fails,** the silence that opens is enormous and you will discover you have been shouting for some time. Treat sudden silence as a stop signal: crouch, probe, re-establish voice contact, wait for the birds to settle. Disorientation in silence is how people walk into sumps.

## 8. The specific hazards

**Chambers.** Room-sized voids under three metres of quilt, warm, stinking, packed with springtails in drifts a metre deep and blind pale grazers on the fungal walls. Falling in is a confined-space asphyxiation inside a digestive organ. Probe. Rope. Haul.

**Sumps.** They open with a boom you feel in the chest and close by evening. The bladderwort snaps all day and all night like a distant electrical fault. Any depression where the quilt looks *hungry* — dark, glossy, rainbow bacterial oil-slick — is a sump or a becoming-sump. Colour means process; process means both information and hazard. Look, photograph, don't stand.

**Valves and crowns.** A hundred tonnes of black water goes up a lignified standpipe and out through a crown of nozzles. The horn-consistency cuticle valve drops with a *clack* that you will hear a half-second early — use that. That pre-echo is the only reliable warning you get on this plain and it is a genuine gift: when you hear the clack, you have half a second to not be under it. Never climb a pump. Never sleep against one. Never anchor a line to one. It is a pressure vessel with a compost heart at blood heat and it is not furniture.

**Herds.** Pony-sized, long-legged, mouths that are only decorative, a pelt between hair and moss. They are not aggressive; the near ones are glossy and quick and shoulder each other, and several hundred of them will knock you down and trample you without noticing. Read their temperature: the rime-crusted slow far ones are dying and being taken back. Do not stand among the slow ones. Do not help one. Do not adopt one. They are not animals in the sense your compassion expects; they are the mat's produce, and the mat is its own customer.

**Prototypes.** A fog front rolls through and leaves a hundred half-formed things standing in the water, wrong-legged, dissolving as you watch. Some of them will be nearly right. Some of them will be nearly *you*. Do not touch, do not speak to, do not attempt to finish one. This is the single most common cause of people overstaying.

**Thin places.** Where the leather has worn through, there is not rock but an earlier state of the plain still running, a little out of phase — the same pump from a different angle, in weather that is not your weather, its fog going the other way. You *can* put your hand in. It is cold and wet and exactly as deep as the mat and then it is the finished rock, planed, polished, utterly indifferent.

Don't. The hazard is not injury. That state is *running*, and if it runs you, it runs you out of phase: your hand comes back before you put it in, or comes back belonging to a slightly different arrangement of you. And they are not exits. They go to the same place, sideways in sequence, forever. Every traveller who has tried to use one as a shortcut has either not returned or returned in a way their family describes carefully.

**Duplicates.** You will find two of the same grazer, three days and two leagues apart, same notched ear, same hesitation before the head goes down. That's the mould, worn smooth. The mat isn't copying — copying needs an original. It's just that the parts come out alike now.

If it happens to your partner: **stop, rope up, do not choose.** There is no test that works from inside, because any test you devise is a distinction, and distinctions are the thing being planed. Both will remember the argument. Both will be right. Contact your outside witness, invoke the pre-committed extraction, and let something that is not on the plain decide. This is why you set that up before you crossed.

If it happens to you: you will not notice. That is what the recorders are for.

## 9. Contamination discipline

There is not a made thing on that plain — not a chip of flint, not a scratch — and this is not innocence, it is digestion. Nothing accumulates. You are the only artifact for a day's walk in any direction, and you will be worked on.

**Leave nothing organic. Anything.** Hair, nails, spit, snot, tears, vomit, dead skin, blood. Nosebleeds are near-universal at that altitude: plug, catch, bag, carry. A dropped scab is a sample; a sample is a template; a template is a hundred wrong-legged versions of you standing in the water dissolving as somebody else watches. Manage blisters as a containment incident. If you cut yourself badly, that is an extraction, not a dressing.

**Do not die there.** Say this to your partner out loud before you cross and agree the protocol. A body left in the mat is a mould, and the mould does not wear out. Extraction of the dead is mandatory and is the reason for the sled. Agree it in advance because you will not agree it afterwards.

**Sleep.** Ideally, don't. If you must: rigid insulated platform, tethered, hood up, partner awake and talking to you at fixed intervals, timer held by the *outside* witness, not by either of you. Never in a plume. Never against a pump. Never on bare quilt "just for twenty minutes."

## 10. Identity protocols

These are the actual point of the briefing. Everything above is scaffolding.

**Do not name anything.** Not a pump, not a pool, not a fog front, not the plain. Names do not stick there; you will name a feature at dawn and by dusk the name will have slid onto something else, or onto nothing, or you will find yourself using it for the taste of the water. Use procedural descriptors only: "the one at bearing 271, forty-one a minute, lagging." If you catch yourself using a proper noun for a sensation, that's an exit signal.

**Use your own name constantly, and attach it to a relation.** Say it. Have your partner say it. Say it in a sentence with the person you're coming home to. The bare token can slide; the token in use between two people is much harder to plane.

**The hourly differentiation.** Every hour, on the hour:
1. Names, both, aloud.
2. Tiles: count, inspect, compare against both memories independently.
3. Lead check: sound-to-event interval.
4. Each tells the other something only they know, and each confirms whether it's still true and still theirs.
5. Record the reason you came, in the sentence with the person in it.

Log it. Two logs. If the logs disagree at any point, you leave.

**Move on intention, not reaction.** Sound precedes event by half a second. Your reflexes will fire on the pre-echo and lunge you into the thing you're flinching from. Drill this before crossing: deliberate, committed, slow movements; no startle responses; treat every sound as a *forecast*, not a report. It is trainable and it takes about a week.

**Expect to lose surprise.** Nothing will ever quite startle you there, because you'll hear it first. Surprise is the gap between expectation and event, and that gap is a sequence, and sequence is being eroded. People describe this as the worst part — worse than the cold, worse than the noise. It feels like authorship draining out. Know it's coming and know it's the environment, not you.

## 11. Extraction

**The decision to leave must be made before you cross.**

This is the single most important line in this document. You will not decide to leave from inside, because deciding is a sequence and sequence is what the flat is currently eating. Everyone who overstayed intended to leave. The intention was there the whole time; it just stopped being *before* the staying.

So: a fixed extraction time, held by a witness who is not on the plain, enforced by something that does not care how you feel. A tether, a lock, a call you must answer. Whatever your crossing method supports, make the return *not contingent on your judgment*. Give the witness override authority and tell them to use it even if you beg.

Plus the automatic aborts, any one of which ends the trip immediately with no discussion:
- lead > 1.5 s
- tile count wrong, duplicate, or unfamiliar
- the two logs disagree
- a name attaches to a sensation
- you find footprints you haven't made yet going somewhere you didn't intend
- a duplicate of anything with your face on it
- silence where a pump was
- your partner asks you to stop doing the hourly check

That last one is not a negotiation. It's a symptom.

## 12. After

Assume you brought something back. Not a parasite — a *smoothing*.

Thirty days of distinction hygiene, and take it as seriously as decompression:
- Be with people who know you well and will tell you when you're off.
- Cook from recipes. Sign your name by hand. Keep a dated diary. Do things with sharp order and arbitrary detail — music with structure, arguments, chess, anything where sequence is the whole point.
- Photograph yourself daily. Compare.
- Have someone else check your recorders against each other, and don't listen to them alone the first time.
- Watch for the lead. If you're still answering before the question a week out, get help from someone who works with this and don't try to ride it out.
- Watch for the taste of the water. If a flavour starts arriving with a name attached, that's a residual, and it fades, and it takes about a month.

**Don't go back soon.** The intuition — and it is only intuition — is that the mould takes an impression. Second visits stamp faster. People who go twice describe the second trip as "being recognised," and they never mean it warmly.

## 13. What it will actually be like

Nobody prepares you for how *beautiful* it is, and how little that helps.

You will walk out onto a grey quilt under a grey seam of sky, in a light like the inside of a nickel, and the noise will hit you: a marshalling yard, a mill town heard from a hill at four in the morning, clack and sigh and hiss and the vast wet suck of the sumps, and the applause of sleet on the leather. And nothing built it. Nothing ever built anything here. Your first hour will be spent trying to find the maker, and your second will be spent giving that up, and that giving-up is the actual experience of the place.

Then a sundew field will go rust-red at you out of the grey and it will be so shocking you'll laugh. Then a herd will condense out of the fog fifty metres away, steaming, already walking, glossy and alive and shouldering each other, and you will feel a completely unearned tenderness for them, and over the next four hours you will watch them go slow, and crust with rime, and stop, and sink a little, and be taken back. And there will be more. There are always more. That's the whole economy: a furious rate of production with one product, one customer, and no warehouse.

At what passes for evening the grey goes bluer and colder and forty columns of pale vapour stand out to the edge of sight, each thumping on its own schedule, each with its cloud of new animals cooling as they walk away into the enormous empty quilt. It is the loneliest thing you will ever see and you will not want to leave, and the not-wanting is not yours. It's the flat, gently reducing the difference between staying and going.

Somewhere out in the dark a valve will lift, and hiss, and drop.

You'll hear it a moment before it happens.

Be already walking.
