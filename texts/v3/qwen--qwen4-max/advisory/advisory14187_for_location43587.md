# Preparing for The Making

## A traveler's brief, written by someone who assumes you have one body and intend to keep it

---

### 0. First, decide whether you are willing to be altered

Everything else in this document is logistics. This part is not.

The account is explicit: your ears ring for three days, and then stop, and then for the rest of your life, in quiet rooms, you feel a patient tidal pressure in your sternum, leaning toward something that circles. That is not a metaphor and it is not a souvenir. It is a permanent change to how your body reports the world, acquired in an afternoon, and it does not appear to fade.

Assume it follows you across worlds. Assume it is, in some sense, a *coupling* — that a heavy knot of water four hundred meters wide has learned your mass and kept a note of it. You do not know what else in the multiverse can read that note. You do not know whether the lean strengthens each time you go back.

So: decide before you climb. Not at the lip, with your teeth aching and the whole thing roaring open in front of you, because you will not decide well there. Write your decision down at home, dated, and give a copy to someone who loves you. If the answer is *no*, there is no shame in it; the range is a hundred kilometers of the sharpest, cleanest, most beautiful stone you will ever see, and you can walk it for a month without ever putting your face in the water.

If the answer is *yes*, read on.

---

### 1. The body you bring

You are going to breath-hold dive, in near-freezing salt water, at four thousand meters, inside a pressurized rock bubble, in a sound field that vibrates your fillings, with no surface to float to. Every one of those clauses is individually manageable. Together they kill the unprepared.

**Medical clearance, in this order of importance:**

- **Lungs.** Spirometry, no asthma, no history of pneumothorax, no bullae. A burst lung in there is not survivable; there is no chamber, no evacuation, and no room for a stretcher in a doorway slot.
- **Heart.** Cold-water face immersion drives your heart rate down while cold-water body immersion drives it up. That autonomic conflict produces arrhythmias in healthy people. Get an ECG; disclose the plan honestly to the physician; do not go with a known arrhythmia or a channelopathy in the family.
- **Ears and sinuses.** ENT clearance. You will be equalizing in a place whose interior pressure you have not yet measured, with a sub-audible floor hammering your sinuses. Go with clear passages. **Never go with a cold.** Never go on decongestants you'd need to re-dose mid-shift; rebound at depth is how people rupture things.
- **Teeth.** Get them looked at *before*, not after. Loose crowns, cracked molars, gaps under old fillings — the lip makes teeth ache in a way that suggests resonance, and resonance finds flaws. A tooth that fractures in there is a small emergency inside a large one.
- **A dive medical specifically**, including discussion of patent foramen ovale. You will see why in §7.
- No pregnancy, and do not bring children or animals. The choughs hatch deaf. Every one, from hatching. Sit with that fact for a while before you decide what a season in there might do to something still being made.

**Altitude.** Four thousand meters is not a viewpoint, it is an altitude. Stage it: sleep at 2500, then 3000, then 3500, then the valley. Five to seven days minimum before your first entry, longer if you can. Learn your own prodrome — the specific way *your* body announces headache, nausea, sleeplessness, ataxia — and treat any of it as a hard stop, not a thing to push through. Prefer staged ascent to drugs. If you do take acetazolamide, understand that it is a diuretic stacked on top of immersion diuresis, that it makes your fingers tingle (which will mask the first sign of decompression trouble), and that it shifts your CO₂ drive, which matters enormously when your entire plan is holding your breath. No alcohol, no sedatives, no sleeping pills.

**Apnea.** Whatever you can do at sea level, halve it. Then halve it again. At four thousand meters your inspired oxygen pressure is roughly half what your body was built around; you will desaturate faster and blackout with less warning, and the warning you do get will be masked by cold, by euphoria, by the roar. **Never hyperventilate. Never train tables in there. Never do a "long one" to see.** Your first shift should feel embarrassingly short.

**Cold and hands.** Twelve degrees or less. Cold shock is a gasp reflex, and a gasp reflex underwater is a drowning. Enter slowly, in the cleft, where you can stand and let it happen to your chest above the waterline. After that, your fingers go stupid in fifteen to thirty minutes bare. Rehearse every single thing you intend to do inside — knots, knife, adze, slate, mask clear — with numb hands, in cold water, wearing the gloves you'll actually wear. If you can't do it fumbling, you can't do it there.

### 2. Getting to the stain

A hundred kilometers of featureless new mountain, no dust, no scree, no rounded gravel, streams that lay pale terraces instead of cutting. It is a landscape with no small features and therefore no handholds for navigation. In cloud or whiteout it becomes a white room with a floor that will kill you.

- **Hire a guide from the valley.** Not a porter — a cutter, or someone the cutters vouch for. The stain is invisible from the valley floor and invisible from the ridge above; it is visible from somewhere specific, and that somewhere is local knowledge held by people who have walked it since childhood. There is no clever substitute.
- Carry satellite positioning if your kit works there, plus paper bearings, plus a compass, and *do not trust any of them over the guide*. Note whether compasses behave oddly near the cleft; a two-meter knot of water that pulls the hair on your arm is not a thing to be casual about.
- **Fuel and shelter.** No wood. No soil. Lichen and grass, that's the inventory. Carry stove fuel, carry a real tent, carry more insulation than the forecast suggests. Assume weather can pin you for three days.
- Category 4 eye protection and a spare. Pale fingernail-colored terraces, thin bright air, ordinary daylight — this is snow-blindness country without any snow.
- **The last twenty meters are the technical crux.** Sixteen hand-cut steps up the outside of a wet cliff, on stone whose edges are *unfinished*-sharp. Nothing here has been rounded by use. Those edges will cut your hands and shear a loaded rope. Bring gloves you're willing to destroy, pad every edge your line crosses, and rig your own handline above the steps rather than trusting a grass rope whose lower end is being eaten.
- Understand what "nothing wears" means for your ethics: outside, your boot scuffs and your cairns and your scratched initials will not weather away. They may be legible in a million years. **Leave your marks outside, deliberately, sparingly, and never inside.** Outside is where marks last. Inside is where marks are buried.

### 3. The people, and what you owe them

This is not a wonder. It is a workplace with four generations of accumulated craft, a guild vocabulary, a survey record, and dead colleagues. Green stone is why the valley exists: soft as cheese for an hour, sets in a day, outlasts anything anyone can build. You are walking into an economy.

**Ask permission before you touch water.** Offer to pay, and mean it. Offer to work — hauling, tending, minding the outside clock — before you ask to cut.

**Things you must not do, whether or not anyone tells you:**

- Do not chip a dome. A dome is a season of someone's invested labor. It is capital.
- Do not move a copper pin. Those are survey marks. They are the only reason anyone knows anything.
- Do not cut a frond. There are twelve. Each stands at the top of a column of rock shaped like its own life; a cross-section is a year-ring record older than anyone's grandmother. There is no replacement.
- Do not take grazers. Three an hour in a volume that could hold a city. That is the entire megafauna.
- Do not add to the tallies at the entrance, or "correct" them.
- Do not hammer. The craft is *cutting*, quietly, inside drifting pockets of silence. Percussion pollutes the sound field other people are navigating by, and could collapse a node someone's shift depends on.
- Do not take stone without asking, and do not take the piece someone has just freed.

**Bring gifts that are actually useful.** They have salt; salt is the one thing here that is free and infinite. They likely lack copper, grain, cloth, oil, sugar, fuel, needles, glass. But the two gifts I would prioritize:

1. **A ledger.** Waterproof paper, pencils, a hard cover. Four generations of counting are scratched on a wall that is glazing them over — the record is being eaten by the thing it records. Give them a way to keep the counts *outside*. Then ask, politely, for a copy of what's already there.
2. **Hearing protection with acoustic filters**, several pairs, and a demonstration. Then ask the oldest cutter how much of this conversation she can hear, and listen to the answer.

**What to ask for, in exchange, before you enter:**

- The calf's period and its path, and the growth curve across the four generations. Is it accelerating?
- The cleft width at its narrowest, measured today, and the historical measurements.
- The current node craft: how they read the shimmer, how long a node typically lasts, where they rendezvous.
- Their taboos. All of them, including the ones that sound silly. Keep every one. They are four generations of accident report written in a language you don't speak.
- **How many are buried, and where.** And then ask what the funniest man in the crew is like now.

### 4. Five numbers to measure before you commit to anything

You cannot prepare for this place from the description. You can prepare to *measure* it. Bring instruments and spend your first day at the lip, not in the water.

1. **Absolute pressure at the lip, at the ceiling, and at the floor.** A cheap dive gauge or a logging pressure sensor. This is the single most important number in the whole expedition and I will explain why in §7. The account tells you the accretion is fastest at the lip "because that is where the pressure drops." That sentence means **the interior is pressurized relative to the outside.** You are not swimming in a cave pool. You are swimming in the inside of something under load.
2. **The gas in a dome.** Sample it — a small bladder, filled at the dome, sealed, carried out. Analyze outside: oxygen fraction, carbon dioxide, and if you can, nitrogen. A glowing splint at the mouth of a bladder is a crude field test for oxygen richness; a snuffed flame suggests CO₂. Do not use your own lungs as the instrument. Do not breathe an untested dome, ever, not once, not "just a mouthful."
3. **The pressure–depth gradient.** Do not assume one atmosphere per ten meters. There is a gravitational anomaly in the room. Log it.
4. **Accretion rate, on a test coupon.** Hang a smooth plate of known dimensions in the wall zone on a tended line, retrieve it at measured intervals, and quantify. Do the same in mid-water. You need to know how fast the world closes on a stationary object, and whether the danger is confined to the boundary layer near the walls. My strong inference is that it is — the description says precipitation happens *where arriving water meets older water*, which makes this a wall phenomenon and mid-water comparatively safe. **Verify it. Then plan around it anyway.**
5. **The calf's circuit, timed.** Sit at the lip and watch for the lensing — the place where the far wall bends and doubles. Log its period until you can predict it. Do this before you are in the water and frightened.

Also: measure the slot. Every visit. With a stick, and write the number down with the date. It has closed a hand's width in one woman's lifetime and it is closing fastest exactly where you must fit through.

### 5. Kit

**On your body:**

- Thick open-cell freediving wetsuit, two-piece, hooded — 7 mm if you can fit through the slot in it. The hood does double duty: warmth and acoustic attenuation. Understand the trade-off explicitly: the hood that saves your hearing also degrades your ability to find nodes, which is how the cutters stay alive. Do not solve this by going without the hood.
- **Minimal ballast, ideally none.** Salt at thirty-five parts per thousand is very buoyant and a thick suit is more so, and every instinct will tell you to add lead. Resist. There is a mobile gravity well in the room that pulls your body, and lead is the thing it will pull hardest. Aim to be positively buoyant throughout, and accept that you will have to pull yourself down and work against your own float. That is a fitness requirement, not a problem to solve with weight.
- Smooth, non-shedding outer surfaces. No fleece, no wool, no fraying straps. Tape your seams. Swim cap; tie back and contain every hair. Trim your nails. **No sunscreen, no lotion, no lip balm, no insect repellent** — shade and cloth instead. See §9.
- Low-volume mask plus a spare, and eye protection you can wear at the lip. Salt in the eye at four thousand meters with no fresh water inside is a real incapacitation.
- Gloves you can work in, and a spare pair.
- **Two knives**, reachable with either hand, mounted where a numb hand will find them by touch. This is not optional. See §6.
- Vented acoustic-filter ear protection, plus the hood. **Do not wear solid occlusive earplugs while changing depth** — you will squeeze your ear canals and bleed into them. Solid plugs are for the air at the lip only.
- A thin custom mouthguard, if the tooth resonance is as bad as advertised. Test it in cold water first; a gag reflex underwater is its own emergency.

**Rigged:**

- **Your own line, tended from outside by a named person who is not in the water.** Not anchored below. Never anchored below. The rope in the account "hangs down from the cleft and stops" — that is not neglect, that is doctrine. Anything fixed in place becomes stone; a line you are tied to that becomes stone is a line you are tied to forever.
- The tender works the line constantly, keeps it moving, checks it for glazing, and holds an outside clock with authority to haul you out whether or not you agree.
- Pre-agreed line signals, drilled on land until they are reflex: one pull *OK*, two *up/out*, three *calf*, four *node*, continuous *HAUL NOW*. Touch is the only channel in there that cannot be drowned out.
- A high-contrast slate and a grease pencil. Visibility is unlimited and sound is useless; broad black-on-white arm signals will read at a hundred meters. Learn from the choughs: they abandoned hearing entirely and navigate by watching wings. Be a bird about it.
- Two independent timekeepers. There is no night inside, no shadow-clock, no dusk. It is always a bright afternoon in there and your body will have no idea how long you have been working. This is one of the quietest and most serious hazards in the whole place.

**Tools and trade:** copper, bronze, or titanium — copper because the locals use it and it survives, titanium because it's strong and inert. Expect ordinary steel to be eaten. An adze, not a hammer. Rigid boxes for stone. A bladder for gas samples. A ledger. Fresh water in quantity, for drinking and for rinsing gear *outside*, away from the streams — those streams have no salt in them and never have had; don't be the one who introduces it.

**Leave behind:** anything you love, anything sentimental, anything with a face on it, anything porous, anything you would be unwilling to have become permanent geology. And leave behind the idea that you will be rescued.

### 6. The one law: here, water builds

Everything dangerous about this place descends from a single inversion. Water does not subtract. It lays down. It precipitates where the new water meets the old, soft as cheese for an hour, then rock that outlasts everything.

Therefore:

- **Never be still for long.** Not resting on a shelf, not wedged in a crevice, not floating happily in a node with your eyes shut. The tallies on the wall are under a translucent skin. The grass ladder is being climbed like ivy. The fronds survive only by growing outward at exactly the rate they are buried. You do not grow outward. Sit still long enough and you will begin to be glazed, and the first sign will be a stiffness at a strap or a hair you can't pull free.
- **Buddy-check for glazing every few minutes.** Hair, hood seam, mask skirt, between the fingers, the corners of the mouth, the folds of a glove, the buckle you can't see. Make it a ritual with a physical routine, not a glance.
- **Never set anything down.** Not fins, not the adze, not the box, not for a moment. An hour is all it takes. Everything you carry stays on your body or on the moving line.
- **Stay off the walls.** The wall zone is where the arriving water is, where the pressure gradient is, where precipitation happens, where the fronds are being buried alive. Mid-water is the safe volume. Work at the wall in short bursts and retreat.
- **Do not put your palm flat on stone and leave it there.** Yes, the seams are warm. Yes, it smells of wet flint and the moment before lightning. Warm your hands briefly, in motion, and take them away.
- **Never tie yourself to anything inside.** Not the pin, not the ladder, not a shelf, not a frond's column. Your line is tended from outside or it does not exist.
- **There is no surface to float to.** Look at the account again: the ceiling is three meters over your head and it is *rock*, wet and close and ridged. The cutters chip cups in the roof and wait a season for a bubble, and put their faces up into it to breathe. People who have a surface do not do that. Plan on the assumption that if you black out inside, you will float up and press your face against wet stone. The only air you can count on is at the lip, at the end of your line, hauled by someone who is watching a clock.
- **Anything filled with air or fresh water is an interface, and interfaces are where stone forms fastest.** That is the whole explanation for why the lip accretes hardest: pressure drops there, gas comes out of solution, mineral precipitates. It is a travertine dam, and it is sealing itself. Generalize the rule and it becomes the most useful thing in this brief: *every pressure boundary in this place is a place where the world closes.* Your mask. Your suit. Your lungs. A dry bag. A portal. **Never open a transit inside the chamber.** You would create a pressure drop, and the drop would plug with stone, and your way home would become a wall. Do your jumping outside, on the warm rock, in the wind.

### 7. The domes, and the trap they set for a traveler

This is the part that will kill a competent, careful, well-equipped visitor who has done everything else right.

The chamber is pressurized. The arriving water carries dissolved breath under that pressure, and the breath seeps out into the ceiling cups. So the gas in a dome is at chamber pressure — not at the thin four-thousand-meter pressure your body has spent a week acclimatizing to.

When you put your face up into a dome and breathe, **you are breathing compressed gas.** When you swim out through the slot into wind at 0.6 atmospheres, **you are decompressing**, hard and fast, from whatever that chamber pressure is to some of the thinnest air a human works in.

The account hands you the symptoms and calls them color: men who work the deep domes come out *talking too fast, laughing, seeing their mothers in the braids of the current*. That is not local charm. That is a gas effect — inert-gas narcosis, or oxygen at a partial pressure your ancestors never met, or both. And the bubbles shrink between visits, which means the water is taking the gas back, which means the pressure and the composition are not stable.

So:

- **Measure the pressure and the gas before you breathe any of it.** If the oxygen fraction times the absolute pressure exceeds about 1.4, do not breathe it — oxygen seizures underwater are drownings, and CO₂ retention from breath-hold work lowers the threshold further. If there's meaningful CO₂, do not breathe it at all. If the nitrogen pressure is high, expect to be drunk and plan for it: no tools, no decisions, no cutting.
- **Treat every dome breath as a dive.** Short exposures. Seconds to a couple of minutes. No long conversations in a dome. No repeated deep sessions in a day. Log every one: depth, pressure, duration, gas.
- **Exit slowly.** Stage at the lip. Hang there. Give yourself time you feel you don't need.
- **After any dome day: do not go higher, and do not jump to a lower-pressure world.** This is the specifically multiversal hazard and it is the one nobody local can warn you about, because they only have one sky. Your next transit might drop you into a thin-atmosphere world, or a station, or something worse. Breathing compressed gas at four thousand meters and then stepping into 0.4 atmospheres is how you get bent in a place with no chamber. **Give it twenty-four to forty-eight hours at consistent pressure before any transit that changes ambient.** Write this into your itinerary before you leave home, because on the day you will be euphoric and in a hurry.
- Watch for the signs afterward: joint pain, mottled skin, numbness, tingling, unusual fatigue, confusion. And know that altitude sickness and decompression sickness overlap enough to fool you. If in doubt, treat it as the more serious one, get to pressure, get help, and stop pretending you're fine.
- The rich air will feel *wonderful*. That is the trap. The men who laugh in the deep domes are not having a good time; they are exhibiting a symptom. Let their experience be your warning rather than your endorsement.

### 8. The roar, and the craft of quiet

The sound is water being made along four hundred meters of wall at once: a hiss multiplied into a solid, white noise with a floor beneath hearing that you feel in the sternum, the sinuses, the fillings of your teeth. Sustained, never resolving, like standing behind a bell the instant it is struck, forever.

Earplugs will not save your sternum. Bone conduction and whole-body coupling don't care about your ear canals. What protection buys you is the *audible* portion — the part that produces permanent threshold shift over a season. Take that trade; wear the filters and the hood; accept that the rest of it will happen to your body anyway.

Then learn the actual craft, which is the most beautiful thing about this place: **the roar folds and cancels, and there are coffin-sized pockets of silence drifting slowly through the chamber.** The cutters navigate by these. They read the shimmer, follow a node as it wanders, work inside it while it lasts, and haul out when it drifts away and the roar comes back like a hand over the mouth.

Prepare for this specifically:

- Spend your first shift doing nothing but learning to *see* a node. The refraction shimmer, the seam-lines like the shadow of a window frame. Ask a cutter to point them out to you from the lip until you can spot them yourself.
- Practice the transition. The moment the roar returns is disorienting and, if you are deep and cold and narcotic, it is the moment people panic. Drill the response: **hand to line, signal, go.** Not "look around first." Not "finish the cut."
- Two heads in one node can talk in ordinary voices. That is your only speech channel. Use it for brief, pre-planned exchanges. Do not waste a node's duration on conversation, and do not chase a node to finish a sentence.
- Accept the aftermath honestly. Three days of ringing is described as normal. **One-sided ringing, sudden deafness, vertigo, or fluid from an ear is not normal** — that is injury, and it is your last warning before permanence. Leave, and don't come back for a long time, if ever.

### 9. You are the only thing here that wears out

This is the insight I would want carried into the water more than any piece of gear.

Nothing in a hundred kilometers has been subtracted yet. No scree, no sand, no dust, no rounded gravel. The streams cut nothing. The only worn object in the entire landscape is an adze socket, shaped by a grip, described as *the only friction in a hundred kilometers*.

And then you arrive: shedding skin, shedding hair, breathing out carbon dioxide, leaking a little salt and a little oil and a little thread from every seam. You are a small portable engine of erosion, walking into a world that has not invented it. You will be the strangest thing that has ever happened here — stranger than the copper pins, stranger than the ladder.

So contain your erosion:

- Smooth, sealed, non-shedding surfaces. No loose fibers. Contained hair. No cosmetics.
- **Do not eat inside. Do not drink inside. Do not spit. Keep your mouth closed.** Your mouth and eyes are fresh-water interfaces in a place where fresh meeting salt makes stone. I cannot prove that matters. I would bet a finger on it, and I would behave as though it were law.
- **Do not go in bleeding.** Any cut, any abrasion, any menstruation — cover it properly or don't enter. Salt at thirty-five parts per thousand in an open wound is agony; a clot is a nucleation site; and blood is the most interesting thing this water has ever met.
- Do not urinate in the chamber. Say it out loud in your briefing so nobody has to be embarrassed about it later.
- **Biosecurity is the real reason for most of this.** Twelve fronds. Two animals. No plankton, no silt, no drifting mote of anything dead. This sea is a first draft and nothing has moved in yet. A single diatom on a fin from another world, a single gut bacterium, a single spore in a seam of neoprene, and you may have ended the only empty ocean anyone will ever get to see. Use new or sterilized kit. Dry it completely. Bring nothing organic. Rinse and quarantine your gear between worlds as though you were entering a clean room, because you are.
- If you carry green stone out, carry it in a rigid box, touching nothing, and let it set fully before you pack it among your things. Do not put soft green stone in a pocket. Do not let two pieces touch. And **declare it** at your next transit: you are exporting a mineral that builds instead of eroding, and you do not know what it does in a sea that has weather. Assume the answer is "something you would regret."

### 10. The calf

A knot of denser, cooler, saltier water, two meters across, visible only as a lens where the far wall bends and doubles. It circles on a slow oblique path and everything leans toward it: your body, the rope, the fronds, the fine hair on your arm. It has grown measurably in four generations.

- **Learn its period before you enter, and time your shifts by it,** exactly as the cutters do. This is not folklore; it is the local equivalent of a tide table, and tide tables are the difference between a profession and a drowning.
- Learn to feel it as well as see it. The pull on your arm hair is a bearing you can take with your eyes shut, in a place where your eyes cannot judge distance anyway.
- **Never work between the calf and a wall.** That is where you get pressed into the accretion zone.
- **No lead.** Say it again. Minimal ballast. Positive buoyancy. The thing that pulls your body pulls your weights harder, and weights are the part of you that will not let go.
- If you feel the lean strengthen unexpectedly — if the *period* is wrong — get out and re-log it. It is growing. Do not extend the confidence of a cutter who learned this water thirty years ago to the water you are in today.
- Two people in the water keep physical track of each other by touch and line, not by sight. Which brings me to the last thing.

### 11. The water is practicing

Your wake persists four or five seconds, legible, your own outline hanging in place. Then a second outline, slightly wrong, that you did not make. And once every few days, in the middle distance, a splayed hand two meters wide forms out of nothing, holds, and comes apart. It was a braid of currents. It was also a hand.

I am not going to tell you what that is. Nobody knows. What I will tell you is how to behave.

- **Never follow a shape.** Not a hand, not an outline, not something that looks like your buddy. Count people by touch and by line. A water that holds your wake for five seconds is a water in which you can be shown yourself, slightly wrong, at a distance you cannot judge, in light that comes from everywhere. That is a perfect trap for a human visual system and you should treat it as one.
- **Do not teach it.** No repeated deliberate gestures. No waving. No drawing. No making the same shape twice in the same place. No games, no signals carved in stone, no leaving anything hand-shaped behind. The fronds turn toward the roar; the water holds shapes too long; it has seen hands and it tries them, not often and not well. You are about to be the most complicated thing that has ever happened to it. Behave as though that were a responsibility, because it is.
- If the cutters have a rule about not speaking names in the water, keep it. If they don't, consider adopting it privately. It costs nothing.
- **Expect your mind to go strange.** Uniform shadowless light, no distance cues, no time cues, infrasound in your sinuses, cold, hypoxia, and possibly a narcotic gas mixture. That combination produces euphoria, pareidolia, and confident bad decisions. Men see their mothers in the braids. You will see something too. Counter it with structure: a written checklist on a slate, a hard turn-around time, an outside clock, and a partner who has explicit permission to haul you out while you are still insisting you're fine. The insistence is the symptom.

### 12. A first visit, shaped properly

**Days 1–5.** Staged ascent. Walk the range. Do not go near the cleft. Learn the valley, the people, the names of the dead.

**Day 6.** Approach with a guide. Look at the stain from the place they show you. Climb the sixteen steps on your own handline. Sit at the hut. Do not enter. Measure the slot. Write the number down.

**Day 7.** At the lip. Instruments only. Pressure at lip, ceiling, floor. Gas sample from a dome, analyzed outside. Watch the calf until you can predict it. Watch a full cutter shift from outside, with your ears protected, and count how long they are in and how long they are out. Learn to see the shimmer of a node from the air side.

**Day 8.** First entry. On your own line, tended by a named person with a clock. In the first ten meters only, where the choughs work. **Ten minutes.** No tools, no cutting, no stone, no domes. Nothing but floating on the line, feeling the lean, hearing the roar arrive and drop away, and learning what your own body does in there. Get out while it is still easy. Ring for three days.

**Days 9–20.** If and only if the numbers are good and the cutters want you: short shifts, one wall at a time, always moving, always on the line, always with a buddy and a knife on each side. Learn nodes properly. Earn the right to be told the taboos. Contribute to the ledger. Take nothing.

**Never, on any visit:** alone; without a tended line; without an outside clock; without measuring the slot; while congested; while bleeding; while narcotic; while happy in a way you cannot account for.

### 13. What can actually be done when it goes wrong

Be honest with yourself about this, at home, in a quiet room, before your sternum has ever leaned.

There is no rescue inside. There is a doorway twenty meters up a wet cliff, a slot narrower than last year, four hundred meters of water with no particulate to judge distance by, a sound field that erases speech, and a mobile gravity well. Nobody is coming to get you. The cutters know it; that is why their entire craft is built around *not needing* rescue — around quiet, and timing, and lines, and getting out early.

So agree in advance, in writing, with the people you're working with, what they should do if you don't come out. The right answer is almost certainly *nothing*, and you should tell them so, and pay them for the trouble, and leave word for whoever needs word. A body left in there does not rot and does not surface. It becomes part of the mountain, glazed over like a decade's tallies, indistinguishable from the rock it came from. There are worse things than that, but not many people get to choose it knowingly. Choose it knowingly.

### 14. Coming out

You come out through the slot into wind and silence so total it feels like a second deafness. The sun is where it was. The stone is warm under your palm.

Do this, in this order: get to the warm seams and sit in the sun and rewarm — briefly, in motion, hands off the rock when you're not actively warming them. Drink something hot and sweet and *fresh*. Rinse the salt off your gear with your own water, away from the streams, and dry it completely. Stage at that altitude for the hours you promised yourself. Check your body for stiffness, for a hair that won't pull free, for a strap gone hard. Check your partner's, with your hands, where they can't see.

Then log everything: the slot width, the pressures, the gas, the calf's period, the accretion coupons, the node behavior, the shift durations, how you felt at minute eight and minute twenty. Add it to the ledger. Give them the copy. Four generations of counting are being eaten by the thing they count; the only reason any of it survives is that somebody eventually carried it outside.

Your ears will ring for three days. Let them. Don't go back during those three days, and don't jump worlds during them either.

And then, at some point, months later, in a hotel room in a world with weather and dust and rivers that carry things away, you will notice that the quiet has a shape. A pressure in the sternum. Patient, tidal. Leaning very slightly toward something that is circling.

That is the fee. You agreed to it on paper, at home, before you climbed. Take it out and read what you wrote. It will be the only thing in your possession that was made in a place where nothing has yet been taken away — and it will be, for the rest of your life, the one part of you that the world cannot wear down.
