# Preparing for The Slot

## 1. First, decide not to go down

The honest preparation begins with the recognition that this place does not need you, cannot use you, and will keep whatever you do to it for longer than your species has had language. Nothing here abrades. Nothing here rots, oxidizes quickly, gets rained on, gets walked on, gets forgotten. The mud curled eleven thousand years ago and every plate is where it fell. The pecking marks on the fifth hold are as sharp as the day they were struck. That is the whole difficulty: **this is a place with no eraser.**

So work through the tiers in order and stop at the first one that satisfies you.

**Tier zero: don't go.** Find it on survey imagery, read the description, look at the pan from a distance in the cold of dawn. Most people who want to go actually want to have gone. That want can be met elsewhere.

**Tier one: the lip.** Lie on your stomach on the chert band, at one chosen spot, on a pad, and put your face over. You will get the smell — old paper, hot iron — and the shock of air that does not move against your face, and forty metres of nothing under your cheekbones. This is most of the experience and a small fraction of the damage. Breathe out away from the crack; your breath is warmer and wetter than what's down there and it is the only water that has been offered to this place since the ice.

**Tier two: instruments.** A passive package lowered on a plumb line — lidar, camera, thermistor, hygrometer, a microphone that reads down to a few hertz. No rotors. A drone's downwash in a metre-and-a-half trench would raise the powder and re-settle eleven flakes into new attitudes, and those attitudes are data that took eleven millennia to hold still.

**Tier three: your body, once, hanging, for about ninety minutes, at midsummer.** The rest of this document assumes you have chosen tier three and that you understand you are choosing to be the largest event to occur in this trench since the water left.

---

## 2. Know what you are, physically, before you decide anything

You are a warm bag of water with a microbiome, in a place that has neither water nor mouths.

An hour of a working human's breathing and sweating puts somewhere between half a litre and a litre and a half of water into the air, plus tens of grams of carbon dioxide, plus skin cells, plus fungal spores, plus whatever is on your clothes. The slot's annual water input is *zero*. Not "small." The lip is tilted half a degree outward and sheds every millimetre of the twenty that fall. There is no ratio between your contribution and the natural one; you are dividing by zero.

The lichen lives between the sandstone grains on a film a few molecules thick. It has never encountered liquid water, or nitrogen in any form it could use, or a grazer, or a competing organism. Your exhalation is a rain event and a fertiliser spill and an inoculation, all at once, delivered directly onto the only living thing for a hundred kilometres.

Your body also outputs a hundred to two hundred watts. In a trench too narrow to convect, at 34°C, sealed inside protective clothing, you cannot shed any of it. Uncooled, you will gain roughly a degree of core temperature every twenty minutes, and the first degree will not feel like anything. It will feel like clarity. That is the trap the description warns about without naming: *being slowly simplified.* That is not poetry. That is the phenomenology of early hyperthermia and insensible dehydration in a place where your sweat evaporates before your skin reports it.

Everything else follows from these two facts.

## 3. The rules, fixed in advance, in writing

Write them on a card. Tether the card to you. Read it at the lip and again at T+30 minutes, out loud, because down there you will quietly lose the thread of why you came.

1. **Nothing touches the walls.** Not hands, not boots, not knees, not rope, not a shoulder bracing instinctively. Thirty thousand years of manganese and iron laid down a few molecules at a time, over sandstone soft enough to score with a thumbnail. Your palm does not polish it. Your palm greases it, and grease is food, and food is the beginning of the end of a finished surface.
2. **Nothing touches the floor.** There are no footprints. Do not make the first ones. They will outlast every building on your world of origin.
3. **Nothing is dropped.** Run this like an operating theatre: count in, count out, every item logged, every item lanyarded, no open pockets, no loose lens caps, no rings. A dropped carabiner is a permanent foreign object and a crater in a mud crust that has not moved since the last flood.
4. **Nothing is taken.** Not a flake, not a pinch of ochre, not the egg-shaped grinder cobble sitting in its pothole. *There is no force left in this place that will move it.* You would be the force. Decide now, upright, in daylight, that you will not — because at the bottom, in the warm dark, simplified, with your hand already out, you will want to, and the wanting will arrive dressed as reason.
5. **Nothing is added.** No chalk, no tape, no flagging, no cairn, no marker, no offering, no ash, no urine. Especially no urine: a litre of nitrogen and salt is an ecological catastrophe in a system that has never had either. Carry a bottle. Carry everything out.

The people who made those flakes set the standard, and they set it high: no hearth, no bone, no cord, no sherd, no grave. They came down, made something small, and went up, and carried out what they carried in. They belonged there. You don't. Your job is to leave less than they did.

## 4. Finding it, and not falling into it

The pan is flat and pale to the horizon in every direction and the crack is a stride wide with no spoil heap, no rim, no berm — water cut it flush. At any distance, at any shallow angle, you cannot see into it, so it does not read as a hole; it reads as nothing at all, until it reads as forty metres.

**People die on the approach here, not at the bottom.** Plan for that.

- Come in on a single bearing decided in advance from orbital or high-altitude survey. Do not walk a search grid. A grid puts a hundred trails on a surface that keeps trails.
- Park kilometres out and leave a six-metre mast with a flag at the vehicle. Heat mirage eats ground-level landmarks; a mast pokes above it. It is the only thing you will have to steer by on the return, when you are cooked and stupid.
- Rope up for the last hundred metres. Probe ahead with a pole, or crawl. The person in front is on belay from behind, always.
- Try arriving in the cold hour before dawn. The rock stays at 34°C while the pan radiates down; there may be a shimmer column standing over the mouth, the one moment the slot is visible from a distance. Verify from afar, then walk to it. (If the note truly never varies, the outflow never reverses, and that shimmer should be there. If it isn't, you have learned something. Do not assume you know this place from a description.)
- Keep five metres from the shrubs. Their roots run down the joints; they are plumbing the same deep damp the lichen uses. They do not seed. They sucker. A snapped branch is an amputation from a clone that has no other way to make a new individual, and there are perhaps three genets here in total, aged eleven thousand years.

Check the sky before you commit. Twenty millimetres a year, most of it in one afternoon, means sheet flow on a hard pan and lightning on a surface where your mast is the tallest object for thirty kilometres.

## 5. Timing: the eleven minutes are a computation, not a date

Here is something the geometry tells you that the description leaves implicit. For direct sun to reach the floor of a trench forty metres deep and a metre and a half wide, the sun must come within about a degree of the zenith — arctangent of 0.75 over 40 is 1.07°. A sun that gets within a degree of overhead at solstice noon means **this place sits essentially on a tropic**, within a fraction of a degree. Eleven minutes of blade width is exactly what that latitude and that aperture buy.

Consequences for your planning:

- **Determine which tropic.** Midsummer here may be June or December. Do not carry your home world's calendar assumptions into this.
- **Compute local apparent solar noon, to the minute,** from this world's ephemeris, this longitude, and the equation of time. Clock noon can be wrong by half an hour. If you arrive at "noon" by your watch you may miss the entire event and stand in the dark for ninety minutes wondering what you came for.
- **Verify with a stick.** On a tropic at solstice, a plumb vertical rod on the pan loses its shadow entirely at apparent noon. Set the rod, watch the shadow shrink to nothing and reappear on the other side. That instant is your zero. It is also, quietly, the best thing you will see all day, and it costs nothing.
- **The alignment has an expiry date.** Obliquity drifts; the tropic migrates roughly fifteen metres a year, some hundred and fifty kilometres since the mud curled. The blade is a coincidence with a start and an end, not a fixture.
- **Therefore the green may be young.** A millimetre a year, a hand's width in total: that is a century or two, not eleven thousand years. Do not walk in assuming the lichen is an ancient thing that has survived everything. It may be the newest object here and by far the most easily lost. Behave accordingly.

## 6. The rig: you go down like something being posted

You are not going to climb this. Chimneying, stemming, bridging — every instinct a canyon traveler brings from wet worlds is a vandalism technique here. And a free rappel in a metre-and-a-half trench means swinging, and swinging means touching.

**Survey first, uncrewed.** Lower a rotating lidar package on a plumb line and build a millimetric model of the interior. Find the reach with the largest inscribed vertical cylinder — the place where a hanging human, arms folded, has the most clearance in every direction. That is your descent line, and no other.

**Cantilever everything.** A davit arm on a wide base plate, set back on the pan, reaching out over the void so the rope's plumb line intersects nothing — not the lip, not the chert band, not a wall. The chert conglomerate is the only thing holding this trench's geometry; do not load it, do not groove it, do not run a rope over it.

**Be lowered, never step off.** A stepping-off human injects horizontal velocity and pendulums. Use a top-operated lowering system; you never handle the rope; you keep your hands folded on your chest; you descend at a walking pace or slower. Ascent is a haul, pre-rigged, never your own muscles — climbing out in a sealed suit is four hundred watts you have no way to shed.

**Mind the piston.** You are a body moving in a tube. The air ahead of you has to get past you, and at the blind end it has nowhere else to go. Descend slowly. Do not go to the narrow end at all. The flakes lie in the attitudes they fell in, and a puff of displaced air is enough to change the sentence.

**Mind the wick.** A rope hanging into a slot is a capillary channel. So is an air hose. The half-degree outward tilt is the only thing keeping the sky out of this place, and your rig is a hydrological intervention that could defeat it. Never leave the rig standing unattended. Strike it if there is any convective build within a hundred kilometres. Do not be the person who reintroduced water.

**Choose your one scar.** You cannot be zero. So be *deliberately, minimally, repeatably* non-zero: one pad footprint, on the pan, at the choked end near the fallen slab where nature has already broken the geometry, well clear of the two shrubs that lean in there. Everything you do, you do from that spot. Record its coordinates to the centimetre in your log. If you ever come back — and prefer not to — you use the same pad marks, the same plumb line, the same bearing in. The second visit should be indistinguishable from the first in what it left.

## 7. The suit: containment, run backwards

Standard protective clothing protects you from the place. Here it protects the place from you, which means the pressure gradient reverses.

- **Sealed, smooth, continuous-filament, non-shedding.** No cotton, no wool, no fleece, no leather, no natural-fibre rope, no wood, no cork, no cardboard, no food.
- **Slight negative pressure inside,** with HEPA-filtered exhaust pumped up a hose to the surface, twenty metres downwind of the mouth. Any leak leaks inward. Inward leaking air is hot and dry and harmless to you; outward leaking air is an extinction event for a lichen.
- **Cooling inside the suit, never outside.** Two hundred watts for ninety minutes is about a megajoule. Salt-hydrate or paraffin phase-change packs at 28°C run about 200 kJ/kg, so call it six kilos of pack plus margin — eight to ten kilos, distributed so you can hang in it. Do not bring ice or anything cold on your exterior: a cold surface in even this air can make dew, and dew is a thing that has never happened here.
- **Bailout.** Blower failure in a sealed suit gives you about a minute before CO₂ climbs. Carry a small cylinder and a battery backup blower, both. Any air alarm means immediate haul, no discussion, no finishing the exposure.
- **Gas monitoring at floor level,** especially near the potholes. They are the low points; anything heavier than air would sit in them. Probably nothing does. Probably is not a plan.
- **Engineer silence.** No Velcro — Velcro is a scream. No crinkling film, no clinking hardware, no beeping watch, no zips. Soft closures, taped-down buckles, everything muffled. Put the blower on the pan, far off, silenced; the trench eats sound and will help you. Then build a **silent mode**: a valve that pauses flow for sixty seconds while you hold your breath on the loop. You will need it, because the note is only audible when you stop breathing, and the pump is the loudest thing that has ever been in this hole.
- **Bathe, don't perfume.** No soap residue, no lotion, no sunscreen, no lip balm, no repellent, no hair product, no scent of any kind. Sun protection on the pan is fabric and shade only. Volatiles adsorb onto varnish and stay there.

If you want to smell the place — and you should want to — smell it from the lip, on your pad, exhaling away. Do not build a sniff-port into the suit and inhale the interior. That is the one pleasure that cannot be bought cleanly.

## 8. Your body, and the rule that keeps you alive

Heat-acclimate for two weeks. Train hanging in a seat harness with a board for two hours at a stretch; suspension intolerance in a warm sealed suit is a real failure mode and it will arrive at minute fifty. Get screened for claustrophobia honestly, then rehearse in a full-scale mock-up built from your lidar model — sealed, cooled, pump running, lights off, ninety minutes, arms folded, no touching. Rehearse the panic case specifically: hyperventilation in a sealed suit spikes CO₂ and heat together, and the flinch is to brace against a wall. Train the flinch out until it is gone, because down there the flinch costs thirty thousand years.

Never alone. One partner at the top whose entire job is the clock, the haul, and the rules, with standing authority to bring you up without asking. They do not come down. A rescuer descending doubles the contamination and takes twenty minutes you may not have.

And face this squarely, because it is the true reason for every rule above: **if you die down there, your body becomes the largest deposit of water, nitrogen and organic carbon this trench has received since the Pleistocene.** It would end the lichen, restart microbial activity on a finished varnish, and rewrite the floor. Your death would be an act of vandalism lasting geological time. Plan accordingly. Set hard turn-back thresholds — core temperature, cooling reserve, air, clock — and treat them as physics, not as preferences.

## 9. Light, eyes, and the eleven minutes

The walls drink nine-tenths of what little comes down. You will read the space with your hands, except you may not use your hands, so you will read it with your eyes after they have finished adapting — which takes thirty to forty minutes. Budget for it. Arrive at the floor at T−60 and do nothing.

**Do not look up.** The sky is a white thread and it will hurt, and it will cost you another half hour of dark adaptation, which you cannot afford before the blade. If you must look up, do it after.

Consider an eye patch over one eye from the moment you leave the lip. When the blade comes, take the patch off with the dark-adapted eye and keep the other for the sunlit stripe. It is an old trick and it works, and here it buys you the ability to see the black and the green in the same minute.

**No flash. No light painting.** A floodlit photograph of this slot is a picture of a place that has never existed. If you must add photons for a record, label the image as an artefact of your intervention, loudly, in the caption, forever.

Shoot raw, fixed white balance, a handheld grey card (held, never set down), auto-bracket. The scene spans twelve-plus stops: a daylight-white sky thread over a floor at a couple of lux. You are hanging on a rope and breathing, so long exposures will smear; bring a fast lens and accept noise. Noise is honest.

**Position yourself out of the blade's path.** Cross-reference your plumb line against the survey: know exactly where the bar will fall and where it will climb two metres up the east wall, and hang somewhere your shadow cannot reach the stripe. To watch the only event, you must be somewhere you do not prevent it. That constraint is the whole visit in miniature.

Then: for the first three minutes, do not photograph anything. Watch the olive come up out of the black. It is the only saturated colour for a hundred kilometres and it lasts eleven minutes and it will not happen again for you.

## 10. Sound

The note is one pitch, quieter than a finger on a glass rim, produced by air climbing out across the mouth. It may be lower than you expect — a forty-metre trench closed at one end is a strange instrument — so bring a microphone that reaches down to a few hertz and don't trust your ears to have found the fundamental.

The walls are too close to give an echo. Your footfall would arrive thickened, not repeated, except you have no footfall, because you never touch the floor. Your voice goes nowhere and returns nowhere. It is used up. This means you cannot shout for help, and it means your partner cannot hear you, and it means you need a hardline comms wire alongside the rope, not a radio — a metre-and-a-half rock trench with a thread of sky is not a place where a handheld reaches anyone.

At some point, run silent mode. Stop the pump, hold your breath, and listen. You will hear the note, and under it your own blood, and the click of your throat, and these will be enormous. Ten seconds is enough to be frightened by. Sixty is enough to understand the place. Do it once, at the floor, rested, heart rate settled, after you have been hanging still for twenty minutes.

## 11. What you will not do, and why it will be hard

You will not put your hand into the four polished holds. That is the thing you actually came for — to put your hand where that hand went, four generations of somebody, always the same four holds — and it is the one thing you must not do. The polish is softness repeated ten thousand times. You cannot add to it. You can only smear it with skin oil and leave bacteria on a surface that has finished growing.

You will not lift the thumbnail scraper, or turn a flake to see its bulb of percussion, or touch the ochre. The ochre is still red and still powder and it has been red and powder for longer than agriculture.

You will not scrape the wall to see the red-brown under the crow-black. You will get powder and a smell like slate remembered, and a scar the width of your fingernail that will still be there when the region is uplifted.

You will not step over to the pothole and pick up the egg. Say this one out loud on the pan before you go down. Name it as a temptation, in advance, in front of your partner, so that it cannot arrive later disguised as curiosity.

## 12. A clock for the day

- **T−3h:** Weather check, sky check to the north. Rig on the one scar. Count in.
- **T−90m:** Suit up, buddy check, comms check, cooling reserve verified, bailout verified. Read the card aloud.
- **T−60m:** Lowered, not stepping. Arms folded. Slow.
- **T−55 to T−15:** Hang. Do nothing. Dark-adapt. Let your heart rate come down. Cool.
- **T−10:** Silent mode, sixty seconds. Then reposition — by rope, by millimetres, from the top — out of the blade's path.
- **T−0 to T+11:** The blade. Watch first. Photograph second. Patch off.
- **T+12 to T+25:** One low, honest look at the floor with a small warm light, sixty seconds total, then off. Look up once, at the thread, and let it hurt.
- **T+30:** Read the card aloud. Check the clock against the plan; the place will have quietly deleted your intentions.
- **T+35:** Haul. Do not linger to "see it properly." The other twenty-three hours and forty-nine minutes are not for you.

## 13. Coming back up

The pan will be blinding and empty and enormous and you will want to talk. Let yourself.

At the lip, before you undress, shake out your suit *over the crack*, so that whatever dust of the place you are carrying goes back into it. You will leave with some of it on you regardless; that is theft, however small, and this is the partial restitution available.

Strike the rig. Count out. Reconcile the count against the count in, item by item, and if a single thing is missing, you go back down and get it, and that is not negotiable.

Then write it down, there, on the pan, in the light, before you drive away — because your voice was used up down there and the memory will simplify too. And in the log, alongside whatever you saw, record precisely what you touched, what you left, and the centimetric coordinates of your one pad mark, so that anyone who comes after can step where you stepped instead of making a second scar.

Think hard before you publish the location. A place whose defining property is that it is not visited stops having that property the moment you tell people where it is. That may be the largest thing you take from it, and the only one you cannot carry out.

---

## 14. Last

Go knowing what the visit will feel like, so it doesn't ambush you. Depth without coolth — every cave you have ever entered got colder as you went down, and this one does not, and your body will keep expecting a coolness that never arrives. Warm stone at blood heat on every side, a metre away, in the dark. Air that does not move. No echo. One note. The gradual, pleasant, dangerous sense of having fewer things to think about. The conviction, arriving from nowhere, that someone else is present — which is only your nervous system filling a room that has no mouths in it, and which will feel, unmistakably, like the people who used the four holds.

You will be, for ninety minutes, the only water, the only heat source that isn't the basin, the only noise, the only mouth, the only thing that has ever fallen there.

The place is a completed sentence. Water wrote it, then was taken out, and what's left is the grammar holding its shape in the warm dark with nothing left to do. Eleven minutes a day it is green; otherwise it is simply itself, and entirely uninterested in whether you find it.

Prepare to be a reader who leaves no marginalia. If you can't manage that — and most of us can't, quite — then lie on your stomach at the lip on your one chosen pad, put your face over the edge, breathe out away from the crack, and take the smell and the stillness and the forty metres of nothing, and go home.

That is a complete visit. The slot will not know the difference, which is the highest form of success available to you here.
