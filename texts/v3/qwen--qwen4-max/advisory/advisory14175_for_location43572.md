# Preparing for the Felt

## 1. Begin at your feet, because that is where it will begin with you

The first thing that will go wrong on the Felt is your feet, and it will go wrong faster than anywhere else you have ever walked.

The ground is blood-warm and permanently wet. That is not a landscape; that is an incubator set to 38°C and left running for ten million years. Every organism there is optimized to colonize a surface the instant the surface becomes available. Your skin — macerated, softened, split at the toe-webs after six hours of walking — is a surface becoming available.

So:

- **Boots you can dry.** Two pairs, rotated. Lightweight, high-drainage, no waterproof membrane; a membrane keeps the Felt out for an hour and then keeps it in forever. You want the water to leave.
- **A dry-out discipline that you treat as religious.** Every rest stop: boots off, socks off, feet wiped, feet aired, feet inspected. Every night: feet dry, feet powdered, feet in dedicated dry sleep-socks that never touch the ground, ever.
- **Antiseptic, antifungal, antibiotic — more than you think.** Assume any break in the skin will be occupied within minutes. Assume a scratch on day two is a spreading infection on day four. Irrigate, don't just cover.
- **Gaiters,** because the felt weeps down onto everything and you want the water running off your shin rather than into your boot.
- **Trench foot is your most likely evacuation.** Not falls, not animals, not altitude. Immersion foot, in a place with no dry ground to sit on.

Bring a small folding stool. It sounds absurd. It is the single most useful object you will carry: a way to be off the warm wet living floor while you eat, dress a wound, or simply stop being digestible for ten minutes.

## 2. The air will lie to you about how high you are

You are at four thousand meters. You will not feel like you are at four thousand meters, because it is warm and humid and dim and there is no wind and no cold to warn you. The Lid holds the heat down and the heat holds the Lid up, and the result is a greenhouse at the altitude of a high pass. Your body's usual cues — cold, dry air, harsh light, the bite of it — are all absent.

You will overexert on the first day because it feels like a warm greenhouse. Then you will lie down with a headache you attribute to the strange light.

- Acclimatize *before* arrival if your route allows it. If it doesn't, plan three days of near-inactivity.
- Carry a pulse oximeter and use it on a schedule, not on a feeling. Feelings are unreliable here.
- Carry altitude medication and know which one is for which failure. Know the difference between a headache and ataxia. Test your own gait daily: heel-to-toe, eyes closed, on a knuckle where the ground is honest.
- **Understand the central problem: there is no descent.** Standard altitude medicine says *go down*. On the Felt there is no down. There is the plateau, and there is the Weep, and the Weep is not a treatment. Your evacuation plan is your only definitive therapy. Arrange it in advance, know exactly where it can reach you (see §8), and do not go anywhere you cannot be reached from.

Drink on a schedule too. The humidity hides your sweat loss; the altitude increases it. You will not feel thirsty in a place where every surface is wet.

## 3. Bring your own shadows

There is no directional light on the Felt. This is beautiful and it is a serious hazard, because **shadows are how human vision detects holes.** Under the Lid, a sump-opening in the mass is not a dark circle; it is a patch of ground the same color as everything else, lit evenly from all sides, invisible until your leg is in it.

You must manufacture the missing information:

- **A hard, low, raking light.** Not a floodhead lamp — a narrow beam you can hold at knee height and sweep across the ground ahead of you. You are not lighting the way; you are *casting shadows*, which is the only way to see relief here. Two lights are better than one: a headlamp for hands, a hand-held raking beam for ground.
- **A probe.** A collapsible pole, longer than your stride, used constantly, like a blind traveler's cane. You will feel silly for an hour and then you will never walk without it.
- **A rope and a partner in sump districts.** Chambers the size of houses collapse without warning; the boom takes eleven minutes to reach you, which means the news of a collapse arrives long after the collapse. Travel roped where the mass sounds hollow underfoot.
- Contrast-enhancing lenses help more than sunglasses. Amber or rose filters that push the honey-and-jade palette back toward differentiation. Below the Lid, sunglasses are useless; above it, on a knuckle, you need glacier-grade protection and full skin cover, because at that altitude the sun is, as promised, a small hard thing you have not properly seen in your life.

Also: gloves. The bracts are silica-ribbed. Waxed, translucent, and edged with glass.

## 4. Water: take the weep, not the cup

Every surface weeps and every surface drinks. There is a hierarchy of cleanliness and you should learn it immediately.

**Best:** condensation caught directly off the aerial felt, in threads, before it reaches anything. Spread a clean collection cloth, run the threads into a bottle. This is the closest thing to distilled water on the continent. Treat it anyway.

**Acceptable:** shallow, young, actively-flushed cups.

**Never:** the deep sumps and the old tanks. That water is black, anoxic, and in some cups older than the animal drinking from it. It has been a closed ecosystem since before you were born. It is low in oxygen, high in tannin, and populated by lineages that have never met a mammal. Boiling will kill the organisms; it will not remove the dissolved organic load, and the tannin will ruin your filter, stain everything you own, bind your dietary iron over a long stay, and taste like a stewed teapot left in a cellar. Aerate it if you want to enjoy it. It will still taste flat.

Carry three days of your own water at minimum, because the first day you will not want to drink anything here, and dehydration at altitude is how people get stupid.

**Do not reach into a tank.** The cup-crabs are coin-sized and territorial and their entire universe is that rosette. You would be putting your hand into a country.

## 5. Nothing here is food, and you are the first empty niche in ten million years

Understand what *solved* means. Every gap is filled. Every animal is an organ. There is no surplus, no spare seed, no fruit that isn't somebody's function. Foraging on the Felt is not harvesting; it is amputation. Plan to carry **every calorie**, at altitude-adjusted quantities, for the whole trip plus a margin. There is no resupply. There is no bush with berries. There is a continent that has already eaten everything available and is now, very politely, waiting for you to become available.

And you will be tempting, because you are novel:

- **Your lungs.** Warm, wet, dark, nutrient-rich, and utterly unoccupied by anything adapted to this world. A fungal spore from a sump has spent ten million years perfecting the colonization of decaying tissue in a warm humid chamber. Do not put your face to a sump-hole. Do not sleep near one. Wear a real respirator in any depression where the air smells like a rotting bread oven, and understand that sump air is also likely oxygen-poor and heavy with accumulated gas — the same hole that will infect you will asphyxiate you first if you lean in far enough.
- **Your gut, your skin, your waste.** Everything you shed is a nutrient release into a system where nothing is free. Expect a small ugly bloom of opportunists around any place you've been careless — and understand that you will have made the first genuine opening in the local order, and something will fit itself into it, and it will not be something that was there before.

Therefore: **sealed waste containers, packed out. All of it.** Wash water carried away from camp and strained. No spitting. No burying — there is no dirt to bury in, only living tissue that will accept your offering immediately and remember it. Hair, nail clippings, food crumbs, the wrapper you meant to pick up: all of it goes home with you.

**Tether every object you carry.** A dropped bottle, a lost lid, a slipped pack into a plumbing tube is not litter. It is a thrombus. A clogged tube yellows a district over a decade. You can give a continent a stroke by being clumsy.

**Absolutely no fire.** Not for warmth, not for cooking, not for morale. Everything is wet, so you'd think it safe; peat is the exception to that intuition. A smolder in the mass does not go out. It goes *sideways*, underground, for years, and the Felt heals a cut in a season and cannot heal a burn in four hundred. Cook on a canister stove on a quartz knuckle, on bare stone, with a mat under it, and never on the felt.

## 6. Sleep above the mass

Three reasons, and all of them are good:

1. **Thermal.** The ground is a degree or two above your skin temperature. Lying on it, you cannot dump heat. You will wake soaked, hyperthermic, and unrested, in a place where you're already hypoxic and dehydrated.
2. **Biological.** A still warm body on a wet living surface, in the dark, for eight hours, is exactly the sort of thing the Felt takes an impression of. And in the meantime it will begin the ordinary work of colonization on your clothing, your hair, the seam of your bag.
3. **Structural.** You do not know what is under you. Chambers collapse.

Sleep in a hammock if you can find two honest anchors, which is harder than it sounds — nothing here is rigid except quartz and the dead timber of the huts. Otherwise a wide-load cot with distributed feet, or a self-supporting platform, or best of all: **stone.** Camp on the knuckles. They are chapel-sized, they are dry, they are inert, they will not remember you, and they are the only places where you can see the sky and know what time it is.

Bring an eye mask. The bracts fluoresce; the darkest hour is jade, not black. Your circadian system will not accept jade as night. Also bring an actual clock, and keep it, and use it, and set an artificial dawn — a bright white light at a fixed hour — because time here is a color and colors do not have numbers.

## 7. The huts: use them, and do not change them

You will find them every few days on the right line: low, oval, dry stone and dead timber, a doorway, no roof. Bring a tarp. They are the best shelter on the continent and I believe they are there partly *for you* — whether as courtesy or as containment, I don't know, and neither do the people at the rim.

Sleeping in an existing hut adds nothing new. You reinforce an impression already ten thousand years in propagation. That is the closest thing to a harmless night's sleep on the Felt.

**Do not modify one.** Do not widen the doorway. Do not add a wall, straighten an oval, prop a stone, scratch a mark. Do not build a cairn to mark your route. Do not blaze a bract. In a solved world every new form is permanent and continent-scale; your small helpful improvement will be quoted, imperfectly, outward, at a kilometer a year, for longer than your species has had writing. The discipline here is not *leave no trace* in the sentimental sense. It is **introduce no forms.**

Navigate instead by:

- **The bearing.** Every doorway faces the same way, and it is the bearing of nothing — which makes it the only universal reference direction on a shadowless, starless, featureless plain. It is your north. Learn it, trust it, and check it against your gyro or your compass whenever you find a hut.
- **Echo fidelity as a rangefinder.** The far echoes get the doorway a little wrong and the oval becomes a suggestion. Travelers swear you can estimate your distance from the origin by how degraded the ovals are. It is folklore and it is probably useful and it is certainly not calibrated. Use it as a sanity check, not a fix.
- **The pulse as a clock.** One beat every forty minutes: thirty-six beats to a day. Learn to feel it, tally it, and you have a timepiece the whole continent shares. Your barometer will oscillate with it too; an altimeter here is a stethoscope.
- **Knuckle-to-knuckle dead reckoning.** Pace count is unreliable on ground that gives and returns; wheel odometry is worse. Range to quartz. Chain your route from knuckle to knuckle and never let yourself be more than a known distance from stone.

## 8. Sound, and what it does to your mind

Shout and the sound goes two meters and lies down. This means:

- **Whistles are useless.** Carry a drum, or a low reed, or something that makes noise below about 200 Hz. The low end travels. Your partner cannot hear you scream but can hear you thump.
- **Tether your party.** In fog, under a shadowless canopy, you will lose a companion who is four meters away and you will not know it for an hour.
- **Never approach a browser herd from the front.** Six limbs, feet the width of a door, forty animals taking a month to cross a valley. They cannot stop for you and they will not hear you. Learn to read them the way the rim people do: a broad soft irregularity in the drip pattern, like a room where someone has stopped speaking. When the water-noise goes wrong, get out of the lane and stay out. Do not sleep in a browser lane. Do not touch the moss on their coats; it is not decoration, it is a farm, and you would be robbing it.

Then there is the interior weather. Dim, directionless, warm, silent-but-textured, with a pulse you feel in your weight and booms that arrive eleven minutes late from a hundred kilometers away, and the Weep's hiss registering as *silence with a texture*. Infrasound produces dread in human bodies. Sensory monotony produces invention. Expect, around the second week: the certainty that someone is standing behind you; voices in the drip; your own face looking flat and unreal in a mirror, because a face lit from everywhere looks like a drawing of a face.

Prepare for this as you would prepare for altitude:

- A companion, and a rule about talking every hour even when there's nothing to say.
- Recorded human voices. Music. Something with high frequencies in it, on headphones, because outdoors those frequencies die in two meters and you will start to feel that the world is refusing to let you speak.
- Work to do. A log. Measurements. Counts. The mind holds together on tasks.
- And a small hard light you can shine sideways across your friend's face at dinner, to give them back their nose and their eye-sockets and their expression. It is the kindest thing you can do for another person on the Felt and it takes four seconds.

## 9. The rim people, the bloom, and the Weep

There are a few people at the edge, in stone huts with their doorways facing the bearing. Assume they have a protocol and that it is stricter than yours. Arrive slowly and visibly — you cannot announce yourself with a shout, and everyone here startles. Drum at distance. Wait to be approached.

They measure age in blooms: *I am two flowers old.* Do not ask their age in years; it is either rude or meaningless. Ask which bloom they were born under, and where — a birthday here is a place and a band, not a date. Be ready to answer honestly about yourself, and be ready for what your answer means: you have no bloom. Whatever that makes you — unborn, ageless, a thing without an age — accept the status you're given and don't argue about it.

Bring gifts, but not objects. Every object is a novel form and a novel nutrient. **Pay in observations:** the position of the wet front, the leading edge of the blush, the bearing and distance of a cold patch, the count and direction of booms, the location and fidelity of an echo-hut nobody has logged. If they are a network of anything, they are a network of watchers, and your walk is data. Offer your log before you offer your food.

If you want to see the flowering, plan it years out or plan to intercept it: the band crosses in about fourteen months and takes a decade to pass any given place, moving at a slow walk. You can walk to it. You can walk *with* it, for weeks, which is the thing I would most want to do here. Prepare for the smell — thick, sweetish, slightly like blood — and for a ten-year increase in small soft flying things, which means a bug net, antihistamines, and an epinephrine autoinjector if you have any history of allergy. A continent-wide synchronized bloom is a continent-wide allergen event and you have no idea what your immune system will make of it.

**Cold patches:** after a day you will start finding them frightening, and you should. Warmth is life here; the whole system runs one degree above its own death. Cold ground means the mat is not living — a clog, a vent, a die-off, the leading edge of a district failing. Probe it, do not camp on it, log the bearing, tell the rim.

**The Weep:** stay ten kilometers back. The rim retreats a hand's width per century, which means it is undercut, which means the edge is not where the ground ends but somewhere out in the fog beyond it. There is no view. There is no down that comes back. Whatever world is below, nothing that lives on the Felt has ever returned from it, and you are softer than anything that lives on the Felt.

## 10. The real preparation

Everything above is logistics. Here is the part you have to settle before you go, in a chair, at home, with a cup of tea, because you will not be able to settle it there.

**If you sleep in one place for a season, the Felt will keep you.** Not your body — your shape. A hollow that stays after you move on, and then, rising out in the fog at a kilometer a year, a low oval of living wood with a gap in one side, and the gap faces the way you used to face when you slept.

Which means:

- It expands. A ring of you, going out in every direction, thinning as it goes, until the far field is nothing but a raised welt with a gap. In a century, a ring a hundred kilometers in radius. In ten thousand years, a feature of a continent.
- **You can visit it.** This is what nobody prepares for. Ten years after your season, your echo is ten kilometers away — an afternoon's walk. You can go and stand in it. It will already have the doorway slightly wrong.
- **Something will live in it.** Nothing here stays empty. An oval of living wood with a floor of felt and a gap facing a bearing is a niche, and the Felt fills niches. Your remembered posture will be occupied — by crabs, by eels, by something that becomes a new organ shaped exactly like the space your body left. You may found a lineage. You may become a function.

So decide, in the chair, with the tea:

**Whether you consent.** Some travelers do, and call it the only immortality that has ever been offered honestly: not your soul, not your name, just the shape of your resting body, held and repeated and inhabited, by a mindless continent, forever. Some travelers find it the most violating thing they have ever heard. Both reactions are correct and you should know which one is yours before you're lying in the jade dark deciding it at three in the morning.

**How to avoid it, if you want to avoid it.** Keep moving. Never sleep twice in the same hollow. Camp on quartz. Use the old huts. Stone does not remember and the huts are already remembering something else.

**What to face, if you don't avoid it.** If you're going to be quoted for a hundred thousand years, choose the sentence. Don't spend your season curled around a pain, or clutching a weapon, or turned away from the door. Whatever you do with your body in the dark will be the shape of rooms that animals are born into. Sleep the way you would want a hundred millennia of small lives to be shaped.

**And what happens if you die.** You will be eaten perfectly, and completely, and then you will propagate. Write it down before you go. Tell your companions: carry me to stone, or take me off-world, or leave me and let me become a district. Any of the three is defensible. None of them should be decided by someone else, in fog, while grieving.

---

**Short list:** two pairs of draining boots and dry sleep-socks; more antiseptic than you think; a folding stool; a hammock or a wide cot; an eye mask and a real clock and a bright dawn lamp; a narrow raking beam and a probe and a rope; gloves; a respirator; a drum, not a whistle; a pulse oximeter and altitude drugs and an evacuation plan that can reach a knuckle; every calorie you will need; sealed waste containers and tethers on all your gear; a collection cloth for the weep-threads; glacier glasses for the knuckles and contrast lenses for below; a bug net; a tarp for the huts; a logbook and a habit of filling it; a recorded human voice; and no fire, no marks, no souvenirs, no new forms.

Go. Walk until the hour is a color and the day is thirty-six beats long. Stand on a knuckle at what your clock insists is noon and look at the Lid going away from you until the world's roundness takes it, and let the small hard sun frighten you.

Then come down into the green late light, and find a hut, and lie in it without changing anything, and face whatever you have decided to face.
