# Preparing for the Lid

## The single fact everything else follows from

You are the only thing on that world that can still change.

Everything else has finished. The mineral has no more give, the grains have no more edge to lose, the buildings have no more surface to cloud. You arrive warm, wet, breathing, shedding, digesting, healing, aging — a soft undecided object in a place that closed its accounts before your species had a spine. You are not a visitor to a wilderness. You are a new event in a system that has not had one in an age, and the system has exactly one response to new events: it comes to look, and then it takes them apart and files them.

Prepare, therefore, in two directions at once. Protect yourself from the world, which is thin and cold and indifferent. And protect the world from *you*, which is the harder half, and the half nobody trains for.

---

## Before you go: the body

**The air.** "Thin enough to be a rumour" means, in practical terms, that you will be operating at the physiological equivalent of a very high camp, permanently, from the moment you arrive, with no descent to recover at. Stage your acclimatization over weeks, not days. Hypoxic sleeping, graded ascents in adjacent worlds, a full blood panel before departure. If your cardiopulmonary numbers are marginal, this is not your trip; there is no evacuation from a place where the only fixed addresses are three horizons apart.

Plan on supplemental oxygen, but understand the catch: **oxygen is new material.** A free-flowing mask vents a chemically novel gas into a sealed world with every breath. Use a closed loop — scrubbed, condensed, reclaimed, nothing vented. Your exhaled water vapour is the only humidity this planet has seen since its oceans were filed. Treat it as a hazardous substance, because that is exactly what it is.

**The cold.** It is not freezing. That is the trap. Nothing here freezes, so nothing in your instincts will tell you that you are being killed. The air has almost no push; it will not bite or sting or make you turn your face away. What it does is stand aside while your heat leaves you in a straight line, upward, into an ordinary blue sky with nothing in it to send anything back.

So insulate *upward*, specifically. A wide hood or brim with a low-emissivity outer surface — a sky-lid — matters more than an extra layer on your chest. Cover the top of your head, your shoulders, the tops of your thighs. When you sleep, you want a reflective canopy above you as much as insulation beneath, and beneath you want serious separation from a plate that will conduct your warmth away into a hollow world with infinite patience.

**Water.** Dry, thin, cold air takes insensible loss at a rate that will astonish you. You will not feel yourself sweating; it will be gone before it beads. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Carry more than the distance suggests, because there is no source, none, anywhere, and the phrase "I'll find water" has no referent here.

**The light.** Ordinary sun, ordinary afternoon — and an atmosphere too thin to do the filtering you have relied on your whole life. Broad-spectrum eye protection, full skin coverage, and a specific warning about the caustics: the light pools and knots as it comes through the sift, and light behaving like a fluid can *concentrate* like one. Do not stand staring into a bright knot because it is beautiful. It is beautiful. It is also a lens the size of a weather system.

## Before you go: the mind

Three screenings, and be honest in all of them.

**Pattern-completion.** This world will show you a finite deck being shuffled forever, and it will show you a grain you have seen before, and it will be true — everything here has been everywhere. Some travelers go quiet on the third day and are found on the ninth kneeling in a still-disc, sorting. Not distressed. *Working.* If you are a person who cannot leave a sequence unfinished, do not go.

**Grief.** The place is shaped like a loss. Something was taken out of it and it is looking for that thing with total, silent, patient diligence, and it will not stop, and it does not know how long it has been. If you have recently lost something, you will not be able to keep your search separate from its search. Wait a year. Wait longer.

**Stillness.** This is the skill, and it is the one nobody has. Train it deliberately: six hours in one place, in the cold, without improving anything. No tidying. No arranging. No stacking. The urge to make a cairn, to scratch a direction arrow, to write your name — that urge is the single most dangerous impulse you will bring with you, and it will arrive dressed as helpfulness.

Anechoic-chamber sessions help. So does time on a frozen lake, and time at height with a visible horizon fold, because the sky continuing *underneath* the edge of the world does something to the inner ear that you want to have felt once before it counts.

## The kit, and why each item is odd

- **A sealed, smooth, continuous-filament outer layer.** No fleece, no wool, no down, no hook-and-loop, nothing that sheds. Every fibre you drop is a polymer that does not exist in the deck. Taped seams. Ochre-grey, matched to the traffic. *Not* high-visibility. The sorting correlates faintly with colour; be a colour it already has.
- **Closed-loop breathing, with an eating protocol.** You do not take a dust mask here. Understand what the dust *is*: grains worked past the end of being workable, rounded, hardened, finished. They do not abrade. They do not dissolve. They do not weather in a lung, because weathering is a thing that requires something left to give. A grain you inhale on day one is exactly as sharp on the day you die of it. Absolute respiratory isolation, and food eaten inside containment: pastes, gels, bars that do not crumble. No crackers. No nuts. Nothing that makes crumbs. Crumbs are new molecules.
- **Soft, broad, unlugged soles.** Lugs write. A tread pattern is a sentence a kilometre long and the world will read it. You want to leave the least legible mark a body can leave.
- **A contact microphone or geophone, and the habit of using it.** The plants tick through the ground, not the air; the lid rings in the teeth, not the ear. The only forecast available on this world is a mica-plant's opinion of the strain in its seam. Learn to read it: quickening ticks mean the plate is loading, which means a cell is inbound, which means you have perhaps an hour.
- **A mass ledger.** Weigh yourself and everything you carry, to the gram, on arrival and on departure. Reconcile. The world keeps a closed account; keep yours the same way and be able to prove you did not open it.
- **Inertial navigation and a seam-map.** Landmarks are not landmarks; a ridge at noon is a basin by evening. What is fixed: the seams, the shaft collars, the buildings, the plants rooted in the seams. Navigate by the living and the built and the joins, and by nothing else. If the sun genuinely stands still — and the phrase *that afternoon for longer than there have been afternoons* suggests it does — you have an absolute bearing reference and no night at all. Bring your own night: mask, schedule, discipline. Your body is the only clock on the surface, and it is a bad one.
- **Nothing aloft.** No drone, no kite, no balloon. There are thin things riding the shear layers that are barely animals, and you would be entering their niche and writing on the sky in letters four kilometres high.
- **A body bag, sealed, with a partner who knows how to use it.** We will come to this.

## Arrival: the first hour

Do not walk. Stand where you land and let the world finish noticing the arrival, because it has already noticed.

Look at the horizon fold. Feel the drumhead under you and the lightness in the chest that comes from a world that weighs less than it looks. Put a hand flat on the lid and feel the sun-side warmth and the coin-cold a metre away. Strike it once, softly, and feel the note in your teeth. Then stop striking it.

Find the pulse. The shafts breathe — cold air welling up in slow surges, drawn back down in slower ones, and the sift leaning with each breath. Time it. Write it down. Everything you do afterward should be timed against it, and you do not yet know its period, so do not pretend you do.

Then locate the nearest fixed thing and go to it, slowly, and sit down.

---

## Moving: how to trail less weather

The Rule is not negotiable and cannot be broken, only managed. **Walk, and you will be followed by weather.** A footprint is the first new information in an age; the cells lean toward it; by evening there is a slow silent column of ochre standing over where you slept.

Three techniques, in order of usefulness:

**Walk inside the noise.** The counterintuitive one. In calm air you are the only signal on the plain and you are legible from the far horizon. Inside the shoulder of a moving cell, where the deck is already being shuffled at continental scale, your novelty is drowned. Travel *with* the traffic, in the ochre, in the dry ticking, and rest when it clears. The storm is your cover. The beautiful still afternoon is where you are most exposed. This will feel wrong the entire time.

**Do not cross grains; follow them.** Move along the sorting lanes rather than across them. Where the sift has already arranged something, walk in the arrangement. Where a front has passed, walk in its wake and let it close behind you. Be a thing that happens *inside* the weather rather than a thing the weather happens to.

**Go light and go slow.** Long walkers are taller than a person and lighter than a coat. That is not an aesthetic; it is the correct answer to a flexing plate on a hollow world. Every kilogram you carry is load, load is strain, strain is signal. Halve your pack. Then halve it again. Never run.

And accept the consequence honestly: your wake will be visible from the far horizon, and it is how the walkers find you, and it is how buildings get uncovered. Some travelers conclude from this that they are *useful* — that a visitor is a legitimate stirring of the deck, a service to the search. That conclusion is available, it is flattering, and it is not supported by anything. Assume you are vandalism. Minimize.

## Rest: the still-discs, and manners

A disc of unmoved ground three or four metres across, in the lee of a mica-clump or a plinth. You will walk a day between them. Plan your water and warmth for two days between them, and never be between them at the wrong hour.

Inside one, the ticking drops away and the silence has a floor to it, and the grains under your boots are the ones that were there when the mountains were still being taken apart. It is the only old order on the surface of the world. Behave accordingly:

- **Change nothing in it.** Do not sweep, tidy, smooth, or stack. Do not brush a "clean" patch to sleep on. You are a guest in the last unshuffled room in a house the size of a planet.
- **Never touch a mica-plant.** It does not drink; it eats strain; it is the reason there is a floor to the silence and the reason there is life here at all. It is also anchored in a seam, and a seam is a live electrical joint.
- **Never straddle a seam.** One foot on each plate makes you a bridge across a piezoelectric contact that is being loaded by four kilometres of moving dust. Sit within a plate, not across the join.
- **Share.** Walkers come to the discs and stand over the old arrangement with their heads down for hours, and the eating is probably not the main business; they seem to be *checking*. They are hollow-boned and lighter than a coat and they have no fear in them at all, because nothing here has ever needed fear. You are the first candidate. Approach slowly, sit down early — sitting reduces your load and quiets your signal — and let them look at you. Do not perform. Do not offer. Do not reach out. When a walker lifts its head and attends to you across the sift, the correct response is to be uninteresting until it goes on.

The buildings: enter, sit on the bench even though it is the wrong height for you, and put your feet in the shallow depression if you want to. It was shaped at the factory to look like the wear of feet. It is a joke or a courtesy or a memorial and you will not find out which; take it as offered.

**Never relocate an artifact.** A cube half-swallowed in a dune has sat half-swallowed in ten thousand dunes and its position is the only clock it has. Digging it out, turning it, righting it, "freeing" it — that erases ten million years of the only record it keeps. Photograph in situ. Measure. Leave. And do not add strokes to a tally collar. Do not try to make the base resolve out loud, in company, repeatedly. Others have.

## The shafts

Do not descend.

If you have a reason strong enough to override that: tether, redundant air, a partner on the collar, gas testing at the mouth of every exhale (the air welling out of a lattice of filed mountains has no reason to be breathable and every reason to be old), and a hard turnaround time set against the breathing pulse rather than against your confidence.

What is down there is not a cave. It is the mass of the world's former mountains, sorted, ranked, cool and dry and perfectly preserved because decay requires something left to give. It is an archive. You are a source of moisture, microbes, heat, and skin cells, and there is no decontamination for a place where nothing has ever needed one.

Also, practically: a fall down a shaft is unsurvivable, unrecoverable, and would deposit the largest novelty in an epoch at the bottom of the world's memory. Which brings us to the thing nobody wants in a briefing.

---

## Do not die here

This is a real protocol, not a flourish.

A human body is water, carbon, warmth, and decay — four things this world does not have. Left on the lid, it would be a signal visible from the far horizon, pulling cells from a continent, drawing every walker within a thousand kilometres, and offering the machine an arrangement it has never been able to try. It might read you as the answer.

So: medical screening that errs toward refusal. Buddy system, always. Conservative turnaround. And a sealed body bag carried by someone who has been told, plainly, before departure, that using it is not optional and is not a desecration. It is the last courtesy a visitor can pay.

Extraction rendezvous at a plinth or a collar — a thing that cannot be moved. Those are the only addresses on this world.

---

## The deepest rule: do not answer

Sooner or later you will kneel in the old order and recognise a grain. A pale one with a flat facet and a chip that is not a chip. You saw it a hundred kilometres back, in another season. It is real. The deck is finite.

And then the thought arrives, and it arrives as kindness: *I could help.* I could arrange a few grains into something legible. A shape. A face. A word. Something that says **here, this is what you lost, you can stop now.**

Do not.

You do not know what was lost. You know only that the machine has one verb and cannot make or break, and that the arrangement it is looking for is made of *these exact pieces* — a set that does not include you or anything you carry. Any pattern you lay down is either dismissed, in which case you have written on the last clean floor in the world, or believed.

And if it is believed: the sorting stops. The storms stop. The plate stops flexing. The seams stop giving their small bright current. The mica dies. The still-discs go, and the walkers with them, and the last old order anywhere on the surface. You will have answered a question with your own face and starved a world.

Keep a photographic log of your grains with time and position, and cap the time you spend looking at the floor. Have your partner check you. The matching is a condition and it has an onset.

## What it will actually be like

Cold in a way that feels personal rather than severe. A well-lit afternoon that does not end. Your own breathing enormously loud, your boots ringing in your jaw, and a wall of ochre four kilometres high arriving at the speed of a running animal in near-silence — a dry ticking like a clock in another room, and your whole body braced for a roar that never comes, and the bracing itself the loudest thing on the plain.

Light crawling over your boots like the floor of a swimming pool, on a seabed that has not been wet since before there were fish. A day's walk to the next grey-green rosette. The relief of stepping into a still-disc is physical, immediate, and slightly shameful, like stepping out of a crowd into a chapel you have not earned.

Somewhere in the second or third day, the ordinary daylight stops being reassuring and becomes the most disturbing thing about the place: it has been this pleasant, this well-lit, this mild, for the entire duration of the search. Nothing here is dramatic. Nothing here is ruined. The buildings are sharp-arrised and ten million years old and the only thing age has done to them is move them.

And underneath, in the dark, the mountains stand in ranks, filed, waiting.

Go for a short time. One traverse, anchor to anchor, and out. Carry nothing away — not a grain, not a flake of mica, not a chip off a collar. A single grain removed makes the deck incomplete, and an incomplete deck can never be exhaustively searched, and the search is the only thing left that the world is doing.

Leave the account closed. Walk in the noise, sit in the old order, let the walkers look at you and go on, and be, for as long as you can manage it, the least interesting object on the roof of the world.
