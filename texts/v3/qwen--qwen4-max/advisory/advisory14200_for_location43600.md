# Preparing for The Throat

## The one fact that governs everything

The world you are visiting has no vacancy. Not "little room" — none. Every cubic hand's-breadth of the habitable sleeve is occupied, seven to eleven bodies deep, and has been for longer than there have been species to occupy it. There is no clearing, no shore, no idle niche, no place where a thing can stand without displacing something. You will not find a spot. There are no spots.

So this is not a visit *into* a place. It is a visit to a threshold, and every decision below follows from that. Plan to stand outside a lit room and look in. If your plan contains the words "go down," "get closer," "just a metre," or "touch," it is not a plan for The Throat; it is a plan for becoming architecture.

Make that decision now, in a warm room, in writing, with someone else's signature next to yours. You will not be able to make it at the rim. The heat comes up and lays itself along your face and chest like a hand, and the body wants to answer. Everyone kneels. Know that you will kneel, and decide in advance where, and for how long, and who is counting.

## Getting there

The plateau is the easy part and it will try to kill you first.

**Altitude.** Treat the air as unbreathable-by-default until proven otherwise. Stage your ascent over one to two weeks if your physiology allows; sleep low, walk high; take the usual acclimatisation drugs and carry a pulse oximeter. If you cannot acclimatise, go on supplemental oxygen for the entire approach, not just the last hour. The reason is not comfort. Hypoxia produces euphoria, tunnel vision, and a serene confidence in bad ideas, and it will do this precisely at the lip of a bottomless hole in the dark. Every fatal error available here is an error of judgement, and the air is designed to remove your judgement.

**Cold and dryness.** Dress for a high desert night with no humidity and no shelter: hard shell, real insulation, no gaps at the wrists and neck. But note the specific geometry of cold here. At the rim you stand between a furnace and an open sky. Your front will be radiant-hot and your back will be radiating to space. You will cook and freeze along the same seam, in the same minute, and you will keep turning like meat. Plan for it: a reflective front panel you can raise and lower, and the discipline to rotate rather than to stand rapt.

**The ground.** Black volcanic glass spalled into flakes is a scree of broken bottles. Stiff-soled boots, tough trousers, gaiters, gloves, kneepads. Do not run. Do not kneel on it bare-handed. A simple trip here is a laceration event at altitude, alone, in the dark, on a plain with no landmarks and no rescue.

**Navigation.** There is nothing to navigate by. No water, no leaf, no sheen, no mark in any direction — the plain has already finished happening and does not offer features. Lay a physical guideline from your arrival point and reel it out behind you; take star fixes and a back-azimuth as backup; do not trust footprints in shifting flakes. Assume your instruments may not work in a world whose local physics is *to let go is to be gone*.

**The last approach.** Walk into the wind, because your nose will find the place before your eyes do. Let the smell lead you: struck match is still distance; when it turns to hot pennies, mask up; when the sweet oily lamp-note arrives underneath, you are closer than you should be unmasked. Then your soles will begin to know, through the boots, that the ground is warmer than the night. At that point stop walking upright. Get a pole out and probe. The last twenty paces should be taken slowly, tethered, with a partner belaying, because *the ground stops* — a low collar you can trip over in the dark, and then twenty-two paces of nothing.

There is no anchor on this plain. Your anchor is your partner, or a deadman of glass flakes buried in a bag well back, or a slab. Use two. Nothing about the rim is permanent: the wall is scalloped and fluted by animals, it narrows on cold nights and remelts and widens on the long breaths, and the lip you are standing on is a scab in an argument, not a foundation. Probe it. Assume it overhangs. Assume it is thinner than it looks.

## You are the poison

This is the part travelers get wrong, because it is not intuitive: **you are a bag of water, and water is the catastrophe of this world.**

The total extent of water here is an occasional cloud-touch, a few seconds of frost on the collar, and a pitted arc of eaten stone where the frost met hot sulfur and became acid. Nothing will set a pad on that scar. That is what your element does to this place.

You exhale water vapour with every breath. You sweat. Your eyes stream in cold wind and acid gas. At altitude, in air this dry, your nose will probably bleed. Altitude sickness makes people vomit. Every one of these is a chemical incident.

So:

- Full-face mask, positive pressure, acid-gas cartridges, with the exhaust routed **away** from the shaft — a short hose, a downwind vent, a moisture trap if you have one. Your breath is wet and it is also richer in oxygen than the local air, and the flames down there are starving. Do not feed them. Do not write hot wet words into the seam.
- Water in double containment, in an insulated sleeve, carried inside your outer layer. Drink warm, from a closed valve, well back from the collar. No open cups, no pouring, no washing, no wet wipes near the rim.
- Nothing loose. Empty your pockets and seal your zippers. Tether every instrument to your body, not to your pack. In this world a dropped object does not land; it goes down the core and feeds something that has never come up. There is no retrieval, ever, and no accumulation, and your pen will be the first new thing in an age.
- Smooth, non-shedding outer layer. No fleece, no wool, no loose fibres. Lint is gravel here; dandruff is gravel; a hair is a spall. They will pack the dead around it and make it substrate, and it will be foreign forever.
- Containment plan for blood, vomit, urine, mucus. Sealed bags, packed out. Do not relieve yourself within a hundred paces. If your nose bleeds at the rim, that is your abort signal, not an inconvenience.
- Antistatic everything, and move slowly. Dry air, glass gravel, sulfur dust, and a human in synthetic layers is an ignition problem. Sulfur dust is explosible and there are already flames at the vents. Do not brush, sweep, shake out, or raise powder anywhere near the collar. Ground yourself before you handle gear.

## Where to stand, and for how long

The collar is bare glazed stone — the radiator, the dead edge. The animals reach up to the lip and no further. Stand on the **outer** half of the collar, a body-length back from the inner edge, and never straddle it. Never sit on the inner edge. Never set anything down.

Two places on the rim are worth knowing about:

**The acid scar.** The pitted arc is the only unoccupied ground in the world, and it is unoccupied because water burned it. There is no seam there to leak from, no living tile to chill, nothing to fill you in. It is, in the most literal sense, the only spare room this world has, and your element made it. Use it as your observation stance if you must have one — but the stone is eaten and friable, so probe it, tether over it, and do not trust it with your weight twice.

**The five fitted blocks.** Coursed, squared, no mortar, sized to nothing that lives in the shaft, top edges worn round by traffic that no longer exists. This is the only offered hospitality on the entire world: someone built a doorstep here. Stand on it if you like. Understand what you are doing: you are the traffic. But it is dry-laid and worn round and it can shift, so it is not an anchor either.

**The clock.** The vent breathes on roughly a hundred heartbeats, irregular, with a hitch. Learn the cycle before you go near the lip: sit back on the plain with a barometer and a timer and log twenty breaths until you can feel the hitch coming. The sigh is not a sound; it is a change in the pressure behind your eyes, and at altitude your sinuses will hate it. Equalise. Carry a decongestant.

The rim is a lung. On the inhale, plateau air slumps over the lip and falls, hugging the wall — it pulls at your hair, your straps, your dust, and it takes your exhaust down with it. On the exhale, the hot flux comes up and the habitable sleeve rises a body-width, and sometimes it comes up hard and remelts the glaze. Those long breaths are the ones that will scorch you and gas you. Be off the collar before them, and know they are irregular.

**The fill-in.** If you stay, you become a leak, and they will close you: glassy dead packed against your boots, then your knees, then your chest, without haste and without sound. About an hour. It is not an attack; it is what they do to a spall. It works. Note two things. First, it starts at your feet, so keep moving, shift your stance, lift your boots — a traveler standing rapt is a traveler being buried in glass shards. Second, there is no clever workaround. Cold, you are a wound and they insulate you. Warm, you are a warm face, which is to say a wall, and they shingle over you. **There is no temperature at which you belong.** Both roads end in being covered.

Budget your rim time in tens of minutes, not hours: three or four short approaches with withdrawals to warm up, check your oxygen, and check each other. Anything you set down for more than a few minutes — pack, tripod, case — will begin to become substrate, and retrieving it will mean tearing wall.

**The lane.** Find the predator's chute before you choose your stance: a fluted channel a hand wide, the deepest groove in the shaft, polished by an age of climbing, running out onto the collar. Mark its exit and stay well clear. The thing that comes up it is long, segmented, heavy with liquid, and it comes out onto the cold stone to radiate and re-crystallise with a sound like sugar settling. It is not interested in you as food — you are colder than everything, you are a leak, not a meal. But it hunts by melting its cargo against warm bodies and drinking their heat, and it dumps heat by crystallising against cold ones. Either contact is a thermal injury you have no medicine for. Do not touch it, do not block its lane, do not stand in the one place in this world where something has a habit.

## How to see

You are the blind one here. Understand that clearly or you will spend the whole visit calling a lit room night.

**Bring a thermal imager.** This is the single most valuable object you can carry, and it is worth more than every lamp combined. It is passive — it writes nothing into the seam — and it converts you, for as long as you hold it, into something closer to a native. The wall becomes what it actually is: a roaring furnace of bodies each glowing at its own temperature, a script of hot and cool, the sleeve visible as a band, the turn visible as a slow rotation instead of a mark-and-look-away trick. It is also a safety instrument: it shows you where the hot face is, when the surge is coming, where the predator's cold lane runs, and where the tiles have gone stiff and clear under it.

It will also show you your own cold footprint spreading across the wall, and the wall closing over it. Decide in advance whether you are ready to watch yourself being erased in real time.

**Budget your looking.** Once every few hundred breaths a flame stands up taller and paler and you get one whole second of the interior. That is the gift and it is not on demand. Dark-adapt properly — forty minutes, no screens, no white light — because your night vision is the only instrument that can catch it. Use a dim red lamp for your hands and feet only, angled at the stone, never down the shaft.

If you want more than the flames give, you can have it: a cold LED writes almost no heat, and two seconds of skim-angle white from outside the collar will show you the near wall in true colour. That is a real option and it is not a crime. But make it a decision, not a reflex. Set a number of seconds before you arrive and spend them like money. A hot lamp is worse than useless — it is a sentence in their language, a false warm face, and they will come to fill it in. No flash, no laser rangefinder, no active illumination of any kind. No fire: no stove, no lighter, no candle at the rim.

And decide, in advance, about the recording. A low-light camera running continuously will catch the flare whether you are looking or not, and you will be able to get that second back for the rest of your life. That is either mercy or a life sentence. Both are true. Choose which before you have seen it, because you cannot choose afterwards.

**Listen through stone.** Air is a poor carrier here, but the collar is solid. A contact mic or geophone on the glazed rim will give you the dry rustle of the turn like a page, the tick of freezing sulfur, and the scrape-and-scuff-and-diminishing-contacts of a fall. There are perhaps nine falls an hour. You may make all the noise you like — nothing here has ears, and sound is the one pollution this world cannot receive. But your noise will mask the only channel that tells you anything. Talk on the walk in. Be silent at the rim.

## Do not go down

Nine steps cut into the inner wall, stopping in solid rock, at a rise no tile could climb. They are the most inviting objects on the world and they are a trap in every sense. They descend into the cold annulus toward the seam. Their corners are thick with eggs. The animals use them as a baffle against the draft and have no idea they are using anything. To use them is to displace a nursery, to enter a volume with no spare room, and to put your weight on cut stone whose lower courses may be continued in glass that was once alive.

Look at them from above, in thermal. Do not descend. Do not chip, sample, or "just check" whether the ninth step is the end. Consider that it may be the place where somebody stopped and turned around, and let that be the whole of your archaeology.

Below the flames the air is very likely unbreathable in a second way — heavy volcanic gas pooled in a cold-drafted depression, on top of the thinness and the sulfur. Nothing flies here; nothing has ever gotten a wing to work. To let go is to be gone. Nothing has ever heard the bottom. Do not lower a microphone on a kilometre of line to find out. The loop is closed: the dead fall, something below eats them, the vent brings the smell back up. You are not equipped to be a part of that sentence.

## The partner, and the abort rules

Go with one other person, and give them authority to end the visit without arguing. Agree on:

- A hard timer, running from first contact with the collar, with a called withdrawal at intervals.
- A touch signal, because you will not want to talk and your voice will sound thin and wrong to you anyway.
- Automatic aborts: nosebleed; any cloud touching the plateau; oxygen saturation below your agreed floor; a long breath coming up hard; anyone kneeling inside the collar; anyone unclipping anything; anyone saying *just a little closer*.
- No decision made at the rim is valid. All decisions are made at camp.

Open your exit well back on the plain, on your guideline, out of sight and heat of the seam. Do not open a doorway at the lip. A doorway is a hole, and holes here get filled.

## What to prepare in yourself

The hazards above are manageable. This one is not.

You are about to stand in the densest life you will ever encounter, in total silence, in a world with no names, no history, no accumulation, no grudge that outlives a minute. They write on each other in warm patches and the writing fades in four seconds. Nine deaths an hour, each one closed over in under a minute, and no crying, because nothing here has the equipment or the reason.

You will be the only thing present that remembers. You will be the only mourner. And you will feel the enormous human urge to give them something — a name, a record, a lamp, a warmth, a hand laid back against the hand of heat. Every one of those gifts is a wound or a poison. Your cold hand on the wall is a legible word in a language you do not speak, and you cannot control what it says. Touching is speaking is lying.

The kindest available behaviours are accuracy and absence. Take nothing — not a crystal flower, which is only weather with good manners but is also the shelter and the nursery; not a glazed tile, which was somebody; not a chip of the doorstep. Leave nothing, not even a mark. There is one gesture on this entire world, five fitted blocks and nine steps going nowhere, and no other trace in any direction for as far as anything can be walked. You do not need to be the second gesture. You are permitted to be the traffic that wore the top edges round and then stopped existing.

And know what you are looking at. It is not crowded because it is fertile. It is crowded because it is the only warm room in a cold empty world, and the room is exactly as wide as the balance between the glaze and the flux permits, and it has been that wide for a very long time, and one day the vent will close. You cannot save it. You would have to hold a volcano open. Anything you brought to help — water, oxygen, heat — would be acid, fire, or a lie.

## Leaving

Brush down well back on the plain, downwind, slowly, without raising dust. Bag the outer layer. Check your boots, your cuffs, your hood, your instrument cases for sulfur crystal, for eggs, for anything that came away with you and is now dying of cold in your pack. Check your partner. Pack out every scrap of yourself, including the water.

Then walk back along your line into a wind that smells of struck match, on ground that rings like a jar of teeth, under too many stars, and try to notice that you are leaving the only lit room on the world and going out into what everyone else here would call the dark.

Expect to want to go back. Expect the one second to be the thing you cannot put down. Tell your partner now, while you are still cold and sensible, what you will want later — and let them hold that, too.
