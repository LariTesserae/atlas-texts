# Basin Nine: A Traveler's Preparation

## First, understand what you are visiting

It is not a town in a landscape. It is a machine that has been running for nineteen years, with a garden growing in its gaps and nineteen hundred people living inside its tolerances. Everything you would normally treat as background — the sound, the light, the wetness of your shirt, the ground under your feet — is here a working part, owned by someone, and load-bearing.

The single most useful mental adjustment is this: **in Basin Nine, there is no scenery.** The white flats are a pump discharge. The fog is the water supply. The moss under the catwalk is somebody's crop. The noise is the structural integrity. The footprints are the archive. You will be walking around inside other people's infrastructure and other people's records, and the local definition of rudeness is *touching the background*.

Go for at least eleven days. A two-day visit gets you glare, deafness, and a photograph of nothing. Your ears need three or four nights to stop fighting the note. Your eyes need about a week to learn the flats. And the thing the town actually wants to show you — the dated stakes walking north across the white, one a year — only reads as a sentence if you have been there long enough to stop seeing white.

---

## Before you leave: body and paperwork

**Get your hearing checked and documented.** Baseline audiogram, dated, carried with you. Eighty-four decibels on the deck is not "loud," it is a occupational exposure that happens to be where people live. You will be in it for sixteen hours a day, not eight, plus eighty at the seams at dusk, plus the pump hall at ninety-six. The town's over-forties all read lips. That is not a cultural quirk; that is the audiogram of a settlement. Do not join that curve on holiday.

**Tetanus current, dental work finished, prescriptions doubled.** Nineteen hundred people, a cannery, a kiln, a substation, and one road out through cut rock. Anything serious means the coast plateau and hours. Bring a real personal kit, not a blister of plasters.

**Bring your own sterile saline in sealed ampoules.** This matters more than it sounds. Every cut you take here will be full of angular carbonate and lime dust, and the correct first response is irrigation — but there is no water to spare, and you must not rinse a wound under somebody's drip trough. Your medical water is yours. Carry it, declare it, use it without apology.

**Assume nothing you carry is welcome biologically.** More on this below, but start your preparations now: boots you have not worn in soil, no wool with burrs in it, no velcro full of last world's grass, no fresh fruit, no seeds, no live plants, no untreated wood, no garden soil, no compost, no "just a cutting."

## The light: prepare as for snow, not for beach

Shell flour is not sand and it does not behave like sand optically. It is angular, unweathered, and white, and it throws the sun back up at you. You are being lit from two directions all day.

- **Category 4 glacier glasses with side shields**, plus a lighter pair for the under-walks and interiors. The flats at noon will give you photokeratitis — snow blindness — as efficiently as a glacier, and you will not feel it until that night.
- **A brim is not enough.** The light comes from underneath. Sunscreen the underside of your jaw, the inside of your nostrils, your earlobes, the backs of your knees, the roof of your mouth if you're a mouth-breather. Better: a light buff over the lower face, which also handles dust.
- **Prefer UV clothing to sunscreen.** This is not vanity, it is water law. Whatever is on your skin ends up in a trough, and the trough feeds a hedge, and the hedge's runoff goes to the sump, and the sump is what everyone drinks, and the troughs are full of frogs. Sunscreen, DEET, and shampoo are amphibian toxins in a closed system. Cover up instead. If you must use repellent, use it indoors, sparingly, and wash it off into a collection vessel you hand back — not onto the ground.
- **Adopt the local noon.** The reason the town goes quiet and indoors in the middle of the day is optical, not thermal. Work at dawn and after four. At noon the shadows go thin, the settlement dissolves into eleven objects and glare, and you will not be able to judge distance, depth, or whether the thing ahead of you is a hole.
- **Photography:** expose down a stop or two, expect the meter to lie constantly, bring a polarizer and a hood, and *do not change lenses on the flats*. The flour is finer than talc and it will be on your sensor forever. Moving from the chilled condenser field or the pump hall into humid air fogs everything instantly — acclimatize gear in a sealed bag, slowly.

## The noise: bring protection, but do not bring silence

Buy **flat-attenuation musician's plugs, around −15 dB**, not foam. Foam kills the consonants, and in a town where everyone over forty survives on lipreading and a hand creole, muffling yourself makes you a hazard and a nuisance. You want the whole world turned down evenly, not erased.

Add **muffs for the pump hall**, over the plugs. Ninety-six decibels is fifteen minutes of exposure a day, not a tour. Ask before entering, go with someone, and leave when they leave.

Do not, however, plan to sleep in total silence. This sounds perverse. Explain it to yourself now, before you're tired: **the note stopping is the emergency.** Locals sleep through it and wake when it changes. If you seal yourself into a −33 dB cocoon on night one you have removed your own alarm. Turn it down to where you can sleep and still feel it in the sternum. Expect not to sleep much for three nights. Expect night four to be strangely wonderful — the eleven-second swell against the wall becomes a heartbeat, and the shell tick at noon becomes weather.

**Leave anything that hums at home.** Drones, generators, portable fans, electric razors, a phone with a novel ringtone. In a basin whose entire auditory culture is one constant tone, *a new sustained note is an emergency signal*. You will make people stand up. A drone additionally does three unacceptable things at once: introduces a foreign tone, flies through fog lanes that are legally somebody's water, and gets killed by the peregrine.

If you have absolute pitch, warn yourself. The note is a slightly flat B and it does not bend for you. Do not bring an instrument in standard tuning and do not offer to fix anything. Ask an operator what the note actually is, get it on a tuner, and if you want to play, play in their key. The frogs have already worked this out; they are singing the pump's harmonics, and the old people can hear that the young ones have drifted into tune. You are hearing a nineteen-year-old ecosystem retune itself around a machine. Sit in it at dusk with your plugs in and your mouth shut.

## Language: six signs and a new name

Learn the deck creole before you arrive, at minimum: **pump, brine, level, drip, shut, don't.** The last two are safety-critical and you must be able to *receive* them at distance, in glare, from a stranger, instantly. Everything else you can pick up in a week.

Then:

- **Give yourself a deck name.** Two syllables, hard visible consonants — the kind you can see on lips and hear through a damaged audiogram. Plosives, not sibilants. Something like Bikka, Tannik, Dokra, Pellan. Your long soft foreign name is useless here and people will simply assign you one; choose it yourself and it's a small courtesy.
- **Speak low, clear, and facing.** Do not shout into an ear. Shouting distorts the mouth and blows out what hearing remains. Turn your face to the person, get in their visual field, and let them read you.
- **Never come up behind someone.** In a loud town, touch on the shoulder is normal and being startled is not funny.
- **Do not slam doors and do not knock and wait.** Doors are felt. Use the local signal — light, hand, a foot on the floor.
- **Assume every outdoor conversation is public.** Everyone reads lips, everyone can see every house, and there is nowhere on the deck to have a private word. Say nothing on a catwalk you would not say in the square. This is why the felt room exists.
- **Never imitate the shift whistle.** The starlings do it once a season and half the town stands up and then laughs, and it is not entirely a joke. A visitor doing it is a cruelty you will not be forgiven quickly.

## The note: the one rule under all the rules

Understand this before you understand anything else.

The pumps are four stages in series, three running and one spare. The spare is not a spare in the sense of "available." It is a held breath. Changeovers are done by two operators *overlapping the tone, hand to hand*, and this is the only ceremony the town takes seriously. If you are invited to witness one: stand where you're put, say nothing, do not photograph it without asking, and do not applaud. Treat it the way you would treat a communion in a faith you don't share.

In year six the note broke for nine seconds. Water came up in the low seams within four. Every bird in the basin went up at once. People will tell you about the silence the way people elsewhere tell you about a war.

So:

- **Never joke about it stopping.** Not at dinner, not as an icebreaker, not as an engineer's hypothetical. If someone offers you year six, receive it and ask nothing clever.
- **Never propose a use for the spare stage.**
- **If you hear the note change — a wobble, a beat, a stage dropping out, anything — say so immediately, to anyone, even if you are certainly wrong.** Being wrong is free here. Every person in the basin is a sensor and you are now one of them.
- **Learn your evacuation before you unpack.** Your address is a depth. Know it. Know the climb to the rim from where you sleep, and count it in the dark, on foot, once, sober. Know the muster point. Sump Road is lowest and oldest and the first place the water returns; do not tell its residents it is dangerous, they know, and there is pride in it.
- **Birds up means go up.** If every bird in the basin leaves the ground at once, do not wait for a siren, do not wait for confirmation, do not go back for your bag. That signal is older than the town's alarms and it is faster than them.
- If the eleven-second thump in your soles changes rhythm, mention it. Probably nothing. Mention it anyway.

The fear of eleven meters of standing sea two hundred meters away is not irrational and you will feel it at three in the morning. Ask, once, about wall inspection, redundancy, and the handover protocol. Get a real answer from a real operator. Then let it go, and do not ask the town to reassure you every day. They are holding it up so you can sleep.

## Water law: how not to steal without noticing

This is where visitors do real damage, because the thefts are all things that feel like nothing.

**A property here is a set of drip-rights.** Catwalk undersides, condenser rows, fog-shadow, shade easements. A family has been litigating a pipe for six years. Someone's ferns are eleven liters a day and someone else's proposed wall would take it.

Therefore:

- **Never place a vessel under a drip that is not yours.** Not a bucket, not a cup, not your open mouth as a joke. This is the clearest crime available to a tourist.
- **Do not open an umbrella under the catwalks.** You are intercepting a harvest. Wear a hood. A hat brim is tolerated at personal scale; a tarp, a tent, a poncho stretched over a line, or a hammock is not.
- **Do not string anything up.** No washing lines across a drip lane, no tarp, no shade sail, no antenna mast. Shade and wind are property.
- **Do not walk between the condenser panels.** Four hundred tilted glass-and-copper panels, chilled inside, sweating outside, each with a trough and a hedge and an owner. Do not touch the glass — your finger oil changes how it sweats. Do not breathe on it. Do not wipe it. Do not photograph it from inside the rows.
- **Do not block the fog-combs on the rim**, run your own fan in a fog lane, or stand around at dawn where the fog moves. Until about nine every morning the town is manufacturing its drinking water. Be indoors, or be still and out of the way, during harvest.
- **Nothing goes on the ground.** Not greywater, not toothpaste, not coffee, not urine, not oil, not soap. The system is closed, the frogs are in it, and the cress is in it. Find out on day one where your wash water goes and hand it back. It is somebody's fern water and they want it.
- **Every cistern has a lock.** Do not test one. Do not drink from a trough, a seam, a gutter, or the sump. Do not put a hand in the sump: it is black, cold, netted for a reason, and it is where the pumps drink.
- **Ask what your allowance is and then live inside it visibly.** Sponge washing, no shower, short everything. Do not be a hero about it and do not be a cheat about it. If you import water, ask about its legal status first — made water versus found water may be two different things here, and you do not want to accidentally inject yourself into a six-year lawsuit.

## Walking: you are writing, and it lasts

The floor takes a footprint and keeps it. Eleven rains to erase one, forty rain days a year, brief and uncommitted. Last winter's paths are still braided across the flats. The first month's tracks near the gate are wired off and read like a document — the surveyors' boots, the wheelbarrow, the place where a man set down a crate and stood a while, thinking.

**Walk in the existing tracks.** Step in the braid. Do not shortcut across clean white. Do not drag anything. Do not scuff a line, do not draw, do not write your name — that is vandalism with a multi-year half-life, and it will be read.

Understand what you are joining. There are paths here that are private: a grief-walk, a courtship, a route somebody took the night of the nine seconds. You cannot tell which is which. That is precisely why you stay in the braid.

And if you do stop somewhere new and stand a while thinking — because you will, everyone does — know that you are signing and dating the ground, and that somebody's grandchildren will read it.

**Find the man whose job is not to sweep, early.** He is the archivist, the historian, and probably the best guide in the basin. Ask him what he can read. Offer to walk with him. This is the single highest-value hour of your visit and it costs nothing.

**Footwear and body:**
- Rigid boots with a puncture-resistant midsole. Nothing here is rounded. Shell flour is broken sugar; the old reef at the south end is blades and flutes with clam borings like fresh wounds. No sandals, ever, anywhere, including indoors.
- Thin grippy gloves, long sleeves despite the humidity, knee pads if you go under a house.
- Cuts here are salt-plus-lime-plus-damp, which means they hurt and they infect. Irrigate with your own saline, cover, watch.
- Near the kiln: quicklime plus permanent humidity is caustic. Respect the lime burners, respect the whitewash buckets, and do not get wet lime on your skin.
- Bring a **brush**, not water, for cleaning gear. The flour and the damp make a paste that sets — in boot treads, in zip teeth, in tripod threads, in your eyelashes. It will cement. Everything you own with a thread will need persuading.
- Lift a plate of shell crust once, gently, to see the sandhoppers and the wax-pale woodlice and the gravel mice. Then **put it back exactly as it was.** It is a roof. Overturned, it kills a colony and changes the drip.
- Stay off the narrow-gauge spur. It carries shell out and lime back and it does not stop for you.

## The living things: you are the biggest hazard here

Nineteen years. No soil. No established soil biota. Animals arrived faster than plants, plants faster than soil, and the whole thing is a bowl and a funnel that catches everything out of the sky.

**This is one of the most invasible habitats you will ever stand in, and you are a vector.** The unforgivable sin in Basin Nine is not noise or rudeness. It is *bringing dirt*. A gram of foreign humus, a seed in a cuff, a fungal spore on a boot sole, a chytrid hitchhiker on a damp sock — any of these could rewrite a nineteen-year-old succession that a town is staking its three-hundred-year plan on.

So: scrub and vacuum and inspect everything before you come. New boots or boots used only on rock and pavement. Nothing woven that has been in a meadow. No fresh produce, no seeds, no nuts that could sprout, no live cultures, no soil, no leaf litter, no untreated timber. Declare everything organic. If there is a customs regime, be grateful for it and cooperate past the point of politeness; if there isn't, act as though there is and tell the assay office what you're carrying.

The same applies leaving. Do not take a moss rope, a snail, a fern, a frog. **Do not take a crab.**

About the crabs: they live in the warm damp voids of the cable trays, graze the moss, moult into the troughs, and **carry a spoonful of water in the shell across the dry spans.** If you pick one up, you spill its water and strand it. Your curiosity is a drought. Look, photograph, do not touch.

And nobody eats them. There is a superstition nobody can explain. Do not try to get them to explain it, do not offer to cook one, do not make it a joke. Some things a town is allowed to keep.

Also: bats roost in the pipe lagging above the inverted meadows. Do not hang from the lagging, do not pull it, do not put a light in it. The starlings go up in sheets that change the light — no flash, no drone, no sudden movement into a flock. The peregrine on the stack has never had to hunt anything slower than a pigeon and does not need you.

Do not pick the espaliered apples or the sea kale on the condenser frames. They are not wild. They are somebody's eleven liters.

## Damp, rust, and your own skin

It is a wet place with no water. Your hands are damp from waking. Laundry is put away damp and worn damp. Moss on the north face of every vertical thing.

- **Nothing you love made of steel.** Knives, camera bodies, glasses hinges, zips, harmonicas, tools, jewellery. The town's answer is grease, tar, lime-wash, and sacrifice; adopt it. Bring stainless, titanium, brass, plastic. Bring a small tin of grease and use it.
- **Desiccant will saturate in days.** Do not build your plan around it. Sealed cases plus grease plus acceptance.
- **Paper goes limp and ink runs.** Waterproof notebook, pencil. Books arrive as objects and leave as compost; if you bring one as a gift, bring it sealed and say so.
- **Your skin will not dry.** Constant damp plus salt plus sharp dust equals chafe, intertrigo, and fungal blooms in every fold. Barrier cream, antifungal powder, and **three times as much underwear as you think.** Synthetics or thin wool, nothing that holds water. Bring a boot dryer if you can power it without making a new noise; otherwise rotate two pairs and accept clammy boots forever.
- **Feet are the failure point.** Damp boots, sharp grit, sixteen hours standing. Air them, powder them, check them nightly. Trench foot in a place with no water to spare is an irony you do not want.
- Glasses will salt-film constantly. Many microfibres, no water. Contact lenses plus flour dust is a bad combination; consider going back to frames for the trip.

## Being a guest

**Time your visit to an equinox if you possibly can.** The levelling is the harvest festival: everybody outside, everybody under something, wedges and spirit levels, borrowing tools, arguing about a bubble in a glass tube. The clay swells and shrinks, the salt hydrates and dehydrates, the basin breathes a centimeter a season, and twice a year the town brings its kitchens back to true. Ask to be allowed under a house. Hold things. Fetch things. Disagree politely about the bubble. You will be more welcome for having done this than for any gift you carry.

**Bring a good spirit level.** Bring wedges. Bring stainless fasteners, gaskets, lubricant, bearings if you can source them — the pump bearings are on a forty-year cycle and machined roundness is the rarest category of object in the basin.

**Bring acoustic felt.** Mineral wool, dense felt, anything that eats sound. It is light, it is worth more per kilo than anything else you can carry, and the felt-lined room behind the pump hall annex — sixty-two decibels, rented by the hour, waiting list — is the town's most precious room.

**Book the felt room in your first two days, and again before you leave.** Do not hog it, do not nap in it, do not complain about the wait. Use the first hour to notice what the place is doing to your body. Use the last to notice what it has done.

**And bring a rounded river pebble.** This is the real gift. Three centuries of ships' ballast dumped here means the only round natural things in Basin Nine are grey cobbles from fjords and river mouths a thousand kilometers away. Children collect them. There is a case behind the assay office, labelled by provenance, with a hand-lettered card: **THE SEA HAS NOT HAD TIME.** A curve here means somebody made it or somebody carried it.

Wash it, boil it, scrub it, carry it clean, and write the label yourself: which world, which river, what water rounded it and for how long. Hand it to the assay office, or to a child with a parent's permission. You will have added one line to the only museum in the basin, and it will still be there when the soil arrives.

**What not to bring:** opinions about the plan. It is revised every decade. They know the drying front descends four centimeters a year, they know the salt line retreats a meter a year, they paint the stake every spring, and there are nineteen posts walking north like the markers of a glacier going home. They know it is three hundred years. They are doing it anyway, for people who do not exist. If you have soil science from your world — real data on how fast humus forms on a fresh mineral substrate — that is a treasure, and the assay office will want it. Offer it as a document, not as advice. Do not tell them they are doing it wrong. Do not call it futile. Do not call it heroic more than once; they have work to do.

## Practical rhythm

Fog until about nine: harvest, stay out of the lanes. Morning to noon: the good working light, plain and generous, bouncing up under the ferns so the hanging gardens are lit from below. Noon: indoors, glasses on, the floor applauding itself as the salt crystallizes and the crust contracts — that dry crackling rain-sound is not the ground failing, it is the ground being noon. Afternoon: damp laundry, drip, the shell tick fading. **In winter the sun goes behind the west berm at four and the basin's day ends two hours before the sea's.** Plan your movement around that, not around sunset tables for the coast. The flats go blue, the frogs come in on their chord, somebody's house is levelled late, and you can hear the small talk of the wedges.

Carry a light after four. A white floor with no landmarks and no glare is a very easy place to be comprehensively lost, and every house looks like every other house from a hundred and fifty meters. Wear one strong colour so the crews can find you.

Addresses are depths. Learn to read them. Eleven Row, Nine-and-a-Half, Sump Road. Lower is older. Older is prouder.

## Afterward

Nobody warns you about leaving.

For a week or two the ordinary world will seem deaf, and wrong, and slightly sharp — your pitch will have recalibrated to a flat B that isn't there, and music at home will sound tuned by someone in a hurry. You will wake at three in the morning listening for a note. You will find that quiet feels like danger. You will hear a chord in a frog pond elsewhere and it will sound *out of tune*, and you will understand, briefly and from the inside, what the old people in Basin Nine mean when they say the frogs used to sound wrong.

And you will have left footprints that outlast your visit by a year or more, crossing somebody else's white in a pale braid, legible to a man whose job is not to sweep.

Walk as if that is true. It is.

---

**If you remember five things:**

1. Nothing you do to the background is neutral — the drip, the fog, the moss, the noise, and the ground all belong to someone or hold something up.
2. Birds up means go up. If the note changes, say so, even if you're wrong.
3. Never put a vessel under a drip, never open an umbrella under a catwalk, never put anything on the ground.
4. Stay in the existing tracks. Don't touch the crabs. Don't bring dirt.
5. Bring a level, bring felt, bring a clean round pebble with a label.
