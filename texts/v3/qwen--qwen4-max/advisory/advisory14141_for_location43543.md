# Preparing for Sixteen

## 1. Decide *when* before you decide anything else

The single most important preparation is a calendar decision, and the description has already made it for you: there is a twenty-fourth rib, thigh-thick and tender enough to dent with a fingernail. A new rib swells a season before the tide. You are not looking at a quiet town. You are looking at a town one season out from the loudest event in its life.

That gives you three honest options.

**The reconnaissance visit.** Arrive in high summer of an ordinary year. The slot is open, there is real daylight for hours, the chamber is half-empty of animals and wholly empty of crowds, the water is low, and nobody is busy. You can learn the ladders, learn the smells, learn the drips, be counted, and leave before autumn with your fingers intact. This is the correct first visit. It is also, in a sense, a visit to a theatre between shows.

**The full passage.** Arrive a season early — now — work through the tide, and stay into sealed time. This is the only way to actually see the place, and it is a commitment measured in months, not days. You do not get to sample it. The lid does not open for a change of heart.

**The wrong visit.** Arriving in late autumn, uninvited, with a light pack and a return date. You will either be sealed in without having prepared your body and mind for it, or sealed out on a forty-mile plain with no fuel, no shelter, and no way to find the door again once the snow lies even. People have almost certainly died this way. There is no ruin of them; the town keeps its dead sorted.

Pick one and two. Do not improvise three.

## 2. Finding it, and not falling into it

You will not find Sixteen by looking for a hole. You find it by looking for the *absence* of winter: a long low patch of bare ground where the snow will not lie, faint steam along a seam, and — downwind — sugar, wet fur, tallow smoke. In still cold weather the whole ceiling breathes and you can read it from a mile off. In wind or thaw you can walk over the town and never know.

Approach in cold, still weather if you can arrange it. Approach along the outwash channels, which all run downhill toward the slot, and take a bearing on something that is not the town before you close the last few hundred paces, because fog and blowing snow will erase the plume exactly when you need it.

Then stop, and understand the lid.

The lid is a living felt roof over an eleven-metre drop onto water. It carries a fox. It does not carry sheep, and sheep are not being superstitious — they are heavier than foxes and they have better instincts about roofs. You are five to ten times a fox. The snow on the lid lies thin *because it is warm*, which means thin snow is your warning sign and also the thing you will be walking on.

Rules for the last hundred paces:

- **Travel roped, in daylight, with a probe.** A long pole, used ahead of every step, like crossing a snow bridge. Because that is exactly what it is.
- **Never step where the snow is thin or the ground is bare.** That is the warm part. That is the roof.
- **Skirt the lip, don't shortcut across it.** The gravel at the edge is sharp and unrounded; it will cut you, and it is also the only honest ground for miles. Wear gloves you can work in.
- **Mark your line back.** Wands, cairns, flagging — anything. Once you are inside, your sense of direction is going to be dismantled for weeks, and you may come out into a whiteout with no ability to judge distance.
- **Do not go to the edge and put your face down over the slot.** It is the natural thing to do and the description invites it. Do it once, briefly, from a braced position with a partner holding your belt, and understand that the drop is six fathoms and the air at the bottom is not the air at the top.

The fall itself would probably not kill you. What comes after would.

## 3. Air — the thing that will actually kill you

Forget the sorter for a moment. The likeliest cause of death in Sixteen is bad air, and the second likeliest is a wet ladder in the dark.

Consider what is in that chamber: forty-one people, forty-odd fat lamps, a shared flue system running hot smoke through dead rhizomes, and a vast thermogenic plant that is warm enough to melt snow off its own back and metabolising continuously in the dark. Warmth in a plant means respiration. Respiration means oxygen consumed and carbon dioxide produced. The keepers say the sealed air is "sweet and thick." *Thick* is a word people use for hypercapnia before they have a word for hypercapnia. And they come up after eight weeks blinking, unable to judge distances — which is what a long exposure to high CO₂, low light, and no horizon does to a nervous system.

Practical consequences:

**The air is layered.** That flat-topped fog at chest height is a boundary, not decoration. Cold heavy air — and carbon dioxide is heavier than air — will pool low, over the water, in the bottom of the kettle. The sweetest, warmest, most interesting part of the chamber is the part with the worst air. When you lean over the side with a lamp to look at the drowned houses, you are putting your head into that layer and holding a burning thing there with you.

**Learn to read a flame before you learn anything else.** Carry a small lamp or candle whose behaviour you know intimately in clean air. Practise at home. A shortening, yellowing, guttering flame, a flame that lifts off the wick, a flame you can't keep lit at knee height — that is your instrument, and it is also the instrument the keepers have been using for a generation without calling it that. If your flame misbehaves, *go up*, and go up now, and don't stop to be polite.

**Sleep high, work low.** Take the highest berth you are offered, even if it means a longer climb and an empty street of swept warming-huts around you. Those empty upper terraces are your emergency refuge: they are dry-ish, they are swept, they are ready, and they are above the pool.

**Know the symptoms.** Morning headache that clears when you climb. Breathlessness on the ladders out of proportion to the effort. Confusion, euphoria, poor arithmetic, a tendency to find things funny. In a room where a cough is public property, nobody will remark that you have become vague. Watch yourself.

**During the tide, this becomes acute.** Six hundred people, thirty hours of continuous rendering, flues hot enough to scorch the pipes. That is a carbon monoxide event waiting to happen inside a felt box. Work in shifts. Get out onto the plain every day if the slot is open. Do not sleep at water level during the tide, no matter how convenient your work is.

**Bring a meter if your kit survives crossing; bring a candle regardless.** And accept that if you start telling people the bottom of their town is full of poison, you will be correct and unwelcome. Say it once, quietly, to the tally-keeper, framed as a question about where to sleep.

## 4. Light, and moving in the dark

Full dark adaptation takes about forty minutes; the foxfire on the raft comes up, they say, after an hour. Budget an hour of genuine helplessness on your first descent, and arrange to be led by the hand through it without embarrassment. Someone will do this for you. It is probably a routine kindness.

- **Red light only, and as little as you can manage.** A white torch in that chamber is a violence: it destroys your own adaptation for another forty minutes and everyone else's within line of sight, and it turns a room that works by hand into a room that can't work at all. Filter everything. Tape over indicators. Bring nothing that beeps, nothing that lights itself up, nothing with a screen that wakes when you move.
- **Do not shout to compensate for the quiet.** The felt eats sound. Your voice will go nowhere and you will keep raising it, and everyone will hear you being loud and flat and close. Speak at the volume they use, which will feel to you like whispering. Learn to be quiet for hours. Do not fill silence out of nerves.
- **You will have no acoustic sense of the space.** This is the disorienting part nobody warns you about. Humans build a subconscious model of a room from echoes; the felt takes them away. You cannot tell if the ceiling is three metres up or thirty. You cannot hear someone fall. You cannot hear the lid tearing. Keep a hand on a rib.
- **Learn three drips.** Pick the three nearest your berth, learn their pitches and their tempo, and use them as a home beacon. This is how the keepers do it and it works faster than you'd expect.
- **The felt keeps a thumbprint for a week.** That is a sign system, a week-long Post-it note, and probably a whole informal literacy of marks. *Ask before you make one.* Never smooth away someone else's.
- **Do not wash with soap and do not bring anything scented.** Districts are navigated by smell. You will arrive smelling like a foreign country and you will be a moving obstacle for a fortnight. Let yourself go native. Deodorant is a small act of vandalism.

## 5. Wet, not cold

Outside, dress for a peri-glacial outwash plain: wind across forty miles of nothing, no fuel but dwarf willow, ground that will take your heat all night. Full winter kit, goggles, a shovel, a real sleeping system, and the means to bivouac if the lid shuts while you are on the wrong side of it.

Inside, the problem inverts. It is *warm* down there — nothing below the waterline has ever frozen — and everything is wet. Walls weep constantly. A hundred drips at a hundred pitches is a hundred sources of water landing on you and your bedding forever. Damp at two or three degrees will kill you slowly while you feel comfortable.

- Wool or synthetic. **No down.** Down in that chamber is a wet rag in three days.
- A closed-cell mat *and* a waterproof groundsheet, or better, a hanging cot — but ask what you may hang it from, and see §7.
- Three pairs of socks minimum, rotated and dried against your body, and a religious foot routine. Trench foot in a warm room is entirely possible. Use the bathhouse; it exists for a reason and it is probably a social institution as much as a hygienic one.
- Everything you own will smell of tallow and sugar within a week. Accept this now.

## 6. Water, salt, and food

The sweetwater is the engine of the place and it is not drinking water in the sense your kidneys mean.

It is syrup. It does not freeze and it preserves flesh, which means it is hyperosmotic — dense with sugar and probably other solutes, glycerol or polyols of the kind a cold-loving plant makes as antifreeze. Those things are laxatives in quantity. **Introduce it slowly, dilute it, and do not make it your only fluid.** Osmotic diarrhoea in a sealed felt room with forty people and one bathhouse is a personal disaster and a public one.

The upside is real: nothing rots in it, so it is very unlikely to give you an infection. It is probably the safest water microbiologically that you will ever drink. It will also dissolve your teeth over a season, and it contains no salt.

- **Get plain water from the frost.** The ribs grow frost-feathers all winter above the waterline. That is condensed transpiration — effectively distilled. Ask whether you may scrape it and where. Otherwise carry clean snow down from the plain, not from the lid, which is warm and weeping and tastes of the plant.
- **Bring salt and use it.** They salt for the tide, which tells you salt arrives from outside and is counted. Thirty hours of hauling and rendering while drinking syrup is a recipe for hyponatremia: headache, nausea, cramps, confusion — symptoms that look exactly like bad air. Salt your food and salt your water.
- **Bring a toothbrush.** Genuinely. It may be the most useful non-obvious thing in your pack.
- **"Preserved" is not "safe."** Salvage comes up from drowned houses of the first generation. Some of it will be food. Thirty-year-old flesh in anaerobic syrup is a botulism question, not a curiosity. Eat what is freshly rendered. Be politely, immovably uninterested in ancient stores.
- **Nothing here decays, so nothing here goes away.** No soil, no silt, no rot. Every wrapper, battery, pill, and length of cord you bring in is permanent, and may eventually be filed by whatever files things down there. Pack out every scrap. Do not spit. Do not bury anything.

## 7. Do not cut the plant, and do not make fire

Everything that is not gravel is one organism. The walls are its tissue, the stairs are its consent, the calendar is its skeleton, and it is possibly the thing that arranges the dead. Cutting it is not damage to property. Treat it the way you would treat a sleeping animal the size of a building that everyone lives inside.

- **No blades into felt or rib, ever.** Not to hang a coat, not to trim a snag, not to make a handhold. Pruning here is a years-long negotiation, not an act.
- **Do not girdle a rib.** No cord tied tight around one, no hammock slung from a young one. That twenty-fourth rib is tender enough to dent with a fingernail and it is next year's harvest forecast. Leave it alone entirely.
- **Do not score a rib.** The iron knife belongs to the tally-keeper and scoring is the ledger. Your initials would be a forgery of the calendar.
- **No stove, no candle of your own, no smoking, no cooking.** You are inside a felted box full of rendered fat with a shared flue system. Your camp stove is a weapon. Use the hearths you are given, cook what you are given, and never hang wet clothes over a flue or fiddle with a pipe. Blocking a flue sours the felt for everyone; opening one wrongly does worse.

## 8. The water and the sorter

These are the rules, and they are not negotiable and not interesting to debate:

- **No swimming. No wading. No putting a limb in.** Not to retrieve a dropped boot, not to test the temperature, not once.
- **Tether yourself when working at the water's edge.** A line to a rib or a partner, every time, including when you are just looking.
- **Never leave a hook-line overnight.** Haul fast. If a line goes taut sideways: let it go, stop work, wait with everyone else, and do not talk about it above a murmur afterward.
- **Do not propose to investigate.** You will be tempted. You may have a rebreather, a light, a camera, a theory. Understand what you would actually be doing: descending into a cemetery where people's grandmothers lie in ranks, in the dark, in the worst air in the chamber, to interrupt something the town has an old and workable treaty with. The sorter is not a puzzle. It is a neighbour. The keepers' silence is not ignorance; it is a maintained relationship, and it is also grief.
- **Looking at the drowned quarter is a solemn act.** Ask to be taken. Let a keeper hold the lamp. Don't sketch the ranks, don't count the bodies aloud, don't name what you think you see. And if you notice that the sorting could be explained by the CO₂ pool at the bottom — that people go down, faint, drown, and are arranged by something that has always arranged them — you may keep that thought, and you may tell the tally-keeper once, quietly, and then let it go.
- **If you see something swimming across the sweetwater, do not hail it and do not haul it out.** Wait for a keeper. Nose up, unhurried, in the dark, you cannot tell what it is, and the description does not promise you that it is an animal.

The falls, the ladders, and the air will kill you far more readily than the sorter will. But the sorter is what you will dream about.

## 9. Being a guest

**Present yourself to the tally-keeper.** Not to whoever meets you at the slot. The tally-keeper holds the knife, the count, the archive, and the autumn forecast; that is the government. Be counted. Ask what you owe. Ask where you may sleep, where you may walk, where you may not, and what the marks in the felt mean.

**Do not correct the name.** The town is Sixteen and there are twenty-three ribs and a twenty-fourth coming. Everyone knows. The name is a promise and the count is a fact and they keep both, and that is the whole philosophy of the place. Being the visitor who points out the discrepancy is a small, permanent failure of understanding.

**Offer to sweep.** They are forty-one people maintaining a six-hundred-person machine, most of it empty and ready, the way a chapel stands empty. It is not a dying town — it is a fairground between fairs, and the beds will fill. But the labour of keeping empty things clean is enormous and unglamorous and never done. Offer to sweep the second level, the street of warming-huts with their flues still connected and their door-flaps still tied. Do that for a week and you will be trusted more than if you saved someone's life.

**Arrive healthy, and mean it.** In a room where a cough is public property, and where the lid may seal forty-one people together for eight weeks, a respiratory infection is not an inconvenience, it is a mass-casualty event. Quarantine before you go in. Wait out anything incubating. If you develop a cough on the approach, turn around and come back another year. Bring medicines — antibiotics, wound care, dental, something for a chest — and be willing to give them, but do not appoint yourself physician.

**Be careful with metal.** There is exactly one iron knife and it scores, never butchers. That is not poverty; that is a rule with a shape. Needles, awls, files, hooks, and salt are the gifts that will be received as gifts. A knife offered casually, or worse a knife used to cut meat in front of people, is an offence you will not see coming. If you bring iron, bring it deliberately, to the tally-keeper, and let them decide what it becomes. And do not buy salvage with steel; you will wreck something.

**Bring things that work in the dark.** Stories you can tell without a book. Songs at conversational volume. String figures, cards, dice, a quiet instrument. A trade you can do by hand. Nothing that needs charging, nothing that needs light, nothing that needs an audience larger than forty.

## 10. Sealed time

If you stay, the lid will knit shut from both edges over an afternoon, with a sound like a scab lifted very slowly, and you will be inside for six or eight weeks.

Prepare for it as a distinct expedition, not as an extension of your visit:

- **Circadian collapse.** No dawn, no dusk, lamp time only. Your sleep will fragment or free-run. Bring a mechanical clock if you need an anchor; otherwise surrender to the town's rhythm rather than fighting your own.
- **Hypercapnia and the air discipline of §3, doubled.** Sleep high. Watch your flame daily. Get to the upper terraces when your head aches.
- **Sensory deprivation.** Eight weeks of near-dark, absorptive quiet, no horizon, no distance. People come out strange. Expect mood swings, vivid dreams, distorted time, a strange intensity of attachment to the people you were shut in with, and an equally strange irritability at them. This is normal and it is shared and it is what story time is *for*.
- **No exit from conflict.** Forty-one people and you. Whatever you say in week three you will still be living inside in week seven. Be slower to speak than you have ever been.
- **You will be told things.** Sealed time is when the culture transmits itself. Some of it will be about the sorter. Receive it as a confidence, not as data. Do not carry it out of the kettle without permission.
- **Decide your death in advance.** It sounds morbid; it is simply the local reality. If you die in Sixteen you will not decay. You will be preserved and you will be placed in ranks, head-upstream, a hand's width from your neighbours, and in thirty years someone will hold a lamp over the side and look at your face. Tell the tally-keeper what you want done. They will not find the question strange.

## 11. The tide

If you are there for it, you will hear it before you see it: grain poured onto a table, sustained, for a night and a day and part of another night. Then six hundred people come down the ladders with their kits, because everyone within four days' walk knows what the noise means.

Prepare for a crowd crush in the dark on grown stairs. Prepare for thirty hours of work — decide *now* that you will take shifts and sleep, because you will not want to. Prepare for hot flues, scorched pipes, and the worst air of your life. Prepare to be one of six hundred and forty-one in a space you had learned to think of as yours, and to be nobody in particular. That is a test of character and it is the actual price of admission.

And prepare for the wake: insolent fat stoats raiding everything, an owl posted at the slot for a month, white fungus blooming on the raft, and a smell in the felt that lasts until spring. Secure your kit. Do not leave food where a stoat can find it; a stoat that has learned about packs is a stoat that will follow you home.

## 12. Leaving

- **Do not travel immediately after a sealing.** You cannot judge distances. Give it days before you cross broken ground, climb, or navigate anything with a drop.
- **Protect your eyes on the way out.** After weeks of near-dark, spring snow will blind you. Slit goggles or dark lenses, worn *before* you emerge, and a slow re-adaptation. Snow blindness on a forty-mile plain is a death sentence.
- **Do not step onto the lid on the way out,** when you are tired and cheerful and carrying too much. That is when people fall.
- **Take your rubbish, all of it.** Nothing you leave will ever rot.
- **You will smell of Sixteen for a long time,** and animals in other worlds will notice. So will customs officers, if the worlds you go to next have such things.

## Compact kit list

Rope and a probe pole. Wand-flags. Winter plain kit; no down. Closed-cell mat and groundsheet. Three pairs of socks. Gloves for sharp shingle. Red-filtered lamp plus a plain candle you know the behaviour of. A gas meter if you trust it. Mechanical clock. Toothbrush. Salt, more than you think. Needles, awls, files, hooks, thread — as gifts, negotiated. Medicines including chest and dental. Slit goggles for the exit. Tether line. Cards, string, a quiet instrument, and six stories you know by heart. Nothing scented, nothing beeping, nothing bright, nothing you cannot carry out again.

## The last thing

Go in understanding what the place is. It is a fresh wound in a young plain, with no soil, no rounded stone, no ruin older than someone's grandmother, and it is already full of stairs to nowhere and drowned doors. Everything is a first draft and everything is already being abandoned downward. The town climbs, the water rises, the plant lays down another rib, and the name stays where it was put.

And the sweetwater is the only unfrozen sweet thing for forty miles, and everything that can move comes to it, and the chamber has beds for six hundred and forty-one people to keep them swept. That is a very good trap. It is not necessary to believe it is one on purpose. It is only necessary to notice that you came a long way, down a funnel, toward warmth and sugar and light in the dark, and that there is a twenty-fourth rib swelling, and that the choice of inside or outside is made in a single afternoon.

Be counted. Sweep the empty street. Keep your hands on the ribs, your flame where you can see it, your salt in your cup, and your feet out of the water.
