# Preparing for the Dew-House

## 1. Understand what you are entering

Not a cave. Caves are gaps. This is an organ — a lung with a stone roof, running one cycle a day for two hundred thousand years, with every surface inside it assigned a job. Nothing in it is spare. Nothing in it is idle. There is no empty niche, no leftover water, no unclaimed square metre, and no sound.

You are about to insert into that arrangement the loudest, wettest, brightest, warmest-breathing, most nutritionally extravagant object that has ever been inside it. Not a predator — the room has predators, and they are quiet and patient and cheap to run. You are something worse: a *leak*. You will shed water, salt, nitrogen, skin, hair, spores, light, noise, and — if you are careless — *ideas*.

The whole of your preparation follows from that sentence. Everything below is either about keeping your leak small, or about keeping yourself alive in a place that is perfectly comfortable for everything except you.

And be clear about one thing before you start buying gear: the room does not need you, has never needed you, and will be measurably worse if you do this badly. If your reason for going is to have been there, don't go. Sit at the swallow-hole mouth on the plateau instead and watch the leaves go down. That is a real and honourable way to visit. If you go in, go in as a surface, not as an event.

---

## 2. Who should not go

Rule yourself out honestly. This is not a place where a manageable condition stays manageable.

- **Any cardiac or vascular condition.** You will be sitting in 36 °C at high humidity for ten to fourteen hours, and then doing seventy metres of hard crawl on the way out while depleted.
- **Asthma, or any reactive airway.** Warm saturated air, fungal spores by the million, midge bodies in every breath, and possibly sulphur gases near the fissure.
- **Anything that impairs sweating** — anticholinergics, some antihistamines, some psychiatric medications, scarring or grafting over large areas of skin. Sweating is your *only* cooling mechanism in there. Radiators don't work when the room is nearly as warm as you are.
- **Diabetes, adrenal insufficiency, anything that makes fluid balance brittle.**
- **Claustrophobia, or a history of panic in the dark or in silence.** More on the silence below; it is not a minor point.
- **Recent illness, dehydration, hangover, or a night without sleep.** Arrive already in debt and the room will collect.

Also: no alcohol for three days before, no caffeine on the day, and go with an empty, quiet gut. Eat a low-residue, low-fermentation meal six hours out. Your intestine is one of the loudest things you own and you cannot switch it off. This is not a joke; people are undone by it.

## 3. Train for the two things nobody expects

**Train for the crawl.** Forty body-lengths — call it seventy metres — of warm scoured limestone with the ceiling on your back. Thirty to sixty minutes each way, in still air at 36-plus, with no draught to dry your sweat. This is the single most physically dangerous part of the visit, and it happens *twice*, and the second time is when you are tired. Find a cave or a crawl-box and do seventy metres with a soft pack on a drag-line, repeatedly, until it is boring. Learn to move on your elbows and the outsides of your feet. Learn to rest *inside* the tube without panic. Learn to read the fluting with your fingertips: the rock is scalloped, and the scallops tell you which way the water used to go. In the dark, that is the only text you will have, and it may one day be the difference between an outlet and a trap.

**Train for the silence.** Book time in an anechoic chamber. Not ten minutes — an hour, then two, then a whole afternoon. Most people, left in true deadness, start to hear their own blood, then their own joints, then things that are not there. Some get vertigo, because we use faint reverberation to know where our body is in space and you will not have any. Some hallucinate. Some panic at forty minutes with no external cause whatsoever.

You need to arrive at the Dew-House already familiar with the sound of your own throat, so that when the room hands it back to you, you recognise it and are not frightened. Practise until your own heartbeat is furniture. Then practise sitting motionless for two hours in the dark, breathing slowly through your nose, without shifting, without scratching, without sighing. This is the actual skill the visit requires. Everything else is logistics.

If you have a companion, practise the local grammar with them: a slow palm along the forearm, a fingertip to the jaw. Not grips. Not taps. Passing-touch. It is the only conversation available to you down there and it will feel, after a while, like more than enough.

## 4. When to go

**Season:** go when the plateau is dry and settled. Not "dry today" — dry for a week, with a stable forecast for the twenty-four hours after your entry.

**The hazard nobody mentions in the poetry:** you enter through a swallow-hole. That passage was made by water and gravel. Water has used it, in one direction or the other, for ten thousand years. Before you commit, spend a day in the crawl with a light, reading it: mud lines, gravel trains, driftwood caught in flutes, the direction of the scalloping, whether there is a high-water mark above your head. If there is any evidence that the passage still takes water in a real storm, **do not enter in a wet season at all**, and understand that you are gambling on the plateau's sky.

**Your rain gauge is inside the room.** The west-wall seep runs for about an hour after real rain falls above. If you arrive and the seep is running, rain has recently fallen on the plateau. If it *starts* running while you are inside, rain is falling *now*. That is your abort signal, and it is a beautiful one — the room tells you, silently, in its own language, that the sky has changed.

Which means the cruellest fact about this place: the seep running is also the crab spawn, the afternoon when the basins boil with larvae and everything comes down from the walls to eat. The most extraordinary event in the room is the one that may kill you on the way out. Decide *now*, at home, in the cold, that you will leave when the seep starts. Do not leave that decision to yourself at the time, because at the time you will want to stay.

**Time of day:** enter in the late afternoon or early evening. You want to be settled, still, and cool-ish before the last hour before dawn. Do not plan to crawl in at midnight; you will arrive hot and loud and blundering into the most active hour.

## 5. Heat: the thing that will actually kill you

Thirty-six degrees at the floor, humid enough to bead on your skin. Understand the physiology precisely, because the poetry will lull you.

Your skin sits around 34 °C. The room is 36. **You cannot shed heat by radiation or conduction — the room is warmer than you are.** The stone will not cool you; there is no cool stone anywhere; the heat comes from below and the floor is the warm part. Your only route out is evaporation, and the air is already loaded with water. You are living on a margin, not a reserve.

Consequences:

- **The room is hotter above.** Warm air rises. Thirty metres of stratification under a dome means the upper storeys may be well into the forties. Do not climb the roots. Do not go up to the rim to look out of the slot. The view is not worth it and there is no rescue from a ceiling.
- **The kiln corner is 44 °C and dry.** Do not go there. Dry heat is briefly more survivable than humid heat, but there is a second reason: a low corner where things die and do not rot may be preserving them by desiccation, or by pooling gas. The fissure breathes sulphur and iron. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and collects in exactly this kind of hollow. If you must approach it, go on your knees with a monitor, and turn back at the first change.
- **Night fog is not the relief you hope for.** When the dome cools and the room fills with fog, the air is *cooler* but *saturated*. Your sweat stops evaporating entirely. The pre-dawn hour is the kindest hour thermally, but it is kind because the air temperature drops, not because you get your cooling back.
- **The crawl out is exertion you cannot avoid.** Budget for it. Reserve for it.

**Rules:**
1. Carry a wrist heart-rate monitor and a core-temperature proxy you can read by touch or by a single hooded glance. Resting heart rate climbing steadily over an hour is your early warning.
2. Set a hard exit time before you enter, and honour it while you still feel *fine*. Heat illness announces itself by being already advanced. If you wait until you feel unwell, you will be doing seventy metres of crawl with a core at 39.
3. Move only in the cool hours. Be a statue in the hot ones.
4. **Stop-sweating, headache, nausea, goosebumps in 36 °C, or confusion = leave immediately.** Not "rest and see." Leave. Confusion in there is fatal, because the exit requires competence.
5. Do not sleep deeply. Doze if you must. Nobody has ever been woken by a room that eats sound.

Bring three to four litres in soft collapsible bladders with electrolytes already dissolved. That is four kilos on your drag-line. It is not optional and it is not negotiable and you must not drink from the basins.

## 6. The water budget, and your own

The room runs on eleven minutes of drizzle a day, eleven basins of a few litres each, and a seep that runs eleven times a year. Every leaf is cupped. Every terrace has a lip. The litter is a sponge four fingers deep. Nothing runs off. Nothing spills. *There is no slack in the water.*

A glass crab carries a spoonful back to its burrow. That is the scale of the economy. You are going to sweat several litres.

So:

- **Bring all your water. Drink none of theirs.** Not filtered, not boiled, not "just a mouthful." Filtering removes the microbes; it does not replace the water. Every sip you take is a sip subtracted from a closed loop that has been balanced since before your species had a name.
- **Your sweat should leave with you.** Wear a smooth, high-wicking, lint-free layer, and periodically wring it into a waste bottle. Do not sit upslope of a basin. Do not lean against the pelt and leave a salt patch four centimetres deep in a living colonial animal.
- **Do not urinate in there.** Not on the litter, not in a fissure, not "a small one." Urine is water *and* nitrogen *and* phosphorus — a fertiliser bomb in a system that has been rationing nitrogen since the Pleistocene. It would change the fungal bloom, the snail grazing rotation, the pelt's growth, in a radius around your berth, for years. Carry sealed soft flasks and gel pouches. Carry them out. This is the least romantic paragraph in this document and the most important one.
- **Same for solids, vomit, blood, spit, and a blown nose.** If you cut yourself, dress it and contain the dressing. If you must sneeze, sneeze into a cloth you keep.
- **Do not eat inside.** No crumbs, no wrappers, and above all no *smell*. In a room where scent is a signature language, the smell of coffee or cheese or a energy bar is a shout in a foreign tongue at maximum volume. Eat on the plateau, before and after.

## 7. Silence: gear discipline and body discipline

Sound dies within a body-length. That is not peace; that is a hunting radius. Several animals in there are listening for exactly the noises you make, and three metres is the range at which a call gets you killed. Nothing in the room has a voice. Voices were culled out. You have one, and it is a liability.

**Gear:**
- No zips. No Velcro. No hard buckles, no snap hooks, no carabiners, no rattling bottles, no crinkling packets, no hard-shell anything. Soft cloth bags, cord ties, knots. Decant everything out of commercial packaging at home.
- No frame pack. A soft drag-bag on a tether, pulled behind you in the crawl, carried by a cloth loop inside.
- Soft-soled cave shoes, well worn-in, silent on wet calcite. Test them wet, at home, on stone, in the dark, listening.
- Nothing that beeps. Every alarm you carry must be **haptic** — vibration against skin. A gas monitor that shrieks in an anechoic room is a dinner bell.
- Test the *whole loaded kit* in an anechoic chamber, wet, while moving. Then fix what you hear. Then do it again.

**Body:**
- Practise slow nasal breathing, low volume. It reduces noise, reduces your thermal plume, reduces midge inhalation, and calms you. It is the single most useful skill.
- Learn to move without joint-clicks: roll the foot from outside edge to inside, keep the knee soft, never lock or snap anything.
- Accept that your gut, your blood, your throat, and your knee will be audible *to you* and possibly to things within a body-length. You cannot fix this. You can only avoid being within a body-length of anything that cares.
- **Do not speak. Not a whisper, not to yourself, not "sorry" when you bump a root.** Especially not on the way out when you are exhausted and relieved. People talk at exits.
- **Do not write in there.** A pencil is a small sound at exactly the wrong range, and graphite is dust. Memorise. Write on the plateau.
- **Do not photograph.** No flash, no shutter, no focus beep, no screen. If you cannot accept a visit with no images, you are not ready for this visit.

## 8. Breath, and the white snake

Here is a piece of luck, and you should understand it because it changes your tactics.

The snake is blind and earless. It reads the warmth of breath at four paces with a pit on each side of its face. In a normal cave you would be a bright thermal beacon against cold rock. **Here, the room is 36 °C and your skin is 34.** At floor level you are almost invisible to it — a slightly *cool* smudge, not a warm one. Your body is camouflaged by the heat that is killing you.

Your **breath** is not. Exhaled air at 37 °C, saturated, in a plume, is the one bright thing about you.

So:

- **Baffle your breath.** A fine mesh or a doubled cloth over mouth and nose does four jobs at once: disperses and cools the plume, filters fungal spores, keeps midges out of your nose and mouth, and muffles the wet click of your throat. Choose something you can breathe through comfortably for twelve hours in the heat. Test it during your crawl training. This is the most valuable single item you will carry.
- **Breathe downward and slowly.** A slow low exhalation into the litter is a very different signal from a panting jet at chest height.
- **Learn the room's convection.** The fissure at the north end exhales at 44 °C and that breath rises. There is a slow floor-level drift toward it and up. Place yourself so that drift takes your plume *away* from you and into the chimney. You can feel the breath on your ankles before you see the crack — use that. It is a wind map you can read with your skin.
- **Stay out of the folds.** The snake lives in the flowstone folds. Never put a hand or a foot into a crevice you cannot see. Never blind-reach for a handhold. In the northern root curtains, where you must part the hanks to walk, move your hands *ahead* of your body, slowly, palms open, feeling.
- **Keep a body-length off the walls** where you can — but the walls are also hung with two-metre sticky filaments from the thread-lamps, so "off the walls" is not a simple instruction. Learn the west wall by its beads: from a distance it looks like a city in fog. It is a net. Walking into it destroys a season's fishing, lights you up in a struggle, and leaves you wearing luminous glue that advertises you to everything for hours.
- **If something large moves near you: stop.** Do not run. Running is noise, plume, heat, and footfalls on snails. Become a cool stone. It is genuinely the best available strategy in a room where you are thermally almost invisible and the predator is looking for a breath.

## 9. Light, and the fact that light is speech

The room's fauna talk in "slow shifts of luminous pattern." Ten thousand blue-green beads on the west wall. Foxfire along the snail roads. You do not know the vocabulary. **Any light you emit is an utterance, and you cannot know what you are saying.** It may be a threat, a mating signal, a lure, or a scream. A headlamp sweeping the wall is a shouted obscenity in a language you have not learned.

- **Primary light: off, stowed, taped.** Navigate by touch, by the foxfire on the floor, by the brown-gold scatter, and by the coin of daylight at noon.
- Carry one micro-light, deep red, hooded, on a cord, pointed only at your own hands, used for seconds at a time. Assume even that is being read.
- **Ban every incidental emitter.** No watch faces, no phone, no glowing zip-pulls, no tritium markers, no luminous compass needles, no reflective tape, no white fabric, no polished metal. Your gear should be matte, dark, and dumb.
- **Do not stand in the sunbeam.** At noon the coin crosses the floor. If you step into it you become the only lit object in the world, on a stage, in a room where almost everything else has evolved to be the colour of a fingernail over a candle. Use the coin briefly and deliberately — it is your one chance to see true colour, check your hands for injury, and orient yourself — then step out of it.
- **Read the green stripe instead.** The figure-eight of chlorophyll is a calendar and a compass. Where it thickens into the waist-high knot is where the coin lingers at the turn of the year. From its shape and thickness you can estimate the season and predict where the coin will be. It is the most informative object in the room and the most fragile. **Never step on it.**

## 10. Moving through six storeys of neighbours

Seventy paces by forty. You will get lost in it. Accept that now, because it sounds absurd and it is true: no echo, no colour, dim light, identical terraces, and a northern third of root curtains you must part like beads. People lose their bearings in twenty metres of this.

**Anchor yourself to four things you can find by touch or by skin:**
- the fissure's breath on your ankles (north),
- the slot's coin and the breakdown hill beneath it (northeast),
- the seep and its little dam (west),
- the stair and its dished treads (south).

Learn the floor-level drift. Count paces religiously. Memorise the terrace pattern outbound as if you will have to return blind, because you will.

**Footfall discipline.** The litter is a sponge four fingers deep and it is *occupied*: fist-sized translucent snails in their hundreds, in slow rotating herds, laying silver roads that other animals follow. Under them, glowing fungal fruiting bodies. Stepping on a snail is a death, a noise, a burst of scent-signature, and the severing of a road other things navigate by.

So: walk on bare flowstone and terrace stone only. Place each foot by feel before you commit weight — toe first, testing, then roll. This will make your progress absurdly slow. Seventy paces may take you an hour. Budget for that and do not resent it. Slowness is not an obstacle to the visit; slowness *is* the visit.

**Parting the roots:** hand over hand, never pull, never break. The hair-thin ones tear at a touch and they are drinking. The thigh-thick ones are thirty metres of somebody's canopy. Move through them the way water would.

**Being touched:** the climbers are warm-blooded, hand-footed, slow, and they touch everything that passes. They will touch you. Their belly skin absorbs water directly — your sweat is a drink of the wrong chemistry, and your skin's oils and salts are not what they evolved to handle. Let them touch your covered forearm. Do not offer bare skin. Do not grip, do not lift, do not restrain, do not pick one up to see its face. If you reply at all, reply with the slow open palm along the forearm that you practised at home. Do not invent gestures. You are a foreigner with no vocabulary; the polite thing is to be brief and legible.

**The basins:** never reach into one. During the spawn, stay well away from all eleven — that is the hour when every larva-eater in the room comes down off the walls, and you do not want to be a warm object standing in the middle of a feeding aggregation.

**Under the slot:** do not berth there. Eleven times a year a rope of white water comes down thirty metres. It is loud for four seconds and then the walls take it, and if you are underneath it you will be under a waterfall with nowhere to go.

## 11. Biosecurity, in both directions

You are a packet of foreign biology entering a filter-feeding room. The pelt combs the air for food. It will eat your skin flakes, your breath's carbon, your lint, your microbiome. Your boot tread may carry a fungus from another world's cave that this room has no defence against; there are precedents in your own world's caves, and they killed millions.

- **Dedicated kit, used nowhere else, ever.** Clothing, shoes, bag, cords. Wash unscented, rinse twice, dry, seal, and air out for weeks — new synthetics and new plastics off-gas, and in a room where scent is language, a fresh neoprene smell is a foghorn.
- **No soap residue, no deodorant, no sunscreen, no repellent, no perfume, no lip balm, no insect spray.** DEET in a closed system with a filter-feeding colonial animal on every wall is a poisoning event. Repellent is not a solution to the midges; a mesh veil is.
- **Shower and rinse thoroughly immediately before entry.** No product. Just water. Clean hair, tied. Trim nails at home so you don't shed clippings.
- **Nothing burns.** No stove, no candle, no lighter, no chemical hand-warmer, no glow-stick. One slot is the whole gas exchange. Your own carbon dioxide is contribution enough.
- **Coming home:** quarantine the kit and yourself. The pelt is an animal-plant colonial thing nobody has classified; the bacterial mat is an chemosynthetic ooze older than animals; there is a heron-sized bird in there and bird guano carries things. Do not carry a souvenir. Not a pebble, not a shell, not a snail, not a scraping. **Nothing leaves that you did not bring.**

## 12. The stair, the hand, and the mason — the one unforgivable mistake

There is a stair of thirty-one steps, dished a finger deep by feet, kept clean for ten thousand years by snails who do not know they are custodians. There is a bowl in a niche. There is a tally panel. There is a handprint behind a skin of calcite.

**Do not touch the handprint.** Do not breathe on it. Your breath's moisture and carbon chemistry will alter the calcite that is preserving it. Do not add a tally mark. Do not take the bowl. Do not "clean" anything. Walk the stair only if you must, on the stone between the dishes, and understand you are adding wear to something that has survived by being licked, not by being walked on.

And then there is the mason: a pale, weak-winged, silent, heron-sized bird that grew up among the old work and builds copies of it. Nineteen knee-high dry-stone niches, three or four stones and a cap, always beside a basin, always with a bowl-shaped pebble inside. It does not use them. It knocks on the capstone and you feel the answer arrive up through your shins.

**This bird is the only copier in the room, and you are the first new thing to happen here in ten thousand years.**

Understand what that means. If it watches you build — a cairn, a tripod, a stone windbreak, a bench, a stack of gear on a flat rock, a knot in a cord tied round a root — it may copy that, and knock, and be answered, and then there will be nineteen of your thing, and then a hundred, and then it will be a *tradition*, and the tradition will be *you*, propagating through a closed culture that has been faithfully repeating a dead people's masonry since before agriculture.

So:

- **Build nothing.** Not even temporarily. Not even a stack of stones to sit on.
- **Leave nothing shaped.** No arranged objects, no cairns, no markers, no scratched arrows, no "temporary" line pegged to a terrace.
- **Do not let it watch you make or unmake camp.** Choose a berth out of its sightlines, do your gear handling under a cloth, and strike everything completely.
- **Do not knock back.** You will feel the tick in your shins and every instinct you have will want to answer. Answering is conversation, is acknowledgement, is *teaching*. Don't.
- **Do not feed it, touch it, or approach its niches.** Look at them from a distance and leave.
- Do a final sweep of your berth by hand for anything you have shaped, dropped, or shed: hairs, threads, a cord, a fragment of cloth, a nail clipping. Count your manifest in and count it out by touch.

Everything else you do wrong in there will heal on a timescale of seasons. This one may not heal at all.

## 13. A workable night

**Late afternoon.** Final weather check on the plateau. Eat. Drink a litre. Toilet. Rinse. Dress. Manifest check. Leave a written plan and a hard check-out time with someone on the surface who has agreed, in writing, **not to enter after you**.

**Dusk.** Crawl. Slowly. Rest inside the tube whenever your heart rate climbs. Expect to lose a litre. Read the fluting with your fingers. Do not rush the last lift into the room; arrive quietly.

**Early evening.** Stand still for ten minutes at the passage mouth and let the room find you before you move into it. Feel the fissure's breath on your ankles. Locate the coin's afterglow, the beads on the west wall, the foxfire on the floor. Establish your four anchors. Then walk — an hour, perhaps — to your berth: bare flowstone, off the walls, out of the folds, not upslope of a basin, not under the slot, in the path of the floor-level drift, within reach of the breakdown hill's shoulder where the night's cool air will land first.

**Night.** Sit. Do not tour. Do not climb. Do not collect. Drink steadily. Wring your layer into the waste flask. Read the pelt with the backs of your fingers: as it grows heavy, the fog is coming. Feel the root filaments. Notice the midges thickening on your face. The room tells the time better than your watch does; learn its tells and use your vibration alarm only as a backstop.

**The last hour before dawn.** Be already still. Be already seated, hands loose, palms up on your knees — cupped and tilted, like everything else in here. Do not be walking when it starts. That is the hour when forty thousand animals are active and the midges hatch in their tens of thousands and everything that eats midges is out, and you do not want to be a moving warm plume in the middle of it.

**The eleven minutes.** Do nothing. This is the entire instruction. It will not strike you; it falls thirty metres and arrives as a mist that settles. Let it settle on your face and your hands and your open palms. The smell will turn to the inside of a melon. The hiss will be less a sound than a change in the air's texture. Do not lift your arms. Do not turn around. Do not reach for anything. Do not narrate it to yourself.

If you weep, weep. Your grief is a few drops of salt in a room with a four-finger sponge of litter; it is negligible. Your *sobbing* is not negligible. Learn, beforehand, the difference, and practise it in the anechoic chamber until you can cry without sound. Many people need to. It is a reasonable response to a room lifting its face.

**After.** Stay still through the drinking and the settling. Doze if you can. Wait for mid-morning, when the floor is dry to the touch and evaporation works again and your body can finally shed heat properly. Use the coin of daylight, briefly, to check yourself — hands, feet, ticks of injury, the state of your kit.

**Then leave, at your hard exit time, while you still feel fine.** Crawl out slower than you crawled in. Count your manifest on the plateau. Sit down. Drink. Write everything, immediately, before the ordinary world overwrites it.

## 14. Emergencies, and an honest account of rescue

There is no rescue. Say it out loud at home.

Seventy metres of crawl means that if you are incapacitated inside — heat stroke, a fall, a broken ankle, a panic attack that will not end — nobody can reach you in time, and nobody can carry you out through a tube you can barely traverse alone. Radio does not go through thirty metres of rock and a shoulder-wide slot. Your companion on the surface can only wait, and then tell someone.

So:

- **Solo, or one partner maximum.** Two people means double the heat, double the breath, double the contamination, and no way to talk. If you take a partner, tether at three metres and use touch. Agree before entry that either of you may end the visit unilaterally, without discussion, by a single agreed touch.
- **Pre-commit to abort triggers**, in writing: seep starts running; heart rate above a threshold at rest; headache or nausea; any confusion; kit failure; a gas alarm; the loss of your bearings for more than a few minutes.
- **If you get lost:** stop, sit, and wait for noon and the coin. It is the only reliable orientation event in the room. You have time. Being lost and still is survivable; being lost and moving is how you walk into a net or a fold.
- **If you cannot get out:** the coolest, stillest place you can find, off the floor if you can safely reach a low terrace, breathing slowly, out of the drift. Ration water. Wait for the pre-dawn hour and try again then. Do not try the crawl hot.

And one more thing, which you should decide about at home rather than in there. In the kiln corner there is a curled pale shape in the dry grey dust that has been in that condition for a very long time and has all its eyelashes. It may be an animal. It may not be. Look at it from a distance, in the brown-gold dusk, and do not approach, do not touch, do not photograph, do not take. Whatever it is, it is a grave, and the room has kept it perfectly, because the room keeps everything.

If you die in there, you will be carried to the kiln corner, or crawl there, and you will keep. Your gear will be a permanent foreign object in a sealed system, and a bird that copies things may learn the shape of your buckle. Write that into your instructions to whoever waits for you. It is better to have thought about it than to have your last contribution to this place be an accident.

## 15. Manifest

Everything soft, silent, matte, unscented, dedicated, and carried out again.

- Soft drag-bag with cloth loop and tether; cloth stuff-sacks with cord ties
- One thin lint-free long-sleeved layer, pre-rinsed; spare absorbent layer for wringing
- Soft-soled cave shoes, worn in, tested wet in the dark
- Thin gloves for the crawl and the roots; bare fingertips available
- Fine mesh breath veil, tested for twelve hours of breathing in heat
- 3–4 L water in soft bladders, electrolytes dissolved
- Sealed waste flasks and gel pouches; a small cloth for spit, sweat, tears, blood
- One square of cloth to sit on, that leaves with you
- Haptic-only gas monitor (CO₂, H₂S, O₂); haptic watch; wrist heart-rate; small thermometer/hygrometer
- One hooded deep-red micro-light on a cord; a mechanical backup you will probably never use
- A thin line for the crawl only, retrieved on exit — nothing laid in the room
- Dressing and a sealable pouch for it
- **Nothing else.** No camera, no notebook, no pencil, no knife you don't need, no food, no repellent, no soap, no fuel, no flame, no souvenirs, no gifts, no markers, no offerings.

Leave the tally panel as you found it. It has thirty-one steps and one hand behind glass and nineteen copies of a bowl, and it does not need your name added to it.

## 16. What it will do to you

Prepare for this too, because it is the part people come home unready for.

You will spend twelve hours in a warm brown-gold dusk in which you cannot hear the world, only yourself. Your blood will be loud. Your throat will click. Your knee will announce itself. You will become aware, in a way you have never been, that you are a wet noisy sack of processes that has been going on this whole time without your permission. In an anechoic room, that awareness slides easily into unreality — the sense that you are a thing being held rather than a person standing. The rock at the entrance is fluted like the inside of a throat. The walls are furred like the inside of an ear. The place breathes in at night and out at the fissure and has done so since before there were names. You will feel swallowed, because in every sense that matters you have been.

And you will be *touched*, constantly, by things that are only saying hello, in a room where nothing has a voice because voices were killed out of it. Then the dome will cool, and the fog will gather, and everything will lift its face, and for eleven minutes it will rain indoors on you as well, because you are a surface in the room and the room does not distinguish.

Most people come out of that wanting to go back, immediately and unreasonably. Some come out unable to bear ordinary rooms — the echo, the shouting, the waste, the water running off things and going nowhere. Some come out ashamed of how much noise they are.

Plan for the aftermath: a few quiet days on the plateau, water, sleep, no company at first, and someone at home who has agreed to let you sit in silence without asking what's wrong. Write your account before you talk about it. Talking will simplify it, and the whole value of the place is that it is not simple.

Go once. Go slowly. Bring your own water, take your own water away, make no sound, cast no light, build nothing, answer no knock, and be still when it rains.

That is the entire art of visiting the Dew-House: to be, for eleven minutes, one more surface shaped to receive, and to leave no evidence that anything so loud was ever there.
