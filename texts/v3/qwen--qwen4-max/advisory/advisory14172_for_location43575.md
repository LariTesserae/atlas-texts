# Preparing for the Long Round

## First, understand what you are visiting

It is not a ruin. Ruins are places where something stopped. This is a place where stopping is not available. The shot is four seconds old and will be four seconds old when your grandchildren are dead, and the men who drilled it went home, had lunch, grew old, and died in the ordinary direction.

You are going to walk into a held breath.

The single most important thing to internalise before you descend is this: **you are the only event in the room.** Your metabolism, your rising core temperature, your melting ice, your fraying nerve, your aging — these are the only things happening below that hatch. Everything else is a sustained note. This is not a poetic framing. It is the operational basis of every safety rule that follows, because it means the room will not cooperate with any of your machinery that works by *changing* something, and it means the room will not notice you deteriorating.

You are sixty percent water in a place that has no water at all. To the Long Round you are not a visitor. You are a spill.

## The four things that will kill you, in the order they arrive

**1. Heat.** Everything is 36.4 °C. Your skin, in ordinary life, sits around 33–34 °C, which is how you shed heat by simply being cooler than nothing and warmer than the air. Here the air is warmer than your skin. There is no radiative sink (the walls are at your temperature), no convective sink (no gradient), no conductive sink (touching the wall feels like touching your own hand), and — this is the one people forget — **no evaporative sink**, because the atmosphere is at exact saturation. Your sweat cannot go anywhere. Every avenue your body has ever used to unload heat is closed simultaneously.

Do the arithmetic before you go, so you respect it. A human of 70 kg stores roughly 245 kJ per degree of core rise. Eleven minutes of moderate work in a sealed suit, plus the exothermic CO₂ scrubber, plus lights and telemetry, is on the order of 200 kJ. That is most of a degree, with no relief valve. One degree is where judgment begins to go, and judgment is the thing you need most at minute nine.

You cannot dump heat. You can only *carry cold in and store heat in it*. That is a battery, not a radiator. Carry two kilos of phase-change mass or ice, pre-cool your body before entry (ice slurry, cold immersion, cooling vest worn to the hatch), and expect half your cooling to underperform, because nothing in this room helps. **Bank the cold and spend it on leaving, not on staying.**

**2. Ammonia.** Twelve bar of ammonia and nitrogen at saturation is not an atmosphere you can survive a breath of. Ammonia is hygroscopic and its dissolution in water is violently exothermic: it will strip the water out of your eyes, your airway, your lungs, and cook them while it does. A breach is not suffocation. It is a caustic thermal burn delivered to the wettest parts of you. Understand also that a soft suit at ambient pressure means twelve bar pushing that gas through every permeable membrane you own.

**3. Sound.** 112 dB, continuous, no attack, no release. By conservative occupational arithmetic, unprotected exposure is measured in *seconds*, not minutes. Plugs plus muffs buy you about thirty decibels. Your skull gives back twenty: the chord is in your jaw and the roots of your teeth, and bone conduction does not care what you put in your ears. Accept that you will hear it. Plan an audiogram before and after, and understand that veterans have a permanent threshold shift they can point to.

**4. Edges.** Every surface in the room is a fresh fracture, four seconds out of the break, arrises like glass. And because everything is at 36.4 °C, **you cannot feel them.** The room reports no texture until you are cut. You will not know your suit is compromised. Design for that: assume you are being abraded continuously, and inspect the suit at the collar with a light and a mirror, not with your fingers.

## Your body, before you go

- **See a dentist.** The chord finds every loose crown, every cracked cusp, every failing bridge. People come up with a toothache that lasts a month.
- **Screen yourself as for a strong MRI, then double the margin.** The bus-bar ring's field is the trellis ninety animals run on. No pacemaker, no ICD, no neurostimulator, no cochlear implant, no insulin pump, no ferromagnetic aneurysm clip, no shrapnel, no iron-oxide tattoo pigment, no permanent eyeliner. If you have ever worked with steel, get imaged.
- **Do not go with a head cold, a hangover, or a night of short sleep.** Dehydration removes thermal inertia; a blocked Eustachian tube at twelve bar ruptures an eardrum in a room where you needed your ears to navigate.
- **Train heat tolerance, but understand what you're training.** Acclimation makes you sweat better, which is worthless here. What it buys you is a slightly lower resting core and a slightly larger plasma volume. It buys you margin, not endurance.
- **Get fit enough to be hauled.** Not fit enough to climb — there is nothing to climb — but fit enough to walk 104 metres of perimeter slowly, twice, without your metabolic rate spiking. Every burst of effort is withdrawn directly from your eleven minutes. **Every comfort in this room is bought out of the same purse.**

## The suit

There are two schools and you should choose knowingly.

**One-atmosphere hard shell.** No decompression obligation, no narcosis, no oxygen toxicity math, no ammonia at your skin. But eleven bar of crush load across every joint and seal, poor mobility, no tactile contact with anything, and heavy insulation that makes your core climb faster. First visit: take this one. You will see less and feel nothing and come home.

**Ambient-pressure soft suit at twelve bar.** Thin, mobile, and you can put your palm flat on a dust shelf and feel it push back like a drumskin, which is the reason people go. The costs: helium-based mix (roughly 3–4% oxygen, a little nitrogen for HPNS damping, balance helium — do the partial pressures yourself, do not trust a table from another universe), a real decompression obligation that the four-minute hatch lock does *not* discharge, and a permeation problem that will find you eventually. You need a chamber and a schedule at the collar. The people who go this way have the best stories and the worst audiograms.

**Materials, whichever you choose:**

- **Banned:** copper, brass, bronze, zinc, anything galvanised, silver-bearing brazes. Ammonia eats copper alloys by stress-corrosion cracking and it will do it at a fitting you cannot inspect.
- **Banned:** fluoroelastomer seals (the usual high-spec O-ring). Ammonia and amines attack them. Use butyl, EPDM, PTFE.
- **Banned:** polycarbonate visors. Ammonia crazes them. Bring laminated glass.
- **Outer shell:** aramid cut layer over an impermeable metal-foil laminate over the bladder. Positive pressure inward-to-outward, closed circuit, **zero venting** — you cannot ask this room to accept your exhaust.
- **No closed conductive loops anywhere on you.** Not a bracelet, not a wire-reinforced strap, not a coiled comm line, not a camera body. In that field, a loop moving through it is a heater, and you have nowhere to put heat. Break every ring you wear.
- Titanium and 316 stainless fasteners only. Nothing ferrous, nothing that can become a projectile.

Oxygen and scrubber sized for **thirty minutes**, three times your visit. Not because you plan to stay, but because the exit is where things go wrong.

## The clock, and the person who holds it

Nothing in that room can tell you the time. There is a clock on the wall at 04:17:22 with its second hand trembling between two marks, moving constantly, arriving never. Do not look at it. People look at it and lose four minutes.

**The principle: anything sealed inside your own atmosphere still has a future. Anything exposed to the room does not.** Your watch works if it is against your skin inside your shell. Your electronics may not survive the field regardless. So:

Go with a tender who **does not enter.** They stand at the hatch with an ordinary watch in ordinary air, holding a 60-metre aramid tether knotted every metre, clipped to your full-body harness — full-body, so they can haul you out unconscious without strangling you.

Agree the protocol at the surface, out loud, in air where words stay said:

- One tug at six minutes: *turn around.*
- Two tugs at eight: *walk to me.*
- Continuous haul at ten, **regardless of your protest.**

That last clause is the whole safety case. Hyperthermia removes your judgment before it removes your consciousness, and this room removes your desire to leave before that. You will argue. You will have excellent reasons. Write the haul rule down and sign it and give the tender permission to ignore you, because the version of you at minute nine is not the person who signed.

Do not go alone. Do not go with a tender who likes you enough to negotiate.

## Getting around in a place with no depth

Three lux, amber-grey, sourceless, directionless, **no shadows anywhere.** Edges are brighter than the volumes they bound. Your visual system evolved to read shading and it will be given nothing. Expect the Ganzfeld effects: vertigo, nausea, drifting hallucination, the certainty that the room is a flat grey wall two metres away. It is not. It is fourteen metres to the crown and packed with life.

- **A torch is nearly useless.** It cannot cast a shadow here. Bring one anyway for your own suit inspection at the collar.
- **Bring a stick.** 1.2 m, non-conductive, non-magnetic, blunt, wrist-lanyarded. You will not feel temperature or texture, but you will feel *resistance*, and mechanical impedance is honest. Sweep it like a blind traveller. This is how you find the walls without finding them with your gloves.
- **The tether is your only map.** You cannot leave a mark: no chalk, no cairn, no breadcrumb. Step and the flour closes without a seam. Count knots by hand.
- **Learn the nine heights before you descend.** The shelves are the pressure nodes — that is *why* the dust hangs there, and it is also why the quiet lives there. The loud places are between the shelves. The lowest plain sits around chest-to-chin height for most adults. So: the room's ground floor is at your face, and the survivable sound is at the shelves, and you cannot have quiet and sight at the same time. Veterans walk in a slight crouch with their ears at the first shelf and their eyes in the flour. Crouching costs metabolic heat. Choose.
- **Do not put your head through a shelf casually.** It parts and closes with no seam — including around your visor. You will be inside an opaque grey solid, at three lux, with no depth cues, breathing on a clock. If you want to see the storeys, push your arm through first, from below, and let your eyes adjust to the layer resolving.
- **The famous view arrives late.** From the hatch the room reads as a solid grey block with faint stratification. It resolves when you move, and by minute nine or ten you stop noticing that you are navigating by loudness — which is exactly when the tender hauls. Accept this. Most people leave at the moment it opens. Some go twice. Do not go twice in a week.

## Conduct: the list of things not to do

You cannot write here. **You can only erase.** Every harm available to you is a subtraction.

- **Do not lean on the back face.** The stone re-cements as fast as the cutter breaks. The healed are half-visible because they migrate at exactly the closing rate. You do not. Rest against that wall long enough and you will be buried at the speed rock closes, and you will not be one of the healed, because you are the only thing here that can die.
- **Do not sit, kneel, or lie down.** The floor is a fan of angular blocks standing on edge. Kneeling shreds a suit.
- **Do not cross the bus-bar ring.** It runs around the waist of the room on ceramic insulators and it divides the centre from the margin. Ask, before descent, where it sits relative to the hatch and whether there is a sanctioned crossing at the heel where it drops to clear the compressor. Do not improvise a crossing. Do not step over it. Do not drape your tether across it.
