# A Briefing for The Sink

Read this twice: once while packing, and once on the plain, walking in, with an hour of yellow grass to go.

---

## 1. Decide honestly whether you should go

Some people should not visit the Rows, and it is kinder to know it in advance.

**If you are sensitive to nickel, do not go.** Roughly one person in six is, and many don't know it until they spend a week in a place where the dust is ten per cent metal by weight and the local mushroom is a delicacy. The Stand is a hyperaccumulator; that is the entire economic basis of the town. There is green ash on the drying racks, green salt in the leach house, grey-white bloom on the cathodes, and a fine bright thread of hot metal in the air that you will stop noticing on the second day and should not stop noticing. If you have ever had a rash from a watch back or a cheap earring, this is not your trip.

**If you have a cough, postpone.** This is the single most important line in this briefing. A chest cold makes you a machine for delivering mechanical stimulation to the walls of the room you sleep in, at night, for hours, in a town where noise closes rooms. It also makes you a plague vector in a population of two hundred and thirty-one with one cart a week. Arrive healthy or don't arrive.

**If you snore, say so when you write ahead.** The guest crotch at Row E is framed in dead heartwood and the living wood has closed around the joints. You will be sleeping inside a wall that listens. There are arrangements — a different berth, a felt-collared bunk, a night on the rim — but they must be made in advance, and being told about it at midnight by a neighbour who has been awake since ten is a bad beginning for everyone.

**If you cannot climb, or cannot trust yourself on damp plank at height, come anyway but stay low.** Thirty-four metres of stair, a gallery thirty feet up in the crotches, and above that a lace of rope and drawn wire. The floor level has the retort house, the drying shed, the drawing bench, the undercroft, and most of the conversation. Nobody will think less of you. The canopy is not the point.

**If you need noise to be well — if silence makes you anxious, if you hum, if you talk to yourself, if you cannot sit still without drumming something — think very carefully.** There is a place for that here, and it is on the rim, and it is a job, and it pays. More on this later.

---

## 2. When to go

The town keeps three calendars and you should learn all three before you arrive, because everyone will use them interchangeably and assume you follow.

- **The nine-day campaign cycle.** The furnace runs every ninth day. Between campaigns the basin is at its quietest and coldest and most itself. On campaign day there is a soft roar you feel in your sternum, the retort house light goes faintly orange on the pale wood, the whole bowl smells like a struck match, and children are allowed to stay up. Ask which day of the nine you are arriving into. Plan to be there for one.
- **The eleven-day round.** The Clearers walk it with saws and long chisels, and the Balance Book gets its kilograms and its millimetres. This is the town's heartbeat and its politics. If you can attach yourself to a round for two days you will understand more than a month of wandering will teach you.
- **The Thursday cart.** The outside week, kept for trade. Glass, sulphur, grain, lamp oil, boot felt, die steel in; wire, cathode, green salt out. Thursday is the day for arrivals, departures, letters, and news.

**Come in autumn if you can.** The leaf comes off, the racks fill, the fog river starts running, the cistern fills, the ledger goes black, and washing is generous. Spring is second best: sap runs acidic, the leach is live, and if the flush is on you may be allowed to watch the pollination crews go up with their brushes and their wire tags and their crossing plan, which is the strangest and best thing humans do here.

**Do not come in July.** Nothing falls out of the sky in July. The condensers run off the cell-bank's waste chill and wring a trickle out of air that has to be persuaded, and everyone is careful, and the ledger is red. A guest in July is a mouth in a drought. If you must go in summer, bring water — actually bring it, in a soft bladder, on your back, up the stair — and offer it before you offer anything else.

## 3. Arrive from outside, on foot, in daylight

Do not materialise inside the basin. Not in an avenue, not in a void, not politely in the guest crotch. Two reasons, and both are serious.

The first is that arrival is often loud, and loudness here is not rudeness, it is *load*. Your footprint on this town is measured in millimetres of encroachment and written in a book that goes back to the year Three. Do not begin by making an entry.

The second is that a stranger appearing without warning in a lit avenue will frighten someone, and a frightened person shouts, and a shout is a structural act. You do not want your first contribution to the Balance Book to be a stranger's alarm.

So: come down on the plain, upwind, a kilometre or two out, in mid-morning, and walk. It takes an hour and it is meant to. The grass is yellow at the tips, the limestone shows through like knuckles, the fog lies in the low places. **Those low places are the danger.** This is karst country; the Sink is not necessarily the only hole in it, and fog in a hole looks exactly like fog on a flat. Walk in daylight, keep to the cart ruts, and take a bearing on the turbine — three blades turning at the speed of a slow clock — and on the thread of smoke if there is one. If there is no smoke, it is not a campaign day, and you have learned something.

**Never cross the plain in fog, and never at dusk.** Dusk is when the river comes: the day's warm air lifts off the wood, the cooled air slides down the slope, and for forty minutes cold fog spills over the rim in a slow cataract. On the plain you will be inside it. The rim of the Sink is not dramatic. Two centuries of rain have rounded it to a lichen-crusted lip you could step over without noticing, and then there are thirty-four metres of nothing. People have died being reasonable about this.

Stop at the rim. Wait to be met. There will be someone whose job includes watching for you, and being met at the top is the correct beginning. Let them take you down.

The stair starts in rock worn into shallow bowls by felt boots and halfway down becomes a root the width of a road, with treads the tree now grows itself, thickening under each footfall, offering the next step down like a hand. Take it slowly. It is damp. Felt on damp limestone is treacherous, and felt on damp wood is only slightly better. Use the wall. Do not hurry to be polite.

At the bottom the air changes: a degree warmer, wetter, absolutely still, cold stone and crushed leaf and sugar and that bright thread of metal. Notice it. You will never notice it again after the first ten minutes, and it is worth having noticed.

## 4. Pack so that when you shake your bag it says nothing

This is the practical test. Lift your packed bag and shake it hard, next to your ear. If it clicks, ticks, rattles, clinks, or crunches, repack it. Wrap every hard thing in cloth. Tape buckles. Nothing loose, nothing rolling, no tin of pills half empty.

**Soft pack, not a case.** No wheels — wheels rumble, and a hard case bangs down thirty-four metres of stair. You are carrying it the whole way; there is a funicular but it is forty metres long, rubber-tyred, strolling pace, the only vehicle in town, and children are told not to ride it twice in one day because of the noise. You will walk. Pack for that.

**Felt soles.** Mandatory indoors, customary out. They import boot felt, which means felt is precious, which means arriving in the right shoes is both courtesy and gift. Bring two pairs so one can dry — nothing dries here, but one can be *less damp* than the other. No hobnails, no lugs, no hard heel, no metal cleat, nothing that knocks.

**Wool and fleece and soft cotton. No crackling shell jacket.** This surprises people. Your modern waterproof is a noise machine; every arm swing is a dose. Wool is quiet, warm when damp, and local in spirit. Bring more layers than you think, and a hood, and accept that on day three every garment you own will be permanently, uniformly damp and will stay that way until you leave. Nothing here is ever wet. Everything here is always damp.

**Pencil, not pen.** Ink runs. Paper goes limp. Bring paper that tolerates damp if you intend to write, and a pencil, and no clicking mechanism on it.

**A silent lamp.** They use green glass — nickel makes glass green; the town's light is coloured by its own ore. A shutter lamp is best, because lamps are a signalling channel here, not just illumination. Avoid a switch that clicks; you will use it forty times a day.

**Gloves**, for rope and for the retort house. **A dry bag** for the things that must stay dry. **Two of everything you cannot afford to lose to damp.**

**Leave behind:** anything with gears, anything with a motor, anything with a fan, anything with a mechanical shutter, anything that chimes, ticks, alarms, or whistles. Leave the drone absolutely — rotor wash is vibration and a drone is, in the terms of this place, an animal that moves on its own, and the town has spent two hundred years making itself the only animal here. If you have equipment that must run, ask whether it can sit in a felt-collared shed on the rim. The rim is where they keep the loud things: the turbine, the cart road, the shouting sheds. The basin is the quiet zone. Understand that geography and everything else follows.

## 5. What you must not bring, in order of how badly it would go

1. **A cough, a fever, a rash, a diarrhoea.** Quarantine yourself before you come. Two hundred and thirty-one people, one cart a week.
2. **A stowaway.** There was a rat once, in the year One-Fifty. It gnawed a cord line and scratched in a wall void at night and the void began, measurably, to close. The response is recorded in eleven pages and is the closest thing this town has to a war. Turn your bag out on the plain. Check the seams. Check your boots. Do not bring a dog; there are no dogs.
3. **Any living plant, seed, fruit, cutting, bulb, or soil.** The Stand is one body. No seams a knife can find, one fused organism, four hundred paces by two hundred and sixty. There is no genetic firebreak anywhere in it. Whatever you carry on your coat can go through the whole thing. They pick rust off by hand. Scrub your boots, wear clothes that have not been in a garden or a forest or a grain store, and declare everything.
4. **Pollen.** If the flush is on, the crews are working to a crossing plan drawn up in the ledger room in winter: which bole to which, which traits, which rows kept pure, flowers bagged and wire-tagged. Stray pollen on your sleeve is a year's work ruined. Do not bring honey. Do not bring cut flowers. Do not touch a flower.
5. **Untreated wood.** No wooden instrument case, no walking stick from another world's forest, no packing straw, no wicker hamper. Borers travel in timber.
6. **A musical instrument, a bell, a whistle, a firework, a firearm.** Obvious, and yet.

## 6. Gifts, and the account

They import glass, sulphur, grain, lamp oil, boot felt, and steel for the dies. Bring from that list and you cannot go wrong. Three hundred glass jars in the cell racks means breakage means glass is always wanted. A die blank of good steel is a small treasure. Felt is currency.

Better than the list, if you can manage it: **reading glasses**, an assortment of strengths. This is a town of ledgers, wire tags, brush work, rust-picking, and bearings oiled twice a week by an apprentice with a long-spouted can. Presbyopia here is a slow civic tragedy. **A hand lens.** **Good rag paper and waterproof ink.** **A silent hygrometer.** These land harder than anything expensive.

Expect to be entered in a book. Your water, your grain, your lamp oil, your berth, and — if you are unlucky or careless — your noise. This is not surveillance; it is the only way a maintained balance stays maintained. Ask to see your account. Ask what you owe. Being in debt here is ordinary and legible; being unaccounted is the rude thing.

Do not tip. Do not pay for a kindness with something loud.

## 7. The law, written into your hands

The Stand thickens under mechanical stimulation. Wind, touch, vibration — anything that shakes it — and it lays down shorter, denser, stouter cells and closes its voids. Every tree does a version of this. Here it has been bred for and lived with for two centuries, and it means: **noise closes the rooms.** A dropped anvil in Row C thickens the walls of Row C by a hand's breadth over eighteen months. A season of hammering fills a gallery. In the year Twelve a forge was set up at the north end and the north end ceased to exist.

Understand the shape of this. It is not that one loud sound destroys anything. It is *dose*. It accumulates, it is measured, and it is somebody's problem in eighteen months. Your footsteps are a vote in an argument you have not been briefed on.

So, habits to break before you arrive — practise them at home for a week, seriously:

- **Set things down; never drop them.** Two hands on everything. Practise putting a cup down without the click.
- **Never slam, never bang, never let a door go.** Hinges are oiled weekly by hand; reciprocate.
- **Stop drumming.** Fingers on a frame, heel on a plank, knee bouncing under a table, pen clicking, knuckles on a doorpost. All of it is load.
- **Stop tapping the tree to see what it sounds like.** Everyone wants to. Don't.
- **Do not lean.** Leaning is touch, and touch is stimulus, and a favourite leaning spot becomes a swelling.
- **Do not whistle. Do not hum while leaning on something.**
- **Do not clap.** If you are moved by something — and you will be — ask what people do instead. There is always something. Learn it before you need it.
- **Do not raise your voice to bridge distance.** Walk closer. This is the whole reason the town is full of murmuring and empty of shouting, and the whole reason arguments here are conducted low, intense, and face-to-face at a range that visitors find sinister and locals find ordinary. Nobody is being menacing. They are being structural.

**Learn the emergency signal first, before anything else.** This is the part people skip and it is the part that kills. In a town that cannot shout, how do you call for help? They signal with lamps and with cords — a taut wire network through the canopy, tugged in a code, silent, constantly re-strung because the tree keeps swallowing the anchor points. Ask, on your first hour, for the distress code and the nearest cord to your berth, and practise the pull. Then train the scream out of yourself, because if you fall your body will want to scream and screaming is both useless and expensive. Reach for the wire. Shutter the lamp.

**And do not cut.** The other failure mode. Cut too little and the rooms close; cut too much and the tree answers injury the way it answers noise, and the rooms close faster. Both failures are the same failure. If you see new wood encroaching on your doorway, do not helpfully shave it. Tell someone. A child, ideally — they are taught to listen for loading limbs and they are taken seriously. Someone will write it in the book and a Clearer will take a hand's-breadth off it in the morning. That is the system. Use the system.

**If you need to shout, there is a shed for that.** Out on the rim, dead-wood chambers isolated from the living mass by a collar of felt and air, where a hired person sits two hours a day and makes noise at a chosen limb through a horn. Acoustic forestry. If a beam needs to grow heavier on one side, you yell at it for a season. It is slightly disreputable and it pays well. Ask for a shift. Take your homesickness, your grief, your laughter, your ridiculous joy at being here, up the stair and out to the rim, and put it into a limb that needs it, and get paid in wire. There is no better arrangement anywhere in any world for what a visitor actually feels.

## 8. Water and fire

**Water.** There is no stream, no spring, no pond. A wet seam the width of a hand at the northern corner where snails live is the only natural liquid in the basin. Everything else comes out of the air: nine hundred square metres of nickel-bronze mesh in the canopy, angled to the prevailing wind, beads to wires to gutters to downpipes to a living tank at the base of Row D, forty thousand litres, cool, faintly sweet, tasting of the wood. Two to four cubic metres on a good fog night. Nothing in July.

Ask for your allowance on the first day and stay under it. Do not wash your hair. Sponge. Do not ask for a bath; the question is embarrassing for both of you. Do not drink from the leaf-axil tanks or the flutings — that water stands for a week and evaporates and smells of fermentation, and it is not for you. Do not touch the mesh. It is the watershed, it is taut wire that carries signals, and it is nickel-bronze against your bare hand.

The cistern water may disagree with your gut for three days. This is normal; drink it anyway; do not ask for something else. It is the thing they are proudest of.

**Fire.** You are a guest inside one wooden body, in a basin with forty cubic metres of water and no pump, along with the drying racks and the leaf litter and the lamp oil. Fire here is not property damage, it is the end of the town. So: **no smoking, anywhere, ever.** No stove, no candle, no cooking of your own. Declare every lighter and match at the rim and expect them to be held for you. Use their lamps. Matches work here, but you have to want it, and you should not want it.

**And ask about the undercroft before you go down.** Three hundred glass jars of acid leachate with cathodes thickening in them means gas coming off the plates, in a dry-lined hollow in a living tree, lit by lamps. They will have ventilation and rules and they will be old, tired, obvious rules that everyone forgets to tell visitors. Ask what they are. Never carry a flame into the cell room. Never.

During a campaign, do not linger in the retort house because the light is beautiful on the pale wood. That fine bright thread of hot metal is not a smell to grow fond of. Ask what the campaign-day rules are and follow them exactly, including the ones that seem fussy. Gloves, and wash after — and yes, washing costs water, and that is a real tension, and the answer is to bring your own cloths and ask what may go into the litter and what must be carted out.

---

## 9. Moving around a town that moves

The addresses are the planting plan: Row C between Eleven and Twelve; Row F at Nine, upper. The nurseryman's ledger is in the map room and the street names have not changed in two hundred years, though the streets themselves have moved four metres east. Nine rows still readable out of twelve.

**Get this year's survey. Not last year's.** The surveyor redraws the map annually; avenues narrow and widen, dwellings get swallowed and are re-asked-for thirty paces on, and the switchboard slides steadily north-east because the tree grows toward the warmth of the cables and the cables must follow the tree. The cord routes migrate too. A code you learned on Tuesday may reach a different room by next month. Ask. Locals ask each other constantly and think nothing of it.

Navigate by row and number, counting boles. Do not navigate by feel; a lit avenue between two workshops can be a full minute of nothing at all — pale wood going up and up into the dark, your boots and the drip — and they all look like that.

**Never go up alone, and never at night in your first week.** The emptiness is real. Nine hundred cubic metres of living tissue per person. If you fall in a void at three in the morning you will not be found by accident and you cannot shout. Tell someone where you are going, every time, even when it feels absurd. Especially then.

Don't ride the funicular twice. Don't pluck the wires. Don't lean on the cord network. Learn where the current switchboard is.

## 10. Sleeping, which is the hard part

Visitors cannot sleep. You will lie in the guest crotch at Row E and hear the drip, and the tick of cooling wood, and the far soft roar of the blower, and the enormous rustle of a thing the size of a hill adjusting itself, and you will think: *it's so loud here.*

It is. Your ears will spend three nights recalibrating a noise floor they have never had to find before. **Do not plug them.** You need to hear the wood — a limb loading overhead is information, and a child here is taught to listen for it and to tell her father, who writes it in the book. Blocking your ears makes you deaf to the one thing that matters and does not stop you hearing your own heartbeat anyway.

Plan to be useless for three days. Do not schedule anything important on arrival. Bring something silent to do at three in the morning: a pencil, damp paper, a book. Lie there and let the recalibration happen. On the fourth night it will turn into the most extraordinary quiet you have ever been inside, and you will understand why people from the Rows go to the coast and come back grey-faced after two days.

They have a word for the outside. **Clatter.** Learn it and use it and mean it.

## 11. Among two hundred and thirty-one people

**There is no privacy.** Still air, pale wood throwing sound back and forth, lamps scattered absurdly wide. Assume everything you say at conversational volume is heard three avenues away, and that it will be repeated with attribution. You will overhear everything too. Do not react to what you overhear.

**Do not take a side on the rate.** The balance is not natural; it is maintained, at considerable effort, and it is the entire subject of local politics. Growth and harvest are the same number in two columns: what the Clearers take out goes to the racks and the retort and the furnace, so the ore supply *is* the pruning waste. Every supper table is arguing about kilograms and millimetres. You will be invited to have an opinion. Decline, warmly, and ask a question instead: *what did the last round show?* That is the polite move and it is also the interesting one.

**Do not propose improvements.** Every visitor wants to solve the water with a bigger mesh or a pump. A pump vibrates. They have thought about it for two hundred years. Ask what has been tried; the answer will be long and will include things you would never have thought of, and you will be respected for asking.

**The charter clause.** They are the fauna. They pollinate with brushes, carry seed, cull suckers, clean the axil tanks, thin the litter, pick the rust; everything an animal would do, the people do. It is stated plainly and everyone finds it funny and nobody has ever proposed amending it. You may laugh. Do not improve the joke.

**The rat is not a joke.** Eleven pages. Closest thing to a war. Treat it the way you would treat a war.

**And do not tell them it is beautiful more than once.** It is beautiful — four hours of sun making the bowl into a lantern, pale bark throwing light until the vault glows like the inside of a shell, then the western lip cutting it off, the blue coming, the green lamps one here and one far off there. Say it once, quietly, to someone who is not busy. Then ask about the millimetres. They live in the millimetres. The beauty is not news; the encroachment is.

## 12. What to actually do with your time

- **The map room.** The old surveys stacked in order since the year Three. Flip the corners and watch the town breathe: two hundred frames of a very slow film, a dwelling swallowed and re-asked-for, the switchboard creeping north-east. This is the single best thing to do here and it takes an afternoon and costs nothing.
- **The nurseryman's ledger.** The original planting plan in his handwriting. Twelve rows of forty, grafted stock, a commercial speculation in metal-bearing leaf. Look at the handwriting of a man who did not know.
- **A campaign night.** The blower felt more than heard, the smell of a struck match, the retort light going orange on the wood, children up late. Stay up with them.
- **The river, from the rim, with a guide.** Dusk, the cold cataract spilling over, forty minutes of the bowl filling from the bottom like a bowl under a tap, then down to watch the collectors go along the gutters with their lamps while the mesh begins to tick.
- **A Clearers' round.** Two days of it. Carry something. Say little.
- **The mushrooms.** The only food in the world that tastes of nickel and is loved anyway. Eat them, and like them, and do not eat them at every meal for a month.
- **A shift in a shouting shed.** As above. Bring everything you have been holding.

---

## 13. Leaving

Buy **wire, cathode, or green salt.** Those are the exports; they have been through the fire; they are finished and theirs to sell. Do not take a leaf — a leaf is ore. Do not take a seed — a seed is a year's crossing plan. Do not take a cutting, and if you find yourself wanting one, sit with that want for a while before you voice it, because you are asking for a piece of a body that two hundred and thirty-one people are inside.

Clean your boots and your bag before you go up, and again before you enter your next world. You are carrying nickel dust and possibly spores and possibly pollen. Do not be the way a hyperaccumulating clone with no predators arrives somewhere it can grow.

Climb out slowly. The root will offer the treads; let it. At the lip, stop and turn around and look, because the rim is not dramatic and this is the only angle from which the whole thing reads at once.

Then budget for the clatter. If the Rows does this to its own people in two days at the coast, it will do worse to you. **Do not go straight from the Sink to a city, a machine, a crowd, or a vehicle.** Give yourself two days of somewhere dull and moderately quiet before you re-enter the loud world, and expect the loud world to feel like an assault, and expect that feeling to fade, and be a little sad when it does.

---

## 14. One last thing

The Rows is not a place that was built. It is a place that is being negotiated, and has been for long enough that the negotiation has worn grooves, and the grooves are what everyone calls home. The form stopped changing long ago — the flutes, the buttresses, the great slow curves where a bole meets the plate are as finished as the limestone lip above. What changes is detail, every year, millimetre by millimetre, in a book.

You are not visiting a town in a tree. You are visiting a tree that has been persuaded to contain a town, and the persuasion is renewed every eleven days by people with saws and long-handled chisels who are arguing about the rate.

Nothing you do there is neutral. Your footsteps, your voice, your dropped cup, your helpful knife, your cough, your enthusiasm — all of it goes into a column, and someone reads the column, and in the morning someone goes up and takes a hand's-breadth off a limb before it decides to become a wall.

That is not a threat. It is the whole reason the place exists, and the reason it is worth an hour's walk across yellow grass to see.

Write yourself into the book, if they invite you.

Do not write yourself into the wood.
