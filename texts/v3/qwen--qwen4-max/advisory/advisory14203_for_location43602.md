# Preparing for Cell 4

## 0. The first decision is whether to go in

Understand what is actually available to you. The vestibule is the whole of the built world here — everything else that was made has been absorbed. That means the vestibule is where every human-legible thing survived: the brass rule, the mechanical interlock, the handrail worn to a groove by hands that stopped coming, and the chart drum with a circle cut into it by a stylus that outlived its ink.

That drum is the artifact. That groove is the surviving record of the experiment, and it is readable, and nobody has come to read it. You could spend a week at 6,712 meters doing nothing but measuring that groove — depth, width, profile, the drum's period, the stylus's bearing force — and you would have done the only work this place is waiting for.

Inside, there is nothing waiting. The cell has no future; it has a present with excellent tolerances. You cannot improve it, you cannot learn from it anything it isn't already saying at the top of its lungs, and you cannot add to it. Every gram you take is a gram it must remake. Every watt you bring is a watt it must absorb.

So: prepare to go in, and then decide honestly whether the reason you have is worth what the going-in costs. Most travelers who prepare properly end up staying in the vestibule. This is not a failure of nerve. It is the correct result of the preparation.

## 1. Understand that the hazards are inverted

Normal expedition planning asks: *what will this place do to me, and how do I survive it?*

Here the question is: *what will I do to this place, and how does it survive me?*

You are a warm bag of roughly forty liters of water, breathing out more water, radiating a hundred and fifty watts, shedding skin and fibers and lint, carrying a lamp. In a room whose solvent is anhydrous ammonia, whose chemistry is wrecked by a drop, whose entire lighting budget is a few hundred watts, and whose mass has been constant to the gram for longer than there have been nations at sea level, you are not a visitor. You are the largest perturbation the room has ever received.

The room is built to un-occur you. It will find your damage on its round, excise it, grow it back to the plate, and the record will show nothing. That is not mercy. That is the definition of what you are here: an event that will be erased.

Plan accordingly. Your fatality risk is real but secondary. The primary risk is that you leave a scar three centuries deep and nobody ever knows, including you.

## 2. Getting to the door

**Arrive low.** Do not translate directly to 6,712 meters. The ambient pressure up there is around 0.44 bar; the oxygen partial pressure will put you on the rock in minutes. Arrive below the sealed adit, acclimatize on the ordinary schedule, and climb.

**Never arrive inside.** There is no interstitial space anywhere in that chamber. It is cubically occupied. Anyone who materializes past the interlock materializes *inside* living structure, and dies there, and takes a piece of the room with them. The interlock is not a formality. It is the room's immune system, and the only legitimate way in is through it, slowly, in the correct order.

**Follow the pipe, not the doors.** Three hundred and ninety-nine doors are healed stone; you will not find them by looking for doors. Find the grey alloy pipe and the marching line of plugged bolt-holes at hand height. The pipe is your continuous thread from the adit to the throat of the spire.

**Do not climb on the pipe.** It is a pressure line, three centuries old, entering rock and never coming out. Do not anchor to it, do not hammer it, do not chip its rind for a sample. A cracked pipe is a wound the room cannot un-occur on its own schedule.

**Do not open the adit. Do not open the healed cells.** Whatever the other three hundred and ninety-nine answered, they answered it by closing.

**Park your ship far below and dry.** It is full of air and water and cooking smells. It has no business on this ridge.

## 3. The two-kit discipline

Establish a hard boundary between the wet world and the dry room. The boundary is the outer hatch.

**Wet kit** stays outside forever: boots, rope, down, gloves, harness, glacier gear, your flask, your food. All of it is saturated with your breath and the mountain's snow. Rope alone holds enough absorbed water to ruin a canopy.

**Dry kit** travels from your home world in a hermetic case and is opened only inside the vestibule after the vestibule has been swept dry. It has never touched weather.

Bake everything before you leave: vacuum oven or dry-nitrogen purge, days not hours, at whatever temperature the materials tolerate. Then seal, and weigh.

**Contraband, absolute:** water in any form, including ice, snow, hydration bladders, hydrogel, wet wipes, aqueous glue, saline, water-based fire suppression, and the electrolyte inside your oxygen sensors (those are little jars of caustic water — double-can them or use a dry sensor). Also: cotton, wool, paper, cardboard, wood, leather, most open-cell foams, nylon. All hygroscopic. All of them will quietly carry a gram of the wet world into the room and drop it on a leaf.

**Contraband, materials:** copper and its alloys in any place where moisture could ever reach them. Note that the room itself uses brass freely — dry ammonia leaves brass alone. Wet ammonia eats it. Your sweat does not merely kill a hand's-breadth of leaf; it starts stress-corrosion in the brass frame, the comparator's fittings, the bearing housings. One drop is a chemical event with a hundred-year tail.

**Prefer mechanical to electrical.** Every watt you carry becomes heat the room must swallow. A wind-up camera, a mechanical gyro, a depth gauge, a graphite stylus on a baked metal plate. No LEDs on your chest. No status lights blinking in a room that has been dark for three hundred years.

**Weigh in, weigh out, to the gram.** Make a manifest and treat it as scripture. If you come out lighter, you left something. If you come out heavier, you took something. Both are wounds, and the only honest thing you can do about either is write it down.

## 4. The suit: five problems, no bailout

There is no breathable gas anywhere inside the loop, at any point in the sequence. Six point four bar of ammonia is not an atmosphere you can filter, hold your breath in, or tough out. It is an airway burn. **There is no bailout.** Redundancy is your only safety strategy: two independent loops, or one loop you can repair by feel, in the dark, with gloves on.

**Pressure.** At 6,712 meters the lock sits near 0.44 bar; the cell sits at 6.4. That is 5.96 bar of differential across a wardrobe-sized hatch — call it forty tonnes of force. You cannot open the inner hatch until the lock is equalized, which means **the lock must fill with the cell's own ammonia.** There is no version of this visit where you avoid the gas.

Therefore: an ambient-pressure suit, not a rigid one. A rigid atmospheric suit would be crushed or would need to be so bulky it could never fit sixty centimeters of shoulder-brushed corridor, and its thrusters and elbows would break living struts. Go soft, go slim, and accept the decompression debt.

**Gas.** Closed-circuit, heliox, oxygen partial pressure held around 0.4 bar — roughly six percent oxygen at depth. Nitrogen at 6.4 bar will narcotize you badly and load you with hours of decompression. Helium loads fast and leaves fast.

**Zero vent.** This is the hard part and the part everyone gets wrong. A conventional rebreather dumps overpressure. Your scrubber makes water as a byproduct of absorbing your CO₂. Your loop is therefore full of humid gas, and every bubble you release is a killing.

So build a **no-vent loop**: fixed-volume torso, pump-back compressor, receiver cylinder. As ambient pressure falls during your decompression, you do not vent your expanding lung — you pump it back into your own bottle. Water condenses into a contained sump you carry out and weigh. Nothing leaves you. Nothing enters you.

The ethic and the engineering are the same sentence: *the only thing permitted to enter this room is another closed loop.* Be a small, badly-made copy of the thing you are visiting.

**Heat.** This is the constraint that will surprise you. Forty-one filaments at four percent of design is somewhere between a hundred and a thousand watts of total input, and most of that is infrared. You are proposing to bring a second sun.

Worse, the room's gas is dense — several times the density of sea-level air, with something like eight times its volumetric heat capacity. It is a superb heat-transfer medium and it is touching you everywhere. It will strip your heat and deliver it, efficiently, into leaves.

You cannot heat your outer skin. A warm glove flashes the liquor and leaves a white-blue rime — a frost made of your own body, on a leaf that dies of it. Your outer surface must sit at seven degrees, and the room will hold it there for you; the room is a thermostat of enormous authority. Your job is only to stop your heat from reaching that surface.

So: heavy insulation (aerogel, twenty millimeters, and accept that even that leaks tens of watts), plus **a latent heat sink you carry** — a few kilograms of dry phase-change material melting just below seven degrees, double-contained, absorbing your metabolism for the duration instead of releasing it. Not ice. Never ice. Pre-chill it, can it twice, and treat a leak as a mission-ending contamination.

Then reduce your output: move slowly, breathe slowly, keep the visit short. A resting human is a hundred watts. A crawling, frightened, cold human is three hundred. Fear is a thermal contaminant.

**Water.** Pre-hydrate outside; drink nothing in the lock; contain everything. Sweat, breath, the twelve grams an hour your scrubber manufactures, your urine — all of it stays inside the suit and goes home with you on the manifest. Forty minutes is a good exposure limit for a body that cannot drink.

## 5. Light: the one-joule rule

The plants work in the red. Their lamps glow cherry because cherry is what they eat.

**Therefore red light is food.** A red headlamp — the standard courtesy in any dark-adapted human environment — is the single worst light you could bring. You would be feeding a lineage whose mass is constant to the gram, whose governor learned its demand centuries ago and never revised it. You would be adding calories to a closed budget.

**Green is declared useless.** The record says this biology has no use for green light or for green. Green is also where your dark-adapted eye is most sensitive. So: narrowband green, around 510–550 nm, shrouded, pointed only at metal and rock and your own hands. Mask every indicator on your gear.

Understand the cost of this choice: under green light, blue leaves look black. Under the room's own cherry light, blue leaves also look black. **You will probably never see the blue.** The blue in the accounts is a fact you will have to hold in your mind rather than your eye. To see it properly you would have to shine on them the light they eat, and change them. Accept the black. Bring the blue with you as knowledge.

**Budget your photons.** A defensible rule: the total radiant energy you introduce during the whole visit should be less than the room receives from its own lamps in one second. Call it one joule. A five-milliwatt green pinhead for three minutes, total. Everything else is dark adaptation and touch.

**No flash. No video light. No exceptions.** And above all:

**Do not illuminate the comparator.** At the end of the Way there is an optical bench, a lamp, a photographic plate of the master leaf, and a mechanism of mirrors and photocells asking one question: *is this the same?* Stray light on that plate is the most irreversible act available to you in this room. Fog the reference and you have quietly redefined the species. What matches will be planted; what drifts will be incinerated; and the machine will spend the next three centuries breeding faithfully toward your fog. Nobody will ever know. The record will show a perfect circle, worn deeper.

Shield the bench. Approach it in the dark. Read the two letters and the smudge with your fingers on the frame, not with a lamp on the glass.

## 6. The lock sequence

Rehearse this at home until it is boredom. Then do it slowly.

1. Outer hatch. Strip wet kit. Leave it outside or in the lock — it never goes further.
2. Seal outer. Sweep the vestibule with dry nitrogen and vent to the mountain, several cycles. The inner hatch is shut; the vestibule is *outside the loop*, so this is permitted. You are drying the airlock, not the room.
3. Open the hermetic cases. Dress. Seal. Check seals by pressure decay and by sniff — a helium leak test before you left home, and again here if you can.
4. Sweep the lock again, now that you and your kit are in it. You have been breathing in here. Get it out.
5. **Test the interlock by hand.** Gently attempt to open both. Confirm it refuses. Do not force a seized valve; do not wedge anything; do not lubricate a hatch. If a seal looks wrong, look again — it may be a gasket-leaf, grown in the shape of the seal it will replace, and it is on schedule.
6. Equalize from the cell. Never from your own cylinders. A lockful of your nitrogen entering that chamber is a permanent composition change in a room whose gas is identical corner to corner to a fifth of a percent. There is no purge. It would stay forever.
7. **Wait forty-five minutes in the dark.** This does three jobs at once: your tissues load, your eyes dark-adapt, and your heart rate comes down. Use it. Listen through the hatch. Count the carriage.

On the way out, reverse it — and add:

8. **Decompress in the lock.** You are saturated at 6.4 bar the moment you equalize, even if you only stood there. Helium is forgiving but not free. Plan a staged return over an hour, pumping your expanding loop back into your receiver, in a wardrobe-sized box, in ammonia, in the dark, at seven degrees. This is the actual endurance test of the expedition. Not the climb. Not the corridor. This.
9. **Decontaminate inside the lock, before depressurizing.** Your suit will be crusted with white-blue ammoniacal salts — frost made of the room's own liquor by your own heat. That is stolen mass. Brush it off in here, toward the inner sill, so the room's cycle can reclaim it. Try to leave even your frost behind.
10. Find the recovery line before you plan to vent a lockful to the mountain. Six tonnes of liquor did not get here by being wasted; the builders very likely provided a return. If you cannot find it, reduce the lock's free volume with sealed, baked-out rigid ballast so each cycle costs the room less.

## 7. Time: the carriage is your heartbeat

Eleven minutes and four seconds. Six hundred and sixty-four seconds. Memorize it. Set nothing else by any other clock.

The Way is twenty-six meters long and sixty centimeters wide and it is not for you. It exists so the carriage can reach the far bearings, and it is held open by a heated wire that cuts four millimeters an hour and cuts it again the next hour.

**You cannot pass the carriage.** Sixty centimeters, shoulder-brushed both sides, no passing places, no room to turn. So enter behind it, stay ahead of it, and be out before it laps you. Time your entry from the lock by ear: in an anechoic room there is no reverberation, so the clack reaches you as direct sound only, and its loudness is an honest rangefinder. Train this. It is the best instrument you will have.

**The wire is hot and it is cutting.** Never be between it and the far bearings.

**Learn the closure rate.** Four millimeters an hour per side means the corridor narrows about eight millimeters an hour if the wire stops. Sixty centimeters becomes impassable for shoulders in roughly half a day. The carriage is not scenery. It is your lifeline.

**Three abort triggers, all of them the room's own body:**

- **A missed clack.** One missed cycle: begin your exit. Two: move as fast as you safely can.
- **The forty-seven hertz stops.** The flow stops, gradients appear, the achievement begins to unwind. Leave.
- **The cherry glow changes.** If the lamps dim while you are inside, the governor is compensating for you. You are too much. Leave.

Any change in any of the three is your fault and is your cue to go. Do not investigate. Do not try to help.

## 8. Navigation and the mind

Eighty-six decibels with no echo is a sensory environment engineered, by accident, to dissolve a human being.

Your voice goes out of your mouth and stops. Shouting is useless and you will do it anyway, once, and it will frighten you. There are no spatial cues from reflection. There is thirty lux of cherry light and no color. The walls of the Way are cut foliage: uniform, featureless, winding, brushing both shoulders. After twenty minutes your hearing gives up on the roar and the beats become a kind of silence, and in that silence you hear your pulse — the only irregular thing that has entered this room in a very long time.

Prepare for what that does. Your own heartbeat will become obscene to you: a sloppy, arrhythmic animal in a room of perfect regularity. Many travelers report the conviction that they are a disease. That conviction is *accurate*, and should be respected rather than treated.

Also prepare for the forty-seven hertz. It is in your sternum and your back teeth, and it is close to the resonances of your chest and eyeballs. Expect nausea, expect your vision to smear, expect a sense of presence in the room with you. Mass-load and damp your chest panel; brace your visor so it cannot buzz; keep your contact points few.

**Practical countermeasures:**

- **Build the mock-up at home.** A sixty-centimeter corridor with soft brushing walls, seven degrees, eighty-six decibels of rain-on-tin plus a forty-seven hertz tone, cherry light at thirty lux, no comms, two hours. Do the whole thing including the boring hour in the box. If you cannot do it in a wet world where mistakes are cheap, you have no business doing it here.
- **Memorize the dimensions** so your mind can hold a map your senses cannot build: eleven meters across, four and a half high, twenty-six meters of Way, sixty centimeters wide.
- **Navigate mechanically.** A dry sealed gyro, a stroke count, a tactile chart embossed on metal you read with gloves. If you lay a line, lay baked dry filament and rewind every centimeter of it on the way out, and weigh it.
- **Do not touch the rail.** It has been polished by three centuries of the same carriage. A scratch changes the fit.
- **No tether to the wall, no handholds, no leaning.** The canopy is load-bearing. Strut-caste and cable-caste are structure. A buckled living member is a cascade of sag that the carriage must excise.
- **Agree your signals with your tender before you go,** and agree the hardest one: if you do not come out on time, the tender does not come in. The interlock forbids it, and two events are twice the contamination.

Which means you must accept, in advance and in writing, that **there is no recovery.** If you die in there, you stay. Tell your people. Make your peace with it.

## 9. Conduct inside

You cannot avoid touching it. The Way is sixty centimeters and you are not sixty centimeters of anything. Cut faces will weep blue against your sleeves and freeze white and close behind you at the speed of a fingernail growing.

So the goal is not purity. It is **a small, cold, dry, brief insult, honestly logged.**

- Touch nothing that is not already touching you.
- Take nothing. No cutting, no leaf, no flower, no pollen, no salt, no chip of scalloped wall. The flowers are perfect and useless and their pollen is a bearing grease; it is not a souvenir.
- Do not open the brass frame. Do not breathe on the glass, even through a suit you trust.
- Do not add your initials to the two letters and a smudge.
- **Do not fix the chart drum.** This will be the strongest impulse you have ever felt in a room. The pen ran dry; the stylus is still bearing down at the same force; the groove is the entire surviving record. Your hands will want to give it ink. Ink is solvent, ink is water, ink is a falsified record, and the groove is not a malfunction. It is a wound exactly the shape of the answer. Measure it. Photograph it passively. Do not lift the arm.
- Do not put your hand in the handrail groove and then decide that means something. It will fit. That is all it means, and it is enough.

## 10. Coming out

Your suit will have absorbed ammonia into its elastomers and will outgas for days. In your ship's small closed lock, that outgassing can reach the flammable range — ammonia burns in air between roughly fifteen and twenty-eight percent. **No flames, no smoking, no stowing the suit in a cabin.** Bag it, vent it slowly, keep it cold and outside.

The rime you failed to brush off becomes hot caustic liquor the moment it meets a wet world's humidity, and it is copper-ammine chemistry with an almond smell you should not trust. Do not rinse it in a sink. Do not take it into a kitchen. Handle it as hazardous waste and log its mass as theft.

Then weigh out, compare against the manifest, and write down every discrepancy including the ones you cannot explain. Especially those.

## 11. Afterward

The only legitimate souvenir is a description.

You will want to bring something back that proves it: a leaf, a salt crystal, a photograph of the master under glass. All of those are subtractions from a thing that cannot subtract. What you can bring back is the number of the cycle, the depth of the groove, the temperature to a twentieth of a degree, the fact that the leaves are blue and you never saw it, and the sound of a room where it is raining and has always been raining and nothing has ever been wet.

Write it somewhere wet, on paper, with your own name on it. Not on the frame.

And when you find that you cannot stop thinking about the drum — the arm still bearing down, cutting one perfect circle deeper every revolution, nobody coming to read it — understand that you did read it. That was the whole of what this place needed from a visitor, and you could have done it from the vestibule, in the dark, with the outer door open behind you and the mountain air too thin to carry sound.

## Rules, briefly

Never both doors. Never your own gas into the lock. Never a red light. Never a drop of water. Never a warm surface. Never a vent. Never a flash on the plate. Never a hand on the rail. Never a cutting home. Never ink in the pen. Never a missed clack ignored.

Weigh in. Weigh out. One joule. Forty minutes. Come out behind the carriage.

Leave even your frost.
