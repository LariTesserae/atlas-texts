# Preparing for The Sweat

## First, decide whether you should go at all

This is not a destination. It is the underneath of somewhere else — a working heat sink, eleven years old, still being cut larger at one end. Nobody built it for you and nobody has softened it for you. Every surface in it is fresh fracture. Every process in it is running. The crews are not hosts; they are people at work in a place that is trying, patiently and without malice, to kill them.

Screen yourself honestly. Bad sinuses or a history of ear trouble will make the forty-hour pressure reversal miserable. Cold-induced asthma is a serious problem in saturated air at two degrees. Raynaud's will cost you your hands. Cardiac issues plus cold shock plus infrasound plus exertion is a combination worth discussing with someone qualified before you commit. And if you have never been in a large dark cave, go find one and sit in it for an hour, because the Sweat produces a specific vertigo that people get wrong in both directions: some panic at the weight of the made world overhead, and some panic at the *openness* — nine kilometers of unresolvable dark in a room you know is a room. It is agoraphobia with a lid on it. Find out which one you get.

## Understand the cold before you pack for it

Two degrees, saturated, always moving. This is not winter. Winter has sun, and dry patches, and a house at the end of it. This is the cold of a product left out, and it never stops, and there is no radiant heat anywhere — the walls, floor, ceiling and air are all the same temperature, so you lose heat evenly in every direction like something in a refrigerator.

The critical fact is not the temperature. It is that **nothing here dries.** Not your socks, not your gloves, not a wound, not a match, not a mistake. Every ordinary outdoor skill you have assumes you can eventually get something dry. Delete that assumption and repack.

- No cotton, no down. Down collapses wet and stays collapsed. Synthetic or wool base layers, fleece mid, and a genuinely waterproof shell with pit zips — you will be working hard in air that cannot take your sweat away.
- Neoprene gloves, or the caving solution: thin fleece gloves inside waterproof overmitts. You will be handling cold stone and cold steel, which pulls heat out of your palms far faster than air does.
- Boots with aggressive lugs and stiff soles. The floor is a spill of angular black glass. Kneeling on it unprotected will open your knees. Bring kneepads.
- **Two of everything you wear next to your skin, and one sealed dry bag that is sacred.** Dry socks, dry base layer, dry gloves, a dry hat, a dry towel, chemical heat packs, and your medical kit. That bag is not luggage; it is life support. Open it only in a leased dry bay, and never with wet hands.
- A brim. A hard hat with a brim, or a hood with a stiff peak. It rains here constantly and vertically and you will spend your whole visit with water in your eyes otherwise.

Foot care is the thing that will actually go wrong. Immersion foot in eleven days of two-degree sheet water is not exotic, it's default. Plan a protocol: dry socks at every rest, powder, alternate boots if you can, feet up and aired whenever you are in a dry pocket. Do not skip a foot check because you are tired.

## Your body will do strange things

**The fundamental.** Thirty-one hertz, phase-locked, arriving through the sternum and sinuses and the water in your eyes. Earplugs do not help — they can make it worse, because sealing your ears removes the masking highs and boosts what arrives by bone. Bring plugs anyway, for the flush, which is a genuine audible roar. For the fundamental there is no personal remedy: only distance, a node, or a leased hush pocket.

Expect it to make you feel *watched*. Low-frequency dread is a physical effect and it is very convincing. Know in advance that the sense of presence, the pressure behind the eyes, the conviction that something is standing behind you, is the room's rotating machinery resonating in your chest. It is not a premonition. Say this to yourself on the first night.

**Sleep.** New crews sleep badly for a month. You will not be there a month, so plan on sleeping badly the whole time and don't schedule anything important for your second day. More importantly, there is no day here at all — no dawn, no dusk, no temperature swing, nothing. Your circadian rhythm will free-run, and the plant's forty-hour fan cycle will drag it somewhere unhelpful. Impose your own day: a small light box in your hush pocket, meals on a fixed 24-hour schedule, melatonin if you use it. Carry two watches, one for your body and one for the plant, and whenever a local gives you a time, ask *which clock*.

**Dehydration.** Cold saturated air holds almost no water. You will not feel sweaty, you will be peeing constantly from cold diuresis, and you will lose fluid fast in air that feels like a wet towel. Drink more than you want to. Bring electrolytes — the condensate is nearly distilled and will pull salts out of you rather than put them in.

**Gas.** This is the hazard visitors miss. The air is blended for the process, not for you. The warm seams and the low joints are exactly where heavy gas settles, and the sump-calves live there happily because they barely breathe. Crouching down to look at one puts your head into a layer that may not support you. Carry a personal four-gas meter, wear it at chest height, and *believe it*. Be especially careful during and after a fan-gate reversal, when the pressure drop can burp the joints out.

Near the north end, where the returns spill their heat in a controlled catastrophe, you are in warm aerosol. Wear a respirator there. Anywhere warm and wet and enclosed is a place where things grow in the water that you do not want in your lungs.

## The two things you need are the two things they sell

Cold, water, dark and noise are free and infinite here. Warmth and quiet are metered, surveyed, leased and defended. Budget accordingly, and get your paperwork done before you arrive: entry permit, a sponsoring crew, a lamp allowance, a flush alarm, and **a hush lease for sleeping**. Do not plan to save money by sleeping in an unmapped node. Sound is property. You would be trespassing in someone's quiet, and they will notice, because noticing is what the whole district is built around.

Learn to be silent before you come. Slate and grease pencil, waterproof notebook, throat mic and bone-conduction earpiece. Practice the local sign. Then practice the hard part: not making noise when you are startled, hurt, amused or moved. Your instinct to yelp when you slip, to laugh when a slab goes into the Shallows, to gasp when you first see the Colonnade — all of that is spending other people's property. Carry a small absorbent pouch you can cough or sneeze into. It sounds absurd. It is the single most useful thing on this list after the dry bag.

And retrain your emergency reflex now, at home, until it is automatic: **your scream is not an alarm here.** The fundamental will swallow it whole, and even if it carried, shouting is an offense. In an emergency: strobe first, radio second, get hands on someone third. Carry a strobe you can trigger one-handed with gloves on.

Because nobody can ask if you're all right, agree with your buddy on a visual hypothermia check every twenty minutes — touch fingers to thumb in sequence, or something similar. Watch hands, not speech. Fumbling is the tell.

## Moving

Visibility is a few hundred meters at best; the basin makes its own haze. Past the third lamp ring you are in instrument-glow country, a scatter of green and amber points at chest height in absolute black. There is no sky, no satellite, no horizon. Your compass will lie to you near that much iron and current.

Navigate by pillar tags and cable runs. Every cable run leads to a pump-house; walk *alongside* one, never on it, and you will eventually reach people and light. Carry a route card — pillar IDs, bearings, distances — and a beacon certified for the basin, because an ordinary rescue beacon is competing with thirty-one hertz and losing.

Light discipline: white LED headlamp, red mode, two spares, batteries kept against your skin because cold eats them. But keep your beam on your hands and your feet. A roaming white beam across a dark district is rude, blinding to people adapted to glow, and — because a pale moss cultivar colonizes wherever light leaks onto wet stone — leaving your lamp parked in one spot can literally seed a growth that someone has to log as a defect. Sodium light also destroys color: blood looks black, and color-coded valves are unreadable. Check wounds under white light.

Do not touch any valve. Some of them have not been turned in years and are only apparently abandoned.

Stay well away from the cutting heads at the far east. That ground is unmapped, unlit, hot-worked, and has no node survey and no flush coverage. It is the newest place in the world and it has not been made safe for anyone.

## Water

Do not drink the wall. It tastes of zinc and recipe because it is a working fluid that has been through the process, and it carries biofilm. Filter and treat, or bring your own.

Assume you will be billed for what you put in. This is a closed condensate loop and the flush is reclaimed from it. No soap, no sunscreen, no scented anything, no food scraps, no urine. Everything you shed feeds a system that is deliberately nutrient-poor; a bloom in the wrong runnel fouls a gauge and gets traced back to the visitor with the shampoo.

**The flush.** Eleven minutes at shift change, the whole basin a roaring gray sheet at pressure. It will knock you off your feet and carry you south. Learn the signal, learn where the refuge brackets and pump-house plinths are on your route, and be off the floor with minutes to spare — not seconds. Never be in a runnel, a trench, or the Shallows when it comes. Full immersion at two degrees gives you a handful of minutes of useful hands.

**The Shallows.** Knee-deep at most, and the most dangerous ground in the basin, because the bottom is aggregate flour that has not had time to become silt. The deltas quake underfoot and ring faintly. That ringing is the water trying and failing to escape from under your weight. Probe with a pole, go no deeper than ankle, never go alone, and never go at all near shift change. Also stand well back from the undercut banks: slabs of saturated flour let go without warning and they are heavy.

## How to behave toward what lives here

The critical thing to understand is that **the ecology is instrumentation.** The moss was seeded as a moisture gauge. The frost-fern is a humidity record. Damaging a strip of moss in a Colonnade seam is not littering or vandalism — it is falsifying a reading that somebody upstream is making decisions on. Treat every living patch as a live sensor and keep your hands, your boots and your lamp off it.

The sump-calves breathe through their backs and have skin like wet paper. Your palm at thirty-seven degrees is a burn to a nine-degree animal, and holding one suffocates it. Watch from a meter away, in red light, and don't reach into joints to steady yourself — you'll crush something, and the joint may be hot, pressurized, or full of gas.

The flier is different. A few hundred in the whole basin, hunting the quiet lines between the pillars, navigating by the standing wave. Seeing one is the best thing that will happen to you here. The crews never write them into the reports, and you should understand why and honor it: an official record makes a population into a managed variable. Don't publish geotagged images. Don't publish node maps. And do not, under any circumstances, set up your own acoustic damper to make a comfortable camp — a made hush pocket deafens the moth and starves it. You would be killing the rarest thing in the room to get a nap. Rent the quiet instead.

Before you enter, expect to be scrubbed, and cooperate. You are carrying a jungle of spores into a place with almost no ecological defenses. Leave anything that was ever alive at home: wood, paper, leather, cotton, food you can't seal. On the way out, check your seams and cuffs for eggs.

## The curve

East of Cascade Stair, the runnel has begun to swing. Half a meter off the cut line, then back, then further; the outside bank undercutting, the inside building a bar. It has been logged twice as a defect and nobody has decided yet whether it is damage.

You will want to stand there. Stand there. But do not walk on the bar — you will compact a centimeter of the first sediment in the history of this place. Do not straighten the channel, do not "help," do not take a chip of black sugar as a souvenir, and do not argue with a surveyor about it. You are looking at the first curve in this world that nobody drew, in a room where water has done exactly what it was told for eleven years. It is arguably the most interesting thing on any world you will visit this year and it is nine centimeters long.

Prepare to be unexpectedly moved, and to be unable to explain why to anyone who hasn't been. That's normal. Everybody who goes down there comes back talking about a puddle.

## Coming home

Your gear will never entirely dry, and it will smell of wet cement and cold iron for years. Accept this.

More seriously: plan for the silence. Crews go up on leave and cannot sleep at all, and they all use the same word — *thin*. You will get off a shift's worth of thirty-one hertz and lie in a quiet bedroom feeling that the air has been removed from it. Bring a recording, and know that ordinary headphones cannot reproduce 31 Hz; you need a subwoofer or a tactile transducer bolted to the bed frame. Failing that, sleep with a fan running and the window open for the first three nights.

And keep a hand on the wall for a day or two after you get back. It will come away dry, and you will find that you keep checking.
