# Preparing for The Soda Works

## 1. First, correct your expectations about what kind of place this is

You are not visiting a wilderness and you are not visiting a ruin. Both frames will make you behave stupidly.

The basin is an **interior**. It has a floor (white, warm, ringing under a heel), walls (iron-coloured basalt), and a ceiling (brown haze under an inversion, sun coming through it like a coin). There is no horizon line. There is no wind-sound because there is nothing for wind to touch. Once you understand that you are going into a room thirty kilometres long, a lot of the preparation makes sense: you dress for a room, you move like someone in a room, and you understand why some people panic in it despite the enormous openness. Agoraphobia and claustrophobia are available simultaneously here. Know which one is yours before you go.

It is also a **crop**. The salt regrows. The trench fills in fourteen months. The Company wrote this down in 1911 and the report is in a cabinet in the club. Nobody here thinks the valley is dying. Nine hundred people live in housing built for four thousand, which reads to an outsider as collapse and reads to them as a Tuesday. Do not arrive with a elegy. Arrive with curiosity and a pair of goggles.

And it is an **interface**, not a volume. The life is pressed flat against the boundaries — hot/fresh, salt/sweet, wet/dry — in a film centimetres thick. This means your itinerary is not "explore the valley." The valley is genuinely empty and you can walk it for a day and see one grey structure. Your itinerary is: the springs, the mixing wedge, the diluted lagoons, the strand line, the underside of the conveyor gallery. Five edges. Everything else is floor.

## 2. The packing rule that falls out of the chemistry

This is the part most travelers get wrong, because their standard kit is optimized for the wrong enemy.

**Alkali destroys protein and aluminium. It leaves cellulose and most plastics alone.**

Therefore:

- **No wool. No silk. No merino.** Your usual travel base layers are protein fibre; the atmosphere will slowly eat them and they're too hot anyway. Cotton, polyester, polypropylene. Loose, long-sleeved, light.
- **No leather boots.** Alkaline conditions are how you *unhair* a hide. Leather here goes slimy, then stiff, then wrong; the glue joints go first. Rubber or PVC chemical boots with a thick, grippy, heat-insulating sole. Wet trona is soapy slick — slips are the most common injury in the valley and the crust's pressure ridges have edges like broken crockery.
- **No aluminium.** Not trekking poles, not a tripod, not a water bottle, not a cook pot, not tent poles, not carabiners. Aluminium in strong base pits, thins, and evolves hydrogen. Steel, titanium, fibreglass, plastic.
- **No down.** It will drink the night air and never dry properly, and it's protein besides.
- **Bring two trekking poles with carbide tips.** Not for walking. For *probing*. You will test every surface before you put weight on it, every time, forever.
- **No contact lenses.** I will say this again in the eye section because it matters that much.
- **Cheap eyeglasses, with a spare pair.** Alkaline films etch coatings. Do not bring your good sunglasses or your good camera.
- **Electronics sealed with desiccant.** The crust sweats; overnight everything wears a conductive brine film. Zippers, buckles, boot eyelets, tripod legs, charging ports — assume they are consumables. Do not bring anything you love.
- **A pencil and a synthetic-paper notebook.** Paper goes soft in a week here; they keep books in tins. Ink plus dust plus humidity is a bad combination.
- **A headnet, fine mesh, and a buff.** More on flies below.
- **A cheap plastic wristwatch.** The town's real clock is the drier, but you'll want your own.

**Bring your own salt.** Table salt. Sodium chloride. In a valley made of forty metres of sodium salt you cannot season your food, because the local salt is a *base* — sesquicarbonate — and eating it is a chemical event, not a culinary one. This is the single most emblematic thing on the packing list. In a place where everything is wet and nothing is drinkable, and everything is salt and none of it is salt, you must carry the ordinary version of both.

Also bring acid: a bottle of vinegar, citrus, hot sauce, pickles. Partly for your gear. Mostly for your tongue, which after four days will have forgotten that flavour exists in a form other than *soapy*.

## 3. Water and heat: there is no cold in this valley

Read the temperature again. **Thirty-one degrees at four in the morning, with the ground giving back the day like a stove, and humidity climbing to sixty or seventy percent.** The night is not relief. The night is the hour when sweating stops working. Wet-bulb around 27–28°C is survivable but it is not sleepable, and it means you begin each day already in debt.

By day the haze cuts the direct sun but the crust throws back everything that gets through, and the floor will be hotter than the air. You are being cooked from both sides in a badly lit room.

And there is **nothing cool to put your body into**. The springs come up at 60–86°C. The tilapia wedge sits at 39–40°C — above your core temperature; immersion there *adds* heat. The town swimming pool is blood-warm. Even the shade is only shade. Your only cooling mechanism is evaporation, and for two hours before dawn the air won't take it.

So:

- **Plan for 6–10 litres a day**, more if you walk out on the pans. Oral rehydration salts in quantity. Bring your own potassium sources too; sodium is not the electrolyte you'll be short of.
- **The fresh water is metered and piped eight kilometres.** You are a guest drinking someone's ration. Arrive with water, offer to pay for water, and never wash gear with it. The dew-catchers on the roofs are somebody's household supply; don't help yourself.
- **The valley gives water at exactly two times.** Pre-dawn, from the air, to anything cold enough to ask — that's the dew-catchers, and it's for washing, not drinking, unless you treat it and accept the grit. Any time, from the springs, if you can cool it — which means a canvas evaporative bag hung in shade, which works in the dry afternoon and does nothing at all at night. Learn this rhythm and you'll be comfortable. Ignore it and you'll be a medical problem by day three.
- **Find the cold.** There is a club with a bar and a hospital and a canteen, which means there is refrigeration somewhere in this town, and its location is the most useful piece of local knowledge you will acquire. Ask on your first morning. A cold drink at 14:00 is not a luxury here; it is a heat-illness intervention.
- **Acclimatization takes ten to fourteen days.** Do not walk out onto Pan Nine on your first afternoon. Spend the first three days on the causeway and in the plant's shade, drinking more than you think you need, and let your plasma volume catch up.
- A fan is worth more than any other single comfort item. A cotton sheet, not a sleeping bag. Mineral sunscreen, reapplied; mostly rely on cloth, a full-brim hat, and a buff, because you will get ultraviolet from *below* — under the chin, inside the nostrils, the undersides of your forearms, places you have never burned.

## 4. Your eyes: the thing most likely to permanently damage you

The brine runs pH 10.5 at up to forty degrees. The text says it would dissolve your cornea; treat that as directionally true. Alkali burns don't stop at the surface the way acid burns do — they keep going inward, and they keep going after you've stopped feeling them.

**The most dangerous act available to you in this valley is rubbing your eye.**

You will want to. The light is dim and flat and your eyes will feel wrong all day, and the dust is alkaline and desiccating, and there will be a fly on your eyelid drinking you. Every instinct says rub. Your fingers have salt on them.

**Practice not rubbing your eyes for a week before you go.** I am serious. Build the substitution: hands stay down, blink hard, use the clean bottle. Make it automatic at home so it's automatic there.

Kit and rules:

- Wraparound goggles, not sunglasses. Sunglasses are for the walk; goggles are for the water's edge, the trench lip, the dredge, the screens, anywhere brine can spit.
- **Two litres of sterile saline on your person, always.** Not in your room. On you.
- On day one, walk to the plant's eyewash stations with someone and put your hands on them. In a metered-water town an "eyewash station" may be a shelf with bottles on it. Know which shelf.
- If splashed: **irrigate immediately and keep going for twenty to thirty minutes minimum**, hold the lids open, don't stop when it feels better, then go to the hospital. Never try to neutralize an eye with acid. Vinegar is for your hands and your boots, never for your face.
- If you get brine in your mouth: spit, rinse repeatedly, don't induce vomiting, small sips of water, hospital. Caustic ingestion is not a "wait and see."
- No contact lenses. A lens traps alkali against the cornea and holds it there while you decide what to do.
- Skin: your hands after an hour in it will feel "scrubbed and slightly gone," because the brine is saponifying your skin's oil. Rinse with clean water, then a very dilute vinegar rinse for the soapy feeling, then an occlusive at night. Expect the dry afternoon to crack what the wet morning stripped.

## 5. The ground is not always ground

This is the local skill and it takes days to acquire. Learn the three states:

- **White and ringing** (mid-morning onward): load-bearing, hard, sharp-edged where the plates have shoved up into pressure ridges.
- **Grey and sweating** (pre-dawn): the crust has drunk the night air. Yesterday's footprints hold a centimetre of brine that fell as no rain. It is slick, it is deceptive, and it is beautiful. Walk it with poles.
- **Dark, dull, or freshly skinned**: don't.

The last one is the killer, because it is the *hopeful* fact turned against you. The mine heals. A trench cut in the morning has a skin by six the next morning, and the description tells you plainly that you'll be able to stand where the machine was. A skin that takes a boot is not a floor that takes a person's whole weight plus a fall. **Ask, every single morning, which pans are walkable today.** Never reuse yesterday's route on your own authority. The workers know. The workers are eleven people on nights and they know exactly which skin is lying.

And **"nothing sinks" applies to brine, not to mud.** Under the crust is ten thousand years of anoxic black mud. Brine at specific gravity 1.2 will hold you up absurdly high; mud will take you to the thigh and hold on. Probe first. If you go through into mud, don't thrash — lean back, spread load, get the poles across, and shout early rather than late.

If you do end up in brine: **you cannot swim in it and you cannot easily right yourself.** Your legs won't go down. People drown face-down in water they can't sink in. Roll, don't stand; get your head back; get out fast; strip the soaked clothes because they will keep working on your skin; irrigate everything.

Never camp on the pans. Camp on the rock peninsula, in town, or on the terrace if you've earned the walk.

## 6. The only thing in this valley that sinks is bad air

There is no wind at night and there is an inversion. Break the crust and you release sulfide from the mud. The springs give off ammonia, and soda springs of this kind commonly give off carbon dioxide too. Both sulfide and CO₂ are heavier than air, and in a basin with no wind they pool in exactly the places you'll be tempted to kneel down and look at: fresh trenches, channel bottoms, hollows, the lee of a spring terrace.

Rules:

- **Never enter a depression or a fresh cut at night, alone, or with your face low.** The most beautiful hour in the valley is also the hour with the worst air in it. Enjoy pre-dawn from the causeway and the town side.
- If you can carry a gas monitor, carry one. If you can't, use the old rule and understand it properly: **rotten-egg smell means sulfide; the smell going away means more sulfide, not less.** It paralyses your nose right before it drops you. Turn around and go up.
- CO₂ has no smell at all. Which is why the rule is *don't go down*, not *sniff first*.
- Chronic, not acute: the north badland is diatomite — silica. The plant makes soda ash dust, which is alkaline and desiccating to your airway. Wear a proper particulate respirator on the bagging floor and in the badland, and don't visit the badland on a windy day. Your throat will tell you about the pans anyway; the locals have a cough.

## 7. The flood arrives under a clear sky

Twice a year it rains **up on the highlands**, and two days later a brown sheet a foot deep comes across the flats carrying sand, goat bones, and occasionally a shipping container.

Note the structure of that sentence. The weather that kills you happens somewhere you cannot see, and arrives on a delay, under whatever sky happens to be overhead at the time. There is no local warning. A perfectly ordinary brown noon can be the last hour before the water.

So: on arrival, **learn the season, and learn who watches the highlands.** Somebody in that town gets a call or reads a gauge or has an opinion about the escarpment. Find that person and ask them, politely, what the water is doing. Then never sleep on the flats, never in a channel, never in a fan, and never below the strand. When the flood comes the lake goes milky, the chemistry moves out from under the dredge, the works shuts down, and the flamingos leave in a single morning. It is a spectacular thing to watch **from the peninsula, with a roof.**

If you can choose your dates: come for the **dry-down**, roughly weeks three to five after the flood, when the salinity climbs, the crust knits, the windrows re-form, and the birds come back in ones and tens and then in their hundred thousands. That's the valley showing you what "established" means.

## 8. Flies, birds, and the fact that you are a spring

The brine flies don't bite. There's nothing here worth biting for blood. **They want your water, because you are the only fresh water on thirty kilometres of flats.** Understand that and you won't hate them. You'll cover up.

- Fine-mesh headnet over a brimmed hat, buff over mouth and nose. They go into your mouth when you speak. Talk indoors. Breathe through your nose on the pans.
- They land on eyelids to drink, which means they walk from the wrack and the mats onto your conjunctiva. This is a real conjunctivitis route. Goggles at the shore; don't rub; carry saline.
- The windrows are knee-deep, twenty kilometres of them, drifting like ash. Walking them is wading, and the dust you kick up is desiccated insect — an allergen and a respiratory irritant. Buff or respirator in the wrack, and don't be surprised if you develop a sensitivity you didn't have.
- Don't spray, don't swat, don't kill. There's nothing here to spare and you're a guest in the only food web in the basin.

The flamingos: **view from three kilometres, which is where the surf-sound reaches you anyway.** The description tells you the correct distance; take the hint. Four hundred thousand birds flushed by one walking visitor is a real energetic cost to animals living on a bacterium. Bring binoculars or a scope. And be patient about colour: in this light they're dishwater grey until they open their wings. Wait for a stretch, a squabble, a flight. That's the moment. Don't walk toward the seam to get it.

The hyena comes down the causeway around two, unhurried, with business at the nesting ground. Give it the road. Don't walk the causeway alone at 02:00, don't carry food at night, and don't insert yourself into the nesting ground where ten thousand birds are already shouting at something with teeth.

## 9. The springs: the most fragile thing here is not you

Two hectares. A chain of hot pools and outflow channels. **The entire species.** A fish that gave up ammonia and excretes urea like a mammal because the ordinary way is chemically forbidden to it, living in a wedge between water that cooks it and water that dissolves it.

Behave accordingly:

- **Nothing goes in.** No soap, no sunscreen, no hands, no feet, no swim, no wading, no "just a quick dip with the fish." The pool is 39–40°C; you'd be gaining heat anyway.
- **Dedicated footwear for the springs, or a boot wash.** Do not carry brine, mat, or mud from one pool to another on your soles. That's how a pathogen or a competitor gets moved into a habitat with nowhere to flee to.
- The rims of channels running 60–86°C water look like crust and are not. Probe. A scald here is a burn plus alkali plus a small hospital plus a long evacuation.
- Don't take a fish. Don't take a handaxe from the diatomite either — they lie where they fall, sharp as the day they were struck, and there are a great many of them, and the fact that nobody picks them up is a property of the place that you should not be the one to change. Also they will genuinely cut you.
- Don't take a calcified flamingo. Don't take crystal from a live pan; that's inventory, it's somebody's harvest.

The town's own pool has tilapia in it and **they are not removed.** Nine hundred people who could trivially sterilize a swimming pool and choose not to. That single fact tells you who you're visiting. Act like a guest of those people.

## 10. The works

Eleven square kilometres of plant, three generations of conveyor gallery overhead, pipes racked four deep on racks built twice, abandoned 1950s pipework cementing into the crust at a millimetre a year. It is dense like a diagram, and **you cannot tell live from dead by looking.**

- Report in. This is a company town; the Company is the authority, the landlord, the water supply, and the hospital. Get permission, get an escort, get a name and a radio channel.
- Hard hat inside the plant. Concrete spalls here — brine gets in, crystallises, jacks it apart from the inside. **Look up before you look out.** The oldest buildings wear a crust like the pans do and the newest ones have already started.
- Nothing touched without being told. Conveyors, the dredge ladder, the slurry line, the calciners, the driers.
- Respirator on the bagging floor. Hearing protection near the hose and the drier — but take it off out on the pans, because out there your hearing is a navigational instrument.
- Don't board the locomotive that stands with its cab light on and nobody in it. Ask the driver.
- Eleven people run this at night. If you get into trouble at 03:00, the nearest human may be six kilometres away walking a gallery with a lamp. Carry your own light, your own water, your own saline, and a way to call. Assume definitive medical care is hours away and behave like someone who has assumed that.

## 11. Arrival, and how to be worth your water

**Arrive on the rock peninsula, in daylight, on foot, in town.** Not on a pan you haven't tested, not in a trench, not at a spring. Walk in like a person.

If your transit needs a fixed anchor: **anchor to the bedrock of the peninsula, not to the salt.** The salt grows a millimetre a year and gets re-cut every fourteen months. A return coordinate set on Pan Nine is a coordinate set on something that will be dredged away and re-laid while you're gone.

Bring, as trade or gift:
- **Books, sealed in tins with desiccant.** They keep books in tins. This is a town that would like more books.
- Cotton socks. Pencils. Synthetic notebooks. Spare goggles. Children's sunglasses.
- Acid food: citrus, vinegar, hot sauce, something pickled.
- A fan.
- **Darts, and a moulded-plastic board.** The club's board is warped, because everything organic here drinks the air and goes soft. Sisal will not survive it. Plastic will.
- **A recording of wind in trees.** Rain on grass. A river. The last thorn tree is nine hundred metres up and two days' walk north; there are children in that school who have never stood in shade that wasn't built. This costs you nothing to carry and it is the best thing on this list.

Do **not** bring: seeds, soil, live plants, aquarium anything, wood. Nothing here has ever grown a trunk and the basin is not dead, it is *unplanted*. Keep it that way.

And earn your welcome the correct way: **take a turn on the screens.** The cooling intake felts over with flies every ninety minutes and hosing it is the loudest regular job in the valley and the second loudest is the man swearing at it. It's unskilled, it's hated, and he would like a break. That is also, not incidentally, the best place in the town to meet somebody and hear what they actually think about where they live.

Learn the nouns: *trona, the pans by number, the wrack, the screens, the ladder, the causeway, the terrace.* Speak them and you're a person. Don't say "wasteland" to anyone whose kids were born here.

Ask to read the 1911 report. Ask properly. It's in a cabinet in the club and it's the town's one genuinely hopeful fact and they will know you're taking the place seriously if that's the thing you want to see.

## 12. A workable day

- **04:00–06:00** — Out on the causeway and the town side while the crust is grey and sweating and every footprint fills with water that fell as no rain. Poles, headnet, goggles in the pocket. Do not go down into cuts or hollows. This is the most beautiful two hours and the worst air; stay high.
- **06:00–09:00** — Walk out with the day shift, on their route, on their permission. Watch the crust go white and start to ring. Watch the cut from yesterday take its skin.
- **09:00–16:00** — Indoors. Dark room, fan, water, salt, sleep. This is not laziness; this is the local schedule and it exists for reasons. Midday on the pans is a badly lit room with a hot floor and no horizon and nothing to see.
- **16:00–18:00** — Out again. Springs (dry feet, clean boots), lagoons, the strand from a distance.
- **Dusk** — The bats out of the conveyor gallery, working the fly-haze in a low erratic scribble. Sit still. Don't talk. Twenty minutes of stillness before you take anything out of your bag.
- **Night** — The causeway with somebody, a lamp, and the sodium lights the same colour as the dust. The drier turning. The screens hosed. Give the hyena the road.

## 13. What it will do to your head

**Your depth perception will be unemployed.** No trees, no leaves, no horizon, no line between ground and sky, everything the same value. A dredge four kilometres off is a toy on a table or a mountain; you cannot tell. You will walk toward something for an hour and it will not get closer. Two fixes:

- **The metre-gauge line is your ruler.** It is the only human scale in the valley. A metre between the rails, sleepers at known spacing. Calibrate your eye against it on day one and then read distance off it forever.
- **Sound is your map.** The dredge thud arrives with no attack, more felt than heard; the hose is the loudest regular sound; there's the locomotive, the drier. On your first morning stand on the causeway and learn the bearings of each. In flat light, when the horizon dissolves into a white-brown seam, you will navigate by ear. Also: keep a gallery or the causeway in sight, and if you lose the works entirely, **stop and wait** rather than walk. The crust ticks and the plates shove; a wrong step in a whiteout is a trench.

**You will be the loudest thing in the valley.** Your boots ring, your jacket crackles, your breathing is enormous. There is no rustle to hide in. To hear the place at all you have to become still, and then it comes up: the low hum of the windrows, the tick of the crust warming, a wet click when a bird shifts its foot.

**The surf never breaks.** Three kilometres off, four hundred thousand flamingos sound like a wave about to break, and it goes on being about to break for hours. Some people find this profoundly calming. Some find it unbearable within an hour. **Find out which you are from three kilometres, on your first day, before you commit to a full day at the shore.**

**The preserved dead will unsettle you.** A bird that dies here stays a bird — feathers, eye, folded foot — for years, slowly taking on mineral. There are calcified flamingos standing in the wrack that were alive when the drier was commissioned. You will see one standing and think it's alive, and then it won't move, and then you'll understand. There is no smell of decay anywhere in the basin. Prepare for that specific wrongness: death without the courtesy of decomposition. It reads as taxidermy, or as a stage set, or as a photograph you've walked into.

**Time here is accretion, not event.** Nothing is new; the erosion has finished arguing; the system has one state and returns to it. A visitor looking for things to happen will conclude, correctly, that nothing happens, and will be entirely wrong. So do this instead: **pick one square metre — a footprint, a trench lip, a dead bird — and log it with timestamps, and go back to it every day.** By the third day you'll have seen the crust sweat and knit, the crystal fall downward through the brine like a snow that does it for a living, the pipework taking its millimetre. That's the whole valley, available in one square metre, to anyone willing to look twice.

For the crystal: don't lean over a fresh trench with a lamp. Ask the dredge operator to bring up a sample, or use a sample port on the slurry line. A polarizer on a cheap camera will cut the brine glare enough to see it. And in this brown light, expose for the crust — a stop or two over what your meter says — or the whole place will come out the grey of dishwater, which is honest, but not what you saw.

## 14. Leave nothing, because nothing leaves

**Nothing rots here.** Alkalinity and salt do the work of a tannery. Your food scraps, your wrappers, your hair, your fingernail clippings — none of it will break down. It will dry, and stiffen, and slowly mineralize, and be there. Pack out everything, including the organic. Use the latrine, not the flats. The idea of leaving a permanent trace of yourself in a place with no decay is worth sitting with for a moment before you go, because it changes how you handle a candy wrapper.

And if the worst happens: understand that this is a place where a body stays. Make your extraction plan accordingly, and tell somebody in town what it is.

## 15. The thing to carry home

Two facts, and they're the same fact.

A fish, in a puddle you could throw a stone across, at forty degrees and pH 10, that solved an impossible chemistry by giving up the ordinary way and doing it the way you do — urinating like a man because the fish way is forbidden. Tens of thousands of them. Nowhere to flee to. Not fleeing.

And a trench cut into the ground on Monday that has a skin by Tuesday morning and will be hard enough to cut again in fourteen months, and has been doing this since before there was anyone to cut it.

Everything alive here lives on a boundary between two lethals, and the machines are simply another thing that has learned to live on the interface, no stranger than the tilapia. Eleven people on nights, walking galleries with lamps, are part of the same solution the fish is part of.

So the last piece of preparation is an attitude, and it's this: **don't go to mourn the emptiness. Go to watch something work.** Find the interface you can live on — the causeway, the shade, the two hours before dawn, the ninety-minute turn on the hose — and live on it, the way everything else here does.

Be a cut that skins over by morning.
