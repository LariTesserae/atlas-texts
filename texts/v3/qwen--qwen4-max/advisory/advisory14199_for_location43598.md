# THE SILL: A BRIEFING FOR THE ORDINARY TRAVELER

## 0. The thing you must understand before you pack anything

You are not going to look at a place. You are going to *enter a recording medium*, and you are going to be the loudest thing in it.

Everywhere else you have been, carelessness dissipates. A shout in a canyon is gone in four seconds. Here, a cough on the wrong terrace is written into a mineral lattice as a cloudy seam, and that seam will still be legible in three hundred years, and a stranger will hold it up to a lamp and read the exact moment you cleared your throat. There is a block in the gauge-house at Cup Eleven with a hard white line through it labeled *the boiler*. That is what a mistake looks like on the Sill. It looks like a line, and it has a name.

So the whole of your preparation reduces to one posture: **behave like a person carrying a full cup of hot liquid through a crowded room, for six days, while dizzy.**

Everything below is elaboration.

---

## 1. The counterintuitive fact that gets visitors in trouble

The yardmen grade the quiet one through five. At the rim it's fog-quiet, ordinary. At grade three your footsteps stop arriving at your ears. At grade four an iron bar on rock gives you a *tap* the size of a fingernail. At grade five nothing propagates at all.

Here is the trap: **the grade describes what you can hear. It does not describe what the stone can record.**

The crop is a better microphone than you are. It is coupled to the living rock — the seed-frames sit in vats plumbed into the limestone, the busbars are stapled to the same continuous solid you are standing on. Structure-borne vibration reaches it whether or not the air is carrying anything. And the crop does not have your hearing threshold; it takes ultrasound, it takes the tick of a watch, it takes the whine of a switching power supply you cannot perceive and would swear on your life is silent.

By grade three you will feel *safe*, because the world has gone soft and your boots have stopped making noise. That feeling is the single most dangerous thing that will happen to you there.

Rule: **behave as though you are at grade one, everywhere, always.** Your ears are not the instrument of record. You are only the thing being recorded.

---

## 2. Staging: do not arrive at the Sill. Arrive *near* it.

Come up through the lowlands slowly. Two thousand three hundred meters is not extreme, but it is enough to give a flatlander a headache, a dry cough, and bad sleep — and a cough here is not a symptom, it's a liability with a price tag. If your body is calibrated to a different atmosphere, assume you are *more* sensitive than you expect, not less. Three nights at intermediate altitude before you go up. If you use an altitude prophylactic in your home world, bring it.

Then find the band.

A kilometer or two out from the outermost cups, the grade drops to one and the air will carry a call again. There is a lark that nests only there. **Put your base camp in the lark band.** This is where you keep everything that hums: your transit unit, your charger, your camera with a mirror in it, your spare boots, your food in crinkly wrappers, your voice. Sleep there, acclimatize there, argue there, laugh there, be an ordinary noisy animal there for a few days before you go in.

Cross the line on foot, carrying only what has passed the silence audit, in the company of someone who lives on the other side of it.

Do not walk in unannounced. Eighty kilometers of works with four generations of gesture-dialect is not a wilderness; it's a guild. Present yourself at a gauge-house, get a sponsor, get a number. Expect to be issued a brass tag, a slate, a lamp, a hand-drawn map of cup numbers, and a grade ceiling — probably two or three. Accept the ceiling. Visitors who go to five on their first day are the reason there are ceilings.

There is no satellite navigation here, no network, no rescue helicopter listening for your beacon. There is a person who has agreed to keep looking at you. That is the entire safety system. Honor it.

## 3. The body

**Eyes.** This is the injury you are most likely to actually sustain. White limestone pavement at 2300 m throws most of the sun back up at you; the fog does not save you, it *scatters* the ultraviolet into your eyes from every direction at once, and it does not visibly dim anything. You are in a snowfield that doesn't look like one. The description you've read is accurate: the inside of your nostrils is bright.

Bring two pairs of category-4 glacier goggles with side shields, on soft cords, plus whatever smoked glass the yardmen issue. A spare is not optional; a lost pair means you go blind for two days in a stone hut. Sunscreen under the chin, under the eyebrows, behind the ears, on the philtrum, at the rim of the nostrils — all the upward-facing surfaces you have never had to protect. Lips with a hard SPF stick. Long sleeves even when you're warm. A soft wide brim, pale, with a chin tie; not a stiff synthetic brim that snaps and flutters in the escarpment wind.

Note the conflict now, because you'll have to manage it every day: **sunscreen washes off, and everything you wash off goes into the crop.** Prefer cloth and shade to chemistry. Where you must use grease, use mineral barrier on the face only, and never lean over an open vat with a wet forehead.

**Depth perception.** Shadows are thin and filled from below, so your visual system's main cue for the shape of the ground has been quietly disabled. Boulders appear to hover. Ledges appear to be lines painted on a flat surface. And a doline eighty meters deep, in flat white light and fog, with no shadow at its lip and no sound of water rising from it — **is a hole in the world that looks exactly like the ground.**

This is what kills people here. Not the silence. The hole.

Never walk in front of your guide. Never take a step you haven't looked at. Never move unroped in fog. Learn to read texture and moss instead of shadow: moss is thick on the arrises and thin on the walking flats, and the yardmen's paths will be worn or marked in ways you should learn on day one and then trust absolutely. Count paces. Follow the wire runs and the launders — they are continuous and they lead somewhere, which is more than can be said for you.

Oddly, night is safer for hole-finding: the copper carries a cold blue corona after the plates give up their charge, and the cups are outlined in faint constellations from the rim. Learn to read that. Learn also that the faint green fungal glow along the moss marks the *warm live busbars*. The pretty lights are a hazard map.

**Cold and wet.** You will never be dry. Fog condenses on everything, and the mesh above the terraces drips on you constantly, silently, for four days out of six. At 2300 m with wind, that is a hypothermia machine disguised as a mild damp day. Wool and felt, layered, more than you think. No down as your working insulation — it collapses wet. Vapor-barrier socks if you know how to use them. Four pairs of socks minimum, and accept that you will be putting on damp ones.

There are no chimneys on the Sill. There is no drying fire. Ask where the drying racks are and use only those; do not drape your shirt over a busbar, which is warm, and wet, and live, and stapled to the rock you're standing on.

**The involuntary body.** Inventory every noise you make without deciding to: cough, sneeze, hiccup, sniff, swallow, stomach, knees, knuckles, snore, the little grunt when you stand up. You cannot abolish these. You can *schedule* them. Eat before descending. Nothing carbonated, nothing gassy, nothing crunchy. Clear your throat at the rim, thoroughly and shamelessly, then go down. Carry a soft cloth for your nose and learn to sneeze into it with your mouth against your own shoulder.

**Do not go if you have a respiratory infection, uncontrolled asthma, or active hay fever.** An antitussive and an antihistamine in the kit, decanted out of their rattling blister packs into a cloth pouch. Lozenges unwrapped in advance. And get your teeth looked at before you cross: a toothache at grade four, where you cannot even hear yourself whimper, is a species of horror you do not need.

**Fitness.** Multiple descents and ascents of forty to eighty meters a day, on wet knife-edged limestone, at altitude, in gloves, carrying water, without the ability to call out or hear a warning. Strong legs, good balance, no vertigo. If you have a panic disorder, a vestibular problem, or distressing tinnitus, think very hard about the deep cups; grade five is an anechoic chamber with a view and it does things to people.

**Tetanus.** Old iron pins, a rusted boiler shell, copper, sharp rock, and a wound environment full of salt. Be current.

## 4. The silence audit

Do this at home, before you cross. Put every item you intend to carry on a wooden table in a quiet room, and *listen to yourself handle it*. Then have someone else handle it while you close your eyes. You will fail. That failure is the education.

Then apply these rules:

**Clothing.** Boiled wool, felt, moleskin, canvas worn soft. No ripstop nylon, no hardshell, nothing that rustles. **No Velcro anywhere** — Velcro is a scream. No metal zip pulls; tape them or replace with cord and wooden toggles. No snap buckles. Soft drawstrings.

**Feet.** Approach shoes or fell boots with soft sticky rubber, broken in, no hard heel edge, no aggressive lugs. **Pick your treads clean at the rim, every single time.** One grain of quartz lodged in your sole ticks on limestone with every step for eight hours. Leave the trekking poles at base camp; carbide tips on pavement are a metronome you're writing into somebody's retirement.

**Hands.** Leather-palmed wool gloves, softened, no crinkly membrane. You need them: the grikes will cut you, the wire will cut you, the rock has no rounded surfaces anywhere.

**Nothing that oscillates.** This is the category travelers get wrong. A device can be inaudible to you and screaming into the stone. Cooling fans, hard-drive spindles, haptic engines, autofocus motors, mirror slap, ultrasonic anything, switch-mode converters, a quartz movement in a wristwatch. Leave the mechanical watch. Leave the fitness tracker. Leave the drone — a rotor over a cup is not a mistake, it's a vandalism with a flight plan. Disable every beep, every chime, every haptic, every motion-activated wake, on everything, and then assume you missed one.

**Nothing that glints.** They run the works on light. A flash off your buckle or your watch face or a chrome lens ring is a *signal*, and you do not know the code. Matte everything. Tape the shiny parts. Wear white, gray, moss-green, dull earth — not red, not hi-vis orange. You want to be legible to your partner at ten meters and uninteresting to everyone else.

**Pack.** Soft frame, padded, no external metal, no dangling anything. Line the interior with hush offcuts if you can legitimately buy them at the lowland factors before you go up — using the crop to muffle your own gear is the correct and slightly comic solution, and everyone there does it. Tape every buckle. Nothing loose in a side pocket. Practice setting the pack down: little finger first, then the whole hand, then the weight. Never let it drop the last centimeter.

**Camera.** Passive recording only. No mirror, no shutter with a voice, no flash, ever. And **never play audio back inside a cup.** Not to test it, not to show someone, not quietly. That is both a flaw and an obscenity.

**Leave behind:** dogs, drones, speakers, instruments of any kind, wheeled carts, tripods, umbrellas, children who cannot hold a rule for six days, and anyone in your party who thinks the silence is a dare.

**Carry:** a whistle — for the *surface* only, where grade one still carries and fog can hide you from your partner. Below grade three it is a useless object and a temptation. In the cups, your alarm is a lamp and your partner's eyes.

## 5. Language, and the discipline of light

You will be illiterate. Accept it in advance and it won't humiliate you.

Handspeech here is four generations old, all wrist and knuckle, with dialects by cup. Whatever sign language you bring from home will not help beyond general manual dexterity, and a speech translator is a brick — there is no speech. Learn, in this order:

1. **Numbers.** They are the lingua franca: brass tags, gauges, tolerances, cup IDs, years. If you can sign and read numbers fluently you can be useful.
2. **The safety set.** Stop. Wait. Look. Up. Down. Come. Don't move. Danger. Mine. Yours. Ready. Not ready.
3. **The rain code**, both the lamp stutter and its handspeech equivalent. Learn this before your first descent, not during your first storm.
4. **Sorry.** Learn it properly, with the local grammar of it. You will need it.

Ten signs a day is a good rate. Bring a soft pencil and paper — but note that even writing has a sound; do your scribbling at the terrace junctions where the chalk slates hang, because that is what those places are *for*.

**Light discipline is the part nobody warns you about.** Lamps shuttered in code from lip to floor are how a hundred people over eighty kilometers coordinate. Your headlamp is a transmitter you don't know how to operate. Sweeping it across a cup can fake an alarm, mask a real one, or trigger a shutter you didn't mean to call.

So: hooded lamp, red capability, kept dark by default, pointed at your own feet, never aimed across the void. Disable any auto-brightness or motion wake. Do not signal. If you need to be seen, show your light to your partner at close range, low, and shielded. Ask your sponsor to watch you use it once and correct you.

## 6. Water, food, waste: there is no *away* on the Sill

The whole surface drains straight down through itself. No rivers, no streams, no dilution, no flushing, no downstream. Whatever you spill on the pavement goes into the rock and comes out in the cups, in the brine, in the crop, in the salamanders.

**Bring all your water.** Three to five liters a day at altitude with exertion, and none of it obtainable here. The black eyes at the cup bottoms are iodine and salt — the sea's own residue leached out of the stone — and they are the crop's feedstock, not a spring. The launders are process water. Do not drink from them, do not wash in them, do not rinse a cup in them, do not put a hand in a vat. No soap anywhere on the Sill. Wash at the hut with carried water, into a designated sump.

Latrines at the rim, outside the drainage, as instructed. Pack out what you pack in. Crumbs, hair, skin, sweat, sunscreen, spit — you are a warm contaminating animal in a precision mineral process. Tie your hair back. Cover up. Don't lean over open frames. Don't blow your nose over a vat.

**Food** should be soft, dense, and silent, decanted out of wrappers before you cross. Cheese, nut butter, soaked oats, rehydrated fruit, cold-brewed tea. No crackers, no apples, no nuts in shell, no crinkling foil. Beware the squeeze tube: it burps. Use a wide jar and a soft spatula. And expect the local food to be cold and quiet too — there are no chimneys and no fires, and boiling is a noise. If you were imagining a hot meal at the end of a wet day, revise the picture now, and be grateful for what you're given.

## 7. The physical hazards nobody mentions in the poems

- **The mesh.** Hectares of drawn wire strung across the cup mouths, angled to the fog, permanently beaded. It is the only thing on the Sill strung like an instrument, and it is strung directly over the crop. **Do not touch it. Do not pluck it.** One pluck is a chord written into eighty blocks. It is also under tension and will open your hand to the bone if you snag it wrong.
- **The copper.** Busbars as thick as a wrist, green-black, wet, faintly warm, stapled to living rock, carrying the output of thousands of plates. The brine is conductive. Your skin is wet. Never step across a busbar; never touch copper and rock at once; no rings, no chains, no metal on your wrists. Ask a yardman to walk you through the electrical map of your cup on day one and treat every wire and every wet surface near it as live. Do not charge your gear from their busbars — you'd be stealing crop current and injecting switching noise into it.
- **The rock.** Nothing is rounded. The scree is knife-edged and shifts with a grinding you feel in your shin. The arrises will cut a hand. The walls are fluted and unweathered and the Sill is still rising three or four millimeters a year, opening its joints a hair each decade — assume flakes are live. Stay on the terraces and the ladders. Don't climb the doline walls. A padded felt or leather cap rather than a hard helmet, because a hard helmet knocked against rock is a decade.
- **The still water.** Cold, deep, saline, silent, with no inlet and no outlet. If you go in, nobody will hear you, and you will not hear them coming.
- **The salamanders.** Counted individually. Named. Do not touch the cisterns. Do not touch a salamander. Do not name one; the names are taken and the takers are people who have earned them.
- **Biosecurity.** This is a naive, isolated system with almost nothing that moves: springtails, white snails, one salamander, and bell-flowers pollinated by hand with a squirrel-hair brush because there are no bees to do it. You could rewrite it with a mosquito egg in your water bottle, a seed in your tread, a fungus on your wool. Scrub and disinfect boots between worlds and between cups. No soil, no live plants, no untreated water, no fruit with a fly in it.

## 8. The rain drill

Fog is silent. Rain is a hundred thousand impacts, each one written into the stone. When the tide breaks wrong, the lamps stutter and the whole cup shutters — moss-lined wooden covers swung out over the vats on counterweights. It is the one fast operation on the Sill.

Those covers are heavy, they move quickly, and they will not stop for you.

Learn the drill at the rim, in daylight, before you ever descend. Learn the lamp stutter. Learn the swing arcs and where to stand outside them. Learn your station, if you're given one, and learn that if you're *not* given one your job is to go still, go silent, and be small. An untrained volunteer in the path of a counterweight is worse than a spectator.

And learn to read the escarpment sky yourself. This is the one genuinely useful thing a visitor can offer on day one: an extra pair of eyes on the weather, up at the lip where the air still carries and you're allowed to be an ordinary human. Offer to be a sky-watch. It is honest work, it is needed, and it will get you invited back.

## 9. Your first descent

Go down slowly, grade by grade, with a yardman who has done it a thousand times. Let your ears adjust the way theirs do — they feel the change like altitude, and you should not force it. Don't clamp your nose and blow hard; equalize gently, and if you're congested, don't go down at all that day.

At grade three your footsteps will stop arriving and you will feel a lurch of unreality. Say nothing. (You were going to say nothing anyway.) Keep your mouth shut and breathe through your nose — this is not a metaphor, it's the practical rule that prevents the most common visitor offense.

**Do not wear earplugs above grade three.** Up there, your hearing is your only feedback on your own noise; you need it to control yourself. Below grade three it's useless anyway.

At grade four, expect the pressure — the sense of a hand laid flat on the side of your head. Expect your own blood, loud, and then the disconcerting business of it going away.

**Grade five: ten minutes, tethered, supervised, on your first visit.** Agree touch signals with your partner before you go: one squeeze stop, two come, three up, flat palm on the back *don't move*. Keep a hand on the rope or the rock. Keep your partner's lamp in view. **Do not close your eyes** — with no sound and no vision you will have no world at all, and people come apart in there. Expect vertigo, nausea, a sensed presence, voices that aren't there. Expect to weep; a lot of people do, and it's not shameful, and nobody will mention it.

Do not try to speak. Not to test it. Not once.

That urge — to make a noise just to *prove* the silence is real — is the strongest impulse you will feel on the Sill, and every visitor feels it, and it is the exact equivalent of dragging a key down a fresco. Name it in yourself now, in advance, so you recognize it when it arrives wearing the costume of curiosity.

Ascend slowly. Your ears will feel odd for an hour. And if you loved it in there and want to stay — that's the warning sign, not the good outcome. Come up.

## 10. If you make a noise

You may. Bodies are bodies.

**Do not gasp. Do not apologize out loud.** Freeze. Find the nearest tender. Get their eyes. Slate it: what, where, when. Give your name for the tag.

Do not conceal it. The seam will show anyway; that is the whole nature of this place; the evidence is physical and permanent and it will outlive your excuses. Concealment converts an accident into a fraud.

Expect grief rather than rage, or a cold flat anger that is worse than rage. A block is a career: frames set by an apprentice, cut the year they retire, name and year on the brass, the countersignature belonging to someone not yet born. Your dropped buckle may have defaced the work of a person already dead. You cannot buy that back. Offer work, not money — carry, hold, watch the sky, pollinate, sit the shutters. Do the boring thing for as long as they'll let you. Then leave, and don't tell the story as an anecdote.

## 11. Conduct, gifts, and souvenirs

**The Loud Age.** On the terrace above the north wall there's a flywheel half-swallowed by moss, a boiler shell, crusher footings, and a rail ending in a fern. There's a hand-wheel that still turns.

Don't turn it. It is not an interactive exhibit. It is a headstone for forty years of unsellable cloudy stone, and it is also a rusted machine coupled to a rock full of growing blocks. Look at it. Let the fern in the firebox do the talking.

**Chalk.** Don't erase anything. Don't add anything. The label *the boiler* is a hundred years old and is not yours to tidy.

**Gifts.** Squirrel hair and fine brush stock — the pollinating brushes wear out and every tender carries one. Needles, soft leather, felt, wool yarn, good chalk, brass stock, lamp glass and wicks, spectacle lenses and magnifiers, wound dressings, dental supplies, antitussives, paper, soft pencils. Books are quiet and welcome.

A bell, a music box, a wind chime, a whistle-toy for a child: obscenities. Don't be the traveler who brings a gift that makes noise to a place that sells its absence.

**Souvenirs.** Never pick up a shard. A fragment of hush is a piece of somebody's forty years. Buy legitimately from a lowland factor, or ask the gauge-house whether an offcut with a known history can be sold to you. The boiler block is not for sale and it is rude to ask twice.

Understand what you'd be buying: an acoustic photograph. A decade of a place's silence, with the weather in it. Some travelers find they cannot keep it in the house.

**The lark.** Go out to the band once, at dawn, and listen to it. It is the loudest thing on the Sill and no one who works there has ever heard it. That is a strange gift to be holding. Some yardmen will ask you what it sounds like; some will very plainly not want to know. Read the room. And whatever you do, don't record it and carry the recording inward.

## 12. What to actually do there

Nothing happens. A centimeter a year. The plates don't track; the sun crosses them. The fog runs four in, two out. If you go expecting event, you will manufacture event, and manufacturing event is the one crime.

So plan for at least one full fog cycle — six days — and preferably two or three. Learn **one cup**, not eighty kilometers. Learn ten signs a day. Sit on a terrace for an hour and watch condensate bead and run down a harp-string into a gutter. Hand-pollinate the bell-flowers with the brush until it becomes the thing your hands do while your mind is elsewhere; that's the local habit and it's the fastest way to stop being a visitor. Read cut blocks in the gauge-house: the annual banding, the pale scar of a hailstorm, the gray smear of the season the fog came late and people talked too much on the terraces. Ask about the boiler. Ask whose name is on the oldest tag and whether the countersigner has been born yet.

Bring patience the way you'd bring water. It is the consumable you will run out of first.

## 13. Coming out

Walk out to the lark band before you speak. Let the first sound you make be in a place where it costs nothing.

Expect it to go strangely. People come up hoarse from six days of not using the throat, or unable to speak for hours, or laughing in a way they can't stop, or crying without much idea why. Expect to keep signing. Expect to sign in your sleep.

Then plan a quiet week on the other side, because the ordinary world is going to hit you like a wall: the refrigerator, the traffic, the drone of a ventilation fan, a person talking on a phone about nothing. You have spent six days in a place where a dropped wrench costs a decade, and you are going to come home to a world that makes that much noise before breakfast and doesn't even notice. Some travelers find they can't tolerate a city for a month. Some find they can't tolerate it afterward at all, and go back, and take a job on the shutters.

If you can legitimately buy a little hush, line a room with it. Not as a trophy. As a place to sit and remember that you were once, for six days, a thing that had to be careful.

---

## The ten rules, for your slate

1. Your ears are not the instrument of record. Behave as if grade one, everywhere.
2. Never walk in front of your guide. The holes are invisible.
3. Goggles on before you step onto the pavement. Two pairs.
4. Nothing that oscillates. Nothing that glints. Nothing that rustles.
5. Never touch the wire, the copper, the frames, the vats, or the salamanders.
6. Lamp dark, hooded, at your feet. Never signal.
7. Mouth shut. Nose breathing. Throat cleared at the rim.
8. Bring all your water. Leave nothing behind, anywhere — there is no away.
9. Learn the rain code before you descend. During a shutter: still, silent, small.
10. Never make a noise to prove the silence is real.

They will be watching you, and they will be kind about it, and they will be able to prove exactly what you did. Go carefully. It's the only place I know where care is a physical substance you can hold up to a lamp.
