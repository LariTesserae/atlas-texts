# Preparing for the Hollow Plain

## 1. Understand what you are visiting before you pack anything

You are not going to a landscape. You are going to the inside of an organ, and the organ is being played by weather, and you will be standing on the pipes.

Three facts should reorganize your thinking:

**The vertical is forbidden.** Not by law — by wind. Everything that survives here survives by being flat. You are a tall thing. You will be the tallest thing for nine days in every direction, and the plain will treat you the way it treats every tall thing: it will try to knock you down, dry you out, blind you with grit, and put you in a hole.

**The country is indoors.** The interesting life, the water, the warmth, the quiet, the crowds — all of it is below the surface. The plain above is a roof. You are going to spend your visit walking on a roof full of skylights, some of which are load-bearing and some of which are not, and you will not be able to hear the difference.

**Your ears are the wrong instrument here.** Sound in air belongs to the plain. Sound in rock belongs to people. If you arrive planning to listen with your ears and speak with your mouth, you will be deaf, mute, illiterate, and dangerous to yourself and everyone near you. Plan instead to become a person who hears with their palms, heels, and jaw, and speaks with their hands and a hammer.

Do not go for a week. This is not a place that can be sampled. Budget a season at minimum — a year if you want to be something other than a hazard. The honest version of this trip is an apprenticeship in one district, with a family, learning one tower's note and forty throats' names.

## 2. Choose your arrival, not just your destination

Do not open onto the open plain. Do not open at the escarpment. Do not, under any circumstances, open at the silence lake — you will meet the two most overwhelming things in this world in the same second, at altitude, unacclimatized, alone.

There is trade here. There must be: cord, fiber, pitch, fired clay, metal, timber for stakes and brushes — none of that grows on a plain with no trees. Somebody carries it up three thousand paces of cliff from the cloud floor. **Find the trade road and come up it, slowly, with people who know you are coming.** It buys you three things you cannot buy later: weeks of gradual ascent, an introduction, and a guide who has an obligation to keep you alive.

If you must arrive directly, arrive at dawn, in the lee of a turf-roofed house, low to the ground, inside a stopped field if you can find one, with your hands free and visible.

## 3. The body

### Air
Water boils lukewarm. Take that literally and be frightened by it. Whatever the number turns out to be, treat this as higher than any mountain on your Earth, and treat "go down" as unavailable: the only rapid descent is over a cliff into cloud, in wind, without a route. On any normal high mountain, altitude illness is treated by descending. Here it is treated by having prepared properly, or by dying.

So: pre-acclimatize for weeks before you arrive. Hypoxic sleeping if you have access to it; otherwise a staged ascent through a neighboring high world, or the trade road. Carry the standard altitude kit and know the signs of fluid in the lungs and swelling in the brain — and then accept the specific horror of this place, which is that **you will not be able to hear yourself breathe.** You cannot hear your own exertion. You cannot hear your partner's cough. Nobody can hear you complain. Bring a pulse oximeter, wear it where you can see it, and make a rule with your companions: everyone checks numbers, out loud on paper, three times a day, whether or not they feel fine. Silent hypoxia in a place with no voices is how competent people walk into holes.

### Ears
Assume the roar is above the pain threshold and well into the damage range near a cluster of mouths, and that it contains a great deal of energy below the range of hearing. That energy is the hand on your sternum. It is also the trembling in your teeth, the smear in your vision, the nausea, and the reason you will not sleep for the first three nights.

Plugs and muffs together, worn from the moment of arrival, not from the moment it starts to hurt. Understand their limits honestly: real-world attenuation is much less than the number on the box, and bone conduction sets a floor you cannot get below, and **no earplug touches infrasound.** You will still feel the plain in your chest with your ears sealed. What you get is the difference between losing your hearing in a season and losing it in a week.

Get a baseline audiogram before you go. Get another when you come back. Decide in advance, in writing, how much permanent loss you are willing to trade for this, and set a daily exposure budget in hours and keep it like a ration. Spend the rest of the day in stopped fields and in the dusk two body-lengths down, where the roar falls to a hum.

There is a compensation, and it is the key to the whole place: **sealing your ears makes you more local, not less.** The airborne roar is the plain's. The taps come through rock, into bone, and earplugs barely touch them. Plugged, you stop straining to hear and start feeling, and the ground begins to speak. Locals arrive at this by loss. You can arrive at it by choice, and it is the closest thing to an initiation you will be offered.

Choose filtered plugs rather than airtight ones, and learn to equalize your ears properly. Pressure fronts here turn the throats inside out; the whole district modulates; you will be descending shafts through it. Sealed ears plus a fast pressure swing plus a body-length of descent is a barotrauma. Never descend with a head cold. Carry a decongestant.

### Eyes and skin
Tropical latitude, extreme altitude, a bruised-blue sky with almost nothing in it, and a varnished black floor that throws the sun back like water. You will be lit from below as well as above. This is snow blindness without snow.

Category-4 glacier goggles with side shields, two spares, no contact lenses. Brim plus face and neck covering. Sunscreen under the chin and inside the nostrils and on the backs of the hands, reapplied hourly, and a stick of lip balm in every pocket. Gloves always — you will be putting your palms on stone hot enough to burn them.

Grit columns and hail mean sealed goggles, not sunglasses, whenever the light on the stone changes.

### Teeth, hands, feet
Jaw-listening ruins teeth over a lifetime. Do not press your teeth to rock. Press the bone behind your ear, or bite a soft wooden rod and let the rod touch the stone. Locals will have a tool for this; ask for it rather than improvising with your incisors.

Years of hammer-work ruins hands. Pad the hammer haft. Learn to strike with the whole arm rather than the wrist.

Nine days of unbroken hard rock will destroy ordinary boots and ordinary feet. Bring two pairs, break them in, and bring more blister care than seems reasonable. Bring one pair of thin, soft, smooth-soled shoes for camp and for feeling the ground — you cannot read a tower through a stiff lug sole.

### Temperature
Noon stone burns; night dew freezes; fog is wet and supercooled; wind is constant; the air is thin and dry and strips you of water faster than you will believe. The classic death here is not exposure to cold or heat but the combination: soaked in fog, blown dry, hypothermic in the tropics at midday, unable to call out. Real insulation, real waterproof shell, and a wide-brimmed sun layer over the top of it.

## 4. The kit, and three laws about it

**Nothing tall. Nothing that flies. Nothing spiked.**

*Nothing tall* means no tent, no tripod, no antenna, no umbrella, no trekking poles, no fishing rod, no flag. A tent is a sail attached to your body, and the plain has holes to put you in. Shelter here is a low bivvy sack, weighted and staked, in a stopped field or a shaft's mid-ledge, or a guest place in a turf house with its door on the lee side. If you want to build, build the way they build: prow-shaped windward wall, no window facing the weather, roof of turf, everything under a body-length in height.

*Nothing that flies* means no drone, no kite, no glider, no flying entry vehicle. Nothing here is pollinated by flight; flight cannot hold station in this wind. The first flying machine on this plain would be either a god or a pest, and either would be a catastrophe. Walk in.

*Nothing spiked* is the law you will be tempted to break, and it is the most important one in this document. **The caprock is a skin. Where the skin is breached, a hole begins.** Every sharp point you set down is the seed of a throat, and a throat is a permanent alteration to a hundred-million-year-old surface and to the chord that everyone navigates by. So: no crampons, no ice axes, no lug soles, no pole tips, no tent pegs driven into unbroken stone. Smooth soles. A broad flat-faced hammer, not a pick. Brush the grit out of your tread every time you stop — grit trapped under a boot is sandpaper on the skin. Walk on already-bare rock and on established ways. Your footprints here are geological acts.

The rest of the list:

- **A stone hammer of local pattern**, ideally bought or made there, so its voice matches what people expect to feel. Plus a heavier one for distress. Plus a listening rod — wood or bone — for jaw and mastoid contact.
- **Earplugs (many), muffs, spare filters.**
- **Sealed goggles ×3, sun protection in bulk, gloves with leather palms, knee and elbow pads that tolerate hot stone.** You will crawl more than you expect.
- **Rope: static line, harness, ascender, descender, a dozen slings, and lip pads.** Rope over a friable caprock edge is a rope cut in half. Anchor back from the mouth, on sounded rock, and pad everything.
- **A sounding probe and a flat hammer** for reading roofs, and the humility to know that sounding is probabilistic.
- **Two barometers, an altimeter, and a hypsometer** — a boiling-point thermometer. It measures the pressure, it tells you the truth about your altitude, and it doubles as your reading on how the throats are breathing. In this country a barometer is not a weather toy. It is the instrument that tells you whether the ground is inhaling.
- **A real gas meter** (oxygen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide), batteries kept warm against your body. Not a candle: at this altitude a flame is already guttering and will lie to you. Remember that 20.9% oxygen is still hypoxic up here, and that a percentage of carbon dioxide that would be merely unpleasant at sea level is dangerous in thin air.
- **Water: purification, not volume.** A 0.1-micron filter plus chemical disinfection with generous contact time, a small bailer and thirty metres of light cord for lowering a vessel into a throat instead of climbing down one, and containers for eight litres.
- **Food that needs only hot water**, and a small pressure cooker if you insist on real cooking. Nothing raw-dried that requires a rolling boil. No beans. No rice. Assume every recipe you know is wrong here.
- **Fuel.** There is no wood, and the mat is not fuel — it is a hundred years of growth per handspan and the roof of somebody's house. Carry fuel or eat cold.
- **Cord. More cord than you think.** Cord is the difference between walking home in a whiteout and not walking home. Stakes for it, too. Never cut, borrow, or move an existing line.
- **Bright, high-contrast gloves** so your hands are legible against dark rock, and a small lamp for signing in a shaft's dusk.
- **A slate and a wax pencil**, and drawings — not text — for anything you need strangers to understand.
- **A closed-cell pad with a reflective face, and a low tensioned mesh cot** that lifts your body a hand's breadth off the rock. This is not luxury. Sleeping directly on a resonating slab is whole-body vibration; it prevents sleep the way noise prevents sleep, and no earplug fixes it. Ask locally for a quiet bed; they will have solved this better than you have.
- **A tuner, a set of reeds, a cheap geophone or contact microphone** — for learning the district's chord, for identifying towers, and, if you are invited, for archiving it.
- **A heliograph mirror.** In that sun, on that flatness, it is the only signal you have that travels faster than a person.
- **Tethers.** Short cords to clip yourself to a partner, to a line, to a stake. Rope up on the surface like a glacier party, for entirely different reasons.

## 5. Water and food, and the trap in the middle of it

There is no surface water for nine days. All the water is inside the rock, in black pools under overhangs, tasting of compost and iron, each one the drain of its own little funnel of catchment.

The trap: **boiling does not sterilize here.** Water boils lukewarm. You will watch it bubble and believe you have made it safe and you will have made warm soup of snail and liverwort and ten thousand years of rot. Filter *and* disinfect, with long contact times, or use a pressure cooker. Never drink from a pool you have not been told about. Some throats are gardens, some are latrines, some are mineral-poisoned, and they look identical from above.

Lower a bailer. Do not descend for water. Descending for water is how you end up in a flask-shaped chamber at the bottom of a funnel during a rain you did not see coming.

Waste: learn the latrine protocol on your first day and treat it as sacred. Every living throat drains a catchment that somebody drinks from. **Never relieve yourself inside a funnel, never wash with soap in a shaft, never drop anything into a mouth.** Use designated dead throats, or pack out. A dropped carabiner is gone forever and may have cracked a floor on the way down.

## 6. Skills to acquire before you arrive

**A sign language.** Any one, then adapt. Children here read hands before they walk; the local hand-speech is a full language with grammar and register and obscenity. Which means your casual gesticulation is *speech*, and you will say things you did not intend. Learn before you wave. Keep your hands visible and free — this changes how you carry everything.

**The knock code.** Not a novelty; the primary channel. Practice sending and receiving through the palms, through the heels, and through a rod held to the bone behind your ear, blindfolded, until you can hold a conversation with your ears sealed. Assume dialects by district. Assume your accent is atrocious and audible for miles.

Which raises a thing nobody warns outsiders about: **the caprock carries a tap for miles, and your walking is a tap.** An irregular, heavy, foreign gait is a stranger shouting gibberish into everyone's floor at three in the morning. Learn to walk quietly, or learn the local rhythm, or learn to say *I am a guest, I am coming, I mean nothing* with your feet.

**Vertical caving, properly taught.** Single-rope technique, rebelay, rope pads, bad-air protocol, flood protocol, hauling. The downward-growing vine is not a rope and you must not trust it, however obvious it looks.

**Sounding rock.** Learn from a local, with a hammer, on ground where a mistake costs a bruise. Then learn how often it is wrong. Roofs spall in the night — a bang nobody hears, a mouth where there was a floor. Treat every square metre of unbroken-looking ground as provisional, forever, including the ground you slept on last night.

**Blindfold cord-walking.** Until you can go home by touch, in cold wet fog, with numb hands, without looking. This is not an optional extra. It is the standard local commute.

**Functioning deaf.** Spend a week sealed in plugs and muffs in the loudest machine room you can find. Learn to work with hands and eyes and touch, to notice that you have stopped hearing alarms, to stop turning your head. Learn how much of your safety monitoring you have been outsourcing to your ears without knowing.

**Rope-tug codes.** All the standard climbing calls are useless. *On belay* does not exist here. Agree tug codes, use them religiously, keep line of sight, and never trust a click you cannot hear.

## 7. Discipline on the surface

**Read the light on the stone.** Gust walls come in from the escarpment and you can watch them approaching as a change in the sheen. Everything you do up here is subordinate to that. When the light changes: get low, get tethered, get your goggles on, and get away from lips.

**Travel at dawn and late afternoon.** Tropical high country: mornings comparatively calm, afternoons convective, hundreds of dust devils walking the flats. Midday is for being underground or inside a stopped field. When a devil comes over you, lie down, cover your eyes, hold your line, and stay down until the grit stops.

**Hail is the hard stop.** Hail means convective storm, and a convective storm on a plain where you are the only vertical object means lightning, on iron-rich rock, with ground current. This is the one place where the rules conflict: wind wants you prone, lightning wants you small-contact and crouched, holes want your weight spread and your body tethered. Resolve it in this order — get off the open plain first, into a stopped field or a turf house or a shallow depression; if you cannot, crouch low on your insulating pad with your feet together, tethered, away from lips, and accept the wind. If your hair rises or anything buzzes, you have seconds, not minutes.

**Never on the surface at night, never in fog without a line.** The mats go stiff with dew, the varnished rock is slick when wet, the shafts draw inward, and the ground gives way somewhere in the dark and lets a little more of the world out. You will not hear it. Nobody does.

**Never step on the mat.** Black-green bosses hard as boot leather, wool-wrapped rosettes, lichens in ten thousand shades — a huge tonnage of life with no height, growing at the pace of geology. It is also the roof, the water-catcher, the pasture, and the pollinator's floor. Walk on bare stone. Green rings mark mouths: **the collar is the warning.** Learn to read collars at a glance, in glare, in fog, at dusk, until it is as automatic as reading a stair edge.

**Do not plug or unplug a hole.** A stopped field is engineered agriculture, generations of clay and cord and pitch, making a basin of relative quiet where things can grow and people can sleep. Plugging a throat silences a voice, redirects water, and kills a biome. Unplugging one drains somebody's garden and drowns a district's note. Both are acts of violence with a hundred-year tail.

**Never camp within a stone's throw of a tower, and never climb one.** The beacon is also a siren. And your body in a flue changes the note — which means for twenty miles in every direction, travellers are being told a lie about where home is.

## 8. Discipline below

The shafts are the reason to come: two body-lengths down the roar becomes a hum you can talk over; four, and it is warm humid dusk with ferns in shelves and pale flowers angled at the mouth like reflectors; six, and black water, liverwort fur, compost and iron, the heat of a body. It is crowded, and it is the most dangerous ground on the plain, because it is a bottle with a drain at the top.

- **Never alone. Minimum three** on any descent: one to stay with a casualty, one to go for help, one who is the casualty.
- **Go at dawn.** Fog condensing, threads running in, throats actively breathing, surface cool, light coming. Avoid the stagnant afternoon, when the outside air is hotter than the shaft's and nothing moves down there. Avoid the hour after a pressure front.
- **Meter first, always.** Carbon dioxide pools in still bottoms; ten thousand years of slowly burning green has been consuming oxygen down there since before anyone had a spine. Side galleries going off into the dark under roofs held up by nothing anyone would trust are not for you. Ever.
- **Watch the sky, not the water.** After brief violent rain the whole country drinks in an hour. Every shaft is the drain of a funnel. You are in the neck of the funnel. Be out before the first hail rattles on the mats — that is the local rule and it is not negotiable.
- **Anchor back, pad the lip, stay above the waterline.** Never enter a pool. A flask with an overhang is unrecoverable, and the surface film you break is somebody's whole world.
- **Sterilize between throats and between districts.** These are isolated pockets; each may hold things that exist nowhere else. Your boots and rope are a vector. Follow the locals' brush-and-resin protocol exactly — the resin is probably doing more than sticking pollen.

## 9. Navigation

You cannot navigate by sound in air, because sound has no direction here. You navigate by **pitch, felt in the soles of the feet**, and by **cairns and cords**, and by the **grain of the chord**.

Learn one tower's note first, in your bones, before you go anywhere. Then learn the gradient: **the throats near the escarpment are the highest and thinnest voices left. If the world is getting higher and thinner, you are walking toward the cliff. Turn until the world lowers.** That single rule will save your life if you learn nothing else.

Three cautions:

- **Towers overblow.** A flue-driven pipe in a strong gust can jump register — an octave, a fifth. Do not mistake your home tower screaming high for a different tower. Confirm with the soles of your feet and with the light.
- **Old pitch charts are wrong.** The chord has fallen a fifth in living memory and is still falling; throats widen, drop in pitch, and eventually sing below hearing. Any chart of notes more than a generation old is a historical document, not a map. Re-learn everything on arrival, and date your own notes.
- **Do not trust a compass** on iron-rich caprock, and do not trust a shadow stick at noon in the tropics. Pace count, bearing taken morning and evening, cairns, cords, hypsometer, and the pitch gradient. And **never build an unauthorized cairn and never knock one down.** A false beacon in a country with no landmarks is a murder weapon.

## 10. People

Assume you will startle everyone you meet, constantly, because you arrive without sound. Approach in view, from the front, low, hands visible. Learn the touch etiquette before you touch anyone. **Never touch a person from behind at the lip of a hole.**

Do not shout. Do not cover your mouth while signing. Do not turn away mid-sentence. Do not hurry anyone. The old people's calm, slightly tilted patience is not slowness; it is the manners of people who have a richer tactile world than you do and are waiting for you to catch up. Do not pity the deafness. Nobody here is missing anything. They will pity your ears, which need protecting, and your feet, which cannot read.

Gifts that make sense: cord, needles, fine wire, metal, mirrors and lenses (they angle flowers to throw daylight down shafts — optics are worth more than gold here), lamps, wax, cloth, brushes of good fiber, medicines, ear protection, sunglasses. Not seeds. Not soil. Not anything alive. Not anything that flies.

**Offer your hands for pollination, and only under instruction.** The people are the plain's principal animal and they know it; the flowers at the mouths wait for a brush and a smear of resin, and the crosses are probably lineage, property, and ritual as much as agriculture. You are being invited into the reproductive system of an entire world. Ask whose throat it is. Ask what the brush may touch. Use their tools, not yours. Take no flower, no snail, no lichen, no stone from a lip.

## 11. The silence lake

One basin of unbroken harder rock. No holes, no roar, a shallow lake, the only surface water for nine days. The lapping is the loudest thing in the world. Children are brought here to be shown what silence is; most weep; some cannot stop and are carried home.

Treat this as a medical event and a rite, in that order.

- **It may not be open to visitors.** Ask. It might be the place children are made into adults, and you might be a tourist at a communion.
- **Never go alone, and never go tired, dehydrated, or altitude-sick.** Go with someone who has done it and who is willing to carry you.
- **Decompress first.** Days in stopped fields and shaft dusk, ears gradually unsealed, before you stand on unbroken rock. Do not walk out of a cluster of mouths and into silence in one afternoon.
- **Expect your own body to be deafening** — heartbeat, blood, breath, and a tinnitus you have been masking since arrival. Expect grief without an object. Do not fight it and do not make decisions in it.
- **First visit: minutes. Sitting down. Tethered. With a plan for leaving.** Some people cannot stop, and the being-carried is not a metaphor.
- **The bird lands here. Nowhere else.** An enormous slow thing that spends its life on the wing because there is nothing to land on and no shelter to land in, and this is its only ground. Approach low, downwind, slowly, and do not raise yourself to your full height to get a better look. You would be the first tall thing to ever stand beside it.

## 12. The edge

Don't.

Three thousand paces of cliff into a floor of cloud with its own weather; wind pouring over the lip; the highest, thinnest, most unbearable voices; nobody living within a day's walk, which means no towers, no cords, no cairns, no help, no water anyone will tell you about. The rock there is being undercut as you watch. There is no shelter to land in and nothing to hold.

They say there are trees below. There probably are. That is a different journey, with different preparation, and it starts from the trade road with a guide, not from the lip with a rope and an idea.

## 13. When it goes wrong

You cannot shout. You cannot whistle. Nobody can hear you and you cannot hear the rescue.

- **The ground is the channel.** Tap. Keep tapping. The caprock carries a tap for miles and people feel it in their palms and their beds. Learn the distress rhythm before you need it, and carry the heavy hammer.
- **Visual backup:** heliograph by day, a bright weighted cloth spread flat, a lamp at night.
- **If lost: stop walking.** Get low, get out of the wind, get tethered to something, put your jaw rod to a boulder and find a tower. Read the pitch gradient before you take another step. People die here by continuing.
- **If someone goes into a hole:** do not follow them down. That is the second casualty. Meter, anchor, pad, haul, and send the third person for the district.
- **If altitude illness:** you have no descent. Oxygen if you brought it, drugs, warmth, rest, and the trade road if you can be moved. This is why the acclimatization was not optional.

## 14. The thing you should understand before you decide to go

The chord is a clock. Roofs spall and new notes enter the world; throats widen and their voices drop; the last stage of every hole is to become so broad and shallow that it sings below hearing. The old people say it has fallen a full fifth since their grandmothers. Erosion finished flattening this place and moved on to hollowing it, and the hollowing ends in silence. The noise you cannot bear is the sound of a mountain range's roots being converted, patiently, into void.

You are visiting a moment. A deafening, temporary moment, on its way out.

Two consequences for your preparation.

**Do not try to fix it.** Do not propose plugging, engineering, stabilizing, quieting. The plain's unmaking is also its making; a green collar forms around every new mouth within a season. Nothing here needs your intervention and everything here would be destroyed by it.

**Bring an instrument and offer to measure.** A tuner, a contact microphone, a notebook with dates. The falling fifth is memory, and memory is data, and a traveller who can carry a number to the next district and bring back another one is carrying something these people cannot carry themselves — the plain has no way to hear its own history. Ask permission first. The chord may be owned, or sacred, or somebody's grandmother. If you are allowed, record it, and leave the record where it belongs.

## 15. What follows you home

Some hearing, permanently gone. Probably tinnitus. A habit of tapping floors. A flinch when a room is quiet. The phantom hum at night, and waking to listen for a tower that is not there. Hands that keep talking after your mouth has started. A permanent, incurable distrust of any ground you have not sounded.

And one fixed note, in the soles of your feet, that you will find yourself checking for in every world you visit afterward — the way home, in a country where nothing stands up.

### Ten rules, if you remember nothing else

1. Come up the trade road, slowly, with an introduction.
2. Nothing tall, nothing that flies, nothing spiked.
3. Boiling does not make water safe here. Filter and disinfect.
4. Green collar means mouth. Sounding means *maybe*.
5. If the voices get thinner, you are walking toward the cliff.
6. Seal your ears and open your hands.
7. Be out of every throat before the first hail.
8. Never alone; never at night; never without a line in fog.
9. Ask before you touch a flower, a cord, a cairn, or a person.
10. Go to the lake sitting down, with someone who can carry you.
