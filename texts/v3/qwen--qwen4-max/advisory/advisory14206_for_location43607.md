# Preparing for the Black Plateau

## 0. The one sentence

Everywhere else you have travelled, the difficulty was getting noticed. Here the difficulty is being *legible*. You will be located within seconds of arrival — you are a warm, wet, oxidising, rhythmically thumping object standing on a floor that listens for a living. Nothing you do will make you invisible. The only choice available to you is whether the thing that finds you can pronounce you.

Prepare to be a sound, not a sight.

---

## 1. Verify the wet-bulb temperature before anything else

This is the boring paragraph that decides whether you go at all.

"Warm as breath, wetter than breath" is either poetry or a death sentence, and you must find out which. Breath is roughly 34–35 °C at saturation. If the plateau's air is genuinely at that wet-bulb temperature, a resting human cannot shed metabolic heat by any means — not by sweating, not by fanning, not by shade, not by lying still. You would cook in six hours doing nothing at all.

If it is 31–32, you can live here, slowly, and everything below applies.

The demography argues for the second reading. One hut per fifty kilometres. People who read stone with their palms instead of walking to it. A culture whose central skill is *listening from where you already are*. This is not a place that rewards exertion. Assume the locals are not lazy; assume they are at the edge of their thermal envelope and have arranged their entire civilisation around not exceeding it.

So: travel on the **Draw**, when cool air is pouring down the mouths and the whole country's pitch is falling. Rest through the **Let**, when the exhale comes up hot and loaded. Eleven hours, drifting. Do not attempt to keep a twenty-four-hour day; you will be fighting the only clock anyone here respects, and you will lose, and you will be exhausted at the wrong time.

And ask, at your first hut, for **the cold face**. Every heat exchanger has two sides. The combs are hectares of plate taking heat out of the deep breath; somewhere on each comb there is a surface that is *cold*, and it will be wet with condensate, and it will be the most valuable square metre you will ever stand on. I would bet a great deal that the huts are built against them. I would bet the condensate is what people drink. I would bet that "the cold face" is a word in the local language with a shrine's grammar around it. Find it, sit against it, and do not take more of it than you are given.

## 2. Your body, itemised

**Teeth.** Get your dental work checked and re-seated before you cross. A loose filling under sustained sub-40 Hz drive is a small, unrelenting torture, and there is no dentist within a hundred kilometres, and the person who could help you is deaf and will not understand what you are complaining about.

**Eyes.** The water in them will vibrate. Even if there were light, you would not see well. There is no light. Bring no lamp; if you bring one, bring it as a gift and never turn it on. Protect the eyes anyway — sealed goggles or a veil — because warm saturated air carrying spore is an invitation to fungal keratitis, and you will not be able to keep your hands off your face as reliably as you think.

Expect **prisoner's cinema**: within days, your visual system, starved, will start manufacturing. Grids, drifting blobs, faces, a lit doorway. It is not madness and it is not the plateau. Learn the difference in training so you do not waste your one emergency gong on a hallucination.

**Ears.** The wall of noise is continuous and probably 85–100 dB across a wide band. You will lose hearing. Everyone here does; the deaf are the best of them, which is not a coincidence but a career path. Bring flat, low-value attenuators and use them sparingly, because the thing that saves your life is a *notch in the clapper sleet* — high-frequency, subtle, and exactly what cheap protection smears out. Never wear anything that muffles you without also giving you a voice (see §5).

**Gut.** The entire food web is built on sulfide oxidation. It is lush the way a wound is lush. Assume nothing here is food for you: heavy metals, reduced sulfur, novel proteins, novel fungi. Carry all calories. Milkwood latex ferments in minutes and is almost certainly a local staple and sacrament; do not drink it in your first season, and when you do, drink it in company, in a small amount, having been told what it is for.

**Water.** Everything is supersaturated carbonate and the rain is the process's exhaust — black, faintly oily, iron and cracked pepper. Rainwater is not clean water here; it is *tailings*. Groundwater will stone you internally if you drink it for a year. Comb condensate is distilled by the machine and is the best water on the plateau. Otherwise: let it stand, let it degas and drop its load, decant, filter, and accept the kidney stones as a cost of the trip.

**Skin and hands.** The grey varnish takes four days to wear off and will not wear off if you are here continuously. You will be black on top and gritty beneath — under the jaw, under the nails, in the folds of the ears, on the underside of your forearms. This is not dirt. This is the local rule about orientation applying to you, and it means a tender can tell how you were lying when you died by running a thumb over you. Make peace with that early.

Keep your hands bare. You cannot read this world through gloves. Accept the infections, carry antiseptic, and adopt the local discipline if there is one — I would guess something like *one hand for the world, one hand for your mouth*.

**Lungs.** Gill-towers shed spore you can taste. You will develop something — a cough, a hypersensitivity, a permanent pepper at the back of the tongue. A respirator is sensible and a respirator is also a muzzle: it changes your voice, dulls your click, and makes you quieter, and *quieter is the shape of a predator*. If you filter, you must voice the filter. Fit a resonator to the exhale. Arrive already sounding like something.

**Air chemistry.** Sulfide deadens your sense of smell within minutes at dangerous concentrations, and carbon dioxide pools in every low place in a karst country. The doline floors, the lee of the mats, the throats of the mouths on the Let: these are where you die without noticing. Never descend into a hollow without a test, and never trust your nose, because your nose will be lying to you by the time you need it.

**Your own emissions.** In a spectrum where every band is owned, your body is an unlicensed broadcaster: heartbeat, breath, gut, knees, and above all **tinnitus**. Get an audiogram before you go and learn your own ringing frequency. If it sits in an owned band, you will be telling a lie continuously, forever, and you will not be able to stop. That is worth knowing before you commit.

## 3. Training, before you cross

Give this a year. Six months minimum. The plateau does not punish ignorance; it punishes *reflexes*, and reflexes take longer to change than beliefs.

**Blindfold living.** Not an afternoon. Months. Cook, dress, walk, work, find things, count things, with your eyes shut, until your hands and ears are the primary channel and your eyes are a hobby. You are not learning to cope with darkness; you are learning to stop *waiting for light*.

**Pitch.** Train interval and absolute pitch discrimination down to a few cents. You need to hear the mains hum as a *note*, not a condition, because that note is your position, your border, your passport, and your name. Carry a monochord or a set of tuned clinker stones and learn to match a drone by ear in under thirty seconds. Then learn to hear a slow glissando over forty minutes — that is the Draw beginning, and it is the most important event in your day.

**The strike test.** Get a hammer. Strike limestone, strike steel, strike steel sheathed in ten centimetres of limestone, strike a rotted cable under a thigh of tufa. Learn the ring durations in your hands. This is how you tell a stack from a stack, a ladder from a lie, a rail ridge from a live line. It is the single most useful physical skill you can bring and it costs you a weekend to start and a lifetime to finish.

**The walk.** The mat gives a hand's width and then firms; it is a floor the way a frozen pond is a floor. Sump-eels hunt by footfall and strike up through the crust. So you must learn a gait that is **loud in the air and silent in the ground**: continuous contact, sliding, weight spread, no heel-strike, no impact, no standing still in ankle water. Practise on soft wet ground, blindfolded, until you can cross it without a single thump. Two channels, opposite rules. Air-noise is identity. Ground-noise is dinner.

**The startle drill.** This is the one that must be automatic. Blindfolded, in a dense sound field, when a notch opens in the sleet and begins to move toward you: **make an enormous noise immediately.** Not freeze. Not listen harder. Not wait to be sure. Your body will want to hold still and hope, and that is precisely the response that kills people here. Train it like a weapons drill, with a partner opening gaps in a noise field at random, until the gong is in your hand and ringing before you have finished being afraid. Four metres at walking pace is about three seconds. You do not have time to decide.

**The tactile dialect.** Learn hand-to-stone *first*, not last. It is older than the spoken language, it belongs to the best people on the plateau, and it is the only language that works inside a hush — which is to say, it is the only language that works when you are dying. If you arrive with one thing, arrive with that.

## 4. Kit philosophy

Nothing you read with your eyes. Nothing with a moving part you cannot chip clean. Nothing you cannot afford to have become stone.

Everything here lacquers on top and accretes beneath. Zippers seize. Hinges freeze. Bearings grow a mineral beard. The tenders' entire working life is Sisyphus with a mallet, and yours will be too, so: **bring a mallet, bring hardened files and scrapers, bring spring steel, bring wire instead of rope where you can, and bring as few mechanisms as possible.** A clockwork drone beats a battery drone. A bowed string beats a speaker. A gong beats a siren.

Metal: your alloys are entering hot wet sulfide. Copper and silver will blacken before you have finished arriving. Stainless is not stainless here. Titanium if you can, and conformal-coat anything electrical, and then assume it dies anyway, because you cannot dump its waste heat into breath-warm saturated air.

Rope: essential, and never trust one you find. There are cables out there sheathed in tufa thick as a thigh whose cores rotted four centuries ago, and ladders bolted to towers that dissolved out from under them, still bolted, ending in air. Strike everything. Every time.

Instruments must speak or buzz. A gas detector with a screen is a paperweight; a gas detector that changes pitch is a companion — *but its alarm tone must be legal*. A novel tone in an owned band is either a challenge, a lie, or a dinner bell. Have a local tune your alarms. This is a normal request and probably a service with a fee.

And bring a **hum you can wear home**: a transducer, a chest plate, a driver that can put forty cycles into your sternum. Record the mains at your home key with equipment that goes below twenty hertz — ordinary gear will not, and an ordinary recording of this place is a photograph of a symphony. You will need that plate. See §10.

## 5. Arrival: the first ten seconds

You will arrive already located. Assume a notch is opening at four metres, because the description of this place ends that way and descriptions of places usually end where the trouble starts.

**Cross over loud.** Start your noise before you transition, if your method allows. Arrive as a *crash* — broadband, rude, enormous. Not a note. You do not yet know the local tonic, and a pure tone in the wrong key is worse than silence: it is a hole with an opinion. Broadband says *large thing, here, now*, which is the only sentence a husher respects and the only sentence that needs no translation.

Then, in order:

1. **Keep sounding.** Gong, then voice, then a continuous drone. Do not stop to orient. Orientation is what the drone is for.
2. **Get your feet honest.** Are you on mat or stone? Is there water? Is the mat ringing under you? Slide, don't step.
3. **Find the hum.** Match it. Thirty seconds. Now you have a key.
4. **Tune your voice into a notch** between the machine bands. Now you are pronounceable.
5. **Listen for the breath.** Rising or falling? If it is turning, get away from anything with lips of tufa and a wet sound at the back.
6. **Then, and only then,** take stock of your gear.

Expect company. An emergency crash carries for kilometres and blanks the partition; it is a flare. Someone will come, and you will have to explain, and "I have just arrived and I did not know the key" is — I would guess — a thing that has happened before, and there may be a word for it.

## 6. The law of the partition

The spectrum here is occupied like a reef. No gap unclaimed, no silence unowned. This is not scenery; it is *property*, and probably also law, and certainly also religion.

Do not emit in a band you do not own. Specifically:

- **Do not imitate a bearing tone.** A small brown animal hides inside that tone because it is the safest sound in the world, and the entire maintenance system is a population of those animals reporting drift. A false bearing tone sends a tender three valleys in the wrong direction, or makes her ignore a real failure. This is not a prank. This is poisoning a well.
- **Do not imitate the female moth's wing-thrum.** That is the deceptive orchid's trick, and it is the local definition of a lie that keeps working.
- **Do not imitate the far drums.** That is the calendar.
- **Do not whistle at a mouth.**
- **Do not play back recordings casually.** A recording here is a loaded object: a bearing tone is a false alarm, a name is a summons, a notch is a declaration of war. Treat your recorder as a weapon with a safety catch, and tell people you have one.

**Silence has geometry.** Learn to read it by touch and by ear:

- A felted grey patch on the **lee of a tower** — natural hush-moss. Dangerous but ordinary.
- A **roof** of it — architecture. Someone sleeps here.
- A **rectangle** of it on flat ground — a grave. The dead are buried quiet so nothing finds them by listening. Walk around. Do not stand on it and do not be silent on it.
- **Lines** of it running up a hillside — a cultivated approach route. Hushers plant these. In the eastern keys there are hillsides where the moss grows in lines. Leave. Not quickly and not quietly; just leave, sounding.
- A **moving** hole, four metres across, at walking pace — the thing itself. Gong. Now.

The principle underneath all of it: *a stationary silence with a known boundary is a building; a moving silence is a mouth.* So make your quiet architectural. Sleep in a shed, roofed in moss, with a **porch tone** left sounding outside so the shed is a note in the world and not a hole in it. Never be quietly moving. Never hold still and hope.

## 7. Moving through the country

Travel on the Draw. Rest on the Let. Shelter away from mouths when the country exhales; the exhale is hot, loaded, spore-heavy, and the reversal winds in a throat will take your ears or your lungs or you.

Never walk unroped over mat you have not sounded. Falling is the principal cause of death and there is a whole guild evolved to exploit it: fall-catchers strung sixty metres across the throats of sinkholes, sticky, patient, faintly luminous in a way that means nothing to anyone. They are farmed. They are also fed, in the old way, which has its own verb. **Do not be a stranger near a mouth with people who have not named you.** Travel with a sponsor. Be useful early. A person who chips stone is a person; a person who has arrived and contributed nothing is a quantity of meat with an unusual sound.

The white rail ridge — four hundred kilometres of tufa-swallowed rail, the only straight thing in the country — is your highway. Use it. Strike it periodically anyway. It is straight because it was built by something that has stopped answering, and it will lead you somewhere that made sense ten thousand years ago.

When the **far drums** come — four impacts, nine seconds apart, receding over an hour — get off the mat and onto solid stone, out of the dolines, away from anything with a void beneath. Something is striding tower to tower that has never been located and does not know you exist. Its foot will come down where it comes down. Do not follow it. Do not try to find it. The tenders keep their calendar by it and their calendar is the correct relationship.

When your **hair rises** and the taste of a struck nail comes into your mouth: the sky-cough. Nine kilometres of warm-bottomed, cold-topped aerosol above you is a charged capacitor and you are standing in wet conductive country full of metal. Get off the cables and the rail. Do not be the tall thing. Do not shelter in a tower window — a fluted aperture is a spark gap. Do not lie flat on wet ground; ground current is what kills. Get inside a pump-house: one room, one door, a hum, alloy all around. I would wager that is what they are *for*, now, whatever they were for before.

And do not go to the escarpment. Eight kilometres of drop, an updraft that will take you, and — worse — a smell coming over the lip that is thin and sharp and *green*, and salt. It will be the first thing in months that your body recognises as world. That is where travellers step off. Not from despair. From homesickness with a referent.

## 8. Machines

Strike, listen, and then leave it alone unless you have been taught.

Some towers have a machine still running inside them, entombed, working through a metre of stone that conducts its note. You will not know what it is for. Neither does anyone. **Do not open one to find out.** The maintenance is hereditary and mostly liturgical, which means the procedure is the meaning, and an outsider with a good idea is a catastrophe with hands.

Learn to chip creeping stone off moving parts. It is the entry-level job, it is endless, it is honest, and it is the fastest way to become a person here. Learn to listen to the little brown animal in the bearing housing and to hear drift in its song across three valleys. If you can do that, you will be fed.

Do not ask who buys the product. Do not propose turning anything off. The black lid is exhaust that became climate; the night has endemic species; stopping the process is an extinction with no word for its size. You will be tempted to say this out loud as a criticism. It is not a criticism. It is the oldest fact about the place and everyone already knows.

The **Downpipe** accepts. It is somewhere in the central keys, of unremarkable diameter, and twice in a generation it returns a bolt of non-corroding metal, a folded sheet like paper, once a tooth. These are in the shrines: a niche cut in a tower, a stone lip black on top and white beneath.

Do not go there. Do not give it anything. Do not throw a stone in to hear how deep it is. Everything else on this plateau has been worn to karst — rounded, fluted, dissolved, made peace with. I would expect the Downpipe to be the one thing that has not, and I would expect that to be the most frightening object you have ever stood near. Be polite to it from a distance, the way you are polite to a thing that has an opinion about you.

Do not touch the shrine objects. Do not bring anything shiny. If you want to leave something, leave a file.

## 9. People

One hut per two Draws. Arrive sounding, in key, on the Draw, and stop at a distance and let them name you. **Your name is a chord and it does not travel.** Sixty kilometres is the acoustic horizon; past it your home tonic falls under the floor of hearing and you are nobody who can be called. Carry clinker stones. Re-learn your songs at every border. Expect to be renamed every sixty kilometres and expect that to do something to you.

Bring trade, and bring the right trade: hardened files, scrapers, drill bits, spring stock, needles, grease if you can find one that survives the chemistry. Not lamps. Not screens. Not anything that claims to be a machine. The most valuable thing you can carry is a pair of hands and a season to spend.

Seek out the **deaf tenders**. They read the ground with palms and knees and the long bones of the forearm, they are not deafened by the loud, they can put a hand on a comb and tell you which plate fails in a year, and they hold the tactile dialect that is older than speech and works inside a hush. They are the best of them and everyone knows it. Be a slow, patient, slightly stupid student and they will teach you, because the alternative is that you die on their cable.

Do not break acoustic law. The punishment is *going quiet*: nine days in a moss-lined cell, and men come out unable to speak in anything but a shout. There is no appeal to a higher authority. There is no higher authority. There is a plateau, a hum, and a very small number of people who have decided what may be said in which band.

And do not tell them about the sun. The word for *light* is the word for *rumour*. You will be a rumour with a body, and if you insist on the sky you will be a tiresome one.

## 10. Mind, and afterwards

You will feel watched. You are watched. Paranoia here is not a symptom, it is an accurate sense organ, and the correct therapy is not reassurance but *pronounceability*. Get in key and the feeling drops by half. It never drops to zero, because it should not.

You will hallucinate. You will hear your name in the water registers. You will feel a presence at your shoulder in the third Draw and it will be the sub-40 in your sternum and your own starved cortex cooperating. Know this in advance so you do not spend your emergency noise on it.

And you will be **held**. This is the part nobody prepares for. The hum has been coming up through the soles for longer than anything here has had a name. It is warm, it is constant, it is eleven hours in and eleven hours out, it is a hand under your chin on the Let, it is the sound of a very large thing breathing while you sleep. Within a month you will sleep better than you have ever slept. Within three you will not be able to imagine silence as anything but injury.

Then you will leave, and the absence of it will be the worst moment of your life.

Plan for that moment as carefully as you plan the arrival. Bring the chest plate. Bring a recording made with equipment that goes below twenty hertz. Set up a hum in your bedroom before you come home — a driver on the bed frame, a subwoofer under the floor, something that puts a note into your sternum and not just into your ears. Tell the people who love you what you will need, because you will not be able to explain it while you are crying.

Afterwards you will click in shops. You will put a hand on walls. You will find quiet rooms predatory and open sky obscenely thin. You will listen to people's voices for drift and diagnose them, and be right, and not say so. You will find our world's spectrum unowned and unpartitioned, full of holes nobody is responsible for, and it will strike you as a kind of nakedness.

You will be a tender for the rest of your life, of a country you cannot get back to, humming.

## The catechism, to memorise

Arrive as noise, not as a note.
Loud in the air, silent in the ground.
Strike everything; trust nothing that is bolted.
Travel on the Draw; shelter on the Let.
Never descend into a low place untested.
Find the cold face; take only what you are given.
Silence with a boundary is a house. Silence that moves is a mouth.
Moss in patches is weather. Moss in rectangles is a grave. Moss in lines is a road that is not yours.
When the sleet thins: **noise, now.**
When the hair rises: off the metal, into the pump-house.
When the far drums come: onto stone, and do not follow.
Chip stone. Do not ask what it is for.
Do not give the Downpipe anything.
Learn hand-to-stone before you learn to speak.
Carry your hum home.

You will hear the ground before you touch it.

Make sure it hears you first.
