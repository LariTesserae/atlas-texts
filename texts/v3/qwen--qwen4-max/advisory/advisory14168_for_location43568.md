# Preparing to Visit the Warm Shelf

*A traveler's briefing. Read it twice: once for your body, once for your manners.*

---

## 1. Understand the one law before you pack anything

Everything on that plateau follows from a single fact: **moving water lays stone down; standing water takes it back.** The city is not a ruin and not a construction site. It is a held balance, audited by weight, maintained by hand, and the people who live there are the hands.

This means the place is not a destination in the usual sense. It is an ongoing act of maintenance that you will be stepping into, and your body — your mass, your shed skin, your sunscreen, your urine, the seed in your boot tread — is an entry in a ledger that has balanced to the notch for generations.

Prepare, therefore, less like a tourist and more like a surgical visitor: scrubbed, sealed, counted in and counted out, and profoundly aware that you are the largest, loudest, dirtiest thing on the shelf.

Because you are. There are no animals there. **You will be the animal.**

---

## 2. Before you go: permissions, quarantine, and the two hard conversations

**Ask to be let in, and expect to be inspected.** A place with no animals and a closed water loop has every reason to keep you at the stairhead for a while. There may be a waiting period in a dry room, a scrub-down, a change of clothing, an interrogation about what you are carrying. Cooperate completely. Do not be offended. The person meeting you may have "things that come up the stair" as an actual line in their duties, and you are the first item on it in living memory.

**Arrive clean in the specific sense that matters here.** Not perfumed — *sterile and lint-free*:

- Hot-wash and seal every garment. No wool, no fleece, no down, no felt, no leather, no fur, no feathers, no bone, no ivory, no dried flowers in your hatband, no straw hat bought in another world. Plant-fiber or clean synthetic, tightly woven, nothing that sheds.
- Scrub your boots to bare rubber. Pick the treads with a needle. There is no soil on that plateau and no worm; a single viable seed or egg mass in your tread is the kind of gift that ends a world.
- Deworm. Get your teeth and skin checked. Treat any fungal infection *before* you go — you are walking into a warm alkaline wet room the size of a county, and your feet will be macerated within days.
- Be fever-free and honestly well. A small isolated human population with a narrow disease ecology does not need your respiratory virus.
- Trim your nails, tie your hair, and plan to keep it tied. Every hair you drop in a running channel will be welded into a wall by next week. Every hair you drop in a standing room will be erased. Either way, you will have written yourself into the fabric of a city that keeps no events.

**Do not bring an animal. Not a dog, not a bird, not a fish in a flask, not a bee, not a worm in a pot of soil, not a culture, not a "harmless" insect.** Understand clearly: the single most valuable and most catastrophic thing you could carry up that stair is one fertile animal. Someone may eventually ask you for it. Decide your answer now, in advance, in a cold place, and not later while looking into a hopeful face.

**Have the death conversation.** There are no beasts of burden and the stair is very long. If you die there, you will not be carried down. You will be given to a standing room, and in a season there will be nothing to find, and this will be thought proper. Make your peace with it and tell whoever waits for you at home. Sign whatever understanding they offer, and mean it.

## 3. Prepare your body

### Altitude
Assume the shelf sits somewhere between three and four and a half thousand meters — thin hard blue, stars that barely twinkle, air that asks for a deeper breath. Acclimatize properly *before* the stair, not on it. There are no animals to carry your pack and no shortcuts; whatever you bring, you or a hired back will carry up. Arrive fit, arrive slow, and be willing to spend your first two days doing almost nothing but sitting in shade and drinking.

### The heat problem, which is the real one
The water is blood-warm and the rock never cools. Think carefully about what that removes from you:

- **You cannot cool off by getting in the water.** Immersion at 37–40 °C is not relief; it is a hot tub. Working in a running channel under that sun is exertion in a bath you cannot dump heat into. People have died in shallow warm pans without drowning.
- **There is no cool night to recover in.** Your body's normal repair from a hot day depends on a cool evening. Here the floor stays warm as a sleeping animal until dawn. Heat load *accumulates*. Plan for this: short shifts, real midday rest, and a stay measured in a handful of turnings rather than months.
- **Sweat in water tells you nothing.** You will not feel yourself sweating and you will not think to drink. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.
- **The water is warm and tastes of chalk**, which means you will not want to drink it. Carry a vessel you like, and make yourself use it.

Your two available heat sinks are **evaporation into thin dry air** (a wet cloth in the breeze on the bare fields, whether the water is warm or not — evaporation cools regardless) and **radiation to that hard cold night sky**. Sleep on a raised woven cot, out under the stars if the hosts allow, with your body open to the sky rather than pressed to the warm stone. It is the only cold thing on the plateau and it is straight up.

### Your skin
Warm lime-rich water strips fat, opens the barrier, and then begins to glaze you. The locals grease with sedge oil before a shift and scrape after; their palms shine like shell. Yours will crack, split at the fingertips, and sting.

- **Use their sedge oil.** Do not bring your own petroleum jelly, silicone barrier cream, or lotion into the water. Everything you smear on yourself enters a closed loop and comes back as rain.
- Accept the glaze as part of the day and the scraping as part of the evening. Do not pick at it dry.
- Bring proper occlusive dressings and antiseptic for cuts, and keep cuts *out* of the water. A wound soaked repeatedly in mineralizing water heals badly and hurts constantly.

### Your eyes and your skin, from below
White stone across miles, at altitude, under a thin sky, throws light *up*. This is a snowfield problem wearing a warm disguise.

- Real UV-blocking wraparound eyewear, plus a wide brim, plus a light veil for the lower face. A sedge hat alone is not enough for unadapted eyes.
- Sun protection under the jaw, under the nose, on the underside of the forearms, on the backs of the knees. Physical shade rather than chemical sunscreen: nothing you wash off should reach the pans.
- Expect photokeratitis if you are careless — a gritty, sand-in-the-eyes night. Bring something for it.

### Two hazards nobody thinks of until it's too late

**Carbon dioxide.** Water that drops travertine is loaded with dissolved CO₂, and it degasses where it emerges. CO₂ is heavier than air and pools invisibly in low, enclosed, still spaces: covered cisterns, culverts, dry basins, the deeper standing rooms, the shaded halls where everyone spends midday. Add thin air at altitude and you have a genuine killing hazard that smells of nothing. **Never enter a low roofed or walled hollow without asking, and never without a lowered flame or a meter.** If a lamp goes out near the floor, so will you. Do not sit down in a dry sunken room because it looks pleasant and cool. Nothing there is cool anyway; that instinct will lie to you.

**Warm freshwater organisms.** Warm mineral water with thick biofilm and algae is exactly the environment for the things that go up noses and into lungs.

- **Never put your face under. Never duck your head. Never rinse your sinuses with plateau water.** Warm-freshwater amoebae are rare and essentially always fatal.
- The haze at the low edge — ten thousand threads coming apart in the air — is aerosolized spring water. It is the most beautiful thing you will ever stand in and it is a lung exposure. Admire it upwind, briefly, and don't breathe deeply with your face in the drift.
- Bathe only where and how you are told: greased, briefly, in a designated run, scraped after.

### Drinking water
Ask which springs are for drinking and which are not. Deep geothermal water can carry arsenic, fluoride, boron, and more; "warm and clean and chalky" is not the same as potable. Locals will know which pans are safe and will tell you plainly if you ask plainly. Bring a small test kit and the humility to use it. Long stays: watch your kidneys, keep the volume up, and don't be shy about the fact that a liter of warm chalk water is a chore.

## 4. Kit

**Carry little. Everything you carry must come back down with you.**

- Tough-soled, well-siped footwear with real grip; algae-coated wet travertine is treacherous, and the rims will open a careless heel like a blade of cooling wax. Gaiters if you'll be in the runs. Accept the sedge sandals they offer you for the pans, and wear your own boots on the bare fields.
- Gloves for handling fresh stone: thin, grippy, sacrificial. Fresh travertine cuts and abrades.
- Long loose light-colored clothing, brimmed hat (get it *there*, from them), veil, eyewear.
- A signal **mirror**, not a whistle. Light is enormous there and sound is precious. A flash carries for miles and offends nobody; a shriek in that silence is an assault, and it also destroys the instrument the workers are using (see §6).
- A small red-mode lamp. No campfire, no stove: fuel is sedge and withy and one wind-bent tree, and fire is for the lime-burners. Eat what they eat, roasted in the warm ground, or eat your own shelf-stable food cold.
- Your own food, sealed, shelf-stable, protein-adequate, plus supplements. Their diet is tuber, cress, algae, sedge oil, and the pale tree; it is clean and it is thin. Do not offer them your food, do not share leftovers, do not plant anything, ever, including a "harmless" herb.
- A closed waste system, or exact instructions. **Ask where your body's outputs go.** There is no manure on that plateau; fertility is a closed account, and your nitrogen is an import. There may be more than one ledger and your host will know which one you belong to. If they want you to carry your waste down the stair, carry it down the stair.
- A notebook, a pencil with no cap, and everything tethered. Count your kit in and out like surgical swabs. A dropped screw in a running channel becomes part of a wall within a week; a lost battery in a standing room dissolves into the water everyone drinks and rains and builds with, forever.
- No soap. No sunscreen. No repellent. No perfume. No coins for the pools. No offerings.
- Nothing hums. In that silence your device's coil whine and your fan are a scream at half a mile. Leave the electronics that buzz at home.

## 5. Arrival

The stair ends and the first thing is warmth through your soles, and the second thing is a silence so small you will hear your own blood. Give yourself an hour before you speak. Sit down on bare stone in shade — *bare stone, not a rim, not a curtain, not a lid* — and let your ears recalibrate.

Learn these before you learn anyone's name:

- **"Running"** and **"standing."** These are the two states of everything, the whole calendar, and the only question that matters about where you are about to put your hand or your foot.
- **"Which rooms are running?"** Ask it every morning. It is the local equivalent of the date, the weather, and the traffic report.
- **"May I put my weight here?"**
- **"I add nothing and I take nothing."** Say it early, say it seriously, and then keep it.

Approach people in the open, slowly, from a distance. Nothing on that plateau moves except water and people; a walking figure is legible for miles and there is no such thing as arriving unnoticed. Do not appear suddenly beside someone. Do not sneak up as a joke. You are a large moving animal in a world that has never had one, and their startle is not a cultural quirk, it is a whole nervous system meeting something new.

And accept that **you smell like a beast.** Sweat, sebum, breath, gut. You will hear your own stomach and your own breathing and your own snoring for the first time in your life. Children will want to watch you eat, listen to your middle, look at your teeth, see you walk. Let them. Be patient and be gentle and give them nothing with mass.

## 6. Moving through a city made of lace

**Where to put your weight.** Nothing is ancient and nothing is thick for long. A fingernail-thin stone curtain will ring when touched and shatter when leaned on. Undercut lips are undercut. Basins are lobed and lily-padded and roofed in places with lids you cannot distinguish from floor. Walk where you are walked. Never step on a rim, never on a curtain, never on anything that sounds hollow, never on a drying floor — that is the harvest, and your footprint will be cast in it permanently.

**Where not to leave a mark.** Wet setting lime takes prints. A careless crossing of a settling pan writes an event into a city that keeps none, and they will have to *cut* it out, which is surgery in a place where everything is made by forming.

**Silence is not safety.** Learn the sound-map:

- Running water is silk drawn over silk, everywhere, at the floor of hearing.
- Standing water is nothing at all.

Nothing at all means thin walls, quiet deep pans, and possibly the dead. If you are lost or hurt, **walk toward the scraping.** Bone on wet stone carries half a mile; that is a person, and that is help.

**Quiet is not politeness, it is not blinding their instrument.** They diagnose the whole apparatus by ear: a change in the hiss means a run is choking or a rim has breached. Your voice, your laughter, your music, your whistling masks the only diagnostic they have. Speak low, keep conversations short and near, sing only if invited, and understand that a shout is closer to vandalism than to rudeness.

**Do not go alone into the empty districts.** They are not ruins. They are maintained, perfect, running or standing on schedule, and waiting — and there is nobody within a mile, thin lids underfoot, CO₂ in the hollows, and glare that flattens all distance. In that clear thin air a house a half-hour away looks five minutes away, and the lace is self-similar; you will be confidently lost inside twenty minutes. Take a guide, or take a round with someone, or stay in sight of a working run.

**Never touch the notched stick at the outflow.** It is the gauge. It is also, functionally, the constitution.

## 7. The work, and how not to be a disaster in it

There are not enough hands. The honest price of admission is labor: carrying sedge, setting plugs, scraping, turning, walking the round. Offer your back before you offer anything else, and be *accurate* about what you can do.

Then:

- **Never move a paddle, plug, or weir without instruction.** Not to help. Not because a channel looks wrong. Not because you've spotted an inefficiency. The schedule is deeper than your eye, and what looks like a mistake is often a room being deliberately thinned. An amateur lifting the wrong paddle can unmake a wall that took generations, choke a run until it floods a district, or send water into a room where a grandmother is dissolving.
- **Do not innovate.** A better lever, a faster scraper, a clever shortcut — these are not gifts in a system whose entire virtue is that the two numbers are the same. If you have an idea, say it once, humbly, to someone whose age is measured in many turnings, and let it go.
- **Grease before, scrape after.** Take the shift length they take. Sit out the midday in the shaded standing rooms, with the flame check mentioned above.
- **The bone tools.** There are no animals on the plateau and there are bone scrapers. Notice that. Do not ask "whose was this?" as small talk. If a scraper is put into your hand, receive it with the gravity you would give a relic, because that is very likely what it is. If you are told, listen without squeamishness; they will be watching your face, and your face is the first animal face many of them have ever read.
- **Copper is ceremonial and also poison to the green.** Do not bring copper vessels for water, do not leave a copper coin in a pan as a charm. It will kill the algae in a run and the run is somebody's calendar.

## 8. Manners, gifts, souvenirs, and time

**The ledger forbids souvenirs.** Not a chip, not a shard of ringing curtain, not a pebble — there are no pebbles, and a piece of that stone is a subtraction from an audited mass. It equally forbids *additions*: no coin, no offering, no ashes, no lock of hair, no scratched initials. Both directions falsify the notch.

So take **weightless things**: the note a stone curtain makes when you touch it, the pattern of a notch stick copied into your notebook, the schedule of which rooms are running, a song, the exact copper color the pans go at low sun, the way a face looks lit from below. Learn to make a curtain ring and carry the sound home. That is the only legitimate plunder, and it is inexhaustible.

Give weightless things too: labor, attention, silence, and stories of below the cloud. Be careful with stories. Your fairy tales are full of wolves and birds and fish. To a people who say *nothing has climbed yet* without grief, these are either wonder or a wound, and you will not always be able to tell which. Tell them slowly and watch.

**Learn their time.** No months, no seasons. Children are born in the week the north cisterns stand; debts fall due when the Long Run is turned; a person's age is turnings. Keep your own private journal if you must, but do not impose your calendar in public, do not ask what year it is, and do not say "nothing happens here." Everything happens here. It simply comes round.

**Time your visit by the schedule.** Arrive a little before a major turning if you want to see the great work and be useful in a gang; do not arrive on the day debts fall due, when everyone is stretched and a stranger is a burden. Do not arrive at a dissolution unless invited.

**The standing rooms may be tombs.** You may walk into a quiet room and find, softened and half-erased, the shape of a person the water is taking back. Do not touch. Do not speak. Do not photograph. Do not run water into it. Back out and tell someone you were there, if that seems right, and ask what you should have done.

**The empty houses are not yours.** A house alone on a terrace with half an hour of white on every side is not an invitation. Ask before sleeping in any room. Ask twice about a room that is standing.

## 9. Prepare your mind, which is the part people get wrong

**The silence will generate sound.** In quiet that deep, with your own blood audible, the auditory system starts manufacturing: voices, footsteps, a scraper that isn't there, someone calling your name from an empty district. This is normal and it is not madness. Know it in advance so it doesn't frighten you. It passes when you go and work beside real people.

**The nights have no small sounds.** No insects, no birds, no rustle in the reeds, nothing moving in the dark at all. Human nervous systems evolved to read that faint night-noise as *the world is normal*. Its total absence can feel like the world holding its breath, or like being the last living thing. If you are given a dormitory a mile from anyone, ask instead for a mat near people until you settle.

**Time will come unstuck.** Days exist — sun, the same afternoon cloud, the warm chalk rain at its hour, the copper pans, the hard stars — but there are no events to hang memory on. You will lose whole stretches. Count turnings out loud with your hosts. Keep one small private irregularity — a page a day in your notebook — as an anchor, and do not mistake it for their calendar.

**Watch for the two failure modes.** Some travelers slide into the schedule and stop wanting to leave; the rhythm is genuinely narcotic, and there is no season coming to shake you out of it. Others develop an ugly, urgent itch **to make something happen** — to shout, to throw a stone, to break a curtain just to prove that time exists and that they are real. That impulse is common and it is the single most dangerous thing you will bring up the stair. If you feel it, say it out loud to your host, and then go and carry sedge until it passes. They will understand better than you expect. They have felt it too.

**Decide beforehand what you will say about below.** Someone will ask what the cloud is hiding. Someone will ask whether there are really things down there with legs. Someone may ask you to bring one up. Have your answer, know why you hold it, and hold it kindly. You are an event in a place that keeps no events, and how you handle that will be remembered longer than anything else about you — possibly for generations, in a tally hall, as the turning when the animal came.

## 10. Leaving

**Be scraped before you go, in the place they designate, and let the scrapings go back into the pans.** The glaze on your ankles is their stone. Walking down the stair wearing it is theft, and they will notice, and they will not say so.

Expect to be counted out. Expect, possibly, to be *weighed* — you ate their tubers, you drank their water, some of the shelf is now you. Ask what account that falls into and settle it as they ask: labor, or a measured return, or nothing at all because they have decided you were a gift. Do not argue arithmetic with people who have kept the same notch for four hundred years.

Check your kit against your list. Look behind where you slept. Comb out your hair and take it with you. Take nothing that has mass unless it was given to you and entered in the hall.

Then the stair, then the cloud floor, then the world.

**Warn yourself about the world.** For a week or two afterward, everything will be too loud, too cold, and unbearably full of animals. Birds will sound like an emergency. A dog barking will make you cry and you won't know why. Cold stone under your palm will feel like a bereavement. You will hear your own blood in quiet rooms for a month. You will catch yourself listening to a tap running and thinking, with real tenderness, *that one's laying down.*

That's the souvenir. It weighs nothing. It's the only one they let you keep.

---

## If you remember nothing else

1. Moving water builds; standing water dissolves. Ask which, before you touch anything.
2. You cannot cool off in the water and there is no cool night. Short shifts, real rest, drink on a schedule, sleep open to the sky.
3. Never put your face under. Never enter a low still hollow without a flame.
4. Silence is not safety; walk toward the scraping. Carry a mirror, not a whistle.
5. Add nothing, take nothing — not a seed, not a shaving, not a coin, not a hair.
6. Never move a paddle you were not told to move.
7. You are the first animal. Behave like something that has been let in, not something that has arrived.
