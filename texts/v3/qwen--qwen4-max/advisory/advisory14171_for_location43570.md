# Preparing for the Tableland of Standing Water

## First, correct your expectations, because they are the heaviest thing you'll carry

You are not going to a ruin. A ruin is a thing that failed. This is a machine made of shape, still running, and it does not know you exist. There is no one to meet, nothing to decipher, no center to reach, no chamber that will turn out to be the point. If you go looking for the builders you will spend your whole visit angry at the walls for not telling you anything, and you will miss the water.

So decide, before you leave, what you are actually going for. My advice: go to watch a system that has no operator. Go to be, for a few weeks, the only thing in a hundred kilometers that is in a hurry. Go to hear one drip. Anything more specific than that is a plan you have imposed on a place that has no plans, only gradients.

Also decide your scope now, on paper, in a warm room. The tableland is ten thousand square kilometers of near-identical handiwork. It cannot be visited. It can only be sampled. Pick one district — a hundred hectares, a day's walk across at most — and intend to know it completely: every cistern mouth, every moss wick, which ceilings show which caustics at noon, where the snails seal in the dry week. That is a real visit. "Walking the plateau" is not a visit; it is a way of losing a season and coming back with nothing but blisters and a vague sense of grievance.

## The two absences that govern all your preparations

**There is no wood, and there is no fire.**

Nothing on the tableland burns. Not the moss (wet), not the lichen (wet, and a millimeter per millennium), not the cushion plants, not the few centimeters of soil. There is no fuel and never was. This means:

- You cannot dry out. Ever. Not your socks, not your gloves, not your sleeping bag, not your hands. Every fabric you bring will reach the ambient wetness within an hour and stay there for the duration. Accept this at the outset or you will spend your visit fighting a war you cannot win, and the fighting is what makes people miserable and then hypothermic.
- You cannot make heat except heat you brought. Chemical packs, a thermos filled at the lip, a small stove with carried fuel if you must — but see below about the violence of a flame here.
- **You cannot make a splint, a crutch, a lever, a stretcher, a tool handle, a probe, a bridge, or a handhold.** There is no wood here, which is to say there is no rescue. A rolled ankle in a district of coffin-sized cells, three days from the lip, in fog, is a life-threatening injury, and there is nobody to hear you shout, because sound a hundred meters off arrives as though from another day.

Everything about how you move must follow from that last sentence.

## Dress for immersion, not for weather

The mistake everyone makes is packing for rain. Rain falls on you from above and can be shed. Here the water is *on everything*, in films, at ten degrees by day and two at night, and you will be wetted by contact — by shoulders, by hips, by palms, by sitting down. A rain shell is nearly useless; it keeps out the one hour of actual rain and does nothing about the twenty-three hours of touching wet stone.

Think like a caver or a canyoneer, not a hiker.

- Wool and synthetic, close-fitting, in layers you can move in through a slot. No down, anywhere, for any reason; down here is a wet rag with sentimental value.
- Consider thin neoprene against the skin — socks, gloves, a vest. It is the only material that is honest about the situation: it lets a film in and keeps it warm. You will feel slightly ridiculous. You will also be the only comfortable person on the plateau.
- A genuinely dry bag, doubled, with a full change of base layers and one thick pair of socks that you never, under any circumstances, wear while walking. These are for sleeping only. Their dryness is a resource, like fuel, and it is finite.
- A thick closed-cell pad, thicker than you think you need. Wet limestone is a heat sink of enormous patience. Lying on it is like lying on a cold that is being actively resupplied.
- Gloves you can work in, but understand: your fingertips are your eyes in fog. You will take the gloves off. Plan for cracked, macerated, permanently cold hands, and bring the ointment.
- Nothing scented. No soap, no scented sunscreen, no flavored anything you'll be tempted to spit. In a place this quiet and this clean, you smell like a factory.

## Make your body narrow

Corridors where you turn your shoulders. Openings that are slots, not doors. Chambers stacked four and five deep. A rigid framed pack, a rolled mat strapped across the outside, a wide brim, a trekking pole held横 — all of these will jam, and when they jam you will be in a stone throat with your arms pinned, learning something about yourself.

- Soft, narrow, front-and-back load. Nothing wider than your shoulders. Nothing that cannot be passed through a gap ahead of you by hand.
- Assume you will have to unload and hand your bag through, repeatedly, in the deep quarters. Pack so that this takes ten seconds and does not require opening anything to the wet.
- If you are claustrophobic, find out now, in a cave, with a friend, not there.
- The temptation, on the open aprons, will be to bring a shallow tray-sledge and drag your load — the film of water makes polished stone almost frictionless, and it would work beautifully. **Don't.** A dragged load is a lapping compound, and it makes a sound the city has never made.

## Learn to walk again

Do not stride. The pavement sweats, the mica in the limestone gives it a soapy sheen, the lichen crust is slick when active, and the black lichen in the grooves is slicker still. There are cisterns, deep and cold and narrow-rimmed, and there are basins near-touching, thousands per hectare, and in the low quarters the water table sits a finger's depth under the flags.

Walk like someone wading: slide the foot forward, load it slowly, feel for the seat, transfer. Three points of contact in the stacked quarters, always, even when it feels absurd. Never hurry. Never carry a load that can shift your center of gravity without warning.

And learn to listen with your soles. In the low quarters your step makes a soft compression sound, like breathing. That sound has a pitch, and the pitch tells you what is under the flag — a seated block on a full bed, or a flag over a void, or a seam about to take your weight sideways. Spend your first day doing nothing but walking ten meters back and forth, listening. It is not a wasted day. It is the day you learn the only language the floor speaks.

Bring a light you hold at your hip, not your forehead. In fog a headlamp builds a white wall in front of your face; a low raking light shows you the film, the groove, the fingernail-thick lip, the seam. You need to see the *surface*, not the distance. There is no distance.

## Water: drink it, but understand it

The water is fog condensate that has walked over a great deal of limestone. It is clean by the standards of the world below and it tastes of cold rock. Still: filter or treat it. There are pale snails in enormous numbers on every weeping face, and snails in enormous numbers are a fact about parasites, not just about grazing. There are newts in the deep cisterns and insect fall in all of them. Do not wade barefoot. Do not drink from a basin with a snail-line on its lip; go to a weeping face upstream of everything and take the film before it has met life.

Two things people get wrong:

1. Condensate is mineral-poor. If you drink liters of it while working hard and cold, you will dilute yourself. **Bring salt.** Bring more salt than seems reasonable, and eat it deliberately, and bring food that requires no cooking, because cooking is a whole event here.
2. Every drop of water on this plateau is handed onward. There is no such thing as a private basin. Anything you put into the water — soap, urine, toothpaste, a rinsed bandage, the sugar from your drink mix — will be passed lip to lip across a district and eventually across the tableland. Wash away from the stone, in a container, and carry the water out or scatter it on the few centimeters of leeward soil, which is itself a centuries-old accumulation you should be reluctant to fertilize.

Carry out all your waste. All of it. There is no soil to bury anything in, and cold wet limestone is a preservative. Your orange peel will lie in a drainage groove for a human lifetime, and the groove is the last thing that will survive of this city, and you will have written on it.

## Do not touch the plumbing

This is the discipline that separates a visitor from a vandal, and it is not obvious until you understand what the moss is.

The moss is not decoration and not a surface. It is the regulator. It swells wet and throttles flow; it shrinks dry and lets more through. Across ten thousand square kilometers, distribution is governed by nothing but moss doing what moss does. It lives exactly where water is handed over: at lips, wicks, aprons, and the seams of the settling dishes.

Therefore:

- Never step on moss. Never sit on it, sleep on it, wring it, or dry a sock on it.
- Never put your hand into a groove, or your finger over a lip, "just to see." You will change the flow to somewhere you cannot see, for as long as you stand there, and moss takes time to recover from a crush.
- Never dam a basin with your boot while you rest. Rest on bare flags, on your pad, in a cell, above the film.
- Never hang anything on a fog-comb. Every flute you block is area the fog can no longer leave itself on.
- Never move a stone. Not a pebble. There are essentially no loose stones here; each block sits in a shallow dish of its own making, locked by weight and fit, and it finished settling before there were languages. Anything you find loose is either a frost spall — a working part of the process — or something a previous traveler dropped. Leave both.
- Take nothing. No chip, no lichen fragment, no snail shell, no souvenir. The lichen territories abut along borders older than any human border; a thumbnail of crust is a century of somebody's slow map.

## What not to fix

You will find, in most districts, one chamber that was never finished, with a flaw in its ceiling and a single drip — the only irregular water for a kilometer. Every fiber of a competent person will want to seal it, or channel it, or at least put a basin under it properly.

Don't. That drip is a landmark. The swift-like birds navigate by forty such drips across the whole plateau; the harrier hunts by listening for them. You would be deleting a signpost from a map you cannot read, in a language you did not know was being spoken.

Likewise: if you find a groove choked with moss, that is not a blockage, that is a valve. If you find a basin overflowing where you'd expect it to lip cleanly, that is not an error. There is no mechanism here to repair. There is only shape, and the shape includes the flaws.

Understand your own erosion budget, too. The city loses a millimeter per thousand years. A lug sole on wet limestone, a scuffing boot heel in a slot, one impatient hand on an arris — you can remove more in an afternoon than the lichen removes in a millennium. **You are the fastest event that has ever happened here.** Soft, clean, non-marking soles. Scrub and quarantine all your gear before you climb: no soil, no seeds, no spores from below. This is a closed water loop with endemic snails, one newt, one crucifer with an eight-day flowering window, and no immunity to whatever is on your laces.

## Navigate by water, light, and sound — never by hope

There are no landmarks tall enough to see over the fog. There is no center. Walking a month in a straight line gets you nothing but more of the same, and in fog a straight line is a fiction your legs invent.

The city's own coordinate system is hydrological, and you can learn it:

- **The black lichen lines.** They grow only in drainage grooves and mark the whole hydrology in fine dark threads. Learn to read them at your feet: tributary grooves are narrow and branch; trunk grooves are wide, deep, and *converge*. Convergence means an outfall. Outfalls mean the edge.
- **The ceiling caustics.** When the cloud floor drops at noon, the moving films throw net-patterns of light onto every underside, crawling in the direction of drainage. You can read a district's plumbing off its ceilings. Spend your glare hours lying on your back doing exactly this, and sketch it.
- **The combs.** Ranks of fluted walls face the wind. Windward is where the fog arrives; the comb-only districts harvest for chambers elsewhere. If you are among combs with no cells, you are on a margin.
- **The drips.** Count them, note their bearings, learn the forty if you can. They are the only fixed acoustic points on the plateau.
- **The day is a clock.** Fog at dawn, glare at noon, one hour of hard cold rain in the afternoon, clear, freeze at the edges after dark, frost ticking until morning. You always know roughly what time it is, and you always know when the seeing hour is coming. Use it. Do not make positional decisions in fog; make them at noon.

And one absolute rule, worth memorizing until it is reflex: **when the grooves begin to converge, the basins thin out, the snails stop, and the film runs faster and thinner under your palm — stop, sit down, and wait for the glare.** Every one of the hundred places where water leaves the tableland is a waterfall in cloud, and the escarpment has no railing and no lip you can see in white. People do not die here of collapse. Nothing has fallen. They die of walking downhill in fog.

## Silence, and the one hour you may speak

You will be the loudest thing on the plateau. Not metaphorically — measurably. There is no falling water anywhere to mask you; the moss eats reverberation; the slots break standing waves. A cough carries a kilometer. A stove is an obscenity. A conversation is a weather event.

Prepare for this in advance, at home. Spend three days without speaking, without music, without anything in your ears. Find out what your own hearing does when nothing is feeding it: the blood, the joints, the high thin tone, the voices that aren't there. Better to meet that in a room you can leave.

Then, on the tableland, adopt the local convention that visitors have converged on: **the afternoon rain hour is when you may be human.** It is the only loud hour, and even then ten million flutes catch the rain and walk it away without a splash — but it is enough cover to talk, to laugh, to sing badly, to run a stove, to crack a knuckle, to be a mammal. Save it up. Everything you need to say to your companion, say then. The rest of the day, use hands and eyes.

Move at the harrier's pace, not yours. It quarters at wall-top height with its wings in a shallow V, listening for drips, one bird per hundred square kilometers. You are a new sound in its hunting field. Don't shine a light into weep-holes at dusk; the birds nest there. Don't camp in the flawed chamber; it is somebody's signpost and somebody's roof.

And when the grinding note comes — four or five times a year, a room-sized block completing a settling begun before language, felt in the sternum, heard by everything on the plateau — do not brace, do not run, do not look for the source. Nothing is falling. Sit still and let it pass through you and notice that nothing responds. That non-response is the truest thing the city will tell you.

## Eyes and skin

The light is enormous and it comes from underneath as well as above. Wet limestone at altitude, under a shaft of clear cold air, glares like a struck cymbal, and every film throws caustics upward into your face. A brim does not save you. Bring proper glacier glasses or goggles with side shields, and sunblock for the underside of your chin and nose and the insides of your nostrils, and put it on before the cloud floor drops, not after. Snow-blindness is available here at ten degrees in a place with no snow.

In fog, everything is a soft even white with no source, and contrast disappears; you navigate by touch and by raking light and by sound. Bring a slightly warm-tinted lens for the flat hours if it helps you, but don't rely on your eyes for anything important between dawn and noon.

Inspect your skin daily. Nothing here scabs. Cold, wet, macerated tissue plus limestone abrasion plus no dry environment equals infections that will not stop. Antiseptic, occlusive dressings, and the discipline to actually look at your feet every night even when you are too cold to care.

## When to go, and how long

Follow the retreating wet line. Through the warm season the fog floor climbs and whole quarters go under for weeks — not flooded, *fogged* — and if you are in one when it happens you will live in a three-meter world until it passes. That is a legitimate choice, and some people go for exactly that, but do not choose it by accident.

The better first visit: arrive as the wet line begins its descent, a few meters a day down the terraces. Behind it the stone dries chalk-pale, the moss crisps, the snails seal into the seams, and the crucifer flowers in an eight-day window. Travel with that line. You will see the city change state at walking pace, which is the only way anything here happens at a speed you can perceive, and you will be in the leeward districts when the harrier shifts south.

Allow a month minimum, three if you can. Nothing here is quick, and a short visit will read as silence and grey rock. The place only opens at the pace it works at.

## Prepare your mind for being unnoticed

This is the part nobody packs for.

There is no center. There is no climax. You will walk for days through cells and combs and aprons and basins, all slightly different, all unmistakably the same hand, and there will be no moment where it resolves. The scale is not a backdrop; it is an active force, and it will grind at you. Somewhere around the second week many people find themselves weeping in a corridor for reasons they cannot locate, or talking to the walls, or overwhelmed by an urge to *do something* — build, clear, mark, name, stack a cairn, leave a note. Recognize that urge as the symptom it is. It is your species' intolerance of a finished thing that does not need you.

Practice beforehand: long solo walks with no destination, no route, no distance goal. Learn to be bored past the point where boredom becomes interesting. Learn to sit for an hour watching a film of water a tenth of a millimeter thick move down a flute at a gradient you cannot see.

If you go with someone, agree in advance on the rain hour, on the turning-back cues, and on the rule that neither of you will ever hurry the other. Two people is good: you can hear each other's breathing in the low quarters, and there is someone to hold your ankle while you test a flag. More than three is a crowd, and a crowd in a place with no sound-masking is a kind of pollution.

Cache dry clothes and food at the lip. Tell someone below when you will come down and what to do when you don't, knowing that nobody can come in after you.

## Leaving

The descent is twelve hundred meters of wet dark rock streaked with springs, through the cloud belt, through moss-beards and the last crooked shrubs and the smell of cold leaf-rot. You will do it cold, soaked, and depleted, and it will be more dangerous than anything you did on top. Do not descend by following water. Plan the route from below, on the way up, in daylight, and mark it in your memory rather than on the stone.

Then the forest: warm, loud, thick, full of insects and rot and things falling. It will feel like being shouted at.

And afterward, at home, expect the following, which pass in a few weeks: you will turn your shoulders in ordinary doorways. You will look up at ceilings for caustics. You will find a dripping tap either unbearable or the most beautiful sound you have ever heard. You will wake at night listening for a grinding note that is not coming, and feel, briefly, disappointed that nothing in your world is still settling into a seat it made for itself a hundred thousand years ago.

## The short list, to memorize

1. No fire. No wood. No rescue. Never hurry.
2. Slide, don't stride. Three points of contact.
3. Never touch moss, lips, grooves, or combs. Never move a stone.
4. Never fix the drip.
5. Nothing into the water. Everything out with you.
6. Converging grooves in fog: sit down and wait for noon.
7. Speak in the rain hour.
8. Take nothing, leave nothing, and understand that you are the fastest thing that has ever happened here.

Go quietly. It is still running. It was running before you and it will be running when the limestone has been eaten back into the sea, and the last thing to go will be the grooves, because they were cut deepest by someone who knew what the place was for. Walk beside them, and read them, and don't add your name.
