# Preparing for the Cold Room

## A visitor's protocol, with the reasoning left in

---

## 1. Begin by understanding what you are

You are not a visitor to a cave. You are a warm, wet, loud, hard-edged object about to be inserted into a mind that runs on four kelvin and thinks in milligrams.

Everything in the preparation below follows from four facts about your body, which you cannot change and can only mask:

- You emit roughly **100 watts** at rest. The room's entire engine runs on a temperature difference of 4 K and is nearly reversible. Your body is, thermally, a second room.
- You are made of **water at +37 °C**, carrying salts, proteins, and particles that are extremely good at telling water how to be ice.
- You are a **broadband noise source**: joints, breath, clothing, heartbeat, footsteps, the click of a buckle. Broadband noise is the only thing in that chamber that resembles pain.
- You are a **shape** — a scattering obstacle roughly the size of the upper partials' wavelengths, which will make and unmake modes in a field where modes are animals.

You cannot go in as a person. You can only go in as an approximation of a large, cold, quiet film-shrimp. Decide now whether you are willing to do the work that approximation requires, because the alternative is not "a slightly rude visit." The alternative is a chime, and sixty years of healing, and a scar with the wrong grain that outlives your species' current languages.

---

## 2. The case for not going in

Take this seriously before anything else, because it is the correct answer for most travelers.

The room's fastest meaningful modulation is eleven seconds. Its next is nine years. A two-hour visit is roughly one eighty-thousandth of a phrase. **You will not see anything happen.** There is no event to witness, no behavior to observe, no interaction to have. Everything a human could learn by standing on that ledge for an hour can be learned better from a decade of surface recordings, at zero cost to the thing you came to admire.

And the drone leaks. The throat is a port; it was grown as one. You can hear the chamber from the tundra, faintly, on a still night, coming up out of the rift like breath out of a flute. Inside the birch-and-willow ring — the ninety-meter ring where the trees keep the wrong season — the leak is strong enough to put a geophone on bare limestone and get a clean fundamental.

So the recommended visit is: **go to the ring in November, when the birches are leafed and the ground is not, sit outside it with a contact mic and a flask of something hot, and listen to the room from outside for as long as you can stand it.** Then leave. You will have heard the actual thing. You will have injured nothing. You will have a recording that a specialist can turn into a node map, a pitch track, and a cricket census, and you will have earned the right to consider going down in ten years.

If you are still going down, read on.

## 3. Where you are coming from matters more than what you bring

This is the part multiverse travelers get wrong, and it is the part that can kill four hundred square meters of mind in ninety seconds.

The room's water is supercooled and stays liquid for two reasons: geometric confinement, and antifreeze proteins that forbid ice its geometry. Both are defeatable by a **nucleator** — a template that offers water the shape it wants. The room's century-scale chimes happen when a channel widens past the confinement limit. They do not happen because someone brought in a catalyst. Nobody ever has.

You are covered in catalysts.

- *Pseudomonas syringae* and its relatives are ordinary surface bacteria on almost every agricultural world. Their ice-nucleating protein works at −2 °C. **It still works when the bacteria are dead.** Sterilizing your gear does not help. Killing the organism leaves the template intact. You need denaturation and physical removal, not disinfection.
- Artificial snow on ski worlds is frequently made with exactly this protein, sold in bulk. If you have been skiing in the last month, you are a plague ship.
- Mineral dusts — kaolinite, feldspar, most clays — nucleate at colder temperatures but you will be carrying them in your seams and boot treads.
- Natural fibers. Cotton, wool, leather, wood, paper, down. All of them are nucleation-active and all of them shed.
- **Snow.** The single most dangerous object you can carry into the Cold Room is one snowflake, because it is not a template, it is the finished geometry, and the tundra above is covered in it for eight months of the year.

**Rule: no snow past the first ten meters. Ever. Not on a boot, not in a beard, not in a hood seam, not in the bag you are carrying.**

**Rule: never route directly from a farm world, a forest world, a ski world, or a dusty world into this one.** Insert a quarantine world between — dry, cold, sterile, low-biosphere — and spend a day there. This is not bureaucracy. This is the whole visit.

Before descent, run a **droplet assay**: a rack of supercooled microdroplets held at −5 °C, exposed to your suit's final rinse water, its outgassing, and a swab of every seam and closure. If any droplet freezes, you do not go down. There is no negotiating with the canary. The canary is a drop of water and it is smarter than you about this.

## 4. When to go

The room breathes once a year. Learn the calendar before you learn anything else.

**Never during the inhale.** For eleven autumn weeks the four-meter leaf sphincter at the top of the throat is open and winter is pouring in. Three reasons to stay out:

1. The port is working. Airflow through it is part of what the room is doing; you would be a plug in a windpipe.
2. Moving air is broadband. The whole chamber is louder and more sensitive then, and the light is up, which means the accounting is tight.
3. **The valve closes at the first hint of thaw and does not reopen for a year.** You are a 100-watt plume of warm air rising up a cold throat. Do the arithmetic on what a sphincter grown to detect the first hint of thaw might do about you. Best case, you truncate the winter and starve the room of a year's fuel. Worst case, you are sealed in at −1.7 °C until next autumn.

**Recommended window: three to six weeks after closure.** The pool is full, the room is bright and robust after the freeze, the valve is shut and unresponsive, and the margin between the stored cold and failure is at its widest. This means deep winter on the tundra: dark, −30 °C, snow. Accept it. The difficulty of the surface logistics is the price of the safety margin, and the snow is a problem you solve with a cleanroom, not a reason to come in July when the stock is nearly spent and every milligram counts.

**Longer timescales you must check:**

- **The scar.** There is a patch at the southeast rim with the wrong grain, still healing from before the wheel. Do not go near it, do not sample it, do not put a foot on it. It is a wound in a mind that is sixty years into closing.
- **The transition.** The fundamental is falling. Near the working face, the laminate is thickened and buttressed for a mode that does not exist yet — the anatomy of an unborn note. Nobody knows what happens when it arrives; one of the possibilities is that the chamber stops being a place with an inside. **Do not be inside for that.** Track the fundamental from the surface across years. Read the pre-shaped buttressing to estimate the target mode. When the two are within a margin you cannot confidently call large, the room is closed to visitors for a century. There is no version of this where your curiosity is worth the risk of being the perturbation that picks the branch.

## 5. The four injuries, with numbers

Do these sums yourself, roughly, before you go. They are the reason for every item of kit.

**Heat.** Forty microns of film over a square meter is about forty grams of water: a heat capacity of ~170 J/K. Put a resting human's radiative and convective output near it and that patch warms at something like **half a kelvin per second**. Sound speed in water moves ~4 m/s per kelvin. The room's whole life's work is a glissando of a fraction of a cent per year; you can impose decades of pitch drift on a local region in the time it takes to tie a knot, and impose it *unevenly*, which is worse than imposing it evenly, because the sixty-one meters of film are phase-locked and you would be bending the phase.

Integrated over the whole coherent volume, an unmitigated two-hour visit is hundreds of kilojoules — enough to shift the entire instrument by a measurable fraction of a cent. That is a century of the room's own patient work, done in an afternoon, by a mammal.

And remember what warmth *means* here. Springtails excrete tiny warm specks and the plant reads them. Heat is not pollution in this chamber. **Heat is speech.** Your body is a hundred-watt shout, sustained, into a mind whose entire vocabulary is milligrams. You will be screaming one meaningless syllable the whole time you are there.

Target: **net leak into the chamber under one watt, ideally under a hundred milliwatts, with the shell's exterior within ten millikelvin of ambient.** Verify with IR at the transition shelf.

**Water and carbon.** Your breath is taken — harvested sideways into the laminate before it can settle. That means the room will drink you: your water, your CO₂ (~25 g/hour), your acetone, your isoprene, your microbiome, whatever is in your lungs. This is a dim, slow, carbon-limited organism that culls cricket lineages by withholding sugar. A carbon subsidy is not a gift. It is growth, and growth is detuning, and detuning is the destruction of the instrument. **Do not feed it.** Closed circuit, no venting, no exceptions, not even a sigh.

**Noise.** Every drop that forms on the ceiling is caught in a leaf-cup because a falling drop injects broadband noise and broadband noise is pain. The plant has abolished falling water across an entire cathedral to avoid one drop per decade. You are a machine for making drops: meltwater from your own warmth, condensation off a cold buckle, sweat, the shed of a fiber. And you click. Zippers, carabiners, buckles, regulators, camera shutters, the tick of a cooling metal part.

**Shivering is shouting.** Thermal comfort here is not a matter of your convenience; it is a courtesy requirement. If you begin to shiver, the visit is already over. Go up.

**Shape.** A human is a hard, high-impedance obstacle in a medium whose animals are interference patterns. You will not kill the standing ones by touching them — they have nothing to touch. You will kill them by making regions of field they cannot survive being inside, and by splitting modes wrongly. Dozens per cubic meter; a slow traverse of the nave sweeps tens of cubic meters. Budget your damage in the hundreds and then move less than that.

## 6. The shell

The specification is one sentence and it is very hard: **neither mirror nor hole.**

If your suit absorbs the field, you are a salamander — a walking half-meter radius of death, an absence with legs. If your suit reflects it, you are a scatterer, seeding false nodes and teratogenic fissions in every direction. You want to be, acoustically, approximately a person-shaped volume of −1.7 °C water with the same loss tangent as the laminate. You will not achieve this. Get as close as your workshop allows and then subtract your ambition from your route plan.

Practical build:

- **Vacuum-jacketed torso and limbs**, with an outer trim layer held at ambient by a slow circulating loop, so the surface the room touches is the room's own temperature. No warm outer skin anywhere, including the faceplate.
- **Heat sink, not radiator.** You cannot dump 100 W into the chamber and you cannot run a hot umbilical up the throat. Carry your heat out in a bladder: ~25 liters of water starting near 0 °C buys you roughly two hours at resting metabolism before it warms out of usefulness. That bladder is your visit. When it is spent, you leave. **Do not bring ice as a heat sink.** You know why.
- **Soft, wet-look, edgeless exterior.** No corners, no hard shoulders, no protruding hardware. Everything captive: no removable caps, no lens rings, no loose slings, nothing that can be dropped. If it can fall off, it stays above.
- **No natural fibers, no leather, no wood, no paper, no down.** Clean-room synthetics only, ultrapure-washed, denatured, bagged.
- **No moving parts inside the chamber.** No demand valves, no fans, no pumps, no shutter, no rotor. A drone in here is a pain engine. Scrubbing is passive chemical curtain, warmed inside the flask because soda lime at −2 °C is useless. Breathing is bellows and lung, slow.
- **Mitts, not gloves.** You need no dexterity below the shelf. Seams shed.
- **Compliant contact pads** — broad, lossy-matched, at precomputed nodes only. Nothing rigid touches anything.

## 7. The cleanroom at −28 meters

Twenty-eight meters of dead limestone, then twelve of living throat. Use the boundary.

Establish a transition shelf at the base of the dead rock: a sealed, filtered, positive-pressure shelter, hung from anchors set in ordinary stone with a slow hydraulic squeeze, never a hammer. (The throat is a port. A hammer at the top pours straight in.)

Procedure: arrive in surface gear covered in snow. Strip completely. Nothing from above goes below — not the outer layer, not the boots, not the rope bag, not the notebook. Re-dress in pre-staged, assayed shell. Run the droplet assay again, at temperature, in situ. Check exterior temperature by IR. Then, and only then, enter the tissue.

This shelf is also your extraction point, your casualty station, and the place where you will sit for an hour afterward shaking.

## 8. The descent

**Free hang. Do not touch the ribs.**

They are ribbed and the ribs are not for climbing and you will want to use them anyway, because twelve meters of slightly yielding pale tissue with handholds is the most inviting thing you have ever seen. Don't. They are acoustic anatomy, possibly pre-shaped for the unborn note. Gripping them damps stations, crushes cups, bruises tissue, and bruises make broadband noise.

Rebelay in dead rock at the shelf and descend the living section on a free line at a **constant, slow rate**, no bouncing, no sawing, no stopping, no corrections. Jacketed, damped, slack-managed line; no metal-on-metal anywhere in the system; no tensioned wire that can sing.

Understand: **everything here is heard before it is seen, including you.** The throat announces you forty meters ahead. There is no stealth and no surprise. Since you cannot avoid being announced, be announced *well* — one smooth, band-limited, predictable event, at constant velocity, that the room can model and settle around. **Arrive as a chord, not a click.**

## 9. Entry: be a drop, not a chime

Your arrival will register. Accept that. The question is only which register.

A failing leaf-cup, once a decade, produces eleven seconds of ragged chord, every cricket stopping, the light going to nothing, and the shoals scattering to the walls. That is the *best* outcome available to you. Plan for it. Aim to be exactly one decade-drop and no more.

So: come off the line onto the ledge at a node, not wherever is convenient. Become still. **Stay still for a long time** — an hour is not excessive. Let the crickets resume. Let the light come back. Let the shoals re-form. You are waiting for the room to finish the sentence your arrival interrupted, and you cannot rush it, and moving to "check whether it's okay" is another drop.

Watch the light, not the sound. Your ears will fatigue and lie to you within twenty minutes. The light is a readout of the room's work and it is your only honest instrument. Learn its baseline before you do anything. **If the light dims when you move, stop moving.** That is the entire etiquette in one sentence.

## 10. Conduct inside

**Speed.** The perimeter takes four minutes at a local's pace. Take four hours. Any surface of yours moving faster than a few centimeters per second sheds turbulence, and turbulence is broadband. Move like a plant. Plan a route of eleven placements and take the whole visit over it.

**Where to put yourself.** Compute a node map from surface recordings before you go, and sit where your mass couples least. Then learn the crucial distinction, because it will save your life:

> A node is a place where the sound is merely structured. A hole is a place where the sound is *gone*. One is a chair. One is a mouth.

**Touch.** Nothing. The films stand proud with a meniscus you could press without breaking, and you will want to, and pressing is a hand laid on a thought. The working face is off-limits entirely: raw acid margin, sharp fracture faces, the embryonic resonator, the most delicate and most unfinished thing in the chamber.

**The floor.** Nothing here has been worn. No soil, no sediment fan, no rounded edge, fracture faces still fresh after eleven thousand years. You could be the first thing that ever wore it. Walk only on planned placements. Leave the blocks unworn.

**Speech.** Don't. The room absorbs human voice frequencies and hands them back changed, and talking here feels like feeding something, because it is. Beyond that: your breathing sits near the eleven-second band, your pulse is a periodic impulse, and this is an organism that breeds oscillators. You may be recruited. A lineage may start tracking you.

> You will make pets. When you leave, they will be off-pitch, and off-pitch lineages are culled by withheld sugar. You will cause a small extinction by having a heartbeat in the wrong place for two hours.

**Light.** None. Dark-adapt for forty minutes; the room's own blue-green is enough to read a face at arm's length, which is enough. No white light, no flash, no IR illuminators (IR is heat). If you must image, integrate long, on a compliant stand, and accept that the image will not convey anything. The honest souvenir is a spectrogram and a paragraph about what it was like.

**Samples.** None. Not a scraping, not a springtail, not a drop. The room is bounded; that is its mercy and its definition. Keep it bounded. A mode carried out in a film on your suit, in a container of the right geometry, is either a dead animal or a loose one, and you do not know which.

**Fluids.** Nothing leaves your body. No spit, no sweat, no urine, no blood. Cold air and exertion cause nosebleeds; **any bleeding is an immediate abort.** Warm blood is heat, water, salts, and nucleators, delivered directly.

## 11. The mouth

The quiet thing is forty centimeters, eyeless, slow, older than anyone reading this, and not interested in you. It walks through shoals with its mouth open and they cease.

The hazard is not the animal. The hazard is your own nervous system.

After an hour at 90 dB your body will want relief more than it wants anything. And **the only warning of its approach is relief.** You will feel a beautiful easing in your sternum and your sinuses and your teeth, and every part of you will turn toward it and step.

**Do not walk toward relief.** Write this on your wrist. If you feel the drone thin, stop, and back out along your own path at the same speed you came in at, and do not investigate.

Second hazard, subtler: after hours in the field, some of your autonomic rhythm is running entrained to it — the eleven-second modulation sits right on top of your own baroreflex, and the room can pace your blood pressure without meaning to. If a hole passes over you then, it may not feel like relief. It may feel like amputation. Don't find out how much of you has become pattern.

Carry a passive acoustic array, placed slowly at nodes and removed on exit, feeding a very dim wrist map of where the field has gone quiet. One update per second. No haptics; haptics are noise.

## 12. Emergencies

**Your only exit is the way you came.** Do not plan to transit out of the chamber. A door opening down there is heat, broadband, a hole, and possibly a nucleation, all at once, and it would be the first artifact in eleven thousand years. If you cannot be pulled up unconscious by a mechanical system rigged from dead rock, you do not go down. No solo visits. Dead-man's winch, tended by someone at the shelf who has read this document.

**You drop something.** Do not lunge. Lunging is worse than the object. Let it land, become still, wait out the eleven seconds and the cricket silence and the dark, and then leave by your planned route. Retrieve it only if it can be reached at plant speed.

**A cup fails while you are there.** Do nothing. Do not try to catch the drop. You cannot save it; you can only be a second drop, warmer and dirtier.

**A chime starts.** A crack running across the film at the speed of sound in water, the light going out, everything scattering, the loudest event this chamber ever has. Get to a wall, become small, wait. Do not warm it, do not break it, do not "help." Healing takes sixty years and your help is sixty more. Afterward, assay your own gear. You may be carrying the reason, and you need to know, and the next traveler needs to know.

**You begin to shiver, or bleed, or your heat sink warms out, or the light stays down.** Up. Slowly. Immediately. No finishing the observation.

## 13. Exit, and after

Decontaminate in both directions at the shelf. What you carry out includes film, springtails, cricket eggs, and possibly a fragment of something that has no body. Nothing biological leaves the world. Nothing gets planted, cultured, released, or given to a colleague.

Then look after yourself, because the room does not stop at the rim for you the way it does for everything else.

Expect: the chord persisting in your auditory cortex for weeks, which is not tinnitus and will be misdiagnosed as tinnitus. An eleven-second breath. Sleep architecture rearranged. A craving for silence and a horror of it. Dreams of being an absence with a mouth.

The birch ring caught the tune from outside, over centuries, and now keeps the wrong season. You are a much faster organism and you were *inside*. Do not camp in the ring. Do not sleep there. Do not operate a transit rig for seventy-two hours. Get someone to check your resting heart rate variability at week two and week eight, and be honest with them about where you were.

## 14. What not to study

The accounting is slightly ahead of itself and has been for a hundred centuries, and nothing here was built, and there is no seam and no signature. Something is being borrowed and it has not been called on it.

You will want to audit that. You will want to put calorimeters on the rock and close the books.

**Don't.** Two reasons, and either is sufficient.

The first is physical: measurement here means injecting probes, energy, and objects into a near-reversible engine, which is like testing a soap bubble's tensile strength with a fork.

The second is that you do not know what the audit *is*, in a place where warmth is speech and pattern is flesh and everything is heard before it is seen. You do not know whether attention is the call. Nobody does. The room shows no sign of being called on it and has been running that line for eleven thousand years, and the correct posture toward it is the posture you would take toward a stranger's sleeping child.

Leave the books open. Write down that they are open. Go home.

## 15. What it will actually be like

Since you should know before you spend two years preparing:

The last twelve meters are cool and slightly yielding and ribbed and smell like a cellar and the inside of a melon, and your hands will want to trust them, and you will keep them off. The drone that has been in your sternum since the second meter will suddenly have room to be itself, and the sensation is not hearing. It is being *played*, the way a glass is played, from the teeth outward.

Then the ledge, and forty minutes of dark adaptation, and the nave coming up around you in blue-green: vaults, fluted columns, catenary sheets, the organ-pipe fan at the far end, none of it holding anything up, all of it a specification eleven thousand years old. Your first thought will be *someone built this*. Your second, which arrives about ten minutes later and never fully leaves, will be *no one did*.

Your breath will fog and the fog will be taken sideways, visibly, and that is the moment it stops being a place and starts being an animal.

The shoals will resolve as smears, dozens per cubic meter, heat-haze in a cold room, turning together with a stutter in the light. You will not be able to stop watching them. Somewhere along the ribs, thousands of pure tones in grown cups, and you will find one and want to put your face in it, and you won't.

Nothing will happen. Nothing at all. The chord you hear when you arrive is the chord you hear when you leave. The room will not greet you, acknowledge you, or perform for you. The flicker is not language; it is loss; it is the photons the engine cannot help shedding, and it is the most beautiful thing you will ever see and it is not for you.

And somewhere in the second hour you will understand, in your body rather than your notes, that you are not a guest here. You are an event. A small warm loud event that the room will absorb and re-tune around and remember, in its way, as a ragged eleven seconds and a local dimming and a few cups gone quiet — and that if you were careless, it will still be healing from you when everything you have ever touched is dust.

That is the visit. Prepare for it as if you were preparing to be a very careful, very brief, very cold thought in someone else's head.
