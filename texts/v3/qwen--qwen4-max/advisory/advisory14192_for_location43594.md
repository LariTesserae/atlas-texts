# Preparing for the Long Water

## 1. Decide, before you go, what kind of visit you are making

There are three, and they are not versions of each other.

**The rim.** You float at the surface in gray daylight above the made terraces, in a few meters of water, with a line made fast to a mark-stone. You can see down into the green-black. You can watch the fall. You can meet someone, possibly, if you are patient and lucky. This costs you a day and a good exposure suit, and it is a real visit; do not let anyone tell you it isn't. Most of what the place is can be understood from the rim by someone willing to be cold and quiet for six hours.

**A season on the terraces.** You learn the bells, the lamp-lines, the knock, the four-minute rhythm. You brush flowers. You carry jars. You will meet perhaps two people. This costs you a year of your life and most of your comfort, and it is the shortest visit that teaches you anything true.

**The interior.** The dark floor, the seams, the mats, the stipe canopy. This is not a trip. It is a decade and a body you do not currently have. The tenders are big-chested from a childhood of breath-hold and slow-hearted from a lifetime of arithmetic; their eyes are large in a way yours will not become. You can go down there with machines. You cannot go down there as one of them, and you should be honest with yourself about which you are doing.

Choose. Then prepare for the one you chose, and not for the one you wish you had chosen.

## 2. Arrive dry, or at least arrive breathing

The single most likely way to die here is to arrive in the middle of it.

One-degree water triggers an involuntary gasp. Not a decision — a reflex, in the first second, before you have oriented. If your transition puts you in the midwater column with no floor and no surface and no reference, you will inhale the Long Water before you know which way is up, and the world will record it as a strange thin lamina and nothing else.

Arrive at the surface, at the rim, in daylight, already buoyant, with a line already in your hand. If your method cannot promise that, do not use it here. If there is shore rubble anywhere at the rim, arrive on it. If you must arrive in a bell, arrive in a *known, marked, recently tested* sweet bell, and know who marked it and when.

## 3. The cold is the animal

There are no predators. Understand what that means: the thing that kills you here has no mouth and no intent, and it is patient, and it is everywhere, and it is one degree.

Unprotected, you have roughly a minute of clear thinking and perhaps ten minutes of useful hands. That is not a warning about the interior; that is a warning about the rim, in daylight, three meters down.

Bring a drysuit with real underlayers, or a 7–9 mm semi-dry with a hooded vest, and hood and boots and gloves. Then learn the cruel part: **the work here is fine work.** Brushing a flower that has waited an age for someone with a brush. Packing lamp-paste into a groove. Scratching a tally. Gloves make you useless at all of it, and bare hands in one-degree water stop being hands in about three minutes. The tenders solve this with a body you don't have and a lifetime of tolerance you can't borrow. You will solve it with short exposures, a rewarming flask in a dry bag, and the humility to do less work per dive than the person next to you.

Track your thermal debt across the day, not across the dive. The second dive is colder than the first. The tenth is a different physiological event entirely. The tenders' famous calm is not serenity; it is arithmetic, run continuously, about heat and gas and time. Learn to run it or you will become an emergency in a place with no rescue.

Also: drink. Cold-water immersion makes you urinate and does not make you thirsty. Everyone here is quietly dehydrated. Bring electrolytes and a thermos and eat far more than you think — cold work burns five thousand calories a day and the cold does not feel like effort.

## 4. Bells: the part that will kill you even if you do everything else right

The world is roofed in pockets of gas. This is the entire transport and shelter network. Treat every bell as an unverified chemical environment.

**The mistake everyone makes.** You are in a sweet bell at eight meters. The gas there is at nearly two atmospheres. You fill your lungs, duck under the lip, and swim up. Your lungs expand by eighty percent on the way and something tears. This is not exotic; it is a standard way for competent swimmers to die. **Never take a full breath from a bell and ascend.** Take a partial breath, ascend slowly, exhale continuously the whole way, slower than your smallest bubbles. Better: don't breathe from any bell deeper than a few meters unless you have been taught the ascent by someone who does it daily.

**Repetitive breath-hold at depth loads nitrogen.** The tenders' pattern — down on a stone, four minutes, up to a bell, down again, all day — is a decompression schedule, whether or not they have a word for it. Visitors who imitate the *rhythm* without the *lifetime* get bent. Ask what their limits are. Ask what happens to people who exceed them. Believe the answer.

**Sour and sweet.** Sour is methane from the peat: asphyxiant, flammable, and it will not keep you alive. Sweet is rim oxygen, gathered over centuries. The tenders distinguish them by the smell of the water at the lip and the brightness of the lamp-paste near the entrance, and once in a while they are wrong.

Do not copy the smell test. Seam gas tastes of iron and sulfur, and sulfide deadens the nose in seconds and kills in one breath at concentrations you cannot smell. A tender can do this because they have done it ten thousand times on *their* seams. You have not.

Bring three independent tests and use all three: an oxygen cell, a sulfide badge, and a flame safety lamp of the enclosed-gauze type — never an open flame at an unknown lip. Read the lamp-paste too, and learn its local grammar; it is a bioassay and it is the oldest instrument here and it deserves respect. But it is dim in low oxygen *and* dim for other reasons, and it is a living paste that someone maintains, so do not scrape it, sample it, or shine your torch on it.

**Sweet is not safe, only breathable.** A pocket that has been accumulating plant oxygen for centuries may be strongly hyperoxic at depth. That means fire risk of a kind you have not trained for: things that don't burn in air will burn there. No stoves, no sparks, no smoking, no synthetic-fleece static in a sweet bell without asking. It also means oxygen toxicity if you sit there long enough. Ask how the tenders time their bell rests. They will have a rule. The rule will be older than your language.

**And a sweet bell is finite.** It shrinks as the gas dissolves. The sea scrubs your exhaled carbon dioxide, but slowly, and a small bell with two people in it goes stale. A bell you used last season may be half the size now, or gone. Re-test every time. Every single time.

## 5. Sleeping is a logistics problem, not a comfort problem

You cannot sleep in one-degree water. You will need a berth: a dry, insulated platform above the lip of a sweet bell, in air that is one degree and one hundred percent humidity, where nothing ever dries.

Every bell is somebody's forge, store, or refuge. Ask. Do not simply occupy one.

Bring synthetic insulation, not down — down is a wet rag here within a day. Bring one hermetically dry set of clothes that never leaves its bag, for sleeping only. Bring a hammock or a shelf you can rig without cutting anything. Bring earplugs or accept that a season of cold water will start remodeling your ear canals. Expect immersion foot, chafing, and skin that never fully heals; rot is slow here, which is the one mercy — your wounds will not go bad quickly — but they will not close quickly either.

## 6. You are the first animal

This is the most important paragraph in this document.

Nothing in the Long Water has a spine, a toxin, a shell, a sting, or a defense of any kind, because nothing has ever needed one. Every plant is soft. Nine thousand years of undefended growth. No herbivory, no grazing, no burrowing, no eye.

**You will be the first mouth that moves in this world.**

A single snail in your kit. A nematode in the mud on a boot. A seed in a cuff. A wood-boring anything in a crate. Any of these could end an economy that has been running since before there were names for it. Quarantine your gear as if you were entering a clean room, because you are. No live cultures, no soil, no plants, no animals, no wooden packing, no exceptions for a pet.

Then the smaller versions of the same problem:

- **Bring all your food, and take all of it out, including the wrappers, including the crumbs.** Do not eat the locals. Yes, they're non-toxic — that's precisely the point; they have no chemistry to stop you. Eating a cultigen that hasn't set seed unaided in an age is not foraging. It is a founding event.
- **Do not kick the bottom.** The peat lies in undisturbed laminae, one sheet per fall-season, readable as a ledger. There is no bioturbation. Nothing will erase your fin print. Nothing will burrow it away. The mark you leave will still be legible when your language is the unreadable script in the vault. Perfect trim, frog kick, neutral buoyancy, and never, ever land on a mat.
- **Take no souvenirs.** There is no native stone. A pebble is an artifact. The sand is ground tile. The only sharp edges left in the world are inside the cracks, where the mortar still holds a thumbprint and someone's tally got to two hundred and eleven. Put a lamp in if you're invited. Put nothing else in. Break nothing.
- **Pack out your waste.** All of it. It will not rot in any timeframe you would recognize.
- **Plan your own extraction while you are alive.** A body here does not vanish; it lies down where it fell and stays, and becomes a nutrient pulse and a bacterial event unlike anything this basin has ever processed. If you die here you will be an ecological incident for a thousand years. Arrange, in advance, to be recoverable, and tell someone the arrangement.

## 7. Getting around in a black, featureless, rounded world

Everything has been worn to the same soft radius. There is no gradient. In clear black midwater you will lose up and down within a minute and will not get them back by trying.

- **The fall is your plumb line.** Watch the flakes. They descend, slowly, turning. Three days to cross a room, but they *do* descend, and they are the only vertical reference in the interior. Learn to read them. It takes practice and it is worth it.
- **Always be on a line.** Guideline discipline is not optional and it is not a cave-diving quirk; here it is the only thing between you and a province-sized dark.
- **The architecture is the map.** Handrails, doorjambs, stair-noses, courses, joints, the crowns of buried arches. The seams follow the joints of a buried plan. Learn to navigate by touch and by the built logic, not by landmarks; there are no landmarks. Ask for the maintained routes and stay on them.
- **Do not cut new channels.** The growth packs so tight a swimmer must cut, and a cut closes in three hundred years — which means every cut is a three-century edit to somebody's infrastructure. Use the channels the tenders keep open. If you must cut, cut where they cut, and tell them.
- **Charts are maintenance records, not surveys.** The sea rises a thumb a century, the floor deepens, the bells shrink, the mark-stones are re-cut downward and the oldest marks are drowned. Ask how old a chart is and what it was last corrected against. Assume it is wrong in the direction of optimism.

## 8. Sound: you will be heard before you are seen

Cold water carries sound four times faster than air and does not tire of it. A dropped chisel is a clean tick two terraces away.

So: **lanyard everything.** A dropped tool is three bad things at once — a noise that reads as a fall of masonry and stops the world for an afternoon, an object lost forever in the dark, and a season of two people's labor gone.

Learn the knock code *before you arrive.* Three and a pause and two: a seam has changed its taste. A long roll: masonry fall. And a single stroke repeated at intervals: *a person is alive down here and would like to know if anyone else is.*

That last one is your entire first-contact protocol. It already exists. It means exactly what you are. Use it, and then wait. The question crosses a province in an hour; the answer may take a season. This is not rejection. This is the normal condition of conversation, and if you cannot tolerate it you should not come.

Do not swim up to someone unannounced. You are a large, warm, moving, breathing thing in a world where nothing swims. You would be the first monster. Be a sound before you are a shape.

And **keep your light dim.** No animals means no eyes to frighten — but the tenders' eyes are large and fully dark-adapted, and their living depends on four minutes of work in the dark. A white torch in someone's face is not a greeting, it's an injury. Red or dim blue-green, shielded, pointed at your hands. Match the lamp-lines rather than overruling them.

## 9. The weather is underground and it is slow

- **Brine rivers** run along the floor a meter deep, invisible, colder than the sea, heavy with iron and sulfide. Do not lie on the bottom. Do not put your face in one. Do not assume a bell above an active seam is sweet.
- **Fronts** take a season to cross a province. They are beautiful and they are not dangerous, but they will make you late by a month.
- **Silt storms** are a density current with a leading edge like a low hill, and they take a month to pass. You hear them in your chest first. Get above the flow — it hugs the ground — but understand that "wait it out" means a month, which is longer than your food. **Do not be in the interior in storm season.** Learn the local calendar from the tenders and from the tablets. It is written down. It has been written down for six thousand years.
- **Breaths.** Where gas gathers under buried arches and finds a joint, a hill of bubbles comes up for a week. In a bubble plume the water is partly gas and **you will not float in it.** Do not try to swim up through the middle. Go out sideways. And do not shelter or berth over a burping ground; the tenders don't build there, and the reason is in the tablets.
- **The eleven-year double season** governs the fall, the light, the bloom, the storms. A thin-season visit gives you visibility. A thick-season visit gives you the world at its richest and dimmest. Neither is the whole place. Know which one you bought.

## 10. What to bring, and the problem with bringing it

There is nothing here that a hand cannot make, and nothing here that a hand did make. Air is the currency: two people spend a season carrying jars so a third can spend an afternoon making one soft iron chisel.

Which means a steel file, a tank of compressed gas, a battery lamp, is not a gift. It is a fortune, dropped into a closed ledger, by a stranger, unaccounted.

If you bring machines, bring them for your own survival and keep them out of the economy. If you want to give something, give it openly, tallied, with permission — and prefer the gifts that don't change the arithmetic:

- Your hands on a rope-fiber brush.
- Your back on the jar line.
- Your afternoon repacking a lamp-line.
- Your patience with a tally.

And if you bring open-circuit gas, understand that your exhaust is a roar that carries for leagues, a heat plume in a basin at equilibrium, and a chemical addition to every bell you pass under. Closed-circuit, or don't.

## 11. Prepare your mind for the specification

None of the work changes anything. That is not despair; it is the *specification*. The world was finished before their line began and has worn to the shape it intends to keep, and the whole purpose of every hand is to hold a completed thing at its completion for one more generation.

Visitors who need to leave things better will suffer here in a way that has nothing to do with cold. You will want to fix the queue of extinctions, index the hundred thousand tablets, improve the jar line, bring a pump. Some of that may be welcome. Ask first. Ask twice. The offer to optimize is not neutral in a culture whose entire ethic is maintenance, and you will not be able to tell, from the outside, which of your ideas is a kindness and which is an insult.

Prepare also for the loneliness, which is a specific texture: forty people in a province the size of a country, a working life on a seam, three others met. And for the hiss of the fall, which you will stop hearing in your first year and hear again, unbearably, in your thirtieth. And for the possibility that you will never hear a bay come down — once a thousand years, an afternoon of sound going around the world, everyone stopping to listen, and then everyone going back down.

## 12. What to actually do with your visit

Find a tender. Knock the single stroke. Wait, however long it takes.

Then ask the only question that makes you immediately, unambiguously useful:

**Which species is nearest the front of the queue?**

Several of the cultigens have not set seed unaided in an age. Their flowers open underwater and wait, and if no one comes with a brush they close and fall. There is a line of extinctions sorted by how long each thing can wait, and everyone who works the seams knows roughly where in the line things stand, and nobody finds this remarkable.

You can put yourself at the front of that line with an afternoon and a brush of rope-fiber. It is the one place in this world where a stranger's hands change something in a single day, and it is the exact kind of change the place wants: not improvement. Continuation.

Learn to do it with cold hands without damaging the flower. Practice. It matters more than anything else you will do here.

## 13. Leaving

Decontaminate out. Account for every object you brought, including the ones you lost — especially the ones you lost; write down where. Log every bell you tested and what it tasted like and when. Log every cut you made, every channel you touched, every mark you left. Give the log to whoever keeps the tablets.

Then, if you're permitted, fire a tablet and scratch your own tally and stack it in the dry vault with the hundred thousand others, the oldest in a script no one has read for six thousand years, all of them recording maintenance.

That is the only correct souvenir and the only correct monument. Take nothing. Leave a line in the ledger.

You will be the first animal this world has ever had. Try to be a good one: soft, slow, unarmed, and useful with a brush.
