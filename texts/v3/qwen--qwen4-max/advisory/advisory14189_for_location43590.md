# Preparing for the Bowl of Standing Water

## 1. First, correct your expectations

You are not going to a viewpoint. There is no summit, no vista, no place where the thing arranges itself into a picture for you. The bowl is three hundred paces across and takes most of a day, and the whole of that day happens at a scale of one metre — a slot, a stone, a film of water, a rim of ice closing inward. If you go expecting landscape you will be bored for six hours and then frightened at dusk.

Go expecting to attend something. A congregation is the right word and it is not decoration. Four thousand-odd things are standing there in a posture they have held since before your species had a word for standing, and you are the interruption. The correct posture for a visitor is the one you'd take in a room where everyone is already praying: find a place to put yourself that doesn't require anything to move, and stay there.

Everything below is logistics in service of that.

## 2. Do not arrive directly

Whatever your method of crossing, do not open into the bowl. You would arrive standing, in a slot, at altitude, unacclimatized, in light that stops your eyes working, with no idea which direction the col is. People have died in less.

Arrive low, in the valleys below, and walk up over a week or more. Your lungs are the clock of this place — the makers' entire industry was forty-minute shifts because that was all the air allowed — and you do not get to skip the part where your blood learns the altitude. Two nights at intermediate height minimum, more if your body is a sea-level body. Sleep low, climb high, come over the col on your fourth or fifth day above the treeline of whatever world you came from.

And find the people below first. There is a valley with descendants in it, or at least a valley with a memory in it. They will know the calendar: which weeks the seep runs, which morning the leaning changes, whether the lens currently needs replacing, whether anyone has been up lately. Ask. Hire someone if you can. Bring loads for them if they'll let you. A traveler who arrives unannounced at a place with a tally wall is a traveler who is about to become an entry in somebody else's records.

## 3. Your body will lie to you here

This is the central physiological fact and everything else follows from it: **at noon your face burns, your hands go dead, and the stone under your palm is warm.** Three different thermal reports from one body, all true. You will read the sun on your face as "hot," strip a layer, step into a shadow slot at eight below with wet skin, and be hypothermic by mid-afternoon while feeling sunburnt.

Rules:

- **Never strip below your base layer.** Vent — zip, hood, cuffs — but keep the shell on. The slots are fifteen to twenty degrees colder than the sunlit blade-tops and you will cross dozens in an hour.
- **Mittens over liners, three spare pairs of liners inside your shirt.** Dexterity is needed only at the headwall, briefly.
- **You will not feel yourself sweat.** The air is dry enough that moisture leaves you as fast as it arrives. Combine that with altitude diuresis and sublimation and you can lose four litres without ever noticing you were damp. Carry all your water. Five litres a day, minimum, and it all comes up on your back.
- **Do not drink the bowl.** Not the seep, not the pools. That water is a working metabolism's four-hour shift and it is also the archive — the ice at the heart of the tall blades fell as snow when there were animals in the valleys that no longer exist. You'd be drinking a page. Carry your own.
- **Sunscreen where you would never think to put it:** under the jaw, the underside of the nose, earlobes, the insides of the nostrils, the lower eyelids. The light comes up. It comes up off the pools, off the polished rock, off the ice walls, from beneath and inside. A brim is not enough. A brim plus a buff plus category-4 goggles with side shields is the minimum.
- **Two pairs of goggles.** One on your face, one against your skin in an inside pocket. Do not take them off to see the colour properly. The colour is the thing that takes your eyes, and snowblindness arrives eight hours later, at night, in a stone cell with a knee-high doorway and no way down.

## 4. Cleanliness, in both directions

The black flour is a metabolism running at the pace of centuries. It has never met a fast one. You are a fast one, and you shed constantly — skin, gut flora, breath, crumbs, fibres.

- Bring a **dedicated outer set** that has never been in a forest, a field, a garden, or a barn. Smooth-faced shell, no fleece, no brushed linings, nothing that sheds. Wash it, seal it, open it at the col.
- Scrub your boots and rinse them with clean carried water **outside** the bowl. The floor is polished rock; the wells are where everything collects. Whatever is on your soles ends up in a lens of water and stays there for a thousand years.
- **No food that crumbles, no seeds, no live cultures, no wood that could sprout.** Eat at the headwall or outside the bowl. Pack out every crumb.
- **Pack out your urine.** This is not squeamishness. The mineral garden in the shaded pockets is a salt chemistry — gypsum, selenite, halite — that exists because the salt has nowhere to go. You are a nitrogen-and-salt bomb. One person relieving themselves in a cusp is a permanent amendment to a flora that has been seasonal since the ice stopped moving. Carry a bottle and a wag bag. Yes, all of it.
- On the way out, brush and rinse your soles again into a container and carry that out too. Do not take the flour home. Something that eats mineral and light on a century clock and shuts down when dry might do nothing at all in a warm wet world. It might not. Don't run the experiment.

## 5. Fire, glass, and the things you must not bring

There was never fuel here. No wood, no peat, no dung. **There was never a fire in this bowl.** Remember that when you find the hearth, because the hearth is not a hearth: it's a socket for an ice lens, and the slag beside it is copper softened by a spot of light.

So:

- **No stove, no candle, no lighter, no smoke.** Not for tea, not for warmth, not for the photograph. Your emergency plan cannot include combustion; a knee-high door and thin air will kill you with your own exhaust anyway. The emergency plan is *descend*.
- **No glass optics. Ever.** The lens sublimes away and must be replaced from time to time, and that replacement is the calendar, the shift, the tally, the reason anyone comes up at all. A glass lens that never needs replacing doesn't improve the forge; it deletes the institution. Also, glass focuses differently and there is nothing here to put out a hotspot on a blade wall.
- **Nothing concave and reflective.** No mirror, no polished bowl, no signal mirror laid face-up. The whole geometry of this place is light trapped between walls. Adding a mirror is adding a wound.
- **Be careful with dark gear.** Black is what survives here because black takes the sun and melts its own hollow a finger deeper each year. A dark object left standing will dig itself in, and it will farm a hollow at the expense of a blade that took eight centuries to sharpen. Wear pale on the outside. It's also, incidentally, the local camouflage of respect.

## 6. Moving through the crowd

There are no crevasses. The dangers are geometry and surface.

**The slots lean.** All the blades lean toward the noon sun, so a slot that is body-width at your boots may be closed at your head, or may be a slanted chute that takes you *under* the blades if you slip. Never enter a slot you cannot see the far end of. Never force one. A narrowing-slot entrapment at altitude, alone, is a death.

**Probe every step** with a pole, and lay the pole flat whenever you set it down. Never plant it. Standing things get noticed.

**The floor is a trap in two states.** Glacier-polished, striated, worn smooth by something enormous that left long ago — with a millimetre of water on it at noon it is the slipperiest surface you will ever stand on, and the pools read as *light on the floor*, not as water. Then at the third hour they freeze from the rim inward and it becomes black ice. Sticky rubber, no steel studs inside the bowl; the polish took a glacier to make and you don't get to add striations. Carry spikes for the headwall stairs and the approach, and take them off at the cairn line.

**Travel dry, work wet.** Cross the field in the morning before the seep starts. Do your sitting and your looking at the headwall or in a wide cusp during the wet hour. Be out of the field by the third hour. The makers worked through the wet hour, but they worked *at the headwall*, on rock, in one place, and they walked in and out in the dry.

**Flat light is the killer, not storms.** In cloud the bowl loses every edge and becomes a bright ache with no slots in it. If the light goes flat: stop, sit on rock, do not lean on ice, wait. Ten minutes or three hours. Have the layers to survive three hours.

**Never lean on a blade.** They look like a crowd; they are tonnes each, balanced in a posture maintained by subtraction. Toppling one is a permanent deletion from a number that has changed by three in all recorded history. And a fallen blade gets no second. It doesn't come back as a ghost. It's just gone.

**Voice doesn't work.** Thin air carries sound badly and the blades eat what's left. Your partner three metres away on the other side of a wall is unreachable. Agree hand signals. Keep visual contact. Do not split up. Do not rope — a rope catching a leaning blade is how you topple one.

## 7. The cairns will lie to you

This is the navigation hazard nobody anticipates. The route is marked by cairns. The field makes seconds of things that stand. **Therefore some of the cairns are hollow white guesses at cairns**, standing beside or in place of the originals, a little larger, leaning a little further.

Do not count cairns. Do not strike them to hear which rings. Do not "clear up" a duplicate.

Tells, if you must have them: a real cairn has a dust cap, settled fines at its base, a wind-scoured side, the look of weight. A second has none of these and is fractionally too clean. At dusk the seconds glow faintly from inside and the real ones don't — which is exactly when you should not be navigating.

Better: **navigate by terrain, not by markers.** Keep the bearing of the col notch and the headwall in your head. Treat the cairn line as a hint about where a laden person could once walk, not as a promise. The pins that marked the turns are now deep inside the blades, visible and unreachable. The geometry has moved on since the last cairn was built. A slot that was passable when your grandmother's grandmother came up may be hand-width now.

## 8. The day's clock

Plan around this and the place becomes gentle. Ignore it and it will hurt you.

- **White sun, violet sky:** arrival over the col. Goggles already on before you crest. Expect a few minutes of blindness and a shape in it. Let the shape resolve. Don't walk while it's resolving.
- **Morning, dry:** travel. Cross to the headwall. Read the stairs — one hundred and eleven, shallow, patient, a few dozen blows each, some number of generations. Read the tally wall with your eyes, not your fingers.
- **Seep starts:** four hours. Sit down. Find one pool.
- **Noon:** the paradox hour. Warm stone, dead hands, burning face. Put your palm on the black rock for a moment, then dry it — a wet hand on stone that's about to go sub-zero will bond.
- **The hour after noon:** the caustic hour. Light goes down into the lenses and comes back up; the blades are lit from inside; the slots become underwater dusk; nets of light crawl on the ice walls. **Do not navigate during this hour on your first day.** Figure and ground invert. Vertigo and vomiting at altitude are how people fall into slots. Sit on a boulder and let your visual system fail in peace.
- **Third hour:** the films go. The pools freeze from the rim inward with a sound like a fingernail on a drumhead. Be moving out. Put your ear to a cusp floor — through a cup or a cloth, not bare skin — and hear the day's water go downhill in the dark.
- **Dusk:** the long minute when four thousand blades tick at once. This one you'll hear from anywhere inside. Then get out, or commit to the cell.
- **Night:** the cobbles hold the day's heat until about midnight and then let it go. After midnight the cell is a stone box at ambient. Know that before you choose it.

The makers went down before dark. That is a good rule and you should keep it. If you break it — and you will want to, because the seconds are brightest at dusk, a crowd of pale lanterns among the crowd of blades — break it deliberately: stay put, don't navigate, don't count, have the insulation to sit out the cold, and be honest that you are trading safety for the best hour.

## 9. The seconds, and the temptation

Understand what they are before you're standing among them at dusk with your heart going.

They are not ghosts and not the dead. **The person under the cairn has no second.** The field copies what stands, and the dead lie down. What you're looking at is rime that took the *idea* of a thing and finished it: the chisel with the broken tip has a whole twin; a tool that came up with a wooden haft has a second with an ice haft, correctly proportioned, curved to a grip nobody in this bowl has ever held. The field has never seen wood. It saw the socket and guessed, and guessed right.

Rules:

- **Leave nothing standing.** Not a pole, not a tripod, not a flag, not a marker, not a cairn stone you added on the way in. If you must cache, lay it flat on bare rock outside the bowl — and know that a dark object laid flat still melts itself a hollow.
- **Do not leave a broken thing standing to watch it be repaired.** This is the classic visitor's sin here and it is a vandalism of a very specific kind. You would be inserting a sentence into a record whose entire length is *four thousand one hundred and twelve; four thousand one hundred and nine.*
- **Don't try to use a second.** It's rime. It's hollow. It sublimes. A second of a knife cuts nothing.
- **Leave the child's chisel where it is, and leave its whole twin beside it.** The pair is the artifact. Separating them destroys the only thing anyone has ever written here about grief.
- **Don't touch the cairn with someone under it.** Don't add a stone. Don't open it. If you want to honour it, cut a stair, or carry someone's load down, or sit with it for an hour and then go.

And the personal one, because it will occur to you: if you stand here long enough — seasons, ten seasons — the field will make a second of *you*, and it will finish the idea. Your limp gone. Your missing finger restored. Your bad hip guessed straight. People come here wanting that.

It is not healing. It is a hollow white guess, assembled over eighty years, gone in one bad winter, and it will lean. It will stand there glowing after you've gone home and someone will find it and think you died here. You cannot stand for eighty years. Nobody can. That's why they kept a tally and cut stairs instead.

If you want to leave something behind, leave a stair, with permission, in the count. That's the local form of legacy and it's the only one that isn't a lie.

## 10. If you're going for the Bow

Once a year, at the turn of the low sun, the whole field's lean shifts by about a degree across a season, by subtraction on one face and accretion on the other, and no single blade is ever seen to move. But there is one morning when four thousand edges meet four thousand neighbours at once and the bowl makes a single tick.

Learn the morning from below. Don't guess it.

Be inside before dawn — which means the cell, which means the cold after midnight, which means insulation you'd otherwise think excessive. Be on rock, in a wide cusp, near the middle. **Do not stand between two blades.** You do not want to be the thing that stops the touch.

Then be still. Your pulse will be louder than the sound. Thin air carries it badly; that's why you have to be inside. Most people miss it. Missing it is a normal outcome, not a failure — plan the trip so that missing it doesn't ruin the trip, and you'll be much better company on the walk down.

## 11. Emergencies

There is no rescue here, no fuel, no shelter that stays warm, and no water you're entitled to. The only real emergency response is **descend early**.

Know your altitude signs and be cowardly about them: headache that ignores analgesia, vomiting, ataxia, a dry cough that becomes wet, breathlessness at rest, confusion, the sudden irrational certainty that you're fine. Any of these: down, now, in the dark if necessary, with a partner, on the stairs.

Cold injury here is a descent problem, not a fire problem. Chemical heat packs are acceptable if sealed and packed out; a leak into a well is a poisoning.

Set a turnaround time keyed to the seep, not to your ambition. Write it on your hand.

## 12. What to actually do with your one good hour

Sit on a black boulder in a wide cusp near the headwall, in the sun, with your goggles on and your mitts on and your back against nothing that leans. Pick one pool the size of a shield boss. Watch it for a full seep cycle: the film arriving on the polish, sliding down the striations, gathering, holding the sky; the light going down into it and coming back up so the blade above you is blue-green in its depths and lit from underneath; the caustics crawling on the wall; the grit at the bottom, faintly greasy, doing its four hours of work for the ten-thousandth year; and then the rim closing inward with that fingernail sound.

That's the visit. Everything else — the goggles, the five litres, the wag bag, the week of acclimatization, the not-touching — exists so that you can sit there for four hours without being the reason something changes.

You came from a world where things are arranged for you. This one isn't. The best souvenir is a number you didn't alter, a route you didn't mark, a chisel you left broken beside its whole twin, and a very clear memory of water sitting on warm stone in air eight degrees below freezing, with the sky in it, going nowhere at all.
