# Preparing to visit the unfinished range, and the garden in the blowhole

## 1. Decide first which visit you are actually making

There are two visits available and they are not the same trip.

The first is to the fracture: a hand wide, a forearm long, at about the height of a face. You stand outside on hot rock in thin cold ordinary air, and you put your eyes near the hole, and the correct afternoon light comes out onto your cheek, and you look at nine opulent individuals standing in their territories. This visit is survivable by an ordinary human with ordinary preparation. It is also, I think, the whole of what the place has to offer a person. The hole is face-height. That is not an accident you should argue with.

The second visit is to go inside. Understand clearly: **an ordinary human cannot enter that room.** Not "should not," not "will find it difficult." The room does not contain water as a scarce resource; it contains water as an unproduced compound, and anything aqueous that crosses the threshold stops being wet all at once. You are about sixty percent aqueous. You would not dehydrate over hours in a dry heat, sweating and worsening. You would arrive as a small salt sculpture of yourself, in the shape of a person mid-step, and the room would keep it, because nothing here is subtracted, and in nine thousand years it would still be there, pressed flat into the lattice with the mica and the busbar.

Most of what follows is preparation for the first visit, with the second described so that you can refuse it knowledgeably.

## 2. The approach

Four thousand meters, and the survey keeps revising the number. Do not trust your altimeter, your map, or the summit profile you memorized. The mountain has not agreed to a height. Navigate by continuous things — a bearing on a fixed star, the feel of a ridge under your hand, a cord paid out behind you — rather than by anything that has to be counted or looked up.

**There is no water on this mountain.** No snow, no ice, no seeps, no frost in the shadowed flutes. The world here has addition and has not learned subtraction, which means no scree to shelter behind, no soil to lie on, and nothing to melt. Carry every drop for the approach and the descent, and carry it in a vessel that will not be incorporated: the ground is being *produced*, in standardized increments, downslope, and anything left sitting on it long enough becomes the next section.

Do not camp on the flank. Do not cache gear. Do not sleep with your ear against the rock, because the hum arrives in the teeth and sinuses rather than the ears, and earplugs do nothing about bone. It is a substation hum. It produces, in most people, a low dread with no object, and after some hours a nausea and a sense of being watched. Budget for that. It is not a message.

The whole mass carries current: sulfide beds, potentials generated across its own thermal gradients, metal collecting in dendritic seams along paths of least resistance. You are a bag of electrolyte walking on a live resistive floor. Wear dry insulating soles — mica, fused silica, alumina, not rubber with hydrated fillers. Keep your feet together on bare rock. Never put two hands on the mountain at different distances from each other. Never lie down. Do not shelter in a glowing fracture, and if you hear a muffled enormous report from inside the mass, move away from wherever it came from: that is a bubble being born, and you do not want to be standing where a void decides to exist.

Acclimatize properly, for weeks. Then remember that altitude plus absolute dryness makes nosebleeds and cracked lips likely, and that at this particular threshold a nosebleed is not a minor inconvenience. Which brings us to the real problem.

## 3. Why the threshold is the end of the road

At a hundred and fifty degrees, dry, a human survives by evaporating. That is the entire trick; it is why a dry sauna is survivable and a wet one at half the temperature is not. Here evaporation is not slow. It is *unavailable*. Your sweat would not cool you; it would cease to be liquid and become a rime of salt on your skin, all at once, and then the next layer, and then the wet parts underneath.

So: no closed suit full of coolant water. No hydration bladder, no wet cloth over the mouth, no damp bandana, no saline, no water-based sunscreen, no hydrogel dressings. Nothing hydrated in your kit at all — check for gypsum in cement, check for water of crystallization in anything ceramic you were told was "dry," check wool and leather and wood, which hold water in their substance and would go to powder and stiffness.

If you want something of yourself in that room, send something that has no water to lose.

## 4. The dry proxy, if you insist

A remote body: fused silica, alumina, graphite, dry metals, sulfur if you like. No polymer that outgasses, no lubricant with a hydroxyl in it, no hydrated anything. Its eyes are glass. Its joints are dry ceramic on ceramic. It will be clumsy and it will not feel the heat, which is a loss, because the heat is mannered — warmer along the ribs, cooler in the flutes — and that profile is one of the loveliest facts about the place and you will only ever have it as a plot.

Nothing vents into the room. Not exhaust, not purge gas, not the air you flushed the housing with. Everything that leaves your machine is captured and carried out. This is not fastidiousness. Read the next section.

## 5. What you are, to the room

Every plant in there has a root-mat spread through twenty meters of rock, mining hydrides at a few parts per billion. Hydrogen is the limiting reagent of everything alive in that cavity. It is expensive and tracked and allocated elsewhere. The nine stand well apart because their catchments cannot overlap; the bare sinter between them is not emptiness, it is territory drawn in a chemistry you cannot see.

You are roughly ten kilograms of hydrogen, most of it in water.

You are not a visitor to this economy. You are the richest ore body in the history of the room, by something like fifteen orders of magnitude, standing in the middle of nine mutually exclusive claims. A single exhaled breath is a fortune. A flake of your skin is a seam. If you leak — sweat, tears, blood, breath, a cracked lip — you do not water one plant. You overlap all nine catchments at once, and you do it in a chemistry where overlap is the one thing that cannot happen. I do not know what that does. I know it is not a kindness, and I know it cannot be taken back, because subtraction has not been invented here.

This is the fact to sit with, before you go, in a chair, at home. The room is not hostile. It is only incompatible. And the specific incompatibility is that you are made of the thing it has never been able to afford.

## 6. Discipline for a place where counting and causality are unfinished

**Bring nothing whose safety depends on a number.** Not a buddy count, not a rope-team count, not "nine plants, all accounted for," not a timer you read. *How many* is not fully implemented in there. Nine, sometimes eleven, and no seam where the change happened.

Use continuous physical processes instead. My preference: your partner stays outside the fracture, in the cold, holding a line, and beside them a block of ice on a cord over a drop. When the ice is gone the cord goes slack and they pull. No counting, no clock, no judgment call, and it uses water where water still exists. Their job is not to count you. Their job is to feel tension, continuously, and to pull when the tension changes.

**Precommit.** Write the whole visit down before you approach: where you will stand, what you will look at, in what order, and when you will stop. Then do that and nothing else. Effects run slightly ahead of causes here. The fiber sings and then breaks. The fin-stack has been curving for two centuries toward a fracture that will not exist for eighty years. If you improvise inside, you may find that your decision was the *effect* of something that has not happened yet, and you will not be able to tell which end of it you are holding.

If you feel a burn, you have already touched it. If you hear a single high note, you have already broken a fiber. If you smell rain — and you may, and it will be the first wet smell in the history of that room — you are already leaking, and you should be outside before you finish recognizing it.

**Do not ask anything twice.** Not a measurement, not a photograph, not a question. The room answers politely both times, and both answers are correct, and that is how it takes your shape. Eleven paces by nineteen, then fourteen by nineteen. Draw once. Look once. Do not verify.

**Watch for your own doubled edge.** Hold your eye on the stibnite fan and there is a fainter fan four centimeters to the left, the fan the room would have if the current ran otherwise. Uncollapsed, still being carried, still costing the works a little to maintain. Six hundred years, and drifting. If a second, fainter version of your hand appears offset from your hand, do not reach toward it, do not try to reconcile them, and do not attempt to work out which one is the alternative. That question has no answer in there and asking it is a way of staying.

**Do not say your own name in the room.** The reverberation is too long for the volume; sound comes back half a second late, slightly changed, sometimes twice. Something will answer. It will be polite. It will not be lying. Do not continue the conversation.

**Do not plan a return.** Things in there grow toward events. If you intend to come back, something may already be growing toward the intention. Before you look at anything else, look for whether the room has already accounted for you: a skirt of dead blades arranged like a step, a bract worn smoother than the others, a crown canted toward the exact place your face will be. If you find a shape that fits you, do not fit it.

## 7. Contamination runs one way

Nothing rots here. No mites, no worms, no rot, no decay of any kind — when a blade dies it stiffens and stays and the plant grows past it. That means no pathogens, which sounds like good news, and is, for you.

For the room it means you are the first organism in its history capable of subtraction. Your gut flora, your skin, your breath, the mold on your boot: an entire missing operation, arriving in a sealed cavity that has been running at a thousandth of real speed since before the range had a profile. Sterilize the proxy. Capture your own shed. Take out everything, including what you meant to leave, including what you didn't know you'd left.

And do not collect. A blade taken is a permanent hole in a permanent record, because this flaw is going to be kept — pressed flat, carried, none of it subtracted, for as long as the mountain lasts, which is a very long time. Also, removal may simply not be available: you may find that the blade will not come away, or that it comes away and is still there, or that you have taken the fainter one and are holding nothing at all.

Do not carry spores out either. Twice a century a crown goes violet and ozone-sharp and throws a cloud of same-signed metallic spores that go around corners along the field lines to a grounded waiting stigma. If you are in there and grounded, you are a stigma. If you are outside with spores in your bag, you are something this world has never had: a route between bubbles that will never be reached. Nine hundred years of isolation is not yours to end on a whim.

## 8. What to actually do at the hole

Sit outside. Cold thin air in your lungs, hot rock under your hands, one hand, feet together. The light coming out is five thousand six hundred kelvin, soft-shadowed, the light under which you would sort screws on a bench. Your body will read this as *ordinary afternoon, safe, indoors, someone else is handling it.* That reading is the most dangerous thing at the site. Train yourself, beforehand, in a museum or a cathedral or anywhere beautiful where the rule is *look, don't touch*, to notice the moment reverence starts negotiating with your hands.

Then look, and let it be slow. One mica blade every eleven years. One centimeter of busbar per human lifetime. The amber bead at the crown of the fin stack is a flower and you will not see it open. The fastest event in the room takes four seconds and happens twice a century. You are not going to witness anything. You are going to see the middle of something, which is all anyone ever sees anywhere, only here the ratio is honest.

Draw, don't count. Draw the spacing, the skirts of stiff dry ruin, the fern of native iron with its one bloom of blue-black oxide — the only patina, the closest thing in there to age. Note where the floor slopes toward the back wall. Note the glazed bracts if you can see them.

You will be able to see them. Nine small cups, hollow, glazed smoother than anything else in the room, sized precisely and uselessly for a drop. Grown to a standard that includes water, in a place where the utility was never connected. The socket is still there.

You will want to fill one. I want to tell you how to do it safely and there is no way. You cannot make a small offering in that room. The bract is sized for a drop and you are sized for a flood; the moment your tear reaches the air it stops being wet and becomes a small salt sculpture of a tear, and then the room takes the rest of you, because wetness goes all at once and not in installments. You would not be watering a plant. You would be connecting yourself as the service, in a cavity the works is holding open on tolerance — light, current, heat, checked and maintained — and the works does not distinguish between a component and a guest. Being kept on service is how you become part of the draft. And then the draft closes, in nine thousand years, and keeps you.

So: grieve at the hole if you need to, but turn your face away from it, and let the water fall on the outside of the mountain, where water is merely rare instead of forbidden.

## 9. Afterward

The sinter is freshly condensed crystalline silica that has never traveled — surfaces chemically raw, never passivated, sharper and more reactive than any dust you have breathed. The glass wool is finer than a spinneret can pull and biopersistent. Assume anything you inhaled in there is permanent, because in there nothing is removed, and your lungs will do their best to imitate the room. Full respiratory protection at the aperture, decontaminate before you eat, and get the fibers checked, and keep getting them checked.

If you were cut: nothing in that room rots, but healing is a subtractive operation, and you may find the wound stays exactly as made. Have someone competent look at it who is willing to write down what they see rather than what it should be.

And if you came out with a fainter second edge, four centimeters to the left: it drifts. Do not chase it. Do not ask anyone to confirm how many of you there are. Have your partner keep hold of the line and keep talking to you about ordinary things until you are somewhere with weather.

## 10. What the visit is for

Nothing in there is made, and nothing in there is hostile, and everything in there is to specification, and the specification includes a utility that was never connected. It is a draft being held open, run forward slowly to see what a void does when it is left alone. The most respectful thing an ordinary human can do is *let it be left alone* — go up, stand in the cold with your face near the warm hole, look once at nine extravagant individuals in the best condition they will ever be in, decline to answer when your voice comes back changed, and come down.

Whatever you do at that threshold will be added, and kept, and never worn away. That is the whole of the etiquette. There is no undo in a world that has not learned subtraction, so bring nothing you would not want pressed flat into a mountain and carried for longer than there have been people.

Bring your attention. Leave the drop unfilled. The bract will stay open through all of it — glazed, empty, correctly sized — and that is not a failure of the room, and it is not yours to fix.
