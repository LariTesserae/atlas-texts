# A Briefing for the White Tableland

*Read this before you go. Read it again at the rim, before the sound arrives, because after the sound arrives you will not read anything.*

---

## 1. Understand what you are buying

Three things will be taken from you, and you should consent to them now, in a quiet room, with a pen.

**Your hearing.** Everything born on that plateau is deaf. That is not a curiosity; it is the local verdict on ears. You will be deafened — probably temporarily at first, then permanently in some band you will spend the rest of your life noticing the absence of. Double hearing protection buys you time, not immunity, because the sound does not come to you through the air alone. It comes up through the stone, into your feet, your pelvis, your spine, your jaw, and it arrives at the inner ear from the inside, where no plug can reach it. Plan to come home partly deaf. Decide now whether the trip is worth it. People who decide on the plateau decide badly.

**Your heat.** Nothing there is cold in the way a winter morning is cold. There is no shivering shock, no numb-finger alarm, no moment where you say *we should turn back*. There is only a slow, even, courteous subtraction. You will be the single warm object in forty miles, radiating into a black sky that is effectively a hole in the universe, standing on cool stone that drinks conduction, in a wind that never once lulls. Hypothermia there does not announce itself. It presents as poor arithmetic, a dropped carabiner, a decision to sit down for a moment.

**Your sense of time.** No dawn, no dusk, no lengthening shadow, no evening. The sun goes around the rim of the bowl at a fixed height like a lamp on a turntable. Your circadian system will free-run and then unravel. You must bring your own evening. This is not a metaphor; it is a piece of equipment.

---

## 2. Train before you go

- **Sit in an anechoic chamber.** An hour, then two. The pockets of quiet on the plateau are the most violent sensory event there, because the roar drops out from under you like a floor giving way and you are left alone with your breath, your pulse, your gut. Travelers panic in the nodes more often than in the loud. Learn what your own body sounds like *before* it is the only thing you can hear.
- **Get on a vibration rig.** Low-frequency whole-body shake, chest and skull range. Learn your own resonances. Somewhere around the eyeball's frequency the world will appear to shimmer and crawl; that is your eyes, not the ground. Knowing this in advance is worth a great deal at hour four.
- **Learn a hand-sign vocabulary and a rope-signal code.** On the bare plain you cannot speak, cannot shout, cannot whistle, and cannot hear. Everything between nodes is done with hands, tugs, and written slates.
- **Baseline audiogram, vestibular workup, dental check.** Get a soft bite guard fitted. Your back teeth are a conduction path into your skull; a resilient bite plate that keeps the arches apart measurably reduces what reaches your head. It also stops you grinding your teeth to powder in your sleep, which you will otherwise do.
- **Write the vow** (see §10). Sign it. Date it.

---

## 3. Air, water, and the fact that you cannot smell anything

The air is thin. Assume serious altitude: acclimatize on the approach, or carry oxygen and a pulse oximeter and the drugs for high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema, and know which is which when you cannot think. Hypoxia plus a permanent vestibular insult plus a white ground with no shadow relief is the actual cause of most deaths there. Not the birds. Not the thing in the throat. **Walking.**

The absence of smell removes more warnings than you expect. You will not smell a stove's exhaust, a wound going bad, food turning, a companion's illness, wet rot in your own gear. Substitute instruments and eyes:

- A carbon-monoxide monitor with a **strobe and a vibrating alarm**, not a beeper. You will not hear a beeper. Ever.
- A daily visual and tactile body check: feet, groin, armpits, any fold. Everything you own is permanently wet with dew, at a temperature that never rises. Skin macerates. Trench foot is the standard injury. Two pairs of socks, one always dry in a sealed bag against your belly, and a hard rule about changing them at the same watch-hour every day.
- No appetite cues means no eating. Flavor is gone; only texture, temperature, salt, sour, and the trigeminal burn survive. Bring chili, mint, carbonation, hot broth, crunchy things, sour things. **Eat by the clock, not by wanting to.** Undereating at altitude in a cold sink is how competent people die.

Water is the strange one. Your gear is soaked and *you* are parched — you are the only warm surface for miles, so moisture leaves you continuously into air that is already dumping its load on everything colder. Drink far more than feels reasonable, and **add electrolytes**: the dew is close to distilled, and distilled water plus high output is a recipe for hyponatremia, which looks exactly like the hypothermia and hypoxia you are already fighting.

Do not drink from the seams without treating them. Yes, the water is essentially the loop's own condensate; it has also been through miles of wet living rock, algal film, and everything that fell down the holes. Filter and disinfect. Do not wash in a seam, do not piss in a seam, do not stand in a seam. Those hairline cracks are the only surface arteries in the world, and each one has an eleven-mile column of midges standing over it that you should not put your face into.

**Bring a dew tarp.** The sky overhead is a heat sink of extraordinary quality. A spread sheet of ordinary IR-emissive film will drop below the local air temperature and wring water out of the loop all "day," at a rate you can measure once and then rely on forever. It is the cleanest water source on the plateau and it does not require you to touch anything that belongs to somebody else. Note the contradiction and live with it: the same sky that fills your tarp is emptying your body of heat. Shelter *from* it, harvest *with* it.

## 4. Ears, bones, and the shape of a day in the loud

Protection stack, in order of importance:

1. **Feet.** Thick, damped, closed-cell soles. Never bare feet, never thin boots, never sit or kneel or lean or lie directly on stone. Carry a small damped mat and use it every single time you stop. The rock is the loudspeaker; touching it is putting your head against the cone.
2. **Jaw.** Bite guard in whenever you are in the loud.
3. **Ears.** Plugs *and* muffs, and put them in on the climb, at the first feel of the sound in your sternum — not at the rim. The loudness arrives before the place does. Respect that; it is the only warning the mountain gives.
4. **Skull.** A suspended shell, not foam pressed to bone. A hard hat's cradle is worth more than a padded helmet.
5. **Hands and pack frame.** Damped grips, padded hip belt. Don't let your trekking pole become a stethoscope.

Then learn the schedule of your own deterioration, because it is reliable:

- **Hour one:** the roar stops being a sound and becomes a hand on the chest. Sinuses, back teeth, the water of the eye.
- **Hour two to four:** you stop hearing it and start hearing your blood. This is worse, and it is your abort clock. When the blood arrives, you have used up the useful part of the traverse.
- **Beyond that:** the roar starts to have *structure*. Pattern. Intention. It does not; it is a solved standing wave and it is saying nothing. But a hypoxic, deafened, sleep-drifting brain in a landmarkless white field will find speech in anything. Treat "I think it's saying something" as a hard medical sign, like a fever.

Budget **eight hours in the loud, maximum**, and never sleep in it. Which brings us to the map.

---

## 5. The map is the silences

There are perhaps two hundred nodes in forty miles. They are the only places where anything can be heard, and therefore the only places where anything can be *said*, and therefore the only places you can camp, cook, confer, think, or be a person.

**Your route is not a line. It is a sequence of nodes,** the way the grazers' route is. Between one and the next you may walk a day. Plan the whole crossing as a chain, with alternates, and accept that a day of your trip is a day of enforced deaf-mute marching in order to purchase two hours of conversation at the far end.

Three things nobody tells you:

**The nodes are not permanent.** They are the cancellation points of a chord — and the birds *edit the chord*. A single wedged pebble bends a note for a hundred yards. A tuning dispute can move a node, shrink it, or close it while you are asleep in it. Therefore:

- Keep your hearing protection on in the quiet. Always. Even lying down.
- **Read the lichen.** The cushions are cut exactly to the shape of the silence, because cell walls tear where the sound is. That makes the vegetation a live readout of the acoustic field. A crisp dark edge means the solution is holding. A frayed, browning, translucent margin on one side means the node is migrating or collapsing under you. Check the edges at every arrival and every waking. It is the only barometer in a world with no weather.
- Never leave gear cached in a node you have not re-checked.

**The nodes are the pantry.** At a fathom's height the standing wave holds a shimmer of spiders and legged seeds motionless in the air. In a node, nothing holds them. They fall. That is part of why the green is there. When you camp in a silence you are camping in the one place where food rains out of the sky, which is also where the grazers come to feed and to say everything they will say to each other for the next day. Be small, be brief, be quiet, and do not eat the cushion.

**There is only one node two people can lie down in.** If you travel as a pair, that place is where your entire relationship will happen. Everything else is hand-signs and slates and a day of walking. Choose your companion accordingly, and do not schedule your difficult conversation for the loud, because you will have it anyway, silently, in your own head, for eight hours, and it will not go well.

**Plan your thinking.** You will have a limited number of audible, wakeful, private hours in the quiet. Decide before you go what you intend to think about. This sounds precious. It is the single most useful piece of advice in this document.

## 6. Eyes and feet

White ground, black sky, low fixed sun: a dynamic range your pupils cannot serve. Looking down and then up will transiently blind you for a second or two, and there are holes here from a needle's width to a well's.

- Category-4 glacier lenses, side shields, and cover the underside of your chin, your nostrils, your ears — the ground is white and lights you from below all the way around, because the sun goes *around*. You will burn evenly on all sides, which no sunscreen habit prepares you for.
- **Never look up while moving.** Stop, plant, then look.
- Rope up like it's a glacier. Probe with a pole. The rind is sound — it has already broken as far as it intends to — so the hazard is not collapse, it is the well-mouth you did not see because nothing casts a long shadow to tell you it is there.
- Near the rim, air is being *pulled down into* the high holes. Not hard enough to take you. Hard enough to take a hat, a map, a tarp, a glove. **Leash everything.** Nothing you drop is recoverable; there is no "down there" to go and fetch it from. The loop has it, and the loop does not give things back.
- Your shadow is a compass and an hour hand. It sweeps and never lengthens. Learn the sun's circuit period on day one, mark your shadow's azimuth at each watch-hour, and navigate by it. Cairns get unmade, footprints get sorted away by the wind, and there are no landmarks but the green stops on the flute.

## 7. Neighbors, and how not to insult them

**The birds.** Heron-sized, ash-white, deaf, tool-using, and armed with a held stone. They speak by drumming the stone with a callused heel; the message travels through the rock and arrives in a bird's feet four miles away. They own holes and they own notes, and a wedged pebble is simultaneously a name, a boundary, and an argument.

Therefore:
- **Do not hammer anything.** No pitons, no tent pegs driven with a mallet, no tapping a boot rhythmically on stone. That is not noise there; that is *speech*, and quite possibly a territorial claim or a challenge. Anchor with dead weight, ballast bags, suction, low-profile limped mass.
- **Do not remove a wedged pebble.** You would be deleting an individual.
- **Do not add one.** You would be speaking, badly, in a language whose grammar is a solved equation.
- Do not drum, do not imitate, do not "try to communicate." You will get it wrong in a way that takes a season to undo.
- Assume a heron's opinion of your eyes. Wear the lenses. They have a third lid for the glare and no reason at all to fear you.

**The grazers** walk the nodes and can only hear in them. If you are standing in a silence talking, you are standing in the only room in the world, talking over a calf. Yield the node. Step out into the loud and let them have it. It costs you nothing you can hear anyway.

**The mote layer** is at about head height and closes behind your wrist. Wear a filter over nose and mouth on the bare plain — you will otherwise breathe small lives all day, and the allergic reaction is not something you want at altitude. Do not net it, do not sweep it, do not run.

**The one cloud** drizzles on the same nine paces it always has. Those are the only rounded stones in the world. It is a superb navigation datum and a lousy campsite: it is over the coldest seam, and it is the one place where you can get properly, thoroughly rained on. Visit. Take nothing.

## 8. Kit, annotated

Damped boots and a damped sit-mat · bite guard · plugs and muffs · suspended shell · glacier lenses and a full-coverage hood · low-emissivity shelter outer over a **limp, mass-loaded, non-drumming** inner (a taut membrane in a standing wave is a musical instrument and possibly a weapon) · synthetic insulation only, never down, because nothing there ever dries · sealed dry-bags, one dry sock ration per day · dew tarp and a treated-water kit with electrolyte tabs · pulse oximeter, altitude drugs, CO monitor with strobe and haptics · trigeminal food · slate, grease pencil, hand-sign card · leashes for every loose item · geophones and contact accelerometers instead of microphones (record through the rock; the air will only clip) · a mechanical clock you trust and a paper log · an eye mask and a small red lamp · **no speakers, no whistles, no drones** (a rotor there is a tuning dispute with the sky).

## 9. Conduct

This is a finished instrument still playing. Nothing accumulates: every flake is dropped and sorted back into the lag, every bone goes down a hole and is gone. There are tools and no artifacts, work and no evidence. You will be tempted to read that as *nothing has ever happened here*. It means the opposite, and the correct response is humility about your own footprint.

You are the only thing in forty miles that accumulates. Your pack, your notes, your rubbish, your body. Rules:

- Carry everything out. Everything. Including urine, including a broken buckle, including a tooth.
- **Never plug, cover, dam, or park on a hole.** You would be editing the solution.
- Do not build. Do not stack. Do not carve. Do not name anything out loud in a node.
- Two people, three at the outside. One node at a time.
- Have a retrieval plan and a partner who will execute it. A corpse is the largest artifact anyone could introduce here, and the loop will erase it, and that is not a mercy — it is a contamination with no witness.

## 10. Time, and the vow

**Manufacture an evening.** Fix a watch-hour. At that hour, regardless of the sun's position, you stop, you eat something hot, you write three lines, you dim the lamp, you put on the mask, and you lie down in a node with your protection in. Do it whether or not you are tired. The place will not give you a single cue that a thing has ended, and human beings do not survive well in a world where nothing ever ends.

If you need a night, look straight up. The zenith is nearly black; there will be stars in it while the sun is still going around the rim. Ten minutes of that, hood up, is the only dusk you will get. Bring a chart for that sky. It helps more than you would think.

**Abort criteria, fixed in advance:** you begin hearing your blood and it does not stop when you reach a node · the roar acquires meaning · a lichen margin has browned across your camp · your shadow-clock and your written log disagree by more than an hour · anyone's oximetry drops and stays down · you find yourself wanting to answer something.

**The vow.** Down in the throat, one pipe among thousands sounds wet — a low note with a breath in it, a hesitation the wind does not account for. Nothing has ever been seen there.

Before you go, write this down and sign it: *I will not descend the throat. I will not lower a camera, a microphone, a light, or a line into the throat. I will not imitate the note. I will not wait beside it to see if it repeats.*

Write it because on the plateau you will be deaf, hypoxic, sleepless, and standing in the only place in forty miles where a sound can be heard, and you will construct an extremely good reason to go and look. Everyone does. The reasons are always excellent. The loop has been running at one setting since before there were ears, and the wet note is simply a fact of the place, like the cloud, and it has never needed anyone to come and check on it.

---

## 11. Coming home

Book the audiogram. Expect tinnitus that is not going away and a vestibular rehab period; don't drive for a while. Quarantine and decontaminate both ways — you are carrying a chaff of small lives out of a world with no dust, and carrying your own microbiome into one that has never had to defend against anything.

And warn yourself about the rebound. Smell will come back like a shout: rot, green, coffee, someone's hair, a wet street. Sound will seem thin and appallingly *specific* — every footstep an event, every voice an interruption, an entire world of things happening one after another instead of all at once forever.

Most people find, a month later, that they cannot sleep in a quiet room. They leave a fan on. It isn't the roar. It's just that they learned what silence costs, in the one room on the white plain big enough to lie down in twice, and they have not stopped listening for it.
