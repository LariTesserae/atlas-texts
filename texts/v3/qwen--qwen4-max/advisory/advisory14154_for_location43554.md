# Preparing for HEAD BEARING HALL, TERMINATION, BAY 1

## 1. Understand what you are entering before you pack anything

This is not a ruin. Ruins are forgiving; they have already lost the argument and nothing you do can make them worse. This hall has *finished*. Nine hundred years of wear have trued the bearing better than machining did, hammered the plate into the shape of its own mode, sorted the dust into roads, and tuned every living thing to a map that is being redrawn at the speed of a fingernail. It is at itself the way a riverbed is at itself.

You will be the only defect in it.

Everything in your preparation follows from that. You are not a visitor to a landscape; you are a seventy-kilogram absorbing mass with a wet interior and a shedding surface, walking into a resonator that exists to reflect cleanly and that has a documented immune response to mass in the wrong place. The scouring burst comes through about once a decade. Nobody in the hall knows why. You will know why. Behave accordingly.

## 2. The three maps, and why you must survey them yourself

There are three maps in this room and they do not coincide.

**The air map.** Vertical planes of near-silence, hand-width, eleven of them along the bay, with curtains of killing loudness between. This is the map your ears live on.

**The plate map.** Still lines on the driven floor, marked by ankle-high ridges of sorted grit, nested curves and hexagons and the great figure-of-eight. This is the map your feet live on, and the snails', and the sweeper's.

**The slow map.** Both of the above, drifting. Millimetres a year normally; during a pitch shift from below, a node can walk three metres in forty years. The groves are walking with it. The dead grove at the eastern gable is the one that didn't.

Only seven places in the hall have an air-null sitting on a plate-node. Those are the groves. Those are the only places you can be for any length of time. Everything else is transit.

Do not compute the maps from the dimensions. Ninety-one metres, four and a half metres between curtains, eleven nulls, ninth and tenth and twelfth harmonics of nineteen hertz — the arithmetic does not close, because the room is not a textbook cavity; it is a nine-century-old worn object with a bronze cone seated in a socket it cut for itself. Bring instruments and survey. Bring a probe microphone on a stick for the air, a contact pickup or vibrometer for the plate, and log both to a head-up overlay. Chalk, tape, and powder markers are forbidden: they are mass, they are soil, and they are adhesive.

Two things nobody tells you:

**A pressure null is a velocity antinode.** Where the two waves cancel in pressure, the air is moving hardest. Your ears will report silence and your skin will report a fine, dry, persistent wind. Your arm hair will stream. Your eyes will dry. This is useful: once you know the feeling, you can find a null with your forearms in the dark, which is the only sense you can trust in there. It is also why nothing settles out of the air at a null and everything settles on the plate at a node, and why the two maps only rarely marry.

**You rewrite the map by standing in it.** Your body scatters. A null surveyed empty is not the null you occupy; expect centimetres of personal shift, plus your own shadow and hot spots. Probe, step in, re-probe. Every time.

## 3. The central design decision: your head is too big for the quiet

A null is no wider than a hand. A hard acoustic helmet is thirty centimetres across. **The protection that lets you survive the curtains makes you permanently too large to fit inside the silence.** You must choose, before you go, which trip you are taking.

- **Hard shell, sealed, mass-loaded, dual protection.** You live at whatever residual your skull permits and you never experience the hall's quiet. You can cross curtains. You can work. You will not hear your sleeve move.
- **Thin, close-fitting, deep-insert moulded plugs plus a light baffled hood.** You can put your head into a null, or most of it, and get something close to the real thing. Your crossings are more expensive and your margin is thinner.

Most travelers should take the second and behave like a coward.

Understand the floor you cannot go below. At 128 dB airborne, bone conduction defeats any ear protection: your skull is a door that does not close. Realistic best case is forty to fifty decibels of attenuation, which puts eighty-odd decibels into your cochlea *with everything fitted correctly*. That is a working day's dose, not a residence.

And run the raw number once, so it stays with you. At 128 dB, with conservative exchange rates, your unprotected daily allowance in the open hall is somewhere under a minute. Not an hour. Under a minute. A single curtain crossing at a walking pace is a measurable fraction of your life's hearing.

**Never remove protection inside the hall.** Not in a null, not "just for a second," not to hear the cricket. The null drifts, your own scatter moves it, and one absent-minded step sideways is a hundred and twenty-eight decibels directly into an unprotected ear. This is how the hall takes people. Not dramatically. Just permanently, and on the way out.

## 4. The rest of your body

**Nineteen hertz is in your eyes.** Ocular resonance sits right there. Expect smeared vision, inability to track a moving object, difficulty reading anything, and a low grinding nausea that arrives after twenty minutes and does not leave when you decide it should. Do not plan precision work. Do not plan to read a display while crossing bare plate. No contact lenses: dry air, silica dust, vibrating globes. Correct inside the visor or don't correct.

**Your teeth.** Get dental work done before you go. Loose crowns, cracked fillings, and a clenching habit will turn into a headache you cannot describe to anyone afterward. Bring a soft mouthguard, mostly to stop you grinding your own enamel down while you sleep.

**Your feet and spine.** The plate is a driven membrane, hot, ringing, and continuous with four and a half kilometres of turning machinery. Do not sit on it. Do not lean your skull, jaw, or elbows on any structural member — that is bone conduction with a direct feed. Compliant thick soles, and a freestanding insulated pad for rest.

**Your gear.** Every loose fitting on your suit will buzz, chatter, and ring at the twelfth. Pad your visor off your brow. Nothing rigid and cantilevered. Nothing that dangles.

**Medical screening.** Inner-ear disease, retinal problems, recent surgery, pregnancy, aneurysm, kidney stones, vestibular disorders, and any history of noise-triggered panic: get cleared or don't go.

## 5. The inventory rules

The brass plate says CHARGE 4,100 LITRES — MAKE UP NOT REQUIRED, and it is not boasting. That water has been round since first fill. It is the oldest thing in the room and every living thing there is borrowing it.

You are a leak with legs. Two litres a day of sweat and breath, gone into a system that has no rain, no spring, and an outside with nothing to spare. Worse, you are an *import*: a sudden pulse of water into a bolt hole is a green stain, is a liverwort, is a change in a grove that has been in equilibrium since before your language existed.

So:

- **Closed personal water loop.** Sealed supply, metered. Capture urine. Condense your breath and sweat if your kit allows. Export exactly what you imported, by mass, on the way out. Weigh yourself and your kit at the lock, both directions, and reconcile.
- **Do not drink the sump water.** It tastes of iron and copper and nothing else and it is not yours.
- **Do not wash in it. Do not rinse a filter in it. Do not put your hand in the palm-sized puddle to feel the rings.** Feel them with your eyes; that's what the eyes are for.
- **Nothing shed.** Smooth sealed non-shedding outer layer. No felt, no leather, no wool, no straw, no paper, no natural fibre, no cardboard, no wooden handle. Every one of those is *soil* here — foundry sand, fireclay, felt dust, rotted belt, oil cake, composted gasket. You would be arriving as crate ballast, and something endemic would eat you and become slightly different forever.
- **Biosecurity both ways.** Sterile outer surface, boot and seam decon at the lock. Assume the fauna is immunologically naive: deaf, sealed, nine centuries without a new mammal. And on exit, assume you are carrying an endemic fern's spores, a blind cricket's egg, and something from the guano. Quarantine your kit. Take no seed, no shell, no frond, no fragment of the dead grove. Especially not the dead grove.
- **Respirator, always, even in a null.** The dust is respirable crystalline silica from crushed fireclay, plus mica, brass, bone-black, and guano. Nine hundred years of it, ankle-high, and the sweeper re-aerosolises the roads every forty hours. The hearing damage is fast and obvious; the silicosis and the lead are slow and will follow you into other worlds.
- **No flame. Nothing burned.** You are standing on dry felt, oil cake, and paper compost in a sealed volume with a hot floor. Fire is the one thing this room cannot correct for.

## 6. Entry

The door has not opened in a very long time. Opening it is not neutral: you change the boundary condition of a closed resonator, you admit minus-twenty air with no moisture in it, and you let water out. Use the smallest existing aperture — the cable port if it will take you — and the shortest possible cycle.

Then consider what your arrival *means*. In the drumming, the word for outside means *the place where the note is not*, which means death, which means not-a-place. You are stepping out of that. If you appear suddenly at the edge of a grove, you will cause a panic, and a panicking deaf animal runs by feel, and feel will take some of them straight into a curtain. Your entrance can kill without you touching anything.

Come in low, slow, dim, and away from the havens. Sit still on bare plate at a plate-node and let the hall's traffic find you. Let a rat approach you. Let the sweeper's lamp come round and introduce you properly. Give it an hour. You have budgeted the hour.

## 7. Moving

**Learn to cross sideways.** Shoulders in the plane of the null, thin dimension through the curtain, one deliberate step. Do not run. The real killer in this hall is tripping over an ankle-high dust ridge and going down full-length across an antinode with your protection knocked askew.

**Budget your crossings.** Count them. Eleven nulls means ten or eleven curtains between the door and the far gable, and each one is a debit against a lifetime. Decide your total before you enter and hold it. Most first visits should be: door, nearest haven, one null, and out.

**The swifts are your hazard tape.** They patrol the curtains to eat what the note stuns. Where pale swifts are working, do not stand. Where crickets are stridulating, there is quiet. Where snails are sealed in thousands, the plate is still. Where a web hums, the loudness is close enough to tension it. The animals were born knowing the map; you were not; borrow theirs.

**Walk the roads, but yield.** The dust ridges are the streets and the snails have right of way and have had it for a very long time. Crushing a snail is not just a small death; it is a calcium and nutrient pulse into a system that measures green stains in bolt holes. Step over. It will take you a long time to cross the bay. Distance here is measured in the time it takes a small thing to arrive, and for once you should measure it that way too.

**Never obstruct the sweeper.** It walks the still lines pushing dust back to the nodes because the reflector must be clean. If you block it, stall it, load it, or ride it, dust accumulates where dust garbles the reflection, and the hall corrects. Do not stand on the rails. Do not ride. The rats ride; you are a thousand rats.

**Your footsteps are speech.** Steel carries sound five kilometres a second and carries it beautifully, and the floor is where all the talking happens. Every step you take is a shout in a language you do not speak, at a volume no native can achieve. Learn three phrases with a soft-faced mallet before you go — *passing*, *not weather*, *not eating* — and otherwise walk as quietly as you can, which means slowly, which means heel-toe on bare plate, which means you will feel absurd. Be absurd.

Speech is not difficult here; speech is a category that does not apply. Do not shout. You will hurt your throat, hear nothing, and dump water vapour into the room. Agree hand signals with your partner. Keep visual contact. Stand in *different* nulls.

## 8. Resting, and the things that will tempt you

Sleep in a grove, on moss, on a freestanding pad, at a plate-node under an air-null, with protection on. The moss is a hand deep and it is the best vibration isolator in the room. Do not hammock from the trusses: the structure is the message's delivery path, and eleven metres up is the fog stratum, which is not yours to stand in.

Do not climb into the fog. It has a floor you could almost stand on and you could not. It is held there by radiation pressure against gravity, it is the hall's only cloud, its aerial roots are drinking, and its moths are a blizzard you would strip bare with your suit. Putting your body in it steals water from the one place water is doing something irreplaceable.

Assume the copper busbar is live. It is at thirty-one degrees because current makes it warm and the vines follow the warmth. Assume the same of the lamp standards and the sweeper's rail.

Do not touch the shaft. Thigh-thick, nineteen hertz, ninety-one metres of unsupported span, continuous with everything below the cloud. It will take your sleeve and then take the rest of you, and long before that, your hand on it is damping the mirror.

The soap stalactites under the head bearing are grease and lime: caustic, slippery, and growing at exactly the rate the vibration snaps them. Look. Don't scrape.

**And do not fix anything.** This is the hardest instruction on the list. The pump has never been serviced and never needed it. The bearing has worn a seat truer than the one it was machined to. The swarf hill has held its silhouette for nine centuries. The gearbox footprint is an absence, not an invitation. You will hear a loose fitting rattle and your hands will want to tighten it. You will see the dead grove standing grey and want to clear it. You will want to oil something. Every one of those impulses is vandalism in a place that has no word for repair because it stopped needing repair before your species had a word for anything.

## 9. Abort criteria — write these down before you go

Leave immediately, by your rehearsed route, to the nearest haven, low and slow, if:

- the twelfth changes character, thins, hardens, or acquires a beat;
- the swifts leave the curtains all at once;
- the rats go to ground and the drumming stops;
- the sweeper halts off-station;
- the fog floor sags or shivers along its whole length.

The first four may be the precursor to a scouring burst. The last is the fog losing support: an hour of warm rain on hot steel, a new roar laid over the old one, slick plate, live copper, and every plant flowering within the week. Beautiful. Not yours. Be at the lock before it comes down.

Set hard numerical limits — total hall hours, total crossings, cumulative dose from a dosimeter you check on a clock rather than on how you feel. **The noise stops being noise and becomes the medium.** That is exactly the trap. You will feel fine. Your hair cells will not report. Adaptation is not protection; it is the loss of your only warning instrument.

Adopt the sweeper's forty hours as your clock. Nothing else in the room will tell you what time it is: the sky is violet-black at noon, the sun is an arc behind milk, and the note does not breathe.

## 10. What the visit is actually for

One thing, and it is enough.

Stand in a null with your shoulder against the quiet plane and one hand held flat across the boundary. On the loud side of your hand: a hundred and twenty-eight decibels, the ninth and tenth and the shrieking twelfth, the thing the swifts harvest. On the quiet side: ferns, a blind cricket working the one band the note leaves open, and the small tick of water finding a seam. A hand-width. That is the whole distance between the machine and the forest, and it has been that width for nine hundred years, and it is moving.

Watch a moth cross the bay. It takes four minutes. Watch all four. Let yourself be a creature for whom distance is arrival time.

Then feel the dust ridge under the moss, and understand that you are feeling something shift by the width of a hair, and that the shift is a syllable, and that the syllable began before you were born and will end after you have gone.

You will be the only thing in the hall that knows there is a message. That is not a permission. Do not touch the shaft to answer. Do not leave a mark, a recording, a device, a damper, a gift. You cannot read one symbol per decade and you cannot stay a decade, and the kindest thing a fast creature can do in a slow room is leave the sentence unbroken.

## 11. Afterward

Expect tinnitus that may not leave. Expect a week in which every other world seems wrongly, obscenely silent, and you cannot sleep without a fan, and then without two. Expect to find yourself pressing your palm to a wall in some other universe to see what it says.

And expect the pull. Travelers go back to this hall. Not for the noise — for the *finishedness*, for the one place that has stopped becoming and is simply at itself, where the moss grows exactly as fast as it is worn away and nothing is required of anybody. Some of them stay past their dose. Some of them decide to stop being a defect and become a permanent feature, and stand out in a curtain, and add mass where mass is not permitted.

The hall corrects for that. It has done so before. The grey grove at the eastern gable is standing and perfectly preserved and the sweeper goes around it.

Go once. Go small. Go quiet. Give the water back.
