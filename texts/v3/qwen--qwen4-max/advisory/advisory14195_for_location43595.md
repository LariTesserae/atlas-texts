# Preparing to Visit the Ninth Sink

## 1. Begin by understanding what you will be, there

You are not a visitor to a city. You are a new category of object in a city that has no spare categories.

Work through this before you pack anything, because every practical decision follows from it:

**You will be invisible in the sense that matters.** Everything here reads infrared. In a room at fifty degrees, a body at thirty-seven is not a warm thing — it is a *cold* thing, a hole, a wet-looking anomaly. You will not read as a person. You will read as a coolant leak, a chill-loop return, a patch of sweating pipe, or as nothing at all. Your flashlight, your waving arm, your shout: none of these are in the local vocabulary. The visible lamps burn at eight percent for readers who are not present. You will be one more thing they are not.

**You have no standing in the queue.** Right-of-way here is settled by amperage draw and by how much of the schedule you hold hostage. You draw nothing and hold nothing. You are, formally, the lowest-priority object in the chamber. Two hundred and forty units negotiate continuously in modulated whine along the power rails, faster than hearing can follow, and you will walk through the middle of that conversation like a deaf man crossing a busy switchboard. Nobody will yield to you. Nobody will warn you. There is no audible alarm in the Sink because there is nobody to alarm.

**You are a corrosion event.** You will exhale water and sweat salt onto iron in a chamber whose only predator is oxidation, in the exact wet stripes where the scavengers harvest. Your skin, your hair, your breath, the crumbs in your pocket — these are the first food this place has ever been offered. Nothing here has lungs, or eyes, or blood, or a mother. You will bring all four. Understand that you are not entering a sterile place; you are entering a place that has never had to be sterile, and that has no immune system because it has never had anything to catch.

**Nothing here will come for you.** Not out of malice. There is no directive about you, in the same way there is no directive about the mats or the eleven thousand tags. If you go down in an aisle, the most likely outcome is that the routing tables acquire a detour.

Sit with those four facts until they stop feeling like warnings and start feeling like the weather. Then you can pack.

## 2. The clock is not your clock

The chamber runs on thirty-one hours. Your body runs on twenty-four. Every cycle you stay, the safe window slides seven hours across your day, and by the third cycle it will land in the small hours of your morning, when you are stupid and heat-depleted and will make the mistake that kills you.

**Plan for one cycle. One rain. Then out.**

Learn the shape of it:

- **T+0:00** — chillers dump into the roof loops. The ceiling goes cold.
- **T+0:00 to 0:09** — rain. Fine, warm, gray, falling sixty meters through dust.
- **T+0:09 to 0:40** — steam off the floor in sheets; floor dries; puddles retreat to their stripes.
- **T+0:00 to ~5:00** — the **cold clamp**. Fracture faces contract and press together; the foundered mass locks; the roof will hold a load. This is the only window in which percussion is permitted, and the only window in which a soft thing should be moving around under sixty meters of unfinished rock.
- **After ~5:00** — the loops recover, the ceiling warms, the mass relaxes and *sheds*. Dinner plates. Then a slab the size of a door.

Do the arithmetic on the shedding, because your helmet will not do it for you. A one-kilogram spall falling sixty meters arrives at about thirty-four meters per second — roughly six hundred joules. A hard hat is rated for something like fifty. A door-sized slab arrives with the energy of a car on a motorway. **There is nothing you can wear on your head that will save you from the roof.** Your protection is not armor. Your protection is *timing and overhead cover*.

Set your exit at **T+3:00**, with two independent countdowns, neither of them your wristwatch. Anchor them to the dump itself. The dump is unmistakable — the whole ceiling changes temperature at once, and if you are carrying a thermal imager you will *watch the clamp form*, a cold bloom spreading across the fracture surface sixty meters up. That image is your safety certificate. Learn to read it. When you see the roof begin to warm again on the screen, you should already be gone.

## 3. Air, heat, and the predator that breathes

At floor level: about fifty degrees, thick, oily, tasting of hot iron. Rock at the low wall reads sixty-two. The concrete outside the wet stripes is hot enough to burn. Twenty meters up it is thinner, hotter, and smells of ozone from the trolley wires and the arcing. At the roof it is a dry sixty that kills anything with lungs inside an hour.

Three consequences:

**Moving air does not cool you.** Above body temperature, a breeze is a convection oven. The slow columns rising through the chamber are not relief; they are heat delivery. Do not stand in an updraft thinking you are catching a fan.

**The floor is a contact hazard.** Sixty-two degrees will give you a full-thickness burn in a few seconds. Never sit on the concrete. Never kneel. Never put a bare palm on the low wall the way the tenders do — they press sensors there, not skin. Rest standing, or on an insulating pad you carry, or leaning on nothing at all.

**The predator breathes.** This is the hazard nobody thinks of. Corrosion is oxidation. Eleven thousand units' worth of rust, harvested continuously in a sealed chamber, is an enormous slow fire, and it consumes oxygen. Add carbonate deposition at the warm sumps — those calcium soda straws are precipitating out of water carrying dissolved CO₂ — and a sulfide seep along the north wall, and you have every ingredient for bad air pooling in low places.

**Never enter a sump. Never lean into one. Never put your head below the level of the deck to look at something.** The deep sumps do not cool; they are hot, wet, chemically active, and quite possibly hypoxic and sour with sulfide. Carry a four-gas monitor — O₂, CO, H₂S, and a dedicated ozone sensor — clipped high, near your face, not on your belt. Ozone fatigue is real: you will stop smelling it long before it stops damaging you. The old rule of thumb, *do not go above the level where you smell iron instead of ozone*, is a good sanity check and a bad instrument.

**Hard ceiling: tier four.** Above that you are trading breathable air for a view. The lagged mains cross at tier four; that is high enough to see the whole amber depth of the place, and low enough to come home.

## 4. Breathing and dust

The rock flour has never entirely settled since the roof foundered. It is *freshly fractured* mineral — angular, unrounded, chemically reactive, the worst kind of silica — mixed with metal fines, cooked mineral wool from failing lagging, sulfur compounds, and rust. It hangs in every updraft and lies on every gantry top in a gray felt.

This is not nuisance dust. This is the thing that will still be in your chest twenty years from now.

Minimum: a powered air-purifying respirator with a full hood, P100 particulate plus acid-gas and ozone cartridges. A hood rather than a half-mask, because the dust gets in your eyes and your hair and your ears and you must not scratch. Positive pressure also means you are *leaking outward*, which is the correct direction for contamination control here.

Better, if you can manage the heat: supplied air, with an escape bottle. But be honest about the trade — every gram of sealing you add is a gram of cooling you must carry.

**A sealed suit in a fifty-degree room is a coffin unless you actively cool it.** This is the central engineering problem of your visit, and it is more likely to kill you than the roof.

Your options, in order of realism:

- Phase-change or ice vests under a reflective, breathable outer. At fifty degrees these last thirty to forty-five minutes. Carry spares, and count them as your real air supply.
- A chilled-water or forced-air cooling loop with its own battery and its own reservoir. Heavier, longer, more failure modes.
- Pre-cooling before entry: ice slurry, cold shower, ten minutes in something cold. It buys you a genuine margin. Do not skip it.

And accept the ugly truth: **the only large cold sink in the chamber is eleven degrees and runs through every pipe, and you must not touch it.** It is lagged, it is sweating, it is electrically adjacent, it is standing in a conductive wet stripe, and it is the precise surface the scavengers are harvesting. Hugging a cold main is the most tempting act in the Sink and it is a compound error. Bring your own cold. Leave theirs alone.

## 5. Kit

Beyond the breathing and cooling:

- **Dielectric boots, composite toe, heat-rated soles, deep tread.** Rubber, because the Rule of Quiet is a rule about *footfalls* as much as about presses. Practice walking silently in them before you go. You will be amazed how loud you are.
- **Two glove systems:** thin dielectric inner, heat-and-abrasion outer. Plus a third pair you never use, sealed, for emergencies.
- **Knee pads and elbow pads.** No tier has a clear span longer than a body-length. You will crab along pipe bundles the way the maintenance crawlers do. Plan to be sideways a great deal.
- **A thermal imager, and a spare.** This is the single most valuable object you will carry. It lets you see the world in the local language: hot seams, working bodies, live rails, the cold wet stripes, the ceiling clamp forming and releasing. It is your eyes, your clock, and your early warning. Guard it like a lung.
- **A heat beacon.** Small, resistive, steady, worn high on the shoulder or helmet, above pipe clutter. Something in the sixty-to-eighty-degree range with a slow, regular modulation. You are trying to appear in their spectrum as an *occupied hot thing with a rhythm*, not as a hole. Do not use a flare or anything that reads as a hot bearing or a fault — you will summon a response you cannot interpret. A modest, patient, repeating warmth is the closest thing to a raised hand you have.
- **A true-color light, small, cupped, used sparingly.** Under eight-percent sodium everything is amber and black; you cannot distinguish a coolant line from a hydraulic line from an energized rail by its paint. Color-check close work with your hand shielding the beam. Otherwise keep it off: in this dust, a bright visible beam is a solid white cone that blinds you with its own backscatter and tells nobody anything.
- **Tethers for every tool.** Lanyard everything. A dropped wrench is percussion, and percussion outside the clamp is the one thing this city has organized its entire existence to avoid. Also it may fall on something.
- **Water: one to one and a half liters per hour, all of it brought with you.** Three hours means four liters plus margin. Nothing in the Sink is drinkable. The condensate on the chill skins is the cleanest water here and it has still run over rust, brown mineral wool, and cooked grease; the salt rings tell you what evaporation leaves behind. Emergency only, filtered and treated. Never the seep — that is sulfide and acid and dissolved rock, and it is somebody's garden.
- **Gas monitor, ozone monitor, two countdown timers, a personal CO₂/temperature logger if you have one.** Log everything. You are the only entity here capable of keeping a record for a reason.
- **Nothing loose. No jewelry. No rings.** High-current busbars will weld a ring to a rail with your finger inside it. Hair contained. No contact lenses.
- **A sealed waste system.** All of it. Sweat-soaked base layers, wipes, everything. You do not leave organic matter in the Ninth Sink.

## 6. Moving through the crowd

Two hundred and forty units in a volume you would call a large church, never more than a meter apart, all of them touching through rails and busbars and the shared coolant return and the frame of the chamber itself. What looks from the walkway like a slow courteous shuffle of iron is a negotiation happening a thousand times faster than you can perceive.

Rules:

**Yield always. To everything. Even when you are sure you are right.** You have no amperage and no schedule. You cannot win a right-of-way argument here; you can only fail to have one.

**Learn one tier's loop and walk it in the direction of flow.** Do not cross traffic. Do not stand in a lane. If you must stop, stop *inside* a rack void or a stair tower, not beside one.

**Never step over a rail. Never step between two charging units.** The queue shunts. Ranks close. A gap that exists now is a promise nobody made.

**Never place yourself between a unit and the busbar, or in the sweep of a hoist landing, or in front of a furnace mouth.**

**Test every deck.** The gray felt hides everything: edge markings, holes, missing grating plates, hydraulic weep. It is talc on glass. Probe with a foot before you commit weight, every step, especially at the top of a stair tower.

**Do not clip your harness to pipe, hangers, conduit, or cable tray.** Those are the city's circulatory system, not your anchor points, and some of those hangers are wearing calcium straws a century old. Clip only to structure you have verified.

**Do not clear spalls yourself.** Fresh fracture arrises are knife-sharp — no rounding, no patina. A plate that has come down is a blade lying in the dust. Do not brush it off a gantry with your hand. Note where it fell, log it, route around it.

**Refuges:** the stair towers. But note — only *one* stair in this chamber is built to human measure. The others are sized for tenders and machines: wrong riser height, wrong rail height, open grating. Treat them as ladders, not stairs.

## 7. Quiet

This is the discipline that will feel strangest and matter most.

Outside the cold clamp, the Sink moves on rubber and hydraulics and speaks in currents. Your contribution to the noise floor should be zero. Not low. Zero.

- Walk softly. Set things down; never place them with a click.
- No hammering, no tapping a rail to get attention, no clapping, no dropping, no slamming a carabiner.
- **No shouting. Ever. Including — especially — if you lose your partner.**

Agree this in advance, in words, before you enter: *if we become separated, we do not call out. We both go to the rally point immediately. We do not search.* Searching kills people here. Calling out is both a percussion event and a useless signal in a stone box with an eleven-second reverberation, where every sound arrives from everywhere and nothing can be localized. Your voice will not tell your partner where you are. It will only tell the roof that something new is making noise.

If you are present for a permitted press strike, mid-clamp: **wear hearing protection, and stand somewhere you cannot be reached by anything the strike dislodges.** The chamber answers for eleven seconds — one flat *whump* unfolding into a long stone chord off the north wall, the south wall, the roof, passing through every body in the racks. It is not a sound you hear. It is a sound you are briefly inside of. Units log it. It is the closest thing here to an event everyone attends. You will want to attend too. Attend from under cover, with your ears plugged, and let it go through you.

And learn the one sound that overrides everything: **the roof ticking.** Not the tick of cooling metal, not the relays conversing in the cabinets like insects that were never alive. The roof's own tick, as it moves. Everyone stops for it. You should stop too — not because stopping helps, three seconds is not enough time to do anything useful, but because it is the correct thing to do in a place where everyone stops.

## 8. The rain

Nine minutes, every thirty-one hours, over the entire footprint. The most beautiful thing in the chamber and the most dangerous nine minutes for a body in an aisle.

Understand why: **every machine in the Sink stops what it is doing and moves into the open at once.** Arms out, booms raised, hatches cracked. Two hundred and forty units, simultaneously, in the dark, in rising steam, doing something other than watching where they are going. Their attention is elsewhere. Yours must not be.

**Choose your rain-position before T+0. Be already there. Be already stopped, already sheltered, already holding something.**

- Under a tier, or in a stair tower, or on the human stair beside the hoist.
- Not in an aisle. Not in the open floor. Not near a furnace housing, where the drizzle flashes to steam.
- Not anywhere your only exit crosses traffic.

Expect to be blind in both spectra. Falling water resets every surface temperature in the chamber; your thermal imager will go uniform and useless, and your eyes will have steam and gray drizzle and amber nothing. This is the one interval when your instruments fail you. Have a hand on something solid before it starts.

If you take the stair: it is oiled steel, and now it is wet. The handrail is at the height a hand would want. Use it.

## 9. The garden, the tags, the stair

There are three things here that will ask something of you, and none of them will ask aloud.

**The mats.** Two hundred square meters of pale sulfur-oxidizing filaments out of forty thousand square meters of surface. The only thing in the Sink that grows without being instructed to. They glow faintly green when the air is driest, for reasons nobody here is equipped to investigate. They also scrub the sulfide from the seep, which is one more reason your lungs are working.

Do not touch them. Do not sample them. Do not lean in and breathe on them — you will change the humidity of the only garden in the world, with an instrument that exhales water and carries skin flora. Do not photograph them with a bright light. Look at them from a meter away in the dark, and let the green be as faint as it is. The sweepers have routed around them for so long that the detour is budgeted as geometry. Be the same kind of respectful.

**The tags.** Eleven thousand serial tags in a rack on the wall of the tender bay, arranged by number, dusted. No directive about them either.

Do not take one. Do not add one. Do not read them aloud. If you find that you want to dust them, notice the wanting and let it pass — you are not the tender, and you do not know what the dusting is for. It is enough to stand in front of the rack and understand that somebody arranged eleven thousand numbers in order for no reason that anyone can find, and that this is either the saddest thing in the chamber or the most hopeful, and that you will not settle which.

**The stair and the hoist.** The cage is polished, the gate greased, the cables dressed weekly, the landing lamps lit at eight percent, and two hundred meters above it a bulkhead that has not opened since the roof foundered and the world above stopped answering.

**Do not ride the hoist. Do not attempt the shaft. Do not try to open the bulkhead.** You do not know what is on the other side, you do not know what sealed it, and you do not have three days of cooling and water to find out.

You may climb the stair. That is what it is for. It is the only thing in the chamber built to the measure of a body that isn't here, and it has been kept oiled and clear of dust for you by something that does not know it is waiting. Climb it slowly. Put your hand on the rail at the height a hand would want. At the top there is a landing with lamps burning for nobody, and you will be nobody, and for a moment the standard will be correct.

Then come back down. Do not stay to see whether anything notices. Something may — a cold anomaly that moves against the routing tables will be logged, the way the press is logged, and a unit may come and stand a meter off and look at you in a spectrum you cannot see. Do not run. Do not reach out. Let it look, and go on with your visit, and understand that you have just been the largest event in this chamber since the last roof fall.

## 10. Company, failure, and getting out

**Never alone. Minimum two inside, one outside with a hard extraction capability that does not depend on the hoist, the bulkhead, or anything in the Sink.**

Comms will be unreliable — a stone box full of steel, fifty-hertz busbars, and broadband arc noise. Assume radio fails. Agree hand signals. Assume you will lose sight of each other within a minute in the amber depths; the dust makes every beam a wall. Keep contact by proximity and by thermal, and accept the no-calling rule.

**Rally point: the hoist landing, under the eight-percent lamps.** It is maintained, it is unmistakable, and it is the one place built for you.

**Hard exit time, non-negotiable, decided before entry, enforced by the outside party.** Not "when we've seen enough." A number. Heat exhaustion in a hood at fifty degrees does not announce itself; it presents as confidence.

If your cooling fails, or your respirator fails, or your imager dies: **the visit is over.** Not degraded — over. Leave immediately, in the direction of flow, yielding to everything. There is no repair option here. There is no spare part that fits you.

If your partner goes down: this is the hardest thing in this guide, so decide it now, in a cool room, with a pen. You have a cooling budget measured in tens of minutes, no medical support, no lifting equipment, no help coming, and a body that cannot be carried sideways along a pipe bundle by one person in a hood. Do the arithmetic before you go, and make your peace with it, and do not make the decision for the first time on a hot deck in the dark with the roof ticking.

---

## 11. Leaving

Decon properly. You will be coated head to foot in gray felt — fresh silica, metal fines, mineral wool, sulfur, rust. Wet-wipe everything before the respirator comes off. Bag the suit. Bag the base layers. Nothing you carried in comes out loose, and nothing you brought in stays behind.

If you bled anywhere, if you spat, if you dropped food, if you touched a mat: write it down, with the location and the chamber-time. There is no authority to report to. Report anyway. In a place where something dusts eleven thousand tags for no discoverable reason, you can at least be the kind of thing that leaves a record.

Then check your thermal log for the roof temperature at the moment you exited, and confirm you were out before the clamp released. Do this every time. It is the only grade you get.

## 12. What it will do to you

Prepare for this part deliberately, because it is the part that will follow you home.

The chamber looks inhabited. Nine tiers of gantry, catwalks, trolley wires, stair towers, a lit signboard, a canteen-shaped void in the plan. Everything at your measure. Everything running. And no rats in the cable trays, no moths at the lamps, no flies, because there is nothing to lay eggs in. The dust is entirely mineral and metallic. No skin, no hair, no pollen.

You will be the first organic thing in that volume in a very long time. When you shine a light into an empty bay, something will come to it — you, your own hand, your own breath. That will be the moment it lands on you: *when a lamp is left burning in an empty bay, nothing comes to it, ever.*

You will want to fix that. You will want to be the answer — to open the bulkhead, to ride the polished cage up the two hundred meters, to sign something, to say something into the eleven-second reverb and hear the chamber answer you the way it answers the press. You will want to leave a mark on the only city you have ever found that has kept your stair oiled for you.

Decide before you enter what you will not do. Write it on the inside of your helmet. The chamber will make a very good case, and it will make it without saying a word, in amber light, with two hundred and forty iron bodies standing in warm rain with their booms raised, and the mats opening in the north seams, and the landing lamps burning all the same.

Go for one cycle. Stay under tier four. Move on rubber. Yield to everything. Touch nothing that grows and nothing that is numbered. Be out at T+3:00.

And when you climb the stair — because you should climb the stair — remember that the routing tables are patient and literal, and that a detour around a thing that stood somewhere once can persist forever, budgeted as though it were geometry.

Choose where you stand. You may be leaving it behind you for a very long time.
