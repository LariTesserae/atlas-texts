# Preparing to Visit the Pond

## 0. First, correct your instincts

You are not going to a dive site. You are not going to a ruin. You are going into a **machine that has been left running long enough to become a climate** — and the machine does not know you are there, has no off switch you can reach, and is not finished changing shape.

Five facts should reorganize everything you pack:

1. **There is no outside.** A headspace under the Lid looks like a sky, has clouds and rain and thunder, and may have no oxygen in it. Seventy years of rusting steel in a sealed air bell eats oxygen quietly. The most likely way to die here is to surface into what looks like weather and take a deep breath.
2. **There is no ground.** Not "deep water" — *no bottom that is not a floor.* Nowhere to stand, nowhere to lie, nowhere to anchor, nowhere to be horizontal and warm and still. Every emergency plan that begins "get them ashore" is void.
3. **There are no rounded surfaces.** Everything you touch was sheared, drilled, or broken, and nothing has had time to be softened. The world's default interaction with skin is *laceration*.
4. **There are two clocks, and neither is yours.** A 24-hour lamp day and a 19-hour pump tide. They beat.
5. **Life is a film, not a soup.** The open blue is empty. Anything large moving in open water is either a predator or you, and the predators have had seventy years to learn which.

---

## 1. The four fast deaths and the three slow ones

**Fast:** bad air in a headspace or a stalled bay; drowning after cold-shock, entrapment, or getting lost in the blue; a flooded compartment letting go while you're inside it; an intake or a duct taking you while the pumps are on.

**Slow:** hypothermia at nine degrees, which is *honest* and will therefore lull you; noise, which you cannot escape and cannot properly protect against underwater; and lead. Note the words on the bulkhead: **white paint on red lead.** Red lead is a lead primer. The orange drifts you'll be stirring up are rust *and* seventy years of shed paint. Assume leaded paint everywhere, assume asbestos in any pipe lagging in the dry voids, assume the dust haze downstream of a collapse is not just dust. There are mercury-vapour fixtures overhead and metallic sodium in the low-pressure ones. Nothing here is inert.

Practical consequence: **you never eat, drink, adjust your mask, or touch your face with unwashed hands.** You never dry-sweep, sand, scrape, or kick up a drift in an enclosed space. You wear a P100 respirator in dry dusty compartments, not a cloth mask. You get a blood lead level checked after any long stay.

---

## 2. Hands: the first thing you buy

The text tells you the first lesson and it is the correct one. Take it literally and build your whole movement style around it.

- **Cut-resistant liners** (HPPE, ANSI D/E) against the skin, **thermal gloves over them**, and a **steel-mesh or armored palm glove** for the hand you actually load. You will lose most of your dexterity. Practice every task — clip, valve, camera, buckle, knot — gloved, in the dark, before you go.
- Better than any glove: **never grip an edge at all.** Learn to move by *pushing with flat palms*, by *clipping*, and by *hauling on your own line*. Bring padded slings and a padded hook on a lanyard so that when you must hold something, you hold your own equipment, not the mill's.
- **The world is steel: carry magnets.** Two or three lever-release or switchable magnetic anchors are the single most useful non-obvious item on the list. They give you handholds and belay points in a place that has no natural ones. Test every one before you weight it — rust drift, paint, and fouling all steal holding force, and a magnet placed on a mussel bed is placed on nothing.
- **Full skin coverage, always.** Not for the cold: for the hydroids, which sting, and the barnacle and mussel beds, which are the only reef here and are made entirely of blades. Knee and forearm guards. No bare wrists between glove and cuff — that gap is where nine degrees lives.

Medical prep: **tetanus current**, a wound kit you can use one-handed (irrigation syringe, saline you brought, waterproof dressing, skin glue), and an antibiotic your doctor has chosen with *Vibrio* and *Mycobacterium marinum* in mind. Every cut here is a contaminated cut. Tell whoever treats you afterwards exactly where you were; fish-tank granuloma shows up weeks later and gets misdiagnosed. And seal every cut before you go anywhere near Sump Nine — the halocline will find it, and the burn of that salt on an open wound is the kind of pain that makes people do stupid things at depth.

## 3. Exposure and thermal strategy

Nine degrees for hours means a **drysuit with a real undersuit**, hooded vest or 7 mm hood, and the discipline to end a dive *before* your hands stop working. The ache in the wrists and the back of the skull is the warning; by the time you're thinking about how cold you are, you've already lost the judgment you'd need to decide to go up.

But this world also has **31 °C plumes**, and that changes things:

- The plume margins are the only hearth in the world. Plan them as **rewarming stations** — a controlled hang in warm water at the end of a cold dive is worth more than any hot drink you can carry, and being warm rather than cold during your shallow stops is physiologically the right way round.
- Do **not** ride a plume core. Cathedral-door diffusers pushing 31 °C into 9 °C water make an elevator you don't control. It will lift you into deck plating and wired glass, and it will do it faster than you can vent.
- Twenty-two degrees of swing, repeatedly, wrecks seals, fogs masks, and makes you sweat inside a drysuit — and sweat is the cold you get later. Carry a spare seal, plan your suit's venting, and accept that thermal cycling is a maintenance problem, not just a comfort one.
- Immersion diuresis plus no dry place to relieve yourself plus no potable water equals dehydration. Bring more fresh water than seems reasonable. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.

## 4. Depth, gas, and the absence of a chamber

Nobody has published a depth limit for the sumps and every core has bottomed out on plate, which means the honest answer is *unknown, and deep*. Treat any descent below the Lid as a **technical dive**: proper training, redundant gas, appropriate mix, computer plus backup timing, a buddy who is not you.

Then apply the rule that matters most here: **there is no chamber, no hospital, no helicopter, and no beach.** A decompression injury in this world is essentially untreatable. So dive absurdly conservatively — no-decompression or minimal obligation, generous margins, no sawtooth profiles, no "one more look."

Better still: **do most of your deep work remotely.** A small tethered ROV with a light and a camera will show you Sump Nine's salt beach and its pickled windrows without putting a mammal in a brine pool. Save your body for the top twenty metres under the Lid, where the light is, where the film of life is, where the world is actually happening.

Weight yourself for the layer you intend to live in. Brine dense as syrup will refuse to let you sink; you'll bob on the halocline like a cork on a table, and the shimmer will hide everything below it. Do not over-weight to defeat that and then be a stone in the seawater above.

## 5. Air doctrine

This is the section to memorize.

- **Never breathe a headspace without measuring it.** A four-gas meter plus oxygen, with a pump and a wand you can push ahead of you. Sample at several heights. Oxygen depletion from corrosion, hydrogen sulphide from any stalled bay, ammonia and carbon dioxide under the gull ledges, and hydrogen from long-anoxic chemistry are all plausible.
- **Never enter a stalled bay.** Loop dead, aerators dead, white mat, struck-match smell. That is sulphide, and it kills at concentrations where your nose has already stopped reporting it. And you will not hear the danger, because the danger *is* the silence.
- Since the world is loud as a foundry, **you will habituate to noise within a day and stop hearing it.** That means your best warning signal is one you can't detect by ear. So: carry a personal H₂S/O₂ monitor with a **vibration alarm**, worn against skin, and train your buddy to watch it. And train yourself to notice the *absence* of the shrimp.
- Learn the normal soundscape as an instrument panel: the 50 Hz in the teeth, the 12 Hz lower down, the hourly water-hammer knock, the check-valve note passing bay to bay. Once you know the normal, put a flat gloved palm on plate now and then and *read the machine through your hand.* Steel will tell you things your ears have stopped noticing.
- The gull ledges are the only real daylight and the only soil, and they are also an ammonia-and-spore environment. Guano aerosol carries ornithosis and histoplasmosis-like fungi. Respirator, eye protection, no dry sweeping, wash before eating. And gulls with chicks will hit your head hard enough to cut it, on edges, in a world where every cut is infected.

## 6. Movement doctrine

**Never cross the blue desert untethered.** A kilometre between columns, no motes, no reference, and a gyre with hundred-metre internal waves that take nine hours to pass. In that clarity you will believe you can see your destination and you will be wrong about the distance, and if you lose the column you have no up, no bottom, and no horizon. Lay line. Clip. Keep steel in view. Ascend along a column or a line, never free in open water — blue-water ascents are where vertigo kills people.

**Navigate by frame numbers.** Stencilled frames are the world's coordinate system and they don't move. Log them like waypoints: *41,200 → 41,800 → the anode halo → the sodium bay.* Do not trust a magnetic compass in ten kilometres of energized steel with cathodic protection running; it will lie to you confidently. Radio and GPS won't reach you. If you want electronic navigation, deploy your own acoustic beacons and accept that the shrimp beds will jam them at short range.

**Ducts, intakes, and diffusers: the nineteen-hour rule.** The pumps run nineteen hours and stop for five. That off-window is the only time to enter a throat, and it is also the world's tide clock, which means it slides five hours per lamp day. Verify the stop *yourself*, treat it as a lockout you do not own, work tethered with a spotter, and be out with a wide margin. Never approach an intake on the assumption it's idle. Never trust a float valve to be honest.

**A dry bay below its neighbours' waterline is a loaded gun.** Eleven metres of head waiting on a seventy-year-old bulkhead. If you find one, mark it, leave it, and tell everyone. Do not open a hatch below waterline, ever, for any reason, including curiosity about a clipboard.

**If you hear a sound you have never heard, go up.** The failure of Bay Nine Thousand went round the world twice. You will not outrun four hours of filling, but you can be somewhere sensible when it starts. Precursors worth knowing: a new weeping hiss, a change in a bay's level relative to its neighbours, fresh bright shear on paint, angular rubble, a dust haze that hasn't decided where to lie down.

**Find the gull road.** Gulls commute. Seeds came in on feathers. That means there is at least one penetration to the actual sky and the actual sea, and it is the most valuable thing you can chart on your first day. Chart every one you find. Those are your exits to breathable air and real sun, and they are the only things in this world that deserve the word *outside*.

## 7. Light, colour, and talking to your buddy

Under the sodium bays, red is black — and so is blue, and so is green. A monochromatic orange world reduces your entire colour-coded signalling system to *bright* or *invisible*. Your red danger flag, your buddy's yellow octo, your green "OK" light: all meaningless.

So: **agree on shape, pattern, motion, and touch — not colour.** High-vis white and retroreflective tape. Strobes rather than coloured beacons. Line-pull signals with a written protocol both of you have drilled. Full-face masks with comms if you like, but plan for them to fail: a hectare of snapping shrimp drowns a voice at three metres, and at lamp-dusk a hundred thousand drum fish make sound into a physical pressure. Touch contact and a taut line are the only channels that never degrade.

Carry a primary light and two backups anyway. You'll rarely need them in the lit provinces — and you will absolutely need them in the failing banks out past Frame 90,000, one fixture a month, where the shrimp have gone quiet and something pale and slow is doing very well. My advice on that region: don't, unless you have a hard tether, gas monitoring, a strong reason, and a willingness to be the first person to find out what lives there.

## 8. The clocks, and how long to stay

Do the arithmetic before you go, because it's beautiful and it's your itinerary:

- Lamps: 24 hours. Dawn arrives as a wave crossing the water at walking pace; morning takes eleven minutes to reach the far wall.
- Pumps: 19 hours. High water slides five hours per lamp day.
- 19 lamp days × 24 h = 456 h = 24 pump cycles. **The Coincidence comes every 456 hours.**

So the natural unit of a visit is **one full beat: nineteen days.** That gives you every phase relationship between light and tide, one Coincidence, and the eleven-day fry window that follows it. Anything shorter and you'll see a world that happens to be in one mood and mistake it for the world.

Carry two clocks — a watch or timer that can display lamp-phase and pump-phase simultaneously — and log lamp-dawn at your bay and high water each cycle on paper. Do not trust your body to infer the time. Sleep on lamp-night with an eye mask and earplugs; the pump noise will fall at a different hour every day and will otherwise shred you.

**Arrive about six days before the Coincidence.** Use those days to acclimatize to cold and noise, lay your lines, learn the acoustic map, chart the gull roads, and locate a safe observation position.

**During the Coincidence, be out of the shallows.** The water goes milky for a kilometre — that is zero visibility inside a mass feeding aggregation, with the big things coming up the warm axis from four bays off and gulls going in until they can't lift. Watch it from a dry gantry above the flood, tethered, with hearing and eye protection, or from the very edge underwater on a hard clip with a line straight up. Do not swim through it. Do not dangle legs off a catwalk. Do not bleed.

And do not shine lights into the fry clouds during the eleven days. That's the world's entire recruitment; a light in a fry cloud is a dinner bell.

## 9. Camp

There is nowhere to lie down. Accept it and pack for it: **a hammock or a suspended cot with your own straps**, hung above the topmost wet gauge mark with generous margin, in a dry compartment that is *ventilated*, structurally boring, and has **two independent exits, neither of them downward.** Never behind a single bulkhead below waterline. Re-read the gauge every few days — the flood is not finished, and the topmost mark is already wet.

Strap yourself in. Sleep clipped. A world with a tide that keeps the wrong time can move your boots while you're dreaming.

Keep the camp away from the gull ledges and away from the tree. That's the nursery and the only forest; it doesn't need a human sleeping in it.

---

## 10. Emergencies, honestly

Write your plan on the assumption that **nobody is coming.** No rescue service, no shore, no radio, no chamber, no flat warm dry place to lay an injured person. Minimum two, preferably three. Hard tethers. A lost-buddy protocol agreed in advance and drilled: *return to the last magnetic anchor at frame X, wait one water-hammer knock, then ascend along the column to the Lid.* Rehearse the whole thing in a pool, in the dark, with foundry noise playing through a speaker, before you go.

Train specifically for: gloved one-handed wound care; mask and regulator work with no visual reference; placing a magnetic anchor blind; a controlled ascent on a line with the world spinning; and hauling an unconscious adult up a column with no ground to stand on at any point.

## 11. Etiquette, which here is mostly biosecurity

This is a closed world with one sediment, one reef, one tree, and no history older than the pumps. You are a contamination event.

- **Sterilize and dry your gear before entry.** No soil, no seeds, no organics, no damp folds from another world's water. The only tree arrived on a feather; you could easily bring in the second tree, or a rat, or a fungus that takes out the mussel beds and ends the world's only reef.
- **Turn no valve.** The duty cycle is the climate. Nineteen hours was chosen by a dead man for load scheduling and it is now the reproductive calendar of every species here. Adjusting it is not maintenance; it is a mass extinction with a wrench.
- **Read the clipboard. Photograph the clipboard. Do not do what the clipboard says.**
- Take no souvenirs — not the mug, not the boot, not a pickled fish from the windrows. That salt beach is the closest thing this world has to a fossil record and it is irreplaceable and unordered. Photograph, don't collect.
- Bring all your food. Eat nothing from here: the filter feeders have been concentrating zinc and whatever else for seventy years, and the food web is nutrient-poor enough that removing biomass is a real subtraction.
- Don't smooth anything. Don't file an edge, don't clear a drift, don't tidy. The unroundedness is the point; you're visiting the first act of a landscape.
- What you *can* give: **record the topmost wet gauge mark with a date.** Photograph the stencils while they're crisp. Count the dead fixtures at the failing bank. Those are measurements nobody is making, in a world with no scientists, and they'll be worth something in a thousand years.

## 12. The mind

Budget for it. A cubic kilometre of nothing with a grey column fading into blue at the edge of sight does something to people — a specific derealization, the *blue stare*, where you hang with your light off and stop being sure which way is up or whether you are moving. Loud-as-a-foundry does something else: after a week you'll stop hearing it and start feeling watched by the machines. And the crisp stencils, the mug, the grease handprints at the height of someone short, with no bones anywhere — that will ambush you at some point, probably on a rest day, probably hard.

Countermeasures are unglamorous and they work: keep a written journal, keep a human schedule that is *yours* and not the drum timer's, keep one small daily ritual, talk to your buddy about something other than the Pond, limit open-water crossings, get real daylight through the glazing or up the gull road every day. Don't anthropomorphize the loop. It isn't a god, it's a duty cycle. Don't name the tree, either — leave it the way you found it, unnamed and nesting.

Expect to dream about edges for a while afterwards.

---

## 13. What to actually go and see

If you've prepared all of the above, spend your nineteen days on these, in roughly this order:

- **Swim with the dawn.** Get ahead of the lamp wave and let it catch you, or match its walking pace and watch the fish rise ahead of it like a second tide. Eleven minutes of morning, over and over.
- **Get into a headspace — on measured air — and stand in the rain.** Fog in banks a hundred metres thick, lit from below. Thunder that arrives twice, once as a slap in the ribs and then again through the air. There is no other place in any world where you can be indoors and get rained on by a sky you are underneath.
- **A green place.** A plume core, a poison halo, a duct throat: three-metre ribbons of sea lettuce after all that grey and orange. It looks like a mistake. It's the only lushness there is.
- **Sump Nine, by camera if you're wise.** The halocline, the salt crust, the ten-year-old fish in windrows sorted by currents nobody has measured. Nothing rots. It's a heap of accidents and it's the world's memory.
- **The tree, from a respectful distance, with a respirator on.** Three metres, wind-pruned by a vent, growing out of bird lime and bone, gulls underneath, cloudy fertile water below them, and the big ones waiting patient in the blue below that. The whole food web in one vertical glance.
- **The Coincidence, from somewhere safe.** The whole world coming up into the light at once.

---

## 14. Leaving

Rinse everything in fresh water you brought — the Pond cannot rinse anything, and brine will crystallize in your regulators and eat your gear from the inside. Dry, then quarantine your kit before your next world; hydroid fragments, shrimp, and algal spores travel beautifully in suit folds. Wash thoroughly, dress any cut properly, get the lead check, and write down what you heard while you can still remember the normal soundscape.

Then put your hand on the rail one last time — gloved, flat-palmed, the way you learned — and notice that it still hasn't softened.

That's not decay you were swimming in. It's youth. Give it a thousand years and there'll be mud made entirely of the dead. You got there for the part where everything is still sharp.
