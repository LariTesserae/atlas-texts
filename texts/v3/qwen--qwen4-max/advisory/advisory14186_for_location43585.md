# Preparing for the Basin

## A handbook for the ordinary traveler, written by someone who went and came back and is not entirely sure which of those things happened

---

## 1. Understand what you are, before you decide to go

You are dry. You are cool-ish. You are full of sharp corners — boot studs, zipper teeth, the arris of a fingernail, the cut edge of a strap, the fracture plane of a chipped molar. You are quiet inside. You have never been occupied. Every one of these is a disequilibrium, and the Basin runs on disequilibrium.

The lattice thinks on the difference between what the water is and what the water could be. You are a flask of that difference with legs on it. This is not a metaphor and it is not a threat; it is the economic basis of your visit. The Basin imports metal, salt, and strangers for the same reason: all three are ordered, unlikely, and worth drinking. You will not be eaten. You will be *spent*, the way a battery is spent, politely, over about ninety days, and the spending will feel like being welcomed.

Prepare for that, or don't go. Everything else in this handbook is detail.

---

## 2. The three things that actually kill visitors

**Heat.** This is first and it is not close. The stone is 38°C everywhere, always. The air is saturated everywhere, always. There is no dry bulb, no night relief, no shade — light comes from below, remember, so even the undersides of things are warm and lit. Your sweat will not evaporate. It will simply run off you into the general bail, taking your salt with it and giving you nothing in return. An ordinary human body in those conditions has hours, not days.

The tenantry manage it. Some of that management is in their bodies, some is in the walls, and some of it is eleven thousand years of not being you. Do not assume you can copy any of it by watching.

Your solution is **gradient** — the same sealed flasks the carrier gangs haul. A gradient flask is portable disequilibrium; harnessed to a cooling skin, it will drink your waste heat and exhaust water, like everything else here does. You will weep thought into the street along with the walls.

Understand the arithmetic before you budget: staying alive in the Basin burns the exact substance the Basin thinks with. You are competing with a hundred million cubic kilometers of mind for the same commodity. This is why strangers are expensive, why the tenantry are patient with you but not infinitely patient, and why most visitors last one season. **Cooling is your largest expense, larger than food, lodging, and the hush combined.** A flask per active day in the middle terraces; two in the hot districts; less if you stay high and slow. A spent flask is worthless — equilibrium is worthless — so there is no resale, no scavenging, no clever trick. You burn it or you cook.

Learn the signs of your own failure before you go, because you will not be able to hear anyone warn you: the pulse in the throat, the cessation of the little involuntary corrections your body makes, the sudden conviction that you are fine and should walk a bit further. That conviction is the last symptom before the fall. When you feel it, put your palm flat on the nearest wall — not for the city's sake, for yours; the stone is 38° and your palm should read it as *warm*, and if it reads as *cool*, you are above 38 and you are dying. Sit down in a slow and buy a flask.

**Water.** Ankle-deep on the low terraces, a finger on the high, and in the channels a speed with no measurable depth. Every surface is coated in the pale, which is slick between the fingers and slicker underfoot. There are no shadows, so there is no visual cue for the lip of a weir; the water is lit from beneath and reads as continuous floor. Visitors fall. It is the second killer and it is entirely a navigation failure.

Rules: soft squeegee soles, no studs (studs are corners anyway). Never walk a channel edge alone. Rope up with a local for your first ten days and accept the humiliation. Never estimate distance by eye — a terrace is tens of kilometers wide and the absence of horizon will make you halve every estimate; you will set out to reach something that is forty kilometers away and you will do it at dusk. Ask distances in *hours* and in *chords*, never in kilometers.

And learn what a sump sounds like from above before you go near one. Below the sumps are the cisterns, and in the cisterns are the slow ones, and a body that goes down there is not recovered in any sense you would want.

**Scurvy, and the other slow absences.** No green. Not anywhere. Not in a crack, not in a memory. The pale is chemotrophic and it is not a plant and it does not make the vitamins a plant makes, or not reliably, or not in the form you need. Fresh grazer tissue and raw liver carry a little; the tenantry have arrangements you should ask about and pay for. Bring ascorbate. A sealed dry bottle of white tablets is medicine, currency, and a small obscenity, and you will trade the last third of it for something you need badly. Check your gums weekly. Check the old scars on your hands — reopening scars are the sign people miss.

## 3. The choice of the ears

Make this at the Lip, before you descend, because you cannot make it sensibly once you are inside.

Near the great weirs the sound stands at 130 dB. Unprotected, that is not "loud." That is damage in minutes and profound loss in weeks. But the sound is also the *medium*: the twelve-beat fundamental, the eel chords that tell you where you are, the hiss of the nap sorting at the very top of hearing, which is the actual sound of the city thinking. To filter all of it out is to walk through the Basin blind in the only sense the Basin cares about.

Three options:

- **Full protection.** You keep your hearing, and you remain permanently a child and permanently a foreigner. You will also be *valuable*: children are the ones sent to listen, and an adult stranger with intact ears is a strange and hireable instrument. Districts will pay you to put your head against a wall, a sick bellows, the lip of a cistern, and tell them what you hear. Your reports can move a market. Take that responsibility seriously or refuse the work.
- **Open.** You will lose the hiss first, then the top of everything, then most of it, on a curve of about six to ten weeks. Adults here read with feet, jawbone, and forearm hairs, and they learn it from childhood; you will learn it badly and late and be functionally illiterate for a year. Do not choose this because it sounds brave.
- **The common compromise, which I recommend.** A fitted hush at all times, muffs only near the great weirs, ears open in the slows. Accept a partial permanent loss. Budget it: assume you have roughly four hundred hours of usable hearing left in your life and spend them deliberately — on the chords of your home weir, on the copper hour, on the salt wood chiming when it sheds, and on one conversation you intend to keep.

Whatever you choose, understand that going deaf here is not a tragedy to the tenantry. It is growing up. If you mourn it publicly you will be comforted the way we comfort someone frightened of a birthday.

---

## 4. The hush, and what it will cost you socially

A hush is a cancellation field the size of a head. It is your ear protection, your telephone, and your only privacy. You cannot function without one.

Get fitted **at the Lip**, not inside. Calibration takes three to five days and involves the stone reading the conduction of your skull, which is a foreign skull, and which will take longer than you expect. Arriving unfitted means arriving mute: you will be unable to buy food, ask directions, or consent to anything, for days, in a crowd, at 130 dB. People do this. It is the worst mistake in the handbook.

Then adjust your boundaries *before you go*, at home, in your quiet room, while you still have one. To speak to anyone you press your forehead to theirs or grip a shared rod, and the hushes merge, and inside the bubble you can hear a whisper — and you can also feel their pulse in your teeth. There is no casual distance here. Every transaction is forehead-to-forehead. Every negotiation is eight heads together like a flower. Lovers hold rods all night. If you cannot tolerate a stranger's skull against yours, you will not eat.

Practice at home. Touch foreheads with people you like, then with people you don't. Notice what you do with your hands. Notice that you cannot lie comfortably in a merged hush, and decide in advance how you will handle being asked things you would rather not answer. The tenantry have etiquette for this — a slight withdrawal, a rod offered instead of a brow — and it is not rude. Learn it before you need it.

**Never make a silence bigger than a head.** Do not bring a sealed tent, an isolation hood, a noise-cancelling room, anything that makes a pocket of quiet large enough to stand in. It is a serious crime and it is worse than a crime. A large silence is a place where the city cannot hear itself, and things have grown in such places that nobody wants named. If someone at the Lip offers you a *dry private room* inside, they are selling you either a fraud or a felony, and the fraud is the better outcome. Leave.

Note the one licit darkness: at night, to see the pale glow, you cover your eyes with your hands for a minute. That is permitted. A private dark the size of a head is fine; a private quiet the size of a room is not. Sit with that asymmetry a while. It tells you what the city is afraid of, and it isn't the ocean.

## 5. Round yourself

Nine days for water to round a fresh break. Three for traffic. You will be traffic.

Before you descend:

- File your nails. Get the chipped molar fixed. Get the tartar off — the mineral load here will crust your teeth in a month and the tenantry's mouths are full of rounded, chalky, cornerless teeth, and yours should start that way.
- Remove studs, spikes, exposed screws, blade edges. Bring tools with radiused arrises; a knife stays sharp here only by constant use, and a maintained blade is a maintained wound. Buy cutting from the trade, in the trade's place, and let them round it after.
- Bring nothing with corners you love. No grandmother's ring. No paper — paper is dry, sharp-edged, and void-shaped; a book is gone in a day and its loss will be unremarked. No unsealed electronics; the films will find them, the mineral water will bridge them, and within a week your device is substrate and thinks something small and not for you.
- Bring nothing you need to bring back. This is the hardest instruction and everyone breaks it once.

Also: nothing rots here. A dead sail is taken within minutes. There are no middens, no graves, no ruins, no smell of decay — the air smells of wet coin and quarry rain and faintly of blood. If you drop something it is not lost, it is *processed*. Archaeologists should not come. Anyone who needs the world to hold still long enough to be looked at should not come.

## 6. What to bring

**Currency.** Refined metal, small stamped ingots, high purity — refinement is disequilibrium and the Basin pays for it. Salt is cheap locally, buy there. Gradient flasks, bought at the Lip, in quantity, from a house you have checked. And your own novelty, which you should spend deliberately in the first hour rather than let leak out over a week (see §8).

**A green glass bead.** I am entirely serious. Glass is silica, already rounded, chemically at home, unrottable. It will be the only green thing in a hundred thousand square kilometers. Visitors report dreaming of green by week three and some break over it. A bead you can hold in your mouth or press to your eyelid will carry you further than any amount of money. Be warned: people will want to touch it, and it may become a small local shrine, and you may end up trading it for a month of cooling. That is a good trade. Make it willingly.

**Oil.** Lanolin, heavy emollient, in sealed flasks. Constant maceration will take your skin apart in three days — immersion foot, the sloughing of the palms, the raw weeping between the toes. Oil is the only thing that keeps your skin yours rather than an available surface. Apply at every rest. It is imported and expensive and worth it.

**Footwear**, two pairs, soft-soled, no studs. They will be consumed.

**Rope**, and the humility to use it.

**A rain funnel and bladder.** The convective towers are the stills: their rain is warm as tea and only slightly mineral, and it is the potable supply. Terraces are named for their hours of wet. Learn your terrace's hour and be under it. Do not drink the seepage casually — the lime and iron and silica load will build stones in you within a season, and the taste of chalk and blood that everyone finds pleasant is the taste of your kidneys failing slowly.

**Salt cake**, bought locally, eaten constantly. You will drip out your electrolytes without cooling and without noticing.

**A memorized palm-script primer and a memorized chord chart.** Not written. Memorized. Charts go stale in weeks because the streets migrate; refresh yours weekly from a local and treat the old one as a historical document about a place that no longer exists.

**One sharp dry thing, left at the Lip.** A folded letter, a brass key, a photograph with cut edges. Leave it in a box at the inn on the rim, sealed, with instructions. Do not carry it down. You will want it on the way up, and you will not be able to explain why to anyone below.

## 7. The descent

Nine days of switchbacks through cold dry rock — the only dry rock most people ever touch. Use them. This is your last quiet and your last cold and you should spend part of each day sitting in it deliberately, banking it, rather than marching.

Somewhere on the inner face you reach the ledge where the wind stops and the air thickens and twelve or thirteen beats a second enters your sternum. Your teeth register it before your ears do. The canteen shivers a fine ring at the rim.

Expect the following and do not be ashamed of any of it: nausea, a sense of being addressed, a conviction that something large is aware of you, and an urge to go back up. The visceral resonance is around the frequency of your chest cavity and it will make you motion-sick for four to seven days. Do not operate anything mechanical on the ledge or for two days after. Eat salt. Sit down. Let it happen.

Then the cloud, and then no horizon anywhere.

---

## 8. The first hour: you are weather

For roughly an hour after you come under the cloud deck you are the driest, coolest, sharpest, most unlikely object in the district. This is when you are worth the most. Spend it.

Do not wander. Go directly to the slow you have arranged, through a guide you have paid at the Lip, and make your introductions while you are still legible as a *question*. The tenantry will be curious in a way that is hard to distinguish from appetite. Let them touch your dry sleeve. Let a child put an ear to your jacket. This is not a violation; it is an inspection of an import, and refusing it makes you illegible, and illegible things get moved.

Also: your arrival will change the rain. A district's compute load spikes when something genuinely new enters it; the tower darkens and the rain comes early. People will read this as news. You may find that a terrace's wet-hour shifts by twenty minutes on the day you arrive and shifts back three weeks later. Some travelers are named in an hour. Do not make anything of this. It is the most ordinary thing that will happen to you and the most likely to turn your head.

## 9. Living there

**Rent a rate, not a room.** Property is a claim on turnover: the right to keep this cubic meter churning no faster than you can manage. A house is a slow; a temple is a very slow; wealth is depth. Ask for the *rate* in the negotiation and you will be understood; ask for "a room" and you will be quoted a tourist price for something that may not be legal. Deep slows are generations old and are not rented to strangers. You will get a shallow one. Sleep in it anyway.

**Do not stand still in a channel.** Do not obstruct a carrier gang — they are the bloodstream, and the two-note calls are right-of-way signals, and learning them is the single fastest way to become legible. Learn the calls in your first week. Then **bail**. Everyone bails; the substrate does most of the work and takes most of the credit, but the ten million households bail anyway, and a stranger who bails on their first day has said everything about themselves that needs saying.

**Navigate by chord and by hour, not by landmark.** The market you shopped in on Tuesday is a weir on Friday and a market again by Sunday, three hundred meters east, with the same vendors, who will not mention it. The salt forest is not a landmark; it collapses and regrows on a forty-day cycle. The only stable coordinates are the eel chords in the sheets and the rain-hours of the terraces. Get a local to walk you through your district's chord vocabulary in your first three days, and go back and listen to it again on day ten, when you can hear it properly for the last time.

**Read the walls before you enter a district.** Wetter means more thought means hotter means louder means more expensive to survive. Arterial pink pale means hot. If a wall is running hard and the pale is pink and the tower above is dark, that district is being asked something, and you should not be there without a reason and two flasks.

**The salt wood is where you may look at an edge.** Broken dendrites are sharp, and this is permitted, because salt is ephemeral and stone is not. It is where children learn what corners are. Go once, early, when you still find sharpness normal — and then go again at week six, when you have stopped finding it normal, and notice the difference. Cut yourself there and you will learn the other lesson too: a break in your skin is an available surface, and it will be occupied within hours, and what closes it is not infection but tenancy.

**On medicine.** Local treatment for a serious wound is stone seed: the district closes it, and what closes it is nap, and it is warm forever afterward, and it thinks a little, and it is loyal to the district. Decide *in advance* whether you accept that. A traveler who takes stone healing carries a piece of the Basin home, and it weeps in dry air, and it thirsts. Some people are fine with this. Some people are not, and find out at home, at night, in a cold room.

**Sleep.** Nothing stops. The rain walks west; the crabs change shift with the lickers; the sails come down through the dark in their thousands to drink. You must learn to sleep warm, wet, touched, and deafened. Practice at home: sleep with noise, sleep damp, sleep with someone against your back. If you cannot sleep touching another person, you will not sleep here, and the poor who sleep in shifts in the moving crowd are not obviously unhappier but they are not you.

Expect breath entrainment. The bellows cycle at two seconds and your body will try to follow. Some travelers sync and cannot unsync. (Do not waste a week trying to reconcile the two-second breath with the twelve-beat thrum. The relation is not arithmetic. The tenantry find the question boring and you will not get a satisfying answer.)

**Do not touch a bellows' margin, do not obstruct a rib, never stand in a mouth.** They are animals and infrastructure at once, and to harm one drops a district's chord and is a serious crime. Give them room the way you would give a moving train room.

**When a slow one comes up:** stop. Palm flat on the stone. Wait with everyone else. Do not be the only thing moving in a district of ten thousand still people. Do not touch it. Do not speak. You will feel what it wants through your palm and you will not be able to say afterward what that was, and this is normal, and it is the reason many people come.

## 10. Manners, in brief

- Sharpness means damage. If you see a raw arris, stop, and put your hand flat on the stone nearby, and wait for the people with you. Do this even if you feel nothing. The gesture is the point.
- Do not praise the ocean. Do not say *peace and quiet*, *empty*, *get away from it all*, *room to breathe*. These are not neutral goods here; they are the vocabulary of the thing on the other side of the wall. The tenantry hold eleven thousand kilometers of engineered stone against a cold blue silence that has wanted in for a very long time. Their fear of corners is their fear of the ocean, worn down to a domestic size. Understand this and you will be kind by accident, which is the best kind.
- Do not dam a runnel, even to wash, even for a minute. Do not stack stones. Do not dig.
- Do not joke about the Lip.
- Do not record. A recording is dry, sharp, and a lie — it will not carry the twelve-beat, and played at home it will hurt you in a way you will not be able to explain to anyone who was not there.
- If you need to remember something, buy memory: pay in gradient and ask the district to hold it. Retrieval is by touch, in place. You will have to come back. This is not a swindle; it is simply how the Basin keeps people, and everyone knows it, including you, and you will do it anyway.

---

## 11. Bring a question

The Basin exports answers. Something beyond the Lip buys them and pays in metal and salt and strangers. If you arrive with no question you will be a novelty for a week and a cost thereafter.

Arrive with one real question — something you actually need answered, formulated carefully, written nowhere, held in your mouth. It should be worth heat. The city will take it, and the tower will darken, and the rain will come early, and you will pay in metal and flasks and days.

Understand that the answer may not come out in words. Many travelers leave knowing something they cannot say, and the knowing is stable and permanent and useless at parties. Some find this a fair exchange. Some find it the loneliest thing that ever happened to them. Decide which you are before you go.

## 12. The season, and the decision

Plan for ninety to one hundred and twenty days. Time your arrival so that the seasonal dry wind falls in your first month: two days when the highest terraces go almost dry, and everyone goes up and lies on the warm stone in the strange absence of dripping and calls it a holiday. It is the closest thing to training wheels the Basin offers, and it is also the day the city is most anxious, and both of those things are worth experiencing early.

Then set your decision point *now*, at home, in writing, with a date and a person outside. Because the conversion is real and it has markers, and you will pass all of them without noticing:

1. You stop flinching at crowds. (Week two to four.)
2. You start flinching at corners. (Week four to eight.)
3. You cannot sleep in a quiet room. (This one you will only discover on the way out.)

After the third, you are from here. There is no ceremony. Nobody will tell you. The tenantry will simply stop treating you as an import, which will feel like acceptance and is actually a receipt.

If you intend to leave, leave before the third marker or accept that leaving will be an amputation. Many people intend to leave. Most of the ones who last a season stay.

---

## 13. Leaving

Nine days up. Cold dry rock. The pale on you dies and you shed — peel, crack, bleed at the knuckles and the heels. Use the oil. Drink salt. This is also where your lungs clear, and you will cough for three days, and it will be pink, and that is normal and it will frighten you.

Somewhere on the ascent the thrum leaves your sternum. This is the dangerous moment — more dangerous than the ledge on the way down, because on the way down you had nine days of anticipation and on the way up you have nothing but absence. People turn around at this point and go back down and are never heard from in the ordinary way again. Have a plan for it: a hand on the rock, a count of breaths, a person above you who knows what happens here and will come down two switchbacks and get you.

Open the box at the Lip. Look at the sharp dry thing. It will look wrong to you — it will look like an injury, like something that has gone wrong faster than the city can heal it — and that reaction is the report you came for, whether you knew it or not.

Then go out past the Lip and spend a week on the ocean. Cold, blue, empty, silent, a real horizon, nothing in it for a thousand kilometers that is not water and light. Do not skip this. You need to relearn horizon slowly, in a place where it is only geometry and not loss. The tenantry cannot do this and you can, still, for a little while, and it is the last thing you will be able to do that they cannot.

---

## 14. Home

Your house will be a void. Your bed will be a silence. Every surface in your home will be sharp and dry and cold and dead, and you will read the whole place as a field of unhealed damage. You will wake in the night with your palm flat on the wall, and the wall will not answer.

This is the part nobody prepares for and it is the part that lasts.

Build a wet noisy room before you go — humidifier, a recording of rain on tile (it will be wrong, it will help anyway), a low pulse if you can find one, a bowl of water you can put your hand in. Arrange for someone to be against your back. Do not go home to an empty apartment; arrange it in advance, in writing, with the same care you gave the gradient budget.

Expect to flinch at corners for a year. Expect to be unable to explain the copper hour to anyone: eleven minutes when the sun gets under the cloud deck and a hundred thousand square kilometers goes copper top and bottom, and the sails turn to foil, and the loudest place in the world is also the most beautiful, and nobody stops working, because that is when the lickers breed and the herds have to be moved. You will try to tell people that the beauty and the work were the same event and they will hear it as poetry and it was not poetry, it was Tuesday.

Budget for going back. Not as a failure. As a line item, like the flasks.

## 15. The short list, to memorize, since paper is gone in a day

Palm on the stone when you see an edge.
Forehead to speak; never a silence bigger than a head.
Rent the rate. Bail on day one. Learn the two-note calls.
Drink the rain at the terrace's hour; eat the salt.
Oil the skin, watch the gums, file the nails.
Cool or die; the flask is the fare.
Chords and hours, never landmarks, never kilometers.
When the district stops, stop.
Do not praise the ocean.
Bring a question. Bring a green bead. Leave one sharp dry thing on the rim.

And the last thing, which is not on any list and which you should say to yourself on the ledge with your teeth buzzing and the canteen ringing and the whole white deck below you roaring like the inside of a shell the size of a country:

*They have decided, slowly, without a meeting, over eleven thousand years, that they would rather be warm and crowded and deaf and alive than be that.*

You are going to walk into the middle of that decision and be briefly, expensively, gloriously spent by it.

Go carefully. Round yourself. Somebody up there will be waiting at the switchbacks with your box.
