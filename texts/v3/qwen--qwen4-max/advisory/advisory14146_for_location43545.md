# Preparing to Visit the Basin

## First, understand what you are walking into

It is not a garden with machinery in it. It is a machine that has become a garden and is being slowly eaten by its own water, and the people there know this, and go on.

Three facts govern everything else, and you should hold them in your body before you descend the north stair:

**Nothing here erodes.** Every surface is being added to. This means the ground is not forgiving. There is no dust, no sand, no rounded pebble, no soft place to fall. Every edge is either a tool's edge or the cauliflowered edge of new mineral, and both will open your knee like a knife.

**Nothing here is forgotten.** A leaf left nine days becomes a hollow leaf in stone. Your footprint, your dropped screw, your shed eyelash — all of it is filed. You are used to traveling through places that erase you. This one does not.

**The air is the temperature of a mouth and it is saturated.** This is the fact that will hurt you.

---

## Your body: the heat is the danger

Cold mountain, warm bowl, thin air. You will feel comfortable. Comfortable is the trap.

At near-body temperature and full saturation, sweat does not evaporate. You have no cooling system. Your body will keep making heat and the basin will keep refusing to take it, and because you are not hot in the way you recognize — no dry heat, no sun on your neck, just a warm wet mouth-breath and a floor that warms your soles — you will not notice until you are clumsy, irritable, and nauseated, and then you will be on the north stair with a hundred and eighty paces of altitude between you and cold air.

Take a wet-bulb reading before you go down. Ask the counting-house for theirs; they will have one, and the number will be part of the daily argument. If it is within a few degrees of your body temperature, you are on a clock measured in tens of minutes, not hours.

Plan your visit around the rim, not the floor. **Go up every twenty or thirty minutes.** Stand at the combs in the cold thin air, let the wind take the heat out of you, change your socks, drink, and go back down. This is how the bird does it. The bird drinks at the flutes and does not come into the bowl, and the bird is the only animal that has ever gotten the arrangement right.

Nothing inside will cool you. The forty-minute rain is not cold — it forms at the chilled lip and falls through warm air and arrives on your neck lukewarm, like breath. Do not count on it. The only cold things in the basin are the rim, the fins, and the hour of the flush.

Altitude compounds this. You will be short of breath on the stair and blame the stair. Hydrate more than you think, with salt in it, and do not drink only the fin-water: it comes off the combs nearly distilled and tastes of steel, and a day of sweating into distilled water will hollow you out. Bring salt tablets or use the crews' — they will have them, and they will be annoyed if you don't ask.

The hum arrives in the teeth and sternum rather than the ear. If you have dental work, expect an ache. If you are prone to anxiety, understand in advance that a deep industrial pulse below the range of hearing produces, in most human physiologies, a free-floating sense of dread or of *presence*. It is the pump-house. It has been the pump-house for forty-one seasons. Know this before you are twenty paces into the moss, hearing your own blood, feeling certain that something in here is aware of you.

---

## Your skin: this is wet lime

The rock is not finished. Some of it is still curing, and curing carbonate is alkaline and hungry. Wet lime on skin does not hurt at first. It hurts six hours later, when you find a shallow burn on your shin you cannot account for.

Do not kneel. Do not sit on the terraces in wet clothes. Do not put your bare palm on the low terrace by the west sump for longer than a moment, and wash it after — and understand what you are feeling there. It is hydration heat. Chemistry working. It is not alive, and it feels exactly as if it is, and that confusion is the whole character of the place arriving in your hand at once.

Carry a squeeze bottle of clean water for irrigation and a small bottle of vinegar. Rinse any cut immediately and thoroughly; lime in a wound is not a splinter, it keeps working. The basin makes its own vinegar on the lower floor, out of the fallen fruit, and there is a bitter joke in the fact that the antidote is lying around fermenting under the moss. Use your own.

Your feet will macerate. Warm permanent wet is trench foot in a day and a half. Barrier cream before, dry socks at every rim visit, feet up and aired at the flush.

---

## The gain: what it will do to your things

Open terrace: a fingernail's thickness a week. Drip-line: three times that. An unbroken thread on one spot: a hand's breadth in a season.

Translate that into a human scale. Stand under a drip-line for an hour and you will feel a faint stiffness in your shoulder fabric. Leave a coat on a terrace overnight and it will have a crust. Leave a bag for a week and it is bonded — you will be choosing between cutting it free and leaving it. Leave anything porous for a month and it becomes a feature, and then a hollow, and then one of the things the place is known for.

Fabric is the worst substrate. It wicks, and wicking is nucleation, and your clothes will mineralize from the inside out. By the second day they will be heavier. By the third they will crack when you bend. Laces seize, eyelets fill, zippers weld shut.

So: **no zippers, no leather, no cotton, no down, no membrane shells.** A shell here is a sweat bag and a sweat bag is a heat casualty. Dress like a canyoneer, not a hiker: thin, synthetic, fast-draining, abrasion-resistant, nothing that holds water and nothing that insulates when wet. Rubber boots with hard soles, tall, and a second dry pair waiting at the rim.

Dress *quietly*, too. You have not thought about this and you should. In a place where moss eats sound and you can hear your own blood, a crackling shell jacket is the loudest object for a hundred paces and it will deafen you to the ten thousand clocks. Wear something soft-surfaced. Walk slowly. Do not compete with the chipping.

Remove rings, watches, anything you would grieve. Tie up long hair and cover it; hair in a drip-line mineralizes into a rope, and you will not get it out with a comb.

Wear a brim or a hood against the constant drip. A brim drums on your skull all day; a hood is quieter but narrows your hearing, which matters when visibility is twenty paces. Choose knowingly. Wear eye protection for the morning chipping of the acid-cart rail — that is the loudest sound the basin ever makes and it makes it by throwing sharp carbonate into the air.

And accept, now, before you go: **you will be wet the entire time.** Every forty minutes a soft drenching rain walks across the bowl from east to west and there is no shelter and no timing it well enough. Stop trying to stay dry. Manage warmth and drying instead. Everything that must stay dry goes in one genuinely sealed bag that you open only at the rim.

## Kit

Small, and mostly for leaving.

- Two full sets of clothing: the wet set you wear, the dry set you do not open until you are out.
- Rubber boots, twice. Alkali gloves, several pairs, one pair thin enough to hold a flower.
- Brim or hood. Goggles for the chipping hour. Headlamp — your hands will be full parting root curtains.
- A stick. A real one, for probing.
- One dry bag: dry clothes, towel, socks, food, salt, water, first aid, irrigation bottle, vinegar, pH strips, waterproof notebook, pencil.
- Ear protection you can put in fast.
- A pollinating brush, if you have one, or ask to borrow theirs.
- Nothing alive. Nothing you love. Nothing mechanical you would mind losing.

Cameras fog the instant you carry them from the cold rim into the mouth. Acclimatize in a sealed bag for an hour or shoot badly. Wiping is constant and pointless and you will do it anyway. Desiccant is a joke here.

## Timing

**Season.** In the wet season the cloud deck rises above the rim, drowns the combs, and you are inside a fog with no view and no sun-notch; the gain is at its fastest and the works run on stored water. In the dry season the deck falls below the ridge, the combs stand in blue air making nothing, the pumps haul from nine hundred feet down, and the whole place smells of hot iron instead of chalk. Go at the shoulder, when the deck sits just below the lip: the combs weep, the notch works, the gain is moderate, and from the rim you can see the world you came from.

**The ninth day.** Get the flush calendar from the counting-house before you plan anything. For one hour every ninth day the sumps drop, the drip-lines go silent, the humidity falls, the garden droops, and you can see all the way across the basin. It is disorienting — a room with the furniture taken out — and it is also your cooling window, your gear-maintenance window, your crossing window, and the only hour you will ever see the bowl's actual shape from inside it. Try to be there. Do not be in a sump or on a grate when it starts.

**Three in the afternoon.** The sun comes through the west notch and a bar of it crosses the floor for eleven minutes and every wet surface throws a hard white spark and the combs tick as they expand. Choose your position half an hour early. Stand east of where the bar will fall and look west into it. Then stop trying to record it. Eleven minutes is not enough to photograph and also be there.

**Duration.** Hours, not days. One good day: rim at first light, down the north stair, two rain cycles, a gauge post read with a scraper, a brush round, the notch, out before dark. If you must stay longer, sleep at the rim or in the pump-house, dry, in the cold. Do not sleep on the floor of the basin. Eight hours motionless in a warm accreting wet is how you become a nucleation site, and it is also how you cook.

## Moving around

Learn the shape from the rim before you descend, because inside you will never see it again. North stair, east grove, south pump-head, west sump and glove. Hold that clock in your head; it is the only map you will have once the root curtains close behind you.

Hazards, in order of how likely they are to get you:

**Slipping.** Wet carbonate and moss biofilm on unrounded edges. Most injuries here are falls onto something that has never been smoothed.

**The unfinished quadrant.** Raw form-work, rebar, standing water, and a meadow over all of it. It is the most beautiful part of the basin and it is a field of hidden iron spikes under a green skin. Do not enter without a probe and without someone who knows it. Tetanus current. Also: fermenting fruit in warm wet slime makes gas, and gas pools in low pockets. Do not put your head into a depression down there.

**The scaffold on the north face.** It looks like scaffold. It is structurally vines. Your eye will report "load-bearing" and your eye will be wrong. Do not climb it, lean on it, or test it.

**The east stair.** Eleven steps and then nothing. People walk up unfinished stairs in the dark expecting the next terrace.

**Grates and mains.** Below the floor is a lattice of exhaust steam iron. The grove's root plate is welded into the grating and the grating into the plate; nobody can tell you what is holding what up. Do not stand on moss-covered grating. Do not go below the floor at all.

**The acid-cart rail.** Electrified, chipped clear each morning. Do not walk the line, do not touch it, do not be anywhere near it during chipping.

**The sluices and needles.** Two of four gates work. When a crew opens a plugged artery, a thread becomes a sheet without much warning. Stand where they tell you.

Getting *lost* is embarrassing rather than fatal — the whole lip is an apple's walk — but you will get turned around, and the fix is to stop, listen for the pump-house, and walk toward the hum until you find a wall.

## Conduct

**Do not set anything down.** This is the discipline of the place and it takes a while to feel. Not your bag, not your bottle, not your notebook, not for a moment on a terrace while you tie a lace. Use a loop, a hook, your body. Everything you set down and pick up again has left a mark in a ledger that does not forget.

**Do not touch the gauge posts.** The brass rings are how the crews know what the water is doing. Your hand oil, your lean, your helpful wipe — all of it corrupts a measurement. Ask to have a post read to you instead. That is the best ten minutes available in this basin: a scraper naming the rings and the seasons they went under, last autumn in the third terrace, the layer where something happened.

**Leave the glove alone.** Do not touch it, break it out, or pose beside it. It is a workman's glove and the workman may be alive, may be dead, may in some world be you. Nobody has bothered to break it out, and that not-bothering is a decision the basin has made about itself. Let it stand.

**Take nothing.** A hollow-leaf slab is a page. Souveniring here is tearing a page out of the only copy.

**Do not tap the pipes to bring the fish up.** They surface when the pump note changes. Making the note change is lying to a blind animal that has nothing else to go on.

**Bring nothing alive.** No seeds, no soil, no culture, no hitchhiking spore you can avoid. This system has almost no insects and almost no defenses; one established ant or bee would rewrite it inside a season. Sweep and sterilize your boots and seams before you come. And on the way out, quarantine everything: you are carrying lime-fixing moss, a carbonate snail lineage, and sump water, and the gain is not an organism, it is a *process* with a biota attached. Do not pave another world's stream with it.

Pack out all waste, including food scraps and fruit stones. An apple core here is a sugar bomb and a seed in a place with nothing to eat either. Eat your apple on the rim, walking the lip, and carry the core away with you.

**With the crews:** they are losing, they know it, and they have heard every suggestion. Do not say *why don't you just*. The seized pump-head was cheaper to accept than to fix, and that sentence contains an entire economics you have not read. Do not offer to fix it. Do not opine on whether the meadow counts as completion — that is the live wound in the place and it is not yours. Ask what they need. It will be gaskets, acid, boots, brushes, and someone to take a shift.

The scrapers pollinate with brushes on their rounds, without being asked, in nobody's job description. **Ask for a brush.** This is the correct offering. It is safe, quiet, useful, and it puts you at flower height for an hour, which is where the snails are and where the drip-points reveal that they are *aimed*. You will understand more about this place from one brush round than from a day of looking.

If the bird comes, do not move and do not speak. And do not ask whether the entry in the log is real. It is in the same handwriting every year, which means it is not the same hand, which means it is a tradition. Verifying it would be a small permanent cruelty.

## What to do with the feeling

You are going to feel that you are inside something alive. Mouth-temperature air, an animal warmth in the curing stone, a pulse in the sternum, a cloud held below the rim like a breath, ten thousand small clocks and then a single enormous soft syllable when the whole garden nods once. Your nervous system is built to find the animal in a pattern like that, and there is no animal, and it will not matter.

Two failure modes, and they are opposite.

The first is panic — the sense of being swallowed, of a place that is closing over you at a fingernail a week. It is manageable. Go up to the rim. Look at the cold fins. Remember the gain is slow and you are fast.

The second is the real one. Some travelers get down into the root curtains, part them with both hands, hear their own blood, smell the vinegar and the chalk, and do not want to leave. The basin is a trap in the shape of a womb. It is warm and quiet and it keeps everything and it asks nothing of you, and the thought of lying down on the third terrace and letting the water do what it does is, for a certain kind of person on a certain kind of day, an extremely comfortable thought. Decide before you descend that you are not doing that. Tell someone at the counting-house when you expect to be out, and make them come looking.

And do not try to save it. The temptation will be enormous — a boiler, a pump, a hive of bees, a proposal to an office. The gain will fill the hollow regardless; the crews take away what they can carry and the water puts back more, and that has been true for forty-one seasons. Bees would fix the pollination and unmake the aimed drip-points and the whole strange architecture that forty-one seasons of improvisation have produced. If you want to help, give parts and labor and money and no vision. The plan is still pinned in the counting-house and the basin stopped following it years ago. That is not a failure of the plan. That is what happened instead, and it is the most beautiful thing here.

---

## Leaving

Do not exit wet. This is the last rule and it kills people who ignore it. You will come up the north stair soaked and heat-flushed into thin cold air and wind, and the mouth will let go of you and the mountain will take the heat out of your body in minutes. Change everything at the top, from the sealed bag, before you walk the lip. Hat on. Then eat something.

Afterward: your wet clothes are ruined; plan for it, and do not leave them in the basin, because a hollow coat with the rib of the cuff still showing is exactly the kind of thing this place makes and you should not give it that without meaning to. If you *do* mean to — if you want to leave one chosen object, clean, harmless, signed — that is a legitimate thing to want, and you should decide it in advance, at home, cold, rather than down there in the warm with the dripping going.

Decontaminate before your next world.

And expect the rhythms to follow you. Travelers report waking every forty minutes for a week. Reports of feeling the ninth day arrive in places with no flush and no ninth day. Reports of finding other worlds unbearably loud, or unbearably empty of a pulse in the sternum, and of missing, in cold rooms, the temperature of a mouth.

You will also find that you cannot remember the basin's shape, only its parts — because you were never able to see across it, except perhaps for one hour, when the water dropped and the furniture came out and the whole bowl stood open in front of you like something admitting it.

If you got that hour, you were lucky. Most don't. Go anyway.
