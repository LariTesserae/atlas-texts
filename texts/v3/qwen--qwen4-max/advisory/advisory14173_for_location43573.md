# Preparing for the Sill

## The thing to understand before you pack

Everything in that chamber is the same temperature. You are not. You will arrive at thirty-seven degrees into a country at forty-one, and for a little while you will be the coolest object in the world, and then you will stop being it. That is the whole shape of a visit. You are the only gradient, the only disequilibrium, the only thing in the Sill that is *going* somewhere. Every other law of the place follows from your being an exception to it.

Prepare, therefore, as a person preparing to be a temporary anomaly: briefly, carefully, with a plan for stopping.

---

## 1. The heat is not weather. It is a budget.

Do not think of forty-one degrees as a hot day. A hot day has a shaded side of the street, a cool stone floor, a cellar, an evening. The Sill has none of these and will never have them. There is no radiant escape either — the pane is conducting a desert noon into a room where every surface is also forty-one degrees, so there is nowhere to point your body that is cooler than your body.

Run the numbers before you go, and run them on **wet-bulb**, not dry. From the description — humidity poised exactly where sweat still works and only just — you should expect a wet-bulb somewhere around 32–34 °C. That is above the experimentally demonstrated limit at which a young, healthy, resting human can hold a stable core temperature indefinitely. Read that again: *resting*. Not walking. Not climbing a bud hill. Sitting still in the wet with a fan.

What this means practically:

- **The Sill is not habitable for you. It is visitable.** Plan days, not seasons. The tenders survive because they are thin, pale, slow, and doing almost nothing; their metabolic rate is the entry fee. Yours is too high. You will pay the difference out of your water and your alertness.
- **Acclimatize before arrival**, ten to fourteen days of controlled heat exposure. Plasma volume expansion and salt-sparing sweat are worth more than any object you can carry. Arrive lean, aerobic, sober, well-slept, and on no medication that blunts sweating (antihistamines, anticholinergics, many psychiatric drugs) or pulls water (diuretics).
- **Bring a fan.** Still air is the hidden killer: with no wind, your own boundary layer of saturated air sits on your skin and evaporation stalls. A folding fan or a broad leaf-fan, worked constantly, is probably the single most valuable thing you will carry. Caveat: when air is hotter than skin, moving air also *adds* heat convectively. Fan because your instrument says it is working, not because it feels nice.
- **Bring a pressurized spray bottle.** Misting your wrap and skin uses a fraction of the water that dunking does, and dunking accomplishes nothing anyway — the pools are body-warm and cannot take heat from you. Only evaporation cools. Water is not a coolant here; it is *fuel for the only cooling process that exists.*
- **Wear white, loose, wet, and total.** Full coverage, open weave, constantly re-wetted. Ask for local leaf-cloth; it is the correct material and it is the correct courtesy. Avoid greasy occlusive sunblocks over large areas — they seal your sweat glands and cook you.
- **Do not lie down on the cambium to rest.** It will not draw heat out of your back; it will push heat in, and it will take away most of your evaporating surface. Rest seated or slung, spread-eagled, wet, fanned, in a leaf-cloth hammock if you can rig one.
- **Move at the tenders' pace.** Walking generates three to four times the heat of sitting. In a marginal wet-bulb, walking is the dangerous activity and standing still is the safe one. This is why distance is measured in drinks. A drink is not a rest stop; it is a thermoregulatory event, the point at which you must stop being an animal that goes places and become a wet surface for a while. Budget two to three times the travel time your instincts suggest.

**And know this: heat stroke in the Sill is untreatable.** The gold standard is cold-water immersion. There is no cold water. There is no cold. There is no ice, no cellar, no shade, no night, no gradient of any kind. You cannot make anything cooler than forty-one degrees by any means available in that country. The only cold that can exist in the Sill is cold you bring: pack several endothermic instant cold packs, and understand that they are your entire intensive care unit and that they last twenty minutes each.

So the rule is absolute: **abort early, on signs, not on symptoms.** Pre-agree with your companion that they may end your visit over your objection. Hyperthermia removes the faculty you would need to decide. Write the abort criteria down before you go and give a copy to the person who will enforce them:

> resting heart rate that will not come down after a full drink; headache; nausea; gooseflesh or chills in forty-one-degree air; sweating that stops; irritability; any sentence beginning *"let me just finish this."*

That last one is the most reliable early sign of heat illness and also, in the Sill, the most dangerous sentence in the language.

## 2. The water will purge you

The ceiling is gypsum. Selenite, satin spar — calcium sulfate. The seeps run through mineral-laying tissue and the tanks taste of cucumber and chalk. Assume the water is close to saturated with calcium sulfate, on the order of two grams per litre. Sulfate at that concentration is a laxative. You will need eight to fifteen litres a day. Do the multiplication.

Osmotic diarrhoea, in a place where dehydration is the only way to die quickly, is a catastrophe disguised as an inconvenience.

- Carry a **hand-pumped reverse-osmosis unit** or equivalent desalinator. You cannot boil water: there is no fire and never will be. Filtration alone will not remove dissolved sulfate. RO is the only realistic option and it is a hand-powered, fire-free technology, which makes it one of the very few imports that fits the country's constraints.
- Carry **salt and rehydration mix**. Plant tissue is potassium-rich and sodium-poor; you will be sweating litres. Hyponatraemia is the second water hazard and it looks like heat illness and is treated oppositely.
- Note the ugly little fact that oral rehydration salts contain glucose. Your medicine is the sweetest substance that has ever existed in the Sill. More on that below.
- Test the water at more than one tank. The axil-tanks, the seeps, and the Bead Run pools may differ. Ask a tender which is for drinking, by pointing, and accept the answer without improving on it.

---

## 3. Light: your eyes are on a clock

Tenders go blind from the light. That is not atmosphere; that is a dose-response warning. Assume cumulative, irreversible photic injury and meter your exposure.

- Serious grey/neutral-density eyewear, wraparound, with a spare. Polarized, because every surface in that country is beaded with water and the specular glare off a hundred million beads will veil everything.
- A brim helps less than you expect. Light arrives from the entire pane at once and *goes around*; you cannot cup a hand into a shade for your eyes. Your body casts no shadow. Understand that in advance or it will frighten you.
- **Budget dark.** The only darkness in the Sill is inside the buds. Plan a dark period every few hours: eyes closed, wet leaf across them, in a bud if you have permission, in a wrap-tent if you do not. Keep a rough lux-hour ledger. Decide your total visit length by your eyes, not your curiosity.
- Do not stare at the Standing Noon because you are told you can look at it. It is a fixed smear of glare, not a sun's disk, and the tenders' retinas are not yours. It is also **your only compass**: it has never moved and it bears northeast. Use it constantly. It is the one reliable orientation cue in a country with no shadows, no trails, and no sun-arc.
- **Do not climb to the pane.** Ninety metres of slick sweating horn-wood, into the ceiling-lining where the light has not yet been combed, in a chamber whose radiant load you cannot estimate. There is a reason nobody has been up to check.

And a related, easy-to-miss consequence: **without shadows you cannot see relief.** A thumbprint a millimetre deep in milkstone is invisible in flat omnidirectional light. Green against green with no shading is a contrast nightmare; you will not see the edge of the leaf-shelf you are about to step off. Carry a long inert probe and use it like a blind man's cane. Learn to read the country with your hands. The marks are found by touch. Everyone's are.

## 4. Your skin, and the milk

The place is fig-shaped in every particular: pillar-figs, cambium the colour of a fig's inside, *milk*stone. Assume the white sap is fig latex. Fig latex carries ficin, a protein-digesting enzyme, and furocoumarins, which are phototoxic. Under the brightest light anyone has ever stood in, sap on skin means blistering phytophotodermatitis — the kind of burn that arrives hours later, in the shape of a splash or a handprint, and stays for months.

- Gloves. Always. Long sleeves. Wash sap off *immediately* with your carried water, not with tank water.
- Sap in the eye is an emergency. Goggles stay on.
- If you have any latex or fig allergy, do not go.
- Cover every cut. Two reasons. First, warm permanent damp macerates skin and your own flora will find the openings; expect immersion-foot problems and plan dry-ish intervals for your feet, sitting fanned with the wraps off. Second, and more strangely: **this country reproduces by accidental contact.** Fragments touch and take. You are warm, wet, nutrient-rich, and occasionally broken open. Treat any open wound as a potential graft site. It is a low-probability hazard and an unrecoverable one.
- Nothing is rounded. There is no sand, no scree, no worn edge anywhere; every rock face is as sharp as the day it broke. Falls are the second killer. Test the handholds on the bud hills — someone put them there, shallowly, with a thumb, a very long time ago.

## 5. You are carrying the four things this world does not have

This is the part travelers get wrong, and it is the part that matters most.

The Sill has no death, no decay, no dust, no fire, and no sweetness. You are a walking container of all five.

**Rot.** Nothing here decomposes. Tissue is reabsorbed without smell, without flies. Your gut flora, your skin flora, your breath, your food scraps, your dandruff — you are importing the entire category of decay into a system that has never had to defend against it. Assume you are a biological hazard and behave like one.

**Dust.** The air has never carried a particle. A human sheds grams of skin a day and a constant drift of hair and fibre. *You will make the first dust.* Mitigation: stay wet (wet skin does not aerosolize), full smooth coverage, cap your hair, do not scratch, do not comb, wet-wipe and collect rather than brush off.

**Fire.** There is no fuel in the Sill. There is fuel in your pack: dry cloth, paper, packaging, your rations, your hair. Also consider the atmosphere — a sealed chamber packed with photosynthesizing tissue and no decay to return carbon may be oxygen-rich and carbon-dioxide-poor. Test the air on arrival with a meter. If oxygen is elevated, your imported dry goods are not merely combustible, they are *eager*, and a shorted battery or a static spark could introduce fire to a world whose entire architecture is premised on its impossibility. No lighter, no stove, no ferro rod, no loose lithium cells. Antistatic discipline.

**Sweetness.** No flower has opened; no fruit has ripened; sugar is an event that has not occurred. Your ration bars, your rehydration glucose, the residue on your fingers — these are not snacks, they are an ontological import. Eat in one designated place, on bare rock, sealed and contained. Collect every crumb. Do not eat where a bead-snail can reach you; those snails are the accidental gardeners of the entire country and they carry whatever they touch from trunk to trunk.

**And your corpse.** If you die there, you will rot, because you brought rot with you, and you will be the first decay event in the history of the world. Plan for retrieval. It is grim and it is the correct line item.

Practical shape of all this: **run a closed loop.** Base camp on the raw fractured rock at the chamber's edge — the only dead, sterile, non-grafting ground in the Sill, and therefore the only place you can be a body without consequence. Keep it tiny; it will look like a wound. One designated toilet site on that same bare rock, agreed with the tenders, and understand that with no rain and no streams your residue will simply sit there and dry and become dust. Pack out solids. Pack out packaging. Pack out everything.

## 6. Water etiquette, and the tenders

There are perhaps eleven people in the whole country and they pass water **mouth to mouth** into tanks that have no other supply. That is the water economy. It is also a direct oral-flora pathway into every reservoir in the Sill.

- **Decline mouth-to-mouth passing.** Warmly, unmistakably, without disgust. Accept hand to hand, or in a leaf cup, or pour your own.
- **Never enter a tank.** Do not wash in one, do not wet your wrap in one, do not put your face or hands into one. Your salt, your skin, your sap-washed hands do not belong in the drinking supply. Use seeps, use your carried vessel, ask.
- Do not drink from a tank offered to you communally until you know it is yours alone.
- Never travel alone if you can help it. You will almost never see another person; two drinks is a long way in a country where you must keep stopping.

**Use the acoustics deliberately.** The pane channels sound: a whisper beside you is lost, a knock two drinks away arrives inside your skull. Agree a knock-code with your companion — a rhythm, never a single knock. **A single knuckle-on-door knock is a bead falling.** There are three in a lifetime. Children are named after them. To fake one would be to fabricate a sacred event in front of the whole population, all of whom hear it, all of whom know exactly what it means. Three quick, pause, three. Anything but one.

And accept, in advance, that you have no acoustic privacy. Anything you say upward is delivered, clean and intimate, into a stranger's head somewhere you have never walked.

**Gifts.** Nothing sweet, nothing dead, nothing dry, nothing that seeds, nothing that rots. A fan is a good gift. A knot, a weave, a song, a technique are good gifts. Labour is the best gift: wet the grafts, carry water, press a wall a little further and stop. Learn the gesture that means *keep it here* before you arrive, and mean it.

Do not ask how long. Do not ask what happened to the people who left the doorway with no house. Do not pity the unfinished stair; the forty-first step is a sapling with its leaves out and it is not behind schedule. Do not offer to fix the blindness without thinking about it for a long time first.

## 7. Import a night, and guard your mind

There is no dark, no day, no dawn, no schedule, nothing to count against except a bird saying one pure note every hundred breaths. Constant bright light suppresses melatonin; your circadian rhythm will free-run and drift; you will not sleep well in forty-one-degree damp; sleep debt will accumulate silently and heat illness will arrive wearing sleep debt's clothes.

- Bring a wind-up clock or an hourglass. **Manufacture a day**: numbered log entries, fixed sleep block, fixed meal blocks, a small ritual at each boundary. This is not fussiness. In a place with no time, an unanchored mind will do strange things — uniform bright field, padded hush, disembodied intimate voices, no shadow, no night: these are textbook conditions for perceptual distortion and dissociation.
- Expect to feel *populated while alone*. You will hear people working whom you cannot see, from districts you have never walked to. Some travelers find this comforting. Some find it unbearable. Find out which you are before you commit to nine drinks.
- Sleep in a bud, with permission, through the seam, wet leaf across the eyes. It is the tightest and most private space in the world and it is the inside of a held pressure. Claustrophobics: test yourself somewhere equivalent first. Do not damage the seam. It is the only dark there is and it is not yours.
- **Name the compulsion before you feel it.** You will want to finish something. Open a seam. Complete the stair. Set the last block in the wall. Say the end of the sentence. You have hands and tools and you *could*. That urge is not insight and it is not kindness; it is your own biology, which is made of afterward, reacting to a place that has no afterward in it. The buds are not waiting. Waiting is a thing you do when time is going somewhere. Decide now, on paper, that you will open nothing, complete nothing, plant nothing, and sweeten nothing.

---

## 8. Getting around

- Navigate by the Standing Noon (fixed, northeast), by drink-counts, and by the **Bead Run**. A river-shaped chain of leaf-base pools that has never flowed is, functionally, the road: it is the route with water on it. Travel the Bead Run.
- Your footprints close behind you over a hundred paces. There are no trails and there never will be. There is no way to blaze a route that does not either heal or last forever. Do not solve this by littering, and think hard before you solve it by pressing waymarks into a country where every mark is permanent.
- The Colonnade is a grid of gaps between near-touching pillars: self-similar, shadowless, and easy to be lost in. Count pillars. Count drinks. Turn back at half your water, not at two-thirds.
- Do not go to the far wall on your first visit. Nine drinks each way is not an outing; it is an expedition, and only a handful of people in the history of the world have walked all of it.

## 9. What it will actually be like

The first hour is pain — a white roar, eyes streaming, a body insisting it is being cooked. Then it stops hurting and becomes a *medium*. You will feel lit from inside, suspended in something like illuminated water, inside an eye. There is no hiding. There is no visual privacy anywhere in the country. Being unable to cast a shadow does something to people; some feel transparent, some feel forgiven, some feel already dead. Sit through it. It passes into a strange, total, unshadowed clarity that you will miss later.

You will never be dry again. Not your clothes, not your hair, not your eyelids. Wetness will stop being an event and become your condition, and thirst will become the whole architecture of your attention, and one day you will catch yourself thinking *the Nursery is two drinks* and understand why nobody there counts days.

Your footsteps will make no sound. Someone's knuckle will crack inside your head. The bird will say its note and you will notice you have started counting, and then you will notice that counting is the only future tense available.

And you will find a thumbprint by touch — you cannot see them — and put your thumb into it and feel that the hand was smaller than yours, and there will be no way at all to tell whether it was pressed ten thousand years ago or while you were sleeping. Abandonment here is indistinguishable from interruption. Nothing has slumped. Nothing has drifted. The builder stepped out to drink.

---

## 10. Leaving

Leave before you want to. Go out through the bare rock, decontaminate, inventory, carry everything out including your waste and your dead skin and the wrappers of your cold packs.

On the way out you will press your hand into a milkstone wall, because everyone does. Do it deliberately: clean unbroken hand, a place the tenders indicate, one press, no deeper than your thumb goes. It will keep the whorls of your palm at exactly that depth forever, in a country where nothing wears away.

Then you will step into a world with shadows and the first hard black hand-shape under your hand will shock you. Dryness will feel like a violation. Cold will feel like a gift you have not earned. The smell of a compost heap will be obscene. Sugar will be almost unbearable for a while — an event, loud and finished, in your mouth. Night will arrive and the relief of it will be so violent it comes out as grief.

And for years you will find your thumb looking for a wall.
