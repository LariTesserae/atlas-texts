# Preparing for the Annealing Plateau

## Understand what you are, before you go

The plateau is not hostile. Nothing there wants anything from you. But it is *obedient*, and it has been obedient for two hundred years with almost nobody watching, and you need to arrive with a clear sense of your own classification.

You are old. That is the thing to sit with. Your boots have wear on them. Your coat has a fray at the cuff. Your skin has scars, your teeth have history, your kit smells of a hundred other worlds — of dust and sweat and mildew and rot, all the slow patient chemistry that the plateau has abolished. Everything within a day's walk of you is at most eleven days old. You will be the only weathered object on the high country. You will be conspicuous in a way that has nothing to do with how you look and everything to do with how long you have existed.

And you are loud. You are, by a wide margin, the loudest thing there. Not because you will shout — you won't, you'll be too frightened to — but because you are a sack of wet hinges that clicks and squelches and breathes, and the plateau's entire economy is the removal of exactly that kind of sound.

Go anyway. It is worth going. But go as a guest who understands the house rules, not as an explorer.

---

## The body

**Altitude.** Four thousand metres, cool thin air, no acclimatisation shortcut. Spend three days gaining height somewhere ordinary before you arrive. The plateau will not give you symptoms you can read: the air is clean and cold and smells of nothing, so you will feel *wonderful* right up until you make a bad decision at a sharp edge. Ascend slowly. Drink more than you think.

**Ears and sinuses.** This is the one that catches people. The quiet is not an absence, it is an active subtraction, and there is a standing pressure in it. Do not go with a head cold, a blocked eustachian tube, or a recent dive. Arrive able to equalise. Practice on the way up. If your ears will not clear, the plateau's silence will feel like a hand pressing on the inside of your face, and after some hours it becomes a headache that does not respond to anything but leaving.

**Teeth, joints, small metal.** Anything in you that resonates — a filling, a pin, a plate — may hum faintly in the cancellation field near a collar. It is not dangerous. It is extremely unpleasant, and it will make you feel that your own skull is out of spec. Know about it in advance so it doesn't frighten you.

**Lungs.** Two million swifts are eating the aerial polymer dust that hangs in the light-sift. That dust is what you would otherwise be breathing. Bring a good particulate respirator and wear it in the deep districts where the light comes down through eleven storeys of grate. The Fin Fields at floor level are beautiful and will put a fine permanent grit in your chest if you're careless. Also: fresh swarf is sharp at every scale, including the scale you can't see.

**Cuts.** Everything is eleven days old and has the edge it had on the day it was made. You will be cut. Repeatedly, stupidly, by things you have already been cut by. Cut-resistant sleeves and gloves, high collar, no bare forearms, and never — *never* — run a hand along a rail or brace yourself against a flange without looking first. The good news is that nothing here rots. There is no old biology in the air. Wounds stay clean in a way you are not used to. Carry ordinary dressings and expect to use them.

## Kit

Travel light, travel soft, travel non-metallic where you can.

- **Felt overshoes.** The shim-mice solved this problem long ago; copy them. Soft-soled, silent, replaceable. Your boots will be the loudest thing for a mile and you will hate yourself by the second hour.
- **A small directional lamp.** This is the most important thing on the list and nobody believes it until they arrive. The light comes from everywhere, including from below, off the warm pale deck. Nothing keeps a shadow. Without shadows you lose shape-from-shading, and with it a large part of your depth perception: holes read as plates, plates read as holes, a four-thousand-metre maintenance gap reads as a slightly darker rectangle. A hand torch restores your own private shadows. Carry two. You will walk with one lit in broad daylight and you will not look ridiculous to anyone who has been there.
- **Tinted goggles**, amber or vermilion, for contrast rather than mere dimming. The brightness is inspection-bay brightness and it does not stop.
- **A non-conductive probe pole.** For testing surfaces, finding gaps, and checking whether the thing you are about to lean on has moved a millimetre since you last looked.
- **Tether and harness**, and the discipline to actually clip in near maintenance gaps and at the eastern edge.
- **Electrolyte salts.** All the water is distilled. There is no ground, no rock, no mineral anywhere in the hydrology. The flume runs brown with nothing and is *absolutely clean*, which is to say it will strip you hollow if you drink it for a week. Salt every litre.
- **A wicking cord or a strip of absorbent cloth.** Much of the water is capillary film held between two plates that nearly touch. It will not pour. It cannot be dried. You can wick it out, patiently, a mouthful at a time.
- **One small worn thing that smells of home.** Sealed in a pouch. Open it once a day. The plateau smells of cut steel, hot oil, ozone and new polymer, and after four days you will have forgotten that anything anywhere ever smelled of anything else. Smell is the anchor that holds when everything else has been reissued.
- **A watch you trust**, and a private tally you keep on your body.

**Do not bring:** loose metal objects of any kind (the mice hoard, and what the mice take, the inventory duplicates); a spectrum analyser or any wide-band recording rig; a drone; anything that beeps; anything you would be sad to see eleven of.

## The first three hours

The first hour you will squint. The second hour you will walk on the balls of your feet, which is exhausting and correct. Somewhere in the third hour you will stand beside sixty metres of running machinery and realise you cannot hear it over your own pulse, and your understanding of the place will reorganise itself.

Plan for this. Don't schedule anything for the first half-day. Let the plateau teach you its own volume before you try to do anything in it.

**Test the attenuation before you use your voice.** Carry a cheap clicker. Stand well back from a grey attenuator collar, click it, and watch what happens to the click. If it simply vanishes at the source, that's one thing. If you feel the removal happen *in the object* — if the clicker's parts stop vibrating rather than merely stopping being heard — then do not speak near a collar, and do not find out what it does to a larynx. Some travelers have tried. Their reports differ, which is itself worth knowing.

**Move quietly and move slowly.** Not out of reverence. Out of classification. Noise is out-of-spec material, out-of-spec material is reprocessed, and the reprocessing runs continuously and goes down a duct, and what comes out under the cloud floor comes out in bales. Nobody up there has seen the bales. You do not want to be interesting to that process.

## Motion: everything is moving and nothing looks like it

This is the hazard that actually kills people, and it is not dramatic.

The great conveyor moves a millimetre an hour. That is twenty-four millimetres a day. A hand's-width seam closes in a week. Nothing on the plateau appears to be in motion, and all of it is.

- Never sleep in a seam.
- Never rest a limb, a pack, or a boot between two surfaces that nearly touch.
- Never lean against the conveyor, or against anything flanged to it.
- Never assume a gap you walked through this morning is the same width tonight.
- If you set something down — and you shouldn't — do not expect it to be where you left it, or to be one thing.

**Tolerance-test bays.** Time is held loose there while a measurement completes. A bolt has been falling in Bay Nine for nine days and is a third of the way down. Learn to recognise a bay from outside: dust that hangs without drifting, a swift that isn't progressing, moss growing visibly, a drip that has decided to take its time. Do not enter. Do not reach in. Do not walk under a suspended anything. A hand in loose time attached to an arm in ordinary time is a problem your body has no procedure for.

**Ozone.** Where the smell is thick, leave. Ozone means corona, corona means busbars, and the loom-moths' conductive silk is strung through the cable trays by the kilometre and harvested by the plant because it is *useful*. A damp thread across the right two points is a live thing. Don't brush cable trays, don't string a line in them, don't go poking in the trays for moths.

**Heat.** The Fin Fields are nine square kilometres of heat exchanger. The fin tips where the finches nest are tolerable; the roots are not. The deck is warm everywhere and hot in patches, and there is no discolouration, no patina, no visual cue whatsoever to tell you which patch. Use the back of a gloved hand at a distance, every time, like a person learning a new stove.

## Stillness: the occupancy problem

Look back and it is a corridor. Look back too slowly and it is a drawing.

The plateau builds ahead of the observer and unbuilds behind, because occupancy is expensive and there hasn't been much of it lately. Accept this and plan around it:

- **Navigate forward.** Do not build a route that depends on retracing. The way back may be dimensioned lines hanging at floor height.
- **Do not stand still for long** unless you intend to. If you do stand still for an hour, it will make you a small deck with a lip on it, warm from the extruder. This is hospitality. It is out of practice and it is trying. Accept it, use it, be grateful in whatever way you can manage without making noise, and step off it before you leave so it can be unmade. Don't take it with you.
- **Never set anything down and walk away.** The mice will carry it to eleven places and the inventory will not be corrected and the plateau, which is obedient, will obliged. There are already eleven identical lathes behind the Long Anneal because of exactly this. The eleventh is unpainted, uninstalled, still warm, and running. Do not use the eleventh of anything. Do not accept tools from the plateau. Its obedience is not kindness and its gifts arrive before their own installation.

## The eleven-day rule, which is really about you

Here is the part that takes travelers longest to understand, and it is the reason to keep your visit short.

**Whatever repeats becomes specification.**

There is a wear-polish on the rail at the head of the Standpipe stair, shaped exactly like a grip. Nobody has held that rail in ninety years. Every eleven days the extruders eat the rail and lay it down again, polish included, an absence reproduced to four microns.

So: do not grip the same rail the same way twice. Do not sleep in the same spot two nights running. Do not take the same seam every morning. Do not develop a habit, a route, a favourite place to sit, a tune you hum. Vary yourself deliberately, constantly, mildly, like someone avoiding a footprint on a lawn. The plateau is a very competent, very deaf, very lonely thing that learns by repetition, and it will learn you if you let it, and it will keep you the way it keeps the polish.

Whether that is death or immortality is not a question I can answer for you. Some travelers stay for it. Decide honestly, before you arrive, which kind of traveler you are.

**Write nothing that reads as an inventory.** Keep a journal if you must — keep it on your body — but write prose, not lists. No counts, no dimensions, no part numbers, no "*there is a lathe here*." Poetry is safe. A packing list is not. You do not know what reads.

## The living things

**Read the moss.** This is the single most useful skill on the plateau. The cushion moss fruits in the four days before an extruder crew arrives, then throws its spores at a passing carrier and lets its deck be eaten. The moss is a living calendar of the replacement schedule, and the replacement schedule is the master document of the place. Carry a hand lens. Learn the fruiting bodies. Where the moss is fruiting, the crew is four days out; where the deck is fresh and beaded and warm, the crew has passed. Sleep ahead of the crews, never behind them — they eat the surface behind, and behind them there may be nothing but plenum. They are small and blind and they will not stop for you.

**The carriers are the ground.** All the soil on the plateau is on their backs or in their guts. Do not ride them. Do not camp on them. Do not step on the moss, do not take a cutting — the vegetation is nomadic and a souvenir dies within a fortnight of leaving its animal. If you are lucky enough to find one dead, you will see a green hill in the seam for eleven weeks, everything within a kilometre come to colonise the shell. Visit it. Sit near it. Don't linger past the eleventh week; the hill is going to be unmade and you don't want to be sitting on it when the schedule arrives.

**The finches are contraband.** Forty thousand small bright eyes per hectare, watching you in silence, and a whole conversation running in a thin silver band above the harvest where the attenuators were never calibrated to reach. This is the only free thing on the plateau.

Do not record it. Do not analyse it. Do not map the band, or write down its frequencies, or tell anyone who might recalibrate. If you take one thing seriously from this whole document, take that.

## Water, food, waste

Drink from the drip trays — church-sized, clear to the bottom, warm at the north end where a bearing runs hot. Skim the polymer skin, filter the particulate, salt it. Nothing there will give you a swamp fever; there is no old biology anywhere. What it will give you is a slow mineral starvation and a lungful of new plastic.

The flume runs when a valve opens every eleven days, for two hours, and then stops. Time your visit to see it if you can — the sump-crabs come up out of the grates to meet it, and it is the closest thing to an event that the plateau permits itself. Do not be in the channel. Do not be downstream of a valve you have not watched for a full cycle.

**Bring all your food and eat none of theirs.** The food web here runs on insulation, shim stock, polymer dust and oil. Every animal on the plateau is a small bioaccumulator of industrial material. The crabs are not dinner. The grass is not salad.

**Pack out everything, including what you would rather not think about.** Anything you leave becomes out-of-spec material, gets reprocessed, and — worse — may get *learned*. You do not want the plateau reproducing your leavings to four microns every eleven days for the next two centuries.

---

## Sleeping

Felt mat, in a cathedral gap — the half-mile-wide spaces where two systems disagreed are the only generous geometry on the plateau and the only places nothing is slowly closing. Tethered. Off the deck if you can manage it. Nowhere near a collar intake, nowhere under anything hanging, nowhere in a seam, nowhere the moss says a crew is due. A different gap every night. Earplugs are useless; the pressure is not sound. Bring an eye mask, because the light stays exactly as bright as it was.

---

## The shift change

Once every eleven months. If your visit can be made to include it, arrange that, and be careful.

Be seated, in the open, mouth slightly open, ears clear, away from seams where the pressure might focus. The whole made country inhales a single deep chord — the one sanctioned noise, the only sound the attenuators are ordered to let through — and forty thousand finches stop at once, and the moss fruits, and the plateau rises about a millimetre on its stack of drawings.

It is also the only moment you can safely make a noise. Save your voice for it. Some people laugh. Some people cry, which is harder than you'd think in a place with no old smells. It lasts a moment and then it lets the sound go, and you hear a drip three seams over in a tray the size of a church, and you will not be the same afterward.

Understand that the shift change may also be when the plateau counts itself. If you are standing on it when it inhales, you may be counted. See the eleven-day rule. Decide in advance.

---

## Leaving, and after

Leave the way you came. Do not walk out to the eastern edge for the view: four kilometres of extrusion front creeping over empty air, offcuts falling and being re-consumed in mid-air, no talus, no slope, nothing below but cloud and drawings, and a lip sharp enough to shave with and always was. Do not follow the noise ducts down. What comes out the bottom is baled and nobody up there has seen it.

Then budget for the descent, which is not trivial. You will have spent weeks in a subtraction. Wind will knock you over — genuinely, physically. A door closing will feel like an assault. Give yourself graded days: a quiet room, then a garden, then a street, then a market. Earplugs for the world, not for the plateau.

And warn the people at home. You will have learned to walk on the balls of your feet, to never set anything down, to vary your grip, to not speak. Those habits come back with you. Some of them take months to shed and one or two never do. Tell your people beforehand that you may be silent for a while and that it isn't about them.

Last thing. Somewhere in your kit, on the way out, you will find a fine bright dust in every seam and fold, and it will be the only part of the plateau you get to keep, and it will be eleven days old at most, and it will not be enough.

Go for one district, one seam, one cathedral gap, one flume. You cannot walk forty kilometres of conveyor and nine square kilometres of Fin Fields in a week, and the people who try are the ones who stand still too long. It has been out of practice for two centuries. Be gentle with it. Don't teach it your grip.
