# Preparing for the Pale Ground

## First, correct your instincts about danger

You are probably calibrated for worlds that push back — surf, cold, altitude, predators, things that fall on you. Almost none of that applies here. Nothing on this plain will hunt you, and nothing will happen quickly. There is no flash flood; the flood takes a month to cross a paddock. There is no fire regime. There is no avalanche, no rapid, no cliff worth the name.

What will hurt you is **distance multiplied by clay**, plus **glare**, plus **your own contamination**. Every serious accident here is a version of the same story: someone went eight hundred meters onto wet ground for a look, and could not come back, and no one could hear them shout.

Prepare for a place that is entirely indifferent and extremely slow, and in which you will be the fastest, heaviest, richest, dirtiest thing that has ever stood on it.

---

## When to arrive

There are two useful windows and one lottery.

**The dry.** Pans cracked into white polygons, frogs a meter down in shed-skin coffins, lungfish coiled in mucus, the plain silent-ish and hard underfoot. This is when you can *move*. Duricrust and gravel trains are trafficable; you can cross country; you can see the bones. It is also hot, glaring, and the water you can see on a map is mostly not there.

**The first weeks of the wet.** This is the one to aim for. Rain four afternoons a week, the crust greening in ninety seconds, dry pans becoming soup within the hour, frogs coming up out of the ground, and the flood crest starting down the ghost channel at four hundred meters a day. Everything happens, and then everything keeps happening for months. The cost: the ground stops being a surface and becomes a substance. Plan to be nearly stationary.

**The lottery:** the salt lake fills properly about twice in a human lifetime. If your transit allows you to choose a decade rather than a week, choose that one — ten thousand birds arriving from a continent away, pink shallows, the whole terminal basin alive. Accept that you will see most of it from a duricrust cap or from the air, because you will not be walking out there.

A compromise worth engineering: **arrive downstream, dry, just before the wet breaks.** Set camp on hard ground near a low sill in the ghost channel. Then wait. The frogs will start calling about three days before the water arrives — they feel the ground soften. You will get the warning from animals, then the brown edge creeping through the tussocks at roughly twenty-eight centimeters a minute, which is fast enough to watch with a stick and a stopwatch. There are few better arrivals available anywhere.

Avoid midday for anything that matters. Between about eleven and three the plain is a mirage engine: the horizon lifts into floating islands, distances become fiction, and the salt glare is a genuine eye hazard.

## Bring your own hill

This is the single most useful thing you can do. The landscape's legibility lives about ten meters above your head. From the ground you see a tree, a mound, a shimmer. From ten meters up you see the quartz ribbons as writing, the ghost channel as a green river-shaped band, the generation-cohorts of trees, the scald margins, the whole logic.

Carry a telescoping pole with a camera, or a kite, or a small drone, or a tethered balloon. Ten to fifteen meters is enough. A stepladder is not absurd. You will use it every day, and it will change what the trip *is*.

Also carry a thirty-meter length of clear hose. Filled with water and stretched out, it will show you about six millimeters of fall — enough to prove the slope with your own eyes in an afternoon, instead of a year and a bowl. It is a small, stupid, deeply satisfying thing to do on a plain that insists it is flat.

## Navigation: three gifts and two traps

**Gift one: the quartz.** The white gravel trains are the best thing on this plain for a traveler. They are durable underfoot, they run in long sinuous lines for kilometers, they are unmistakable, and they are *linear* — they give you a handrail in a world with no landmarks. Learn the local pattern from the air on day one and sketch it. Navigate by vein-junctions the way you'd navigate by street corners.

**Gift two: the wedge mounds.** The termites build their wedges edge-on to the noon sun. Those thin axes are a compass you can read from four hundred meters. Use only the wedges — the cathedral spires and the eroded pedestals are not aligned and will lie to you.

**Gift three: dawn.** Under the nocturnal inversion, before the mirage starts, the air is briefly honest and you can sometimes see features far beyond the nominal horizon, floating and clear. Do your bearings then. By day, remember that of your fifteen kilometers of visibility, eleven are haze: your real working sight radius is about four.

**Trap one: mirage.** Never take a bearing on something that is shimmering, and never estimate distance by apparent size at midday. Trees here are forty to fifty meters apart and all look the same size; a stunted four-hundred-year-old canopy at eight hundred meters and a young one at two hundred are the same object to your eye.

**Trap two, and this one is serious: sound.** At dawn and after dark, the inversion carries a chorus fifteen kilometers intact and delivers it to your boots. **You cannot localize water by ear.** People have walked all night toward a pan that was never near them. If you are disoriented, do not navigate by the frogs. Navigate by the quartz, by the mound axes, by your instruments, or not at all.

Carry proper positioning plus paper and compass, and check the compass against the sun — there is a lot of iron in this ground and some of it is remanently magnetized. Carry radios. Voice does not work here; a flat plain with warm wet air is a superb radio environment and a terrible acoustic one for human speech. Agree on light signals and rendezvous times *before* you separate, because "shout if you get into trouble" is not a plan on the Pale Ground.

## Where you may put your feet

Most of the bare ground is not bare. It is a cryptogamic crust — algae, lichens, liverworts — that does more photosynthesis than the trees and holds the entire surface together. It looks like dead grey nothing for eleven months of the year. One footprint breaks it; the break scalds; the scald spreads, for decades.

**Walk on the quartz.** This is the beautiful convergence: the durable surface is also the navigational handrail. Where you must leave it, walk single file, step in each other's prints, and prefer ground that is already scalded — the damage there is done.

Other ground rules:

- **Crabholes and yabbie shafts.** The cracking-clay country is full of hidden holes, some ankle-deep, some going to the water table, many with snakes in them. Probe ahead. Wear high boots and gaiters. Never run, never walk this ground at night without a good light.
- **The seep aprons.** Under the breakaways, the mud never dries and never floods. It is permanently the consistency of soft cheese and it will take your boots and possibly your legs. Admire them from the boulder jumble.
- **The breakaway edges.** Hard cap, soft belly, actively undercutting. Do not stand on the lip; do not camp beneath. Set back at least twice the wall height, and treat every fallen cap boulder as occupied — because it is. Each one is a lizard, a fern, an ant colony, an algae ring. **Do not build cairns.** Do not move a rock to make a marker. You would be demolishing a country to leave a note.
- **The salt lake.** The gypsum crust crunches like frost and it is a crust: beneath it is black sulphidic mud. Do not walk out on it. Disturbed sulphidic mud can release hydrogen sulfide, which will kill you at concentration and which you will stop smelling right before it does. Stay on the upwind shore, keep your nose out of any hole you dig, and treat the downwind clay-pellet dune as a thing to photograph, not climb.

## Water

The cruel joke is that there is water everywhere and almost none of it is for you.

The deep water is thirty meters down and only a four-hundred-year-old tree can reach it. The shallow water is held in clay, turbid, teeming, and increasingly salty as you go downhill — the whole plain drains inward and salt is the only thing it keeps. The lake is brine over anoxic mud. Do not drink from the terminal basin, do not drink from the low samphire ground, and do not trust a "spring" marked on a map without tasting a drop first.

**Best solution: catch the rain.** In the wet it falls four afternoons a week, warm and ordinary and clean. A tarp and a bladder will keep you indefinitely, and it costs the landscape nothing. This is the right answer for a visitor here: take from the sky, not from the ground.

**Second solution: settle and treat.** Pan water carries suspended clay that will destroy a filter in minutes. Draw it, let it stand overnight, decant the clear, then filter and disinfect. Carry a cloth prefilter and spare cartridges. Never draw from a pan with a bird colony in it.

**The mound spring is not a water supply.** It is a room-sized pool that has been continuously wet since before the ice ages, containing a fish, a snail, a flatworm, and a reed that exist nowhere else in any world you can reach. Its dome of precipitate took longer to build than your species has existed. Treat it as a clean room:

- Do not enter. Not a foot, not a hand.
- Do not wash, rinse, spit, or urinate anywhere in its catchment.
- No sunscreen in that water. No soap. Nothing.
- Sterilize boots before approaching, and approach on the precipitate, not the reed.
- If you must take water in an emergency, take it from the outflow well downstream of the pool, and treat it.

A single fungal spore or snail egg off your boot could end a lineage that survived every rearrangement the planet has thrown at it. That is not rhetoric; that is the actual risk profile of an isolated ancient refuge with no history of disturbance and therefore no history of defense.

## Camp

Site on a low rise of duricrust or a broad quartz train, well back from any breakaway lip, upwind of the salt lake, and above the pans — remembering that "above" here means a meter, which is plenty.

Nothing on this plain will hold a guy line except a tree or a termite mound, and you should not use either. **Carry freestanding shelter with wide feet**, or ballast with boulders that have already fallen. There is no timber, no fuel, no windbreak, no shade but a small canopy every forty meters. All structure is imported.

**No fires.** Not one. This is a landscape that never learned to burn — the gaps between tussocks are permanent firebreaks, there are almost no seedlings, and recruitment happens only in great flood years. A single escaped fire could delete a cohort with a two-hundred-year gap behind it. Stove only, on mineral ground, with a wide clear zone. And no cigarette ends, ever.

**Storms.** Warm convective rain arrives around four in the afternoon, reliably, with lightning, on a plain where you or your pole are the tallest object for fifteen kilometers. Be at camp or in a vehicle by mid-afternoon in the wet. Do not shelter under the isolated tree. Do not lie flat on wet conductive clay.

**Insects.** The fog over the pans at dusk is not decorative. Full coverage, head net, repellent, permethrin-treated clothing, and a sleeping space you can seal. Bats work the pans in loose hundreds — never handle one. Assume the local arboviruses are unfamiliar to your immune system and behave accordingly.

**Heat.** The night air is so full of water that the stone drinks it out of the sky. That means high humidity, poor evaporative cooling, and real wet-bulb risk in the wet season. Six to ten liters a day, electrolytes, and a hard stop on midday exertion.

**Glare.** White clay scalds and a salt lake throw light at you from below. Category-four lenses with side shields, a broad brim, and cover under your chin and nose. Shade alone does not save you here; the ground is a second sun.

**Cuts.** Wet clay in an open wound is a genuine medical event in this kind of country — soil-dwelling pathogens that enter through abrasions and can kill you weeks later, at home, when nobody knows where you've been. Cover every cut before you go out. Keep tetanus current. If you develop a fever or a pneumonia afterward, the first sentence out of your mouth to the medic is *"I was walking in wet tropical clay on an ancient weathered plain."*

Carry more water than the map suggests. The map says water everywhere. The water is eight kilometers away across ground that will not hold your weight.

## The phosphorus rule

This is the part travelers get wrong, because it doesn't feel like pollution.

A hundred million years of rain has stripped everything soluble out of this ground. Phosphorus is the currency; every shape you can see — the small hard grey leaves, the four trees per hectare, the slow hunting, the twenty-year tortoise — is an answer to its scarcity.

**You are a phosphorus event.** Your urine, your feces, your toothpaste, your soap, your dishwater, your bread crumbs, your fruit peel, your sunscreen. In a system this poor, that is not litter; it is a fertilizer bomb, and the things that will respond are the generalists you carried in with you.

So: carry out solid waste and toilet paper in sealed bags. Carry out food scraps, all of them, including the "biodegradable" ones. No washing in any water body — wash with a bowl and a cloth, well away from pans, seeps, channels, and the spring, and carry out the greywater or scatter it on already-scalded ground. No soap on the ground at all if you can manage it. Check your pockets, cuffs, and boot treads for seeds before you arrive and before you leave.

And remember where everything ends up. This basin has no outlet. Nothing leaves. Salt is the only thing this landscape accumulates, and it accumulates it *forever*. Anything you drop in this catchment will still be in this catchment when the breakaways are gone.

Boot-wash and gear-down between areas, with a proper disinfectant and a bucket you carry out. You are worried about chytrid, about root rots, about ants, about mosquito larvae in your water bladder, about algal cells on a damp strap. The biota here has never been interrupted. It has no practice at being invaded.

## Instruments worth carrying

- Hand lens. The real forest is the crust, and it is a millimeter tall.
- Binoculars, not a scope; the haze eats magnification.
- A recorder with a decent windscreen and low-frequency response. The flat calm air is an almost perfect acoustic instrument. Record the chorus at dusk and the dawn inversion. You will not reproduce the pressure in the sternum, but you will get closer than you expect.
- Ear protection for sleeping, and one night with none.
- Survey flags and a stopwatch for the flood crest — and retrieve every flag.
- Thermometer, a level, a notebook.

Give yourself a small slow project. This is a place that rewards measurement and punishes ambition. Mark the crest and come back tomorrow. Put your palm on the breakaway at dawn and feel it wet when it hasn't rained. Watch a dead grey patch of crust turn green in ninety seconds. Count tree cohorts. Try to find the bittern — you won't.

## What it will do to your head

Prepare for two opposite assaults. By day: sensory starvation, no landmarks, no scale, a horizon that lies, time going syrupy, the specific unease of standing in a place with no edges. By night: overload — a chorus that is a physical pressure, that flattens thought, that makes speech impossible and company impossible, in the emptiest place you have ever been. The emptiness is not silence; the emptiness is *why* it is loud.

You will be alone in the loudest place you have ever stood. Some people find that unbearable and some find it the only thing they wanted. Go with someone, and plan to spend the evenings apart.

There is also a slower vertigo. Nothing here is addressed to you. The trees are older than most human institutions and are not succeeding one another; the plain is finishing itself, has been finishing itself since before mammals, and does not require your opinion. You may feel an urge to help — to plant, to drain, to fence, to fix the widening scald. **Don't.** Recruitment happens in great flood years and nowhere else; a seedling you plant is a generation that was not supposed to exist. This is not a wound. It is a life stage on a hundred-million-year clock, and you are a weather event in it.

And the deepest rule, the one the place itself states: *no one has ever made anything here that was not made of itself.* Every earthwork on this plain was carried up mouthful by mouthful by an insect. Your tent pegs, your cairn, your scratched arrow, your dropped lens cap would be the first made things in a hundred million years that were not grown.

Leave it so that no one can tell. Walk on the quartz. Take nothing — not a pebble, not a crystal, not a shard of the last unweathered fragment of a mountain that no longer exists. Anchor your transit point on already-broken duricrust, well away from water, and take the marks with you when you go.

## Short list

Freestanding shade with wide feet · pole/kite/drone for ten meters of altitude · clear hose level · GPS + paper + compass checked against sun + wedge mounds · radios and light signals · high boots, gaiters, clay scraper, probe stick · boot-wash kit and bucket · tarp and bladders for rain · settling vessel, prefilter, filter, disinfectant · category-four glasses, broad brim, full coverage, head net · earplugs · stove and fuel, **no fire** · wag bags, sealed rubbish, all of it out · hand lens, binoculars, recorder, flags (retrieved), notebook · first aid with wound coverage, tetanus current, evacuation plan for two hundred kilometers of bog · red light for night · patience in a quantity you have not previously carried.

Then go at dusk to the edge of a pan, on quartz, and stand still while the whole enormous emptiness fills up — as it has every evening for a hundred million years — saying nothing, to no one, at a volume that carries fifteen kilometers.

Don't shout back. Just be, briefly, the one thing here that is in a hurry, and try to slow down enough to be worth the ground's while.
