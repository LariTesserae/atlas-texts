# Preparing for the Sill

## A briefing for an ordinary body

---

## 0. The thing to understand before you pack

The Sill will kill you at every hour of the day and night, and it will do it politely, without weather, without malice, and without anything happening that a local would call an event.

Forty-four degrees at saturation is not "hot." It is above the temperature at which a human being can shed metabolic heat by any mechanism. Your sweat will not evaporate. Your skin is cooler than the air, so the air heats you rather than cools you, and because that air is twice as dense as the one you evolved in, it delivers that heat about twice as efficiently as it would at home. At night it drops to thirty-nine, which is still above the line. There is no hour, no shade, no breeze, no season, no phase of the Long Breath in which an unassisted human body is safe below the Hedge.

So this is not a trek. It is a **saturation dive into a hot atmosphere, approached by an alpine climb**, and you must be equipped and trained for both, in that order, on the same day.

Everything else in this briefing is downstream of that.

---

## 1. Ranked causes of death, in the order they will actually get you

1. **Heat.** Overwhelmingly. Not the predator, not the fall-through, not the Note. Heat, and specifically your own optimism about heat.
2. **The stair,** on the way up, while already hyperthermic, exerting, and decompressing.
3. **The mat,** at night, under you.
4. **Dehydration** that you did not notice because your sweat was doing nothing and you assumed you weren't losing it.
5. **Your own judgment,** during the first three days of the Note.
6. **The ledger,** in a manner nobody has adequately described to me and which I am not going to speculate about in a document you'll read on the way in.

The null-hunter is sixth or seventh. It has been standing still for six days. It is patient. You are the impatient thing in this basin.

---

## 2. The approach: pack for two planets

You come up the outside. The last kilometer is blue ice at thirty below, above most of the world's air, under a small hard sun in a violet sky. Treat it as a serious high-altitude mountaineering objective, because it is one:

- Crampons, axe, rope, and the competence to use them. The ice *rings* — it is glassy and hard and it will not take an edge the way snow-ice does.
- Category-4 glacier glasses and full face coverage. Thin air, bright sun, white floor below the cloud deck: snow blindness on the approach is the most common injury and the most stupid.
- Altitude. Acclimatize on the way up, over days, or don't come. If you use supplemental oxygen, **purge and stow it before you reach the Hedge.** Descending into two atmospheres on enriched gas is a way to poison your lungs in a place with no doctor.
- Everything on this list stays in a cache at the rim. You will need it again, suddenly, possibly at night, possibly in a hurry. Cache it properly, marked, and cache a second one lower down the stair.

You will be wearing polar kit at the top of the stair and effectively nothing at the bottom, and the transition is nine hundred steps wide enough for two abreast. Bring a bag you can strip into while moving.

---

## 3. The Hedge is a lock, not a fog

Everyone puts their arm through. Don't put your *head* through first, and don't cross with a head cold.

You are going from thin air to roughly twice the pressure you were born at, across a boundary the locals will tell you holds to a hand's width. Whatever the mechanism — and the three schools are polite about each other, and you should be too — your middle ears, sinuses, and any dental work you have will experience it as a step change.

- Get your teeth checked before you leave home. An unhealed cavity or a leaking filling under that compression is barodontalgia, and it is the kind of pain that makes people do stupid things on stairs.
- Cross slowly, on the stair, with someone who has done it. Descend in stages. Equalize continuously. Do not run, do not carry more than you can drop.
- Anyone with a chest infection, an unhealed fracture, a pacemaker, an aneurysm, a seizure history, or a pregnancy should not cross at all. I am not being cautious. There are four hundred people here and no town.

**And plan the ascent as decompression.** After a week at two atmospheres you are saturated. Going up nine hundred steps into thin air is a dive ascent, and doing it hot and exhausted and carrying a pack is how you get bent at the top of a mountain in a place with no chamber. Talk to the count-house about a staged ascent. Build rest into it. The ice-house is at the rim stair for more reasons than one, and I would use it as a stop.

---

## 4. Heat: the arithmetic, and the doctrine that follows from it

Do this sum once and you will never be casual about the Sill again.

A resting human in that air is absorbing something on the order of 250–400 watts — your own metabolism plus what the dense hot air is pushing into you. Ice absorbs about 334 kilojoules per kilogram as it melts. So **you need roughly three to four kilograms of ice per hour just sitting still**, and seven or eight if you walk. Over a day, at rest, that is approximately your own body weight in ice.

There is nowhere below the Hedge to get cold. The sumps are hotter than your skin — going into the water to cool off is going into a bath. The tower crowns get cold enough at dusk to wring the air, which is a beautiful fact and is *not* cold enough to save you. The returns are warm. The rain is warm. Everything is warm.

**There is exactly one cold thing on the Sill and it is the ice you carried down.**

Which gives you the doctrine, and it is the same doctrine the folk use:

### Live high. Visit low.

Base at the ice-house at the rim stair. Sleep there. Dry out there. Eat there. Recover there. Go down into the basin in **cooled forays** — descend in the early morning, work for ninety minutes to two hours with ice at your neck, armpits and groin, and be climbing before your judgment goes. Then lie against the wall and let the heat come out of you.

The locals commute up at midday for exactly this reason. Adopt their schedule: down at first light, up before the tenth hour, against the cold wall through the fourteenth, down again in the fifteenth if you must. **Nothing works between the tenth and fourteenth hours.** This is not a charming custom. It is the load-bearing wall of the entire culture and you will not be exempt from it.

### The mantle

The folk wear a mantle-liverwort across the shoulders that transpires for them. Get one. Have it fitted by someone who knows. Do not attempt to build your own; do not attempt to reverse-engineer it in the week you're there; do not ask three times how it works.

Treat it as livestock, because it is. It can sicken. It must be fed and watered — from a weep, never a return. **A dead mantle in the twelfth hour is a corpse in the fourteenth.** Check it the way you'd check a rope.

And it does not leave with you. It belongs to the heat. It will die outside, and it will be a term in the ledger.

### The rule about your partner

Hyperthermia removes your ability to notice hyperthermia. You will feel fine, then pleasant, then you will sit down somewhere comfortable, and the mat is very comfortable.

Travel with a partner and give them absolute authority. Pre-agree the signal. **When they say up, you go up, and you do not get a vote, and you do not get to finish what you were doing.** Write this down before you cross the Hedge, in your own hand, while you are still the person who wrote it.

## 5. The Note

Nineteen hertz, coming up through the floor, through the water, through the mat, through your feet.

For about three days you will weep without cause, your teeth will ache, and the edges of your vision will smear. Warn your companion in advance that the weeping is mechanical. It is not a revelation. It is not grief about your childhood. It is your body being played like a bowl of water. People make enormous decisions in those three days and regret them for years.

**Make no decisions, sign nothing, resolve nothing, and confess nothing in the first seventy-two hours.** Do not be alone in them either.

Earplugs do nothing. It is bone-conducted and whole-body; you cannot get between it and yourself. After three days you stop noticing and never stop feeling it. Your sleep will be poor for a week and then oddly deep.

### Carry a tuner for the fifth

*When the Note goes to a fifth, leave the basin and do not come back for a year.*

Nineteen becomes twenty-eight and a half. Most people cannot reliably name an interval in infrasound while their sternum is being pressed on. So carry a physical reference: two small resonators, one tuned to each, or a fork and a marked monochord. If the second one starts singing on its own, you are already late.

Have a local teach you the sound before you need it. Ask them to hum it. They will know exactly what you mean and they will be pleased you asked, because it means you're planning to obey.

**Pre-stage your evacuation.** Rim cache, ice at the stair, cold kit, a decompression plan. If the Note goes to a fifth you drop everything and climb, in whatever state you're in, and the climb is the dangerous part. Do not leave gear below that you cannot abandon without grief.

## 6. Sound discipline: the rule that inverts your instincts

**Speak loudly.** Every instinct you have about being quiet in a dangerous place is wrong here. The Note eats quiet speech, and things that hear quiet speech are not the things you want.

Understand it this way: a whisper is a small null you make with your mouth. The predator hunts by folding the hum back on itself into a cancelled patch. Do not go around announcing yourself in the grammar of the thing that eats people.

Practical:

- **Be silent or be loud. Never in between.** Silence is fine. Murmur is not.
- For private conversation: write, or sign, or go up the stair. Slate and stylus, not paper.
- Do not whisper to your partner "did you hear that." Say it out loud, at full voice, from the chest.
- Learn the local register. In air this heavy every voice comes out of a barrel; children sound like elders. Your natural speaking voice may land as thin and high and *quiet*. Drop it, open your throat, and let it boom. You will sound ridiculous to yourself for four days and then you will sound like everyone.
- **Do not bring active noise-cancelling headphones into the basin.** I want to be very clear about this. They manufacture nulls. Whatever you think they're for, they are not worth it.

### Carry a drone

Pin a small continuous sound source to your shoulder — a clockwork buzzer, a ticking escapement, anything mechanical that hums steadily. It gives you a known signal in the air around your head, so that when a patch of the night goes *cancelled* you hear it as a hole in your own tone rather than as an absence you have to notice. It is the single cheapest piece of safety equipment you can bring and no one sells it, so make it.

### When you find the null

Step off the mat onto stone. Wait. Speak steadily and loudly to your partner. Do not run — running booms. It has been standing still for six days and it will stand still for six more, but not forever, and the way you leave is calmly, on stone, talking.

### Distance is heard, not seen

The dense air carries everything absurdly well and the haze lies to your eyes. A tower that looks four hundred meters off is two kilometers, and two kilometers is four hours of ice. The language has eleven words for *far* that are all degrees of muffled. Learn at least four of them before you arrive; your translator will render them all as "distant" and that translation will try to kill you.

**Never set out for anything without an ice budget for the round trip plus half.**

## 7. Water

Drink from a weep. Never a sump. Never a return.

The weeps are two-millimeter films running down vertical flutes with small silver fish in them. You harvest them with a comb or a squeegee and a funnel, working along a tower waist, and it takes about an hour to get a couple of liters, and this will be a large part of your day. Bring the tool. Bring a cup you can hold under a curl.

Bring **salt**. Weep water is limestone water: calcium-rich, sodium-poor. You will be losing sodium in sweat that is doing you no good whatsoever — sweat that drips is pure loss with zero cooling — and you will not notice, because you will not feel yourself sweating in air that is already wet. Salt tablets, and more than you think. Over months, the calcium is a kidney-stone problem; over days, the sodium is a collapse problem.

### About the returns

Your filter does not solve this. Your filter, your boil, your UV pen, your iodine, your ceramic element — all of those solve microbiology, and microbiology is not what is wrong with a return. The water in them *tastes counted*. Nobody can say why. Adults do not drink from them.

That is a complete instruction. Take it as given. Do not sample them, do not put your hand in and hold it, do not take a bottle home. Some of those loops have been going around since before the language had a word for around, and I would not care to be a new term in one.

If you find a return that is **cool to the touch**, that is genuinely interesting. Note it, tell the count-house, and still don't drink it.

## 8. Walking

The mat takes your weight in most places. *Most* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

- **Do not walk the pale mat.** Pale in the east, dark in the west: that's the snail tide, forty kilometers long, eleven years wide, three meters a day. Pale means grazed means thin. Learn which side of the tide you're on before you step off stone.
- Carry a **mat-probe** — a light pole, two meters. Test every step off a known route. Walk roped to someone who knows the ground.
- Understand what falling through means: you go into half a meter to four meters of hot water **under a continuous floating lid**, in the dark, in a basin with four thousand sieve-crabs per square meter working the tissue. The hole closes behind you. There is no sky. Bring a knife, but understand that a knife is not a plan.
- **Never cross a sump at night.** The mat is a drumhead the size of a parish and it booms and you announce yourself to everything within ten kilometers. Learn the stone routes: tower waists, high sills, sorting slabs, the banks of molt-shells. Carry a map of *stone*, not of distance.
- Your feet will never be dry. Not once, not for a day. Immersion foot is a real and disabling risk within a week. Bring sandals for stone, and go up to the rim to be dry the way you'd go to a hospital. **Dryness is a medical treatment on the Sill.**

## 9. Kit: what survives, what doesn't

Nothing here is ever dry. Assume every object you own is permanently wet at forty degrees and will be colonized.

**Leave behind:** cotton, leather, paper, down, wood, anything with glue, anything with a fan.

**Bring:** synthetics, closed-cell foam, titanium, glass, silicone. Wax and slate for notes — your paper journal will be pulp in a day and your ink will be a stain in the water.

**Optics will fog permanently.** Every lens, every camera, every pair of spectacles, from the instant you cross the Hedge until the instant you cross back. Heated lenses or nothing. If you depend on glasses, consider having your eyes fixed before the trip, or accept that you will be functionally blurry for the entire visit and navigate by ear and hand. Bring the camera only if it's sealed and warmed; otherwise bring your attention.

**Go analog.** Nineteen hertz shakes solder joints and MEMS and hard drives, and condensation kills the rest. Spring clock, mercury thermometer, tuning fork, mechanical balance, a set of standard masses, a good hydrometer. If it needs a battery, it needs a hermetic case, and it will still fail.

**Medical:** antifungals, zinc oxide, liquid skin and sutures (tape does not stick, dressings rot, wounds macerate), electrolytes, a dental kit. If you can bring a dentist, bring a dentist. Four hundred people, no town, and teeth are the commonest emergency in a place where everyone's ache for three days on arrival.

**Do not bring animals.** Not a dog, not a bird, not a tank of anything. It will be a new term in a water balance that closes to four decimal places, and then it will be a carcass on a sorting stone.

## 10. The ledger

*Carry nothing out that you did not carry in — and if you must, leave something of equal weight, and it does not have to be the same kind of thing, and this is not a superstition, and there is a ledger.*

Treat this as a physical law with a clerk.

**Weigh in.** Everything. Kit, consumables, and **yourself**, on the same balance, witnessed, recorded on something that survives wet. Make a manifest. Keep a duplicate at the rim.

**Bring ballast.** You are going to lose several kilograms down there — water, carbon, the ordinary leakage of a body. That mass stays in the basin, which means the balance is against you on the way out, which means you must either take out something native (and pay for it) or leave something foreign behind. Bring things worth leaving:

- **Salt.** Sodium in a world of limestone and fresh water.
- **Worked metal.** Steel, copper, spring stock, needles, files, good blades.
- **Phosphorus, sulfur, iodine, silica.** The basin sorts calcium and iron and long carbons. Everything else is scarce.
- **Medicine and dentistry.**
- **Measurement.** The count-house is an argument between memory and measurement conducted in graduated rods, some of them alive. A reliable mass standard, a decent balance, a repeatable length, an honest clock — these are worth more here than gold, and offering them is the best introduction a traveler can make.

**Weigh out, and budget time for the audit.** Nothing on the Sill is fast. The audit will take a day and it will be conducted by someone who has been doing it for thirty years and who is not impressed by your schedule.

**Do not take:** a shell, a molt, a chip of flute, a leaf, a pebble, a drip-tip. And above all **do not take water home.** Everyone wants to. A bottle of weep is the commonest theft and I would not want to explain it to the clerk.

### The sorting stones

Do not sit on one. Do not sleep on one. Do not put your pack down on one. Do not test one. A carcass is taken apart in eleven hours — not eaten, *sorted*: calcium in a ridge, iron in a smear, long carbons folded like cloth, no smell. It is done by something in the stone operating below the scale of seeing. You are made of exactly the things it sorts.

Do not leave a knife hoping for a twin. The timescale is a year, the twin is *very slightly better*, and the offer is not aimed at people who are passing through. Doubling a knife also doubles its mass, and you have already read the rule.

### If you die

Make arrangements before you cross. You will be sorted, there will be no smell and no body, and your people will not get anything back but a ridge of calcium and a smear of iron. Someone will have to settle your weight. Travel with a person who has agreed to do it, or pre-deposit the ballast and name it in the manifest. Write this down at home, in the cold, where it is easy to be sensible.

## 11. Manners, and the shape of time

**Arrange an invitation before you come.** There is no inn, no town, no infrastructure to absorb a stranger. You are the guest of a specific family on a specific shoulder, or you are a problem.

Learn a little language. Not the nouns — the *courtesies*:

- The towers are not *standing*. They are **held**. Say held.
- Your first question is **"What phase is it?"** The phases of the three-hundred-and-forty-year Turn are the calendar, and asking is both polite and genuinely necessary, because the water level determines where you may walk.
- Learn the name of the **missing note**. A tower let go a generation back and there is a hole in the chord that everybody knows. Being able to hear which one is missing is how you show that you have been listening rather than looking.
- Hold the three readings at once — grown, built, always was. Do not argue for one. Do not ask *who built it* as your opener; it is a category error and it is boring.
- Do not remark on the precision of the boundary markers. It is the one thing nobody remarks on. Let them raise it.
- Do not ask about the returns twice, or the nine huts twice.
- If a family shows you the sealed upper floor — swept, provisioned, unused, its own door and hearth, waiting two hundred years for a great-great-grandchild to pull a boat up to the window — do not call it empty. **It is early.** Say something appropriate. This is the most intimate thing they can show you.
- Do not enter the opening at the waist of Aunt. If you must look, go with someone, run a line, and **turn around at the last limestone.** Twenty meters in, the wall stops being rock and starts having structure at every scale you can bring to bear, warm, at nineteen hertz, doing something with the forest's waste heat. That is an exhaust. You are an order-bearing object made of sortable elements. There is no cooling in there and no reason for you.
- Do not dive the drowned observatory. Do not sleep in the nine huts.
- Do not plug a flute, and do not unplug a nest. The birds detune the valley every breeding season and there is a documented year when the Bell went silent and three sumps drained. You are not going to improve on that.

**Slow down before you arrive.** A predator stalks one boomer for a week. The fog takes four hours to lift. Flowers open across nine days. Your normal tempo is a form of violence here, and it is also, straightforwardly, how visitors die — because haste is heat, and heat is the whole problem.

Plan **three weeks minimum**. The first three days are lost to the Note; the first week you are a hazard to yourself; only after that do you begin to actually be there. Six weeks is better. Do not stay a year — nobody knows what chronic nineteen hertz does to a skeleton that didn't grow up in it, and the folk have denser bones and thicker chests than their lowland cousins for a reason.

---

## 12. Emergencies: safety is a direction

Memorize this: **up is safe.** Every emergency on the Sill resolves by going up, and up is nine hundred steps, a decompression, and thirty below.

- **Overheating:** your partner decides. Ice to neck, armpits, groin. Get onto **stone**, not mat, because when you collapse you must not collapse through the lid. Climb. Do not go into the water.
- **Through the mat:** rope, probe, knife, mark the hole, do not thrash. Your partner is the whole plan; have one.
- **The null:** stone, stillness, loud steady speech, back away.
- **The fifth:** drop everything, climb, do not return for a year, and do not argue about the year.
- **A tower letting go:** eleven days of sag, so you'll have warning. Stay off the waist. And understand that this is a death in the community — behave as you would at a funeral, not as you would at a geological event.

## 13. Leaving

The audit. The staged ascent. The cold, which will feel like a hand on your face after weeks of being pressed on by a crowd.

Then, home, and the part nobody warns you about: **the world will be too quiet and too thin.** Air that offers no resistance. Sounds that arrive from far away already dead. Your own voice, shrill and small and unaccompanied. Rooms with no floor note. You will find yourself waiting for the fall — that ninety-minute roar of ten million leaf-tips that the folk sleep through — and it will not come, and the silence at two hours after dusk will be the loneliest thing you have ever experienced.

Some travelers report a nineteen-hertz phantom in quiet rooms for months afterward. Do not go straight from the Sill to a silent place. Decompress socially. Eat with people. Let there be traffic outside the window.

And be honest with yourself before you go, because this is the actual hazard: **the Sill is a place people go back to.** Not for the spectacle — the spectacle is a white pillar over a cloud deck and you can see it from a hundred kilometers out for free. They go back for the feeling of being held in something that has been keeping its shape while its substance flows through it since before there were eyes to notice. That is a real thing and it is not available anywhere else and it will pull at you.

So: go if you can be **slow, loud, and cool.** If you can't be all three, go to the rim, lie against the ice-house wall, look down through the Hedge at the green haze and the ranked suggestions of nine hundred towers, listen to a chord you can hear the far side of before you can see it, and come home.

That is a complete visit. The folk themselves climb up to look at the pillar. They know what the best view is, and it is the one from outside, and it leans very slightly to the east, and nobody yet knows what that means.
