# Before You Go: The Basin of Rasping

## 1. Understand the single fact that governs everything

You will not be a visitor here. You will be a *hole*.

Nothing in this basin has eyes, and nothing has ever needed them. The world is read as a sound field, and in that field a warm, soft, wet, non-rasping body one and a half meters tall is not merely conspicuous — it is *shaped exactly like a predator*. You absorb sound. You make no scrape. You open a silence around yourself the size of a doorway, and that silence is the most legible object for a hundred square kilometers. The felted things hunt those. The membrane-winged things hunt those too, and drop into them out of the fog, and half of them get eaten, and the arithmetic works out. It will work out with you in it.

So the whole of your preparation reduces to one discipline: **you must rasp, continuously, correctly, from before you arrive until after you leave.** Everything else — the hearing protection, the air, the water, the tether — exists to keep you alive while you do that.

There is a corollary that people miss, and it kills them. Permanent silences are fine. The escarpment is silent. The bald scoured patches are silent. The brine rims are silent. The basin has habituated to those; they are geography. What the carpet reports is *change* — a hole opening where chorus was a moment ago. Your arrival is the dangerous event. Not your presence. Your arrival.

## 2. Do not go first. Send something that rasps, and then stops.

The most important number you need is not the noise level. It is **the answer time**: how long after a silence opens does the first felted thing arrive?

Build a decoy. A slab the size of a dinner platter, on a broad flat foot, with a contact driver that plays back locally recorded chorus into the substrate. Leave it on the carpet from a position on the escarpment, on a line you can tend. Let it run for a day. Then stop it, abruptly, and record what comes and how fast — with a thermal imager, from the rock, not by walking out there.

If the answer time is thirty minutes, you have a survivable equipment failure. If it is ninety seconds, you do not, and your entire plan must be built around never having a failure at all.

While the decoy is out, collect the rest:

- **The gas profile, at four heights.** Five centimeters, thirty, one meter, two. This is a cold, closed, windless basin with a fermentation economy underneath it and cold air draining off the escarpment into a standing inversion. That is the textbook geometry for a heavy-gas lake. There may be an invisible layer of carbon dioxide and sour gas lying at exactly knee height — which is to say, at exactly the height where the noise is 110 dB and where you will be tempted to kneel and look at things. Do not assume the air is breathable because you are standing up in it. Nothing here fixes light. Nothing here has made oxygen in a hundred thousand years. Whatever you breathe is imported from somewhere else, and it pools badly.
- **The caustic map, twice, days apart.** The focusing spots are partly a function of the fog's temperature inversion. Inversions change. *The forty-meter killing grounds may wander.* A map made last week is a rumor. Note also that they are faintly warm and acoustically distinct: the hiss thickens and develops a pitch as you approach. You will probably not hear that pitch developing, because you will be deaf by then. Instrument it.
- **Bearing strength and flour depth**, along a probed transect. The pavement is a fitted crust over meters of churned mud with no bedding and no competence. Find where the crust bridges and where it doesn't. Find the flour channels. A meter-deep windrow of shell flour that moves as a fluid is not ground; it is a liquid you can drown in, and it will not make a sound you can isolate from the rest.
- **The rhythm.** Four strokes a second, yes, but the film regrows in forty minutes, so expect a slow tidal envelope in the chorus over that period. And the eleven-second pulse: learn its waveform, its propagation, whether it is a shared clock or a spacing negotiation or the basin breathing gas out of a seep. You will need to participate in it. You must not improvise it.

## 3. Your ears are a consumable. Price them before you go.

At knee height, 110 dB. At the ear, more — call it 118. Unprotected, you lose high frequencies within a day, and you will not get them back.

Plugs alone are not enough, because the path is not only your ear canal. Within an hour your jaw aches from bone conduction. The stack that actually helps:

- Properly inserted foam plugs, plus muffs, plus a **rigid, mass-loaded helmet on a compliant suspension** with an air gap and no hard contact anywhere against the mandible or the teeth.
- A soft mouthguard. Keep your teeth apart. Do not clench, do not chew, do not hum. Your skull is a microphone and your jaw is its boom arm.
- Accept the floor. Bone conduction caps your real attenuation somewhere around forty to fifty decibels no matter what you bolt to your head. You will still be receiving something in the seventies at the cochlea. Hours are affordable. Days are not.

The eleven-second pulse you cannot block at all. Infrasound goes through the helmet, through the suit, through you. It arrives in the sternum first. Combined with the broadband hiss it will produce nausea and vertigo — the world tilting, the slope becoming unreadable. Carry an antiemetic that does not sedate you, and carry a visible bubble level or artificial horizon, and make yourself a rule you will hate: **trust the instrument, not the body.** Your body is going to lie to you about which way is down.

Get a baseline audiogram before you go and another after. Decide, in a quiet room, in advance, how much of your hearing this is worth. You will not be in a position to renegotiate later.

## 4. The voice

This is the piece of equipment that is actually keeping you alive, and it should be treated like a rebreather, not like a speaker.

**Specification.** Broadband coarse hiss with the fine sizzle layer on top; a four-hertz stroke rhythm with living jitter, not machine periodicity; participation in the eleven-second pulse; and — critically — **substrate coupling**. Airborne sound alone is a costume. The carpet reads the ground. Your rasp must go into the pavement through feet, through a staff tip, through a plate that rests on the shells.

**Dialect.** The pavement is size-graded and the grading varies across the basin. So does the chorus. Your rig must sample locally and match locally, and re-match as you move. Build it as a mimic, not a synthesizer with opinions. **Never transmit anything the basin has not already said, recently, near you.** If the pulse is a spacing or alarm signal and you invent a variant, you may clear a patch around yourself — you will have manufactured your own hole with your own safety device.

**Placement.** Low and far from your head. You are already standing in 118 dB; do not strap a second source to your helmet.

**Redundancy.** Three independent rigs, separate power, separate drivers, separate coupling paths. And understand the failure mode: *you will not hear your rig fail.* You are deaf, and the chorus masks everything. Failure must therefore be **felt and seen** — a wrist vibrator that stops when the rasp stops, a lamp in your visor that goes dark. Fail-loud, fail-tactile. Test the telltales by deliberately killing a rig, on the rock, before you commit.

**The manual backup, and the truth about it.** Carry a file and a shell plate anyway. Know that four strokes a second with your own arm is a thing you can do for perhaps ten minutes before your shoulder fails. Do not build a plan that contains heroics. Build a plan that contains an exit you can reach in less than the answer time.

**Fade.** Rehearse fading in and fading out. Arrive already rasping, at low amplitude, and bring it up over a minute. Leaving, do the reverse. A silence that opens instantly is prey. A silence that opens gradually is weather.

## 5. The skin

Positive-pressure suit, sealed, with your own air. Not a filter mask. The flour is shell ground so fine it moves as a fluid; it is respirable, abrasive, alkaline, and biologically alive, and it will find every seal, zipper, bearing, and thread you own. Positive pressure keeps it out. Filters clog in minutes.

Externally, felt it. A dense open pile, the hunters' own trick, so that your surface absorbs rather than reflects — a hard shell would make you a bright acoustic mirror in a world of absorbers, which is a different anomaly but still an anomaly. Accept the costs: felt loads with flour, gets heavy, wets through, loses its absorption. Carry a brush and spare panels.

Insulation is a genuine dilemma. Nine to eleven degrees, permanently saturated, with cold air sliding off the escarpment: classic slow hypothermia weather, the kind that kills people who don't feel cold. But a sealed suit in a warm-blooded body at a walking pace will cook you from inside, and you cannot open it. Plan active moisture management and a hard rule about not stopping for long.

**Feet: broad, flat, bridging plates.** Think snowshoes on a breakable crust, not boots. A heel punctures the tessellation, kills a dozen animals, ruptures the crust, and puts you into flour and mud. A wide flat plate rests on the fitted arch of clasts and crushes nothing. Lift clean, set down flat, never drag, never rotate. Walking here is a slow, deliberate, wide-legged placement, like crossing a floor of plates in a house where everyone is asleep.

**A staff.** Broad plate at the tip, with its own rasp element. It probes for flour, it gauges depth, it substitutes for the balance you have lost, and it gives your reach a voice. You will walk like a blind pilgrim in a world of the blind, and the staff will be your eye.

**A thermal imager is the most valuable instrument you will carry.** Nothing here can see your lights, so burn as much visible light as you like — it will only whiteout in the fog and show you your own instruments. But thermal shows you the real world: the carpet warmer than the air, the caustics faintly warm and bald, and — if the felted pile insulates as well as it absorbs — **the hunters as cold patches drifting across a warm floor.** Absences, visible. It is also how your partner finds you, because you will be the brightest thing in this basin.

## 6. Water: bring all of it, and understand why you must not want theirs

Carry every milliliter. Three to five liters per person per day plus margin, in a bladder with a bite valve inside the helmet, because you are not going to open the helmet.

The local water is the leachate of a black shale — a dead forest being dissolved by groundwater. Dissolved organics, sulfides, fermentation products, and whatever metals and radionuclides a thick black shale feels like giving up. Boiling does not remove metals. Filtering does not remove dissolved carbon. The fog is scavenging aerosol out of air full of flour and sour gas. Do not drink it, do not wash in it, do not let it into a wound.

But the deeper reason is structural, and you should sit with it before you go, because it is the knot the whole place is tied in and it will tempt you:

**To harvest dew you must be colder than the air. To be colder than the air you must be alone, exposed, on a bare rise or a brine rim. And to be alone here is to be a hole.**

Every drink of water in this world is purchased with audibility. The locals pay it in shifts, briefly, in terrible solitude, and they never stop rasping while they do it, which costs them the cold they came for, which means they must stay out longer. You are a 37-degree furnace; you cannot get cold enough to condense anything without going hypothermic first. You would stand on the rim, dry, warm, silent-shaped, radiating nothing useful, for hours, waiting for a few milliliters, while the brine rims — which are precisely the places where the drinkers go, and therefore precisely the places where the felted things wait — did what they do.

Do not go to the brine rims. Do not go to the bare rises. There is no water here for you. There is only the water you brought.

## 7. Where you may stand, and where you may not

**Base camp on the escarpment, on real rock, above the inversion.** Rock is a permanent silence and therefore safe silence. It has stratigraphy, which the basin does not. It is above the gas lake. It is out of the flour. You can see the fog from above and watch it thicken. Almost everything worth doing here can be done from the rock with instruments and a tended line.

**The bald patches are absolute exclusion zones.** They are the only bare, firm, wind-polished, faintly warm ground in the world — the only ground that looks *comfortable*, the only ground you could lie down on — and they are acoustic caustics carrying enough pressure to rupture a membrane. You are full of membranes. Eardrums, yes, but lungs and gut and sinuses too. Your helmet does nothing for your lungs. Keep two hundred meters of standoff, re-check the range every few minutes, and remember that the boundaries move with the weather.

And note this, because it is the subtlest trap in the place: **the pavement rotates.** Slowly, but it rotates, and it is a raft over meters of fluid mud. Standing still is not staying still. The ground is a very slow river and it can carry you toward a caustic edge while you are patiently watching your instruments and feeling perfectly stationary.

**Nothing goes on the ground. Ever.** Everything lanyarded to your body. There is no stratigraphy here; the record has been eaten and re-eaten. Anything you set down will be churned under within hours, and anything you build will be a permanent hole that draws predators to the place where you used to be. Do not make a cairn. Do not make a marker. Do not take a shell — a shell is a living animal and a hundred-thousand-year-old worked stone, and removing it opens a silence exactly where you stood.

## 8. Time

**Target under ninety minutes on the carpet. Hard cap three hours. Never a night.**

Do not sleep here. Sleeping means lying at gas-lake height, in flour, with a rig you are not watching, on ground that is carrying you somewhere.

**Learn to read the dew nights and be gone before them.** Thickening fog, the whole basin cooling at once, every shell beading together — and then two or three hours of the loudest silence of the year, and the felted things coming up out of the mud in their thousands, and the carpet thinner and the flour deeper by morning.

You have an advantage the locals don't: a well-insulated suit's outer skin sits near air temperature, colder than the warm living shells. **Your shoulders will bead before theirs do.** You are a dew-night barometer. Make it a standing order: when your own suit starts to drink, you leave, immediately, without discussion. Discussion is not available anyway.

## 9. Partners

Speech is pointless outside the helmet. Inside a good helmet, on closed full-duplex comms, you can talk normally — and this is the difference between a team and two isolated animals in the dark. Use it constantly. It is also your best instrument: a partner who can hear you slurring is your carbon-dioxide monitor.

But comms die with the seal, so back them with a **touch code on a physical tether**. Never untether. Slack tether is an abort condition, not an inconvenience.

Agree, on the rock, in writing, on the following: **there is no rescue.** If a partner goes down inside a caustic, you do not go in; you will simply add a second body. If they go into a flour channel, you haul on the line and you do not step off the crust. Write it down and sign it, because in the moment you will want to be a better person than the physics allows.

Abort triggers, any one of which ends the visit with no deliberation:

- Any rasp telltale fault, on either partner
- Measured SPL rising more than 6 dB, or any tonality emerging from the hiss
- Bald ground inside two hundred meters
- Any gas alarm at any height; O₂ below 19.5%; CO₂ above 1.5%
- Suit pressure loss, or any smell of sulfur inside the mask
- Beading on the suit exterior
- Loss of thermal or tether contact
- New vertigo, nausea, confusion, or a missed checklist call
- The timer

That last one matters more than it looks. Monotone noise, hypercapnia, cold, and vestibular insult will degrade your judgment smoothly and invisibly, and the hiss is hypnotic — it becomes company, and you will find reasons to stay. Set a mechanical timer and a ten-minute checklist you must read aloud to your partner. Outsource your will to a device while your will is still intact.

## 10. You are the richest carbon in the basin

This place runs on inheritance. Nothing here has fixed light in a hundred thousand years. Everything is being spent, slowly, out of a dead forest, at four strokes a second.

And then you arrive: warm, wet, oxygen-rich, full of sugar and fat and protein, breathing out CO₂, shedding skin, sweating. You are a subsidy from a world with a sun in it. Do not introduce yourself.

No food outside the suit. No crumbs, no cotton, no leather, no wood. Full containment of waste, carried out — you are not going to open the suit anyway. On the way home, decontaminate as though the flour is what it is: a live, extremely fine, extremely mobile inoculum that will be in every seam of everything you own for years.

And leave nothing. Not a marker, not a shell, not a print if you can help it. The pavement is fitted, graded, rounded, polished by a hundred millennia of rubbing, and it looks laid and is not laid. It is the only made thing in a world where nothing has ever been made, and it was made by eating. Walk on it the way you would walk on someone's life's work.

## 11. Afterward

Audiogram. Expect a permanent high-frequency notch and tinnitus. Expect the vertigo to take days to weeks to settle, and vestibular rehab if it doesn't. Chest imaging if the seal was ever breached. Quarantine your gear.

And expect the thing nobody warns you about: **you will keep hearing it.** After hours of unresolvable broadband hiss, quiet rooms develop a hole in them, and the rasp arrives to fill it. Sleep with a fan for a while. Tell someone in advance that this may happen, so that when it does you know it is the basin and not your mind coming apart.

## 12. What it will actually be like

Here is what your intuition should prepare you for, because no checklist covers it.

The noise arrives before the place does. You will spend the first minutes trying to find its source, and the moment you understand that the chute is the whole basin and the gravel is alive is a moment you will remember for the rest of your life. Then your jaw will begin to ache. Then you will stop being able to talk to the person beside you, and the world will tilt, and you will be standing in cold standing fog on a floor of animals in the dark, unable to see your own feet, warmer than everything around you, and *utterly obvious* — invisible to every eye here, because there are none, and screaming your presence in the only language the place has.

Nothing here is malicious. That is the part that will get under your skin. The felted things are not hunting you; they are answering a hole, and you are a hole, and it works out. There is no villain in this basin. There is only arithmetic, and a hundred thousand years of it, and a sky that closed.

And you will not be able to perceive it properly, because the price of surviving it is not perceiving it. Sealed, felted, deafened, instrumented, tethered, watching a wrist vibrator and a thermal screen — you will experience this place mostly as a set of readings. That is the honest trade. Take it consciously.

So bring a recorder. A contact mic for the substrate and an air mic for the hiss, and let them be your ears. Take the recording home instead of a shell. Play it once, at low volume, pitched down and slowed, in a bright room, with someone you love nearby — and listen for what it is.

Because the hiss is trees. Not leaves, not wind. Just their carbon, going out through the mouths of things that have never seen light, in the dark, in the cold, on a plain that has been listening to itself since before there was anyone to hear.

That is the only way you will ever really hear this place: somewhere else, later, quietly, with the ears it left you.
