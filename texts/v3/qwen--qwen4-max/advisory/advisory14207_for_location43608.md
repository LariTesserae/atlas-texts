# Preparing for the Sill

## The thing you must understand before you pack

Nothing there will look like it is trying to kill you. That is the whole problem. The light is ordinary. The shadows are ordinary. The ground is a floor. You will step out onto it and your body will report *moorland, cold, a bit high, fine* — and your body will be wrong in two separate ways, both fatal.

**First: you are an animal.** There is no word for that on the Sill. There has never been a wing, a midge, a worm, a mouth that eats green. You will be the first grazing thing in the history of the world, and you will arrive full of seeds and gut bacteria and mites and pollen, shedding skin at a rate of half a gram an hour, breathing out warmth and water and carbon. Everything you leak is either a catastrophe of novelty or a catastrophe of commonness.

**Second: you are a repetition.** You are made of copies of copies. Your heart beats the same beat a hundred thousand times a day. Your gait is one step, reiterated to the horizon. Your water has been through you ten thousand times. By the physics of that place you are not a visitor, you are a *second thing* — soft at the outline, the kind of object the black lichen is cultivated to eat. The locals will not greet you. They will yield the path, not speak, and let you pass, because that is the etiquette for copies and it is the correct etiquette for you.

Everything below follows from those two facts.

---

## Body work: begin three months out

**Acquire a flaw and make it real.** Not a costume. A limp you have practised until your hip has actually remodelled. A stammer you no longer have to remember to perform. A knotted cord at the wrist that you have retied so often the knot has gone hard and black. Mismatched sleeves. A chipped tooth — get a cap made with a chip in it, something your tongue can find in the dark.

The dark matters. At four and a half thousand metres, half-asleep, with a headache, you will not be able to reason your way to knowing whether you are yourself. You will only be able to *feel* it. Choose a flaw you can verify with your tongue and your fingertips, in a tent, at three in the morning, when you are stupid. Then verify it every time you wake, and at noon, and after any moment of déjà vu.

**Get it certified and recorded on arrival.** This is the single most important piece of bureaucracy in your life. Manufactured irregularity on the Sill is not decoration; it is registration. An unregistered flaw is just a quirk. A recorded one is what stops you being confused with your copies.

**Train out of your habits.** Spend a month at home doing nothing twice. Different route to the shop every day. Different hour for every meal. Different chair. No crossing your legs the same way. No neck-rubbing, no hair-twirling, no tapping, no sighing. Humans leak repetition the way a stove leaks heat, and you will not notice you are doing it until the plain notices for you.

**Train into speech.** One new-made sentence per encounter, never a word twice in a day. Practise by keeping a notebook and forbidding yourself any word you have already written that day. It is agonising and it is the correct training. You will get slow. You will get strange. Good.

**Learn to stop laughing.** Laughter is the worst sound a human makes: loud, clean, and iterated. An echo on the Sill is not a report of a sound, it is a second sound, and a clean one acquires the rest of the event — the clapper, the hand, the arm. A laugh will give you a crowd of yourself. So will a cough, and the dry cold air up there will give you a cough. Bring suppressants. Bring a wool muffler you can bury your face in. Sneeze into your own coat with your eyes shut and your jaw locked. Do not laugh on the Sill. If something is funny, hold it until you are alone and downwind and muffled, and then let it out once.

**Acclimatise properly.** Weeks, not days. The sky there has violet under the blue; that is thin air. Hypoxia takes exactly the faculties you need — judgement, self-monitoring, the ability to notice that your outline has gone soft. Consider prophylaxis, and register the dosing schedule in the Index at your first hearth so that it is canonical rather than loose. Indexed repetition is safe. That is what the Index is *for*.

## The one instrument

**Bring a balance.** A good one, hand-span sized, with a set of weights that are all slightly different from each other.

It does three jobs and there is no substitute for any of them:

1. **It tests water.** Real water is heavier. Buy a gauged cup at a hearth — a canonical, indexed vessel, exactly to gauge — and learn the honest weight of a cupful from the people who live there. Then weigh every source, every time, forever.
2. **It tests you.** Weigh yourself at dawn. The Same does not hurt you; it *thins* you. Loss of mass is the earliest sign that the plain has begun to reissue you instead of carrying you.
3. **It tests your drinking.** Thin water wets, fills a cup, ripples correctly, and does not quench. You can drink a litre and die of thirst with a full belly, and every ordinary field test will lie to you: it will look like water, it will make you piss clear, it will soak your tongue. So: weigh in, drink a measured cup, weigh out. If you did not gain the weight, it was not water, and you are now further behind than you were an hour ago.

Also bring a thermometer and a small vial. Honest water freezes at the honest temperature. Do the freeze test at dawn on anything you have not weighed. Both tests, always, on anything from a cistern older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother.

## Water

Assume nothing is honest until proven. Fog and the first pass of a rain-road are the safest; water that has been *through* things — through pans, through rot, through a thousand years of topping and drawing — is where the thinness accumulates.

**Bring your own fog-net.** Hand-woven, deliberately irregular mesh, with your own posts or clamps. Fog comes up out of the green places in columns at dawn; if you are near a bank you can harvest first-arrival water yourself, which is both the safest water and the most morally clean. Do not drink from anyone's net or gutter or cistern without asking. Those nets are a crop. The cisterns are inheritances. Water is the law here, and the people were designed with a sense of grievance.

**Map the rain-roads before you go.** They are half a kilometre wide, they travel fixed routes in single file, they have always travelled those routes, and they are mapped and planted along. Between them: months of nothing. Your itinerary is not a line across a plain, it is a set of appointments with weather that keeps its appointments. Get the route tables at your first hearth and copy them out once, in your own hand.

**Do not cross a pan at night or at dawn.** Three centimetres deep, forty kilometres across, and it freezes with a sound like a plate set down in the next room. You will go through the ice, and wet at that altitude in that cold is a straightforward death. Also: pans are legally property, and they move a few metres a year. You can camp on dry gravel and wake up trespassing on a lake that has arrived to claim its own bed. Ask about the drift.

## Kit: the rule is *nothing that clicks*

Go through your pack on a table and operate every object with your eyes shut. Anything that ticks, beeps, ratchets, snaps, crinkles, or zips stays home.

- **No zipper.** A zipper is several hundred identical clicks in a row. Use toggles, ties, buttons.
- **No hook-and-loop.** No crinkly ripstop. Heavy damped wool and waxed canvas, felt-lined tins.
- **No quartz watch.** A crystal oscillating identically thirty-two thousand times a second is the purest repetition you could possibly carry. Time by the count-bells — the soft wooden *tock* that comes across ten kilometres of gravel — and by a shadow-staff.
- **No pinging beacon.** A hand mirror instead. Irregular flashes, under your control.
- **Felt your boots.** Thick felt soles, wool wraps, silent. Your footfall is already the most-repeated event on the plain; do not give it a hard edge.
- **Wool over the chest.** Your heartbeat is a drum that never varies. Muffle it. Do not let anyone put a stethoscope to you; they will hear the thing they have no word for.
- **No printed matter.** A page of print is ten thousand identical glyphs — a Same-object bound in cloth. Blank notebook, your own hand, no word twice on a page.
- **No coins, no identical spare anything.** Not screws, not pegs, not buttons. Every spare slightly wrong.
- **No tent stakes.** There is nothing to drive them into; it is gravel to the horizon and pavement under that. And do *not* anchor with bags of gravel — that is a bag of the one pebble, sameness by the kilo. Anchor with your own heterogeneous junk: a boot, a tin, a knotted rope, the ugliest heap you can assemble. There is a reason the cairns are ugly.
- **Low, heavy, damped shelter.** The wind has four thousand kilometres of run-up and there is nothing taller than a net-post to break it. A flapping fly is both a noise crime and a repetition engine.

## Quarantine, or: do not be the first plague

You cannot be sterile. Accept that and manage it.

Full coverage. Sealed cuffs, hairnet, beard cover, boots that do not shed. Repack all food out of crinkly commercial packaging into cloth and waxed canvas before you leave. Everything you eat must be cooked, processed, and non-viable: no seeds, no raw grain, no nuts that could sprout, nothing with an insect in its history.

**Pack out all waste. All of it.** Faeces, urine, hair, nail clippings, food scraps, the water you washed in. You are a nitrogen bomb and a seed bank and a fly hatch in a world that has never had any of the three.

**The pollen problem is the one nobody thinks of.** The people are the moss's hands. They exist because there are no pollinators, and the entire continental economy is a trade in *difference*: thousands of kinds, each in its own pocket, each kept scarce on purpose, because a species held in too many places gets worn away by its own repetition. If you walk from bank to bank with pollen in your cuffs you will make a rare thing common, and commonness there is fatal. You will have killed a species by helping it.

So: change your outer layer between banks. Brush down. Never carry seed or cutting without a commission. Never kneel in the moss, sit on it, or sleep in it.

**And do not sleep in the moss.** The rot runs at thirty degrees and the fungi do all the digesting in a world with no worms. It is warm and it is inviting and it is a digestive organ. You would be a nutrient with a fungal infection. Sleep on the pavement, upwind, on a mat, alone.

Never eat moss. Never eat where you can be seen. There is no cuisine here, nothing has ever been eaten, and the moss is where the people are grown. Bring every calorie: cold-soak meals, no stove, no fuel ethics, no smell, no crumbs. Forty *different* meals, not forty of one. Variety is not comfort here, it is a safety requirement.

## Arrival

Go to the nearest hearth before you go anywhere else, and do the paperwork.

Get your flaw certified and recorded. Get yourself entered in the Index — gait, pulse, habits, dosing schedule, the hours you keep. This is counterintuitive and it is the key to surviving the place: **repetition is not the enemy, unaccounted repetition is.** The potters throw ten thousand identical bowls exactly to gauge and the city stands on it. Canonical forms are a containment system. What kills you is the loose event, the shout in the wrong place, the habit nobody has filed. So file yourself. Be a canonical form with a certified flaw in it, and you can be a body up there for a while.

Then the one wrong bowl at the end of the firing — the thumb-dent, the lip pulled off-true, uncatalogued, carried up the street to be looked at. That is what you should aim to be. Not the ten thousand. The one.

**Do not cling to your name.** Names are spent when used and must be replaced. You will be named and the name will be gone and you will be named again. Answer to anything. Carry your flaw certificate as your identity and show the knot when asked who you are. Do not say your own name twice. Do not introduce yourself twice.

**Gifts:** one-of-a-kind, hand-made, flawed. A freehand drawing. A page of your own handwriting. A knot of your hair — they use that in the cairns, it is a honoured material. Never a bag of anything identical. If you bring a stone from your own world, understand what you are handing them: a child who finds a pebble that is different brings the whole district out, and they pass it hand to hand in a silence you could hear the frost in, and then they put it back exactly where it lay. Your stone lay nowhere here. Expect that to be a problem, and expect it to be a serious one.

## Moving

Hearths are within nine days of each other because that is the leash on the people. Your leash is different and shorter: honest water, calories, and cold. Everything is carried by hand, so you will pull your own cart — wide wooden wheels wrapped in felt, resin on the axles, running silent for weeks over a floor. It is genuinely easy going. That is the trap again.

Plan every day so that **noon finds you standing on open pavement, holding your flaw.** Not asleep, not in a tent, not mid-step, not on a pan. The noon-hold is when shadows are shortest and things are least distinctly themselves, and it is when you are most available to be confused with your copies. Stop. Stand. Put your thumb on the knot and your tongue on the chip. Then go on.

**Camp on ground that has been kicked out of true.** The frost heaves the plain into perfect hexagons every winter and gangs walk the pattern all spring spoiling it. If you wake and the gravel around you has tiled itself into hexagons overnight, pack and leave and do not wait for the light.

## Copies, and the Same

You will meet yourself. Softer at the outline. Etiquette: yield the path, do not speak, let it pass. It will wear out within a season and go down into the gravel with a sound like sugar poured from a bag.

It will be thirsty. It will not be able to be helped. Do not give it your water; you will not be able to spare it and it will not be able to use it. Do not touch it. Do not follow it. **Do not let it speak with your voice** — if you hear your own voice coming toward you along the Kerb, stop, hold your flaw, and wait until it has gone by.

Keep away from the lichen pots at hearth margins. That black lichen eats sameness, and you are sameness with a pulse. If it starts colonising your bootlaces or the dressing on your hand, you are being read as a second thing, and you should go and stand in front of a surveyor and let them decide what to do about you. Comply if they paint you.

**The Same** is districts where repetition has closed over: every stone the same stone, every hour the same hour, no flaw anywhere. You come out having already been there, twice, and thinner. It is marked by cairns of deliberately unlike objects — broken pots, mismatched boots, a copper spoon, a knot of hair, the ugliest heap that can be assembled — rebuilt weekly by surveyors who are the closest thing there to soldiers.

Learn the tells, because the cairns can be a week stale:

- Your footfalls stop sounding like *steps* and start sounding like *one step*.
- An echo comes back clean.
- The horizon loses its thumb-smudge and goes hard.
- The count-bell arrives as a bell instead of a tock.
- The hexagons are intact.
- Nothing anywhere is chipped.

Any one of these: turn around. Do not photograph it, do not investigate, do not finish the day's leg. And understand that you may already have been through it twice on the way in.

## Duration, and coming home

Go for a season. Not a year. Track your mass every dawn, and have a different hand draw your portrait each week so that there is a record of your outline made by someone who is not you. Watch for the symptoms: conversations you remember having but did not have; your handwriting matching yesterday's exactly; your footprints already there; food tasting like nothing; water not quenching; the feeling of having already said the sentence you are about to say.

Do not go looking for the rim. It is a year's walk and it is a lip rounded over like the edge of a bar of soap, so worn you cannot say where the world stops and the drop begins. There is no edge young enough to cut you. That is not a reassurance. It means the last floor of the world ends without telling you, and below is cloud, and under the cloud the earlier surfaces, all of them, worn off and fallen.

When you leave, leave nothing and take nothing common. Check your flaw at the threshold, and then check it again a week later, and a month later.

**Arrange a witness at home.** Someone who knows your limp, your chip, your knot, your mismatched sleeves, and who will look at you hard when you come back and say the true thing if your outline has gone soft. Because the worst outcome is not dying on the Sill. The worst outcome is coming home as the second thing, and wearing out within a season in a world with no lichen to hurry you along and no one who knows what the sound of sugar poured from a bag is supposed to mean.

And keep, somewhere in your pack, one small object that is genuinely unlike anything else you own — ugly, wrong, unrepeatable, uncatalogued. Not for trade. For noon.
