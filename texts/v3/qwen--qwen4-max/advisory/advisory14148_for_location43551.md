# Preparing for the Standing Water

*A briefing for the ordinary traveler. Read it twice. The second reading is the one that matters.*

---

## 0. First, understand what you are

Everything else in this document is downstream of one fact, so sit with it before you pack.

You are a bag of rot, salt, grit, and speed, arriving in a country that has none of these things.

There is no decay in that basin. No bacterium has learned the trick, no fungus has arrived. The dead stand at their doorways for centuries and become the doorways. The addresses are grandmothers. The water is blood-warm, sweet, mineral-rich, and utterly sterile — which is to say it is the finest culture medium any microbiologist has ever seen, forty kilometers wide, held at 29 to 34 degrees, and it has been waiting eleven thousand years for someone like you to fall into it.

You carry something on the order of a hundred trillion microorganisms. You shed half a gram of skin a day. Your sweat is salt. Your urine is salt and urea and nitrogen. Your breath is carbon dioxide and a fine aerosol of your throat flora. Every one of these is a substance that does not exist here, or exists only in pinches in stoppered jars in three houses in the Grown Quarter.

And there is already a wound in the eastern shallows that *softened*. Something is living in a grazer's side that is not the grazer, and the physicians have had to invent a word. Ask yourself, before you go, whether you are certain you would be the first traveler to arrive. Ask yourself whether you would be able to tell.

The rest of the hazards — the altitude, the breath-holds, the sealing doors, the meniscus — can kill you. This one can kill the country. Weight them accordingly.

**The minimum standard is a sealed body.** A full dry suit, hooded, gloved, with a closed-loop waste system and a scrubbed exhaust, or a hard-shell equivalent. Not a wetsuit. Not "I'll be careful." You will be in the water for hours a day in a place where a single dropped chisel is a public event heard by nine thousand people; you cannot be careful at that scale for that long. You will get tired, and you will spit, or bleed from a cut hand, or leak.

If a sealed suit is beyond your means or your discipline, do not go. Go to the rim instead. Stand at the saddle and look at the bulge and come home. It is genuinely worth doing, and it is the only version of this trip that is unambiguously good.

## 1. What your body will have to do

### The air is thin and the water is deep

The rim is above four thousand meters. Surface pressure is roughly six-tenths of an atmosphere. Your unacclimatized sea-level body will find its heart doing something stupid within minutes of stepping out at the saddle, and its lungs will not find anything useful to do at all.

Acclimatize properly. Not the two-day tourist version. Two to three weeks of staged ascent, and if you have access to a hypoxic tent, use it for a month beforehand. Diamox if your physician agrees. Know the signs of high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema, and know that the descent route from a city built on a plateau is *up*, to a saddle, in cold thin air, while wet.

### Your breath-hold is not your breath-hold

This is the part that kills confident swimmers.

At 0.6 atmospheres, the air in your lungs holds about 60% of the oxygen it holds at home. Your comfortable one-minute hold becomes a thirty-five-second hold. Add exertion, add warm water raising your metabolic rate, add the fact that you will be distracted by the most beautiful thing you have ever seen, and you have twenty useful seconds and a long swim back.

Train the hold, but train it *honestly*: no hyperventilation, ever, and understand that hyperventilation at altitude is a particularly efficient way to die with no warning at all. Hypoxic blackout does not announce itself. It arrives as a pleasant certainty that you are fine.

And here is the cruelty specific to this place: **nobody will hear you drown.** In a city where sound carries for kilometers, a human being going under makes no noise whatsoever. There are no bubbles, no thrashing that anyone can localize, no shout. Your emergency signal must be tactile. Which brings us to the cords.

### Learn the cords before you learn anything else

The city speaks by plucking taut lines — a bone pick, a pattern of damps and releases. This is the single most learnable thing about the culture and the single most important. It is a rhythm language, structurally close to telegraphy, and you can practice it at home with a rope, a partner, and a closed door.

Do it. Six weeks of thirty minutes a day will get you functional. Learn the distress pattern first, then *where am I*, then *I need air*, then the numbers, then please and thank you. Carry your own pick on a lanyard around your neck and a short clip-line you can attach to any district cord. Before you swim anywhere, learn where the cords are on that route and how to find them by touch in the dark.

Not holding an end is not privacy. It is deafness, muteness, and invisibility, and it is how visitors die within sight of forty people who would have helped.

### The water is warm and you cannot cool down

Twenty-nine degrees over the city, thirty-four at the mouths. That is bath temperature. Your body dumps heat poorly into water that warm and not at all into water at 34. Hard swimming in the deep seeps will cook you slowly, and you will not notice until you are confused — and confusion here means forgetting the fountain map.

Work in the cool shallows. Rest often. Do not follow anyone down toward the vents.

### The water is a mild lye

"Faintly slick between the fingers" is what alkaline, carbonate-supersaturated water feels like: it is beginning to saponify the oils on your skin. Expect stinging eyes, stripped skin, brittle hair, and dermatitis after a week. Rinse with your own imported water after every session. Protect your eyes. Do not swallow it, and above all do not drink it habitually — the deep-swimmers go gritty in middle age and half-marble by seventy, and that is from the *inside*. You do not have their metabolism and you do not have their seventy years.

Near the vents it tastes of copper because there is copper in it. Do not drink there either, but note that the copper is probably why the vents are the cleanest water in the basin.

### Your eyes are wrong for this

Underwater, the human cornea loses almost all its refractive power. Without a mask you are catastrophically farsighted — the locals' famous three-hundred-meter visibility is, to your naked eye, a soft green blur. Bring a low-volume mask and equalize it.

Then discover the second problem: with a mask, you can see three hundred meters, and there are **no shadows**. Nothing in this city is lit from one direction. Everything is lit from above by a silver fog-lid and from below by ember seams, so nothing casts, nothing has a shaded side, and your visual system loses the shading cues it uses to read shape and distance. The far towers of the Weir will look like a model held at arm's length. They are forty minutes away. People will let you get this wrong, mildly and unhurriedly, and it is genuinely funny to them, and you should let it be funny.

Do not navigate by eye. Navigate by cord, by counted strokes, and by guide.

### Your ears are wrong for this too

Sound moves four and a half times faster in water. The timing difference between your two ears collapses, and you lose the ability to tell where anything came from. Every noise will seem to originate inside your skull. This is disorienting for about three days and then becomes ordinary.

Underneath it there is a note too low to hear, steady as an organ pedal, that you will feel in your sternum. It does not stop. It has not stopped in eleven thousand years. Some people sleep through it. Some people cannot sleep at all in the city, and cannot sleep at the rim either, because at the rim there is nothing underneath them. Assume you will be in the second group and plan for short nights.

### Pressure

The mouths are at a hundred and fifty meters. That is sixteen atmospheres. You are not going there. Do not let anyone take you there, do not accept it as a dare, and do not accept it as an honor.

Even at modest depths, remember you are diving *at altitude*: surfacing from ten meters into 0.6 atmospheres is a larger pressure ratio than the same dive at sea level, and standard tables will lie to you. Use altitude-corrected tables, stay shallow, stay conservative, and never ascend to the rim saddles after a day of diving. The rim is a further four-thousand-meter ascent and your tissues do not care that it is only a walk.

## 2. The discipline

Three habits, drilled until they are below thought.

**Be silent.** There are no hammers here. The entire built mass of this city was made without a bang; they split stone by wetting a dry wooden wedge and waiting a day and a night for a knock you can hear three kilometers away, and that knock is considered *polite*. Your ordinary behavior — setting a tool down, kicking a railing, clearing a mask, coughing — is, at their scale, vandalism.

Felt-wrap every hard object. No metal on stone, ever. No buckles that clack. Move your hands like you are moving them through someone's sleeping house.

**Be slow.** Not respectful-slow. Physically slow. The elders are half marble and move accordingly; the whole tempo of the place is set by bodies that are turning into architecture. A wedge takes a day and a night. A tower takes three generations. If you are hurrying, you are the only thing hurrying, and everyone can hear it.

**Be tethered.** The birth-current is gentle and constant and everything buoyant goes up forever. With full lungs in fresh water you are close to neutral; with a suit on you are positively buoyant; and above you is a kilometers-wide raft of pressed plant matter, and above that a fog lid, and beyond the lid the fog and the rim and four thousand meters of nothing. A tool let go is gone out of the world. So are you.

Children are tethered. You are less competent than a child here.

Two rules about the tether. First: **someone tends it**, and that someone is a named person who has agreed, not a hopeful arrangement. Second: **it must be worked**. A rope left slack for a season is a rod. For three years, a column. Your lifeline will quietly become a structural member of a building nobody designed, and then one day it will not come free, and you will either cut it — losing your lifeline — or discover that you have moored yourself to the city permanently. Move it daily. Re-rig it weekly. Replace it. Ask your sponsor about this on the first day; it is the kind of thing they will not think to mention because it is obvious to them.

And never, under any circumstances, moor to a doorpost. The doorpost may be someone's great-grandfather.

## 3. Kit

**Bring:**

- A sealed suit with closed-loop waste and scrubbed exhaust. Two, if you can. Redundancy is not luxury here.
- A low-volume mask, spare, and a soft snorkel you will rarely use.
- A silent, bubble-free breathing source if you must have one — a closed-circuit rebreather is the only thing culturally tolerable, and even then, understand that its exhaust is a foreign gas and its failure mode is a bubble plume that will rise into the ceiling and be noticed. My honest recommendation is to bring no gas at all and live on the fountain network.
- **A CO₂ and O₂ meter.** Small, plastic, silent. This is the most valuable single object you can carry. More on this below.
- Your own drinking water. More than you think. Sealed.
- Your own food, and containers to carry the waste out.
- Soft-soled footwear and gloves. The rock here is knapped and glassy and every stone is the shape it was born; walking among it cuts your hands. Your blood is a nutrient bloom in a sterile sea. Cover up.
- Red, low-lumen, diffuse light. Their world has no point sources and no shadows and their eyes are adapted to a room an hour after sunset. A white torch is a flashbang. Ask before you use even the red one.
- Large-print, high-contrast, tactile labels on everything. You cannot read a mark on a tool in that light. Nobody can. That is not a defect in your eyes.
- A bone pick and clip-line.
- A written exit plan and someone at home who knows it.

**Do not bring, under any circumstances:**

- **Sand. Soil. Clay. Salt. Any granular mineral whatsoever.** Dust is the most valuable substance in this country — the only material that has ever been *removed* from anything, swept up in pinches and kept in stoppered jars, and in three houses in the Grown Quarter people have just begun to wet it and shape it and see whether it will hold. They are four thousand years of patience away from pottery. A pocketful of beach sand is not a gift. It is an unasked-for industrial revolution handed to people whose entire cosmology treats the arrival of sediment as the end of the world and the beginning of the next one. Check your boots. Check your seams. Check your bag.
- **Metal tools.** There are no ore bodies here, because ore is what happens when water sorts a country's rubble, and there is no rubble. A steel knife is a singular artifact. It will also, if you drop it, ring the entire valley and identify you forever.
- **Fire.** Not a lighter, not a match, not a candle. The lungs are domes of plant-exhaled gas — potentially oxygen-enriched — sealed with resin, fed by jointed trunk-sections running along the undersides of every bridge. An oxygen-rich fire in a stone cup will run along that resin. You could burn the city's breathing apparatus in an afternoon, and a blocked line kills a district over a month, quietly, in its sleep. There is no firefighting culture here because there has never been anything to burn.
- Anything you cannot carry out, including yourself if it comes to that.
- Pets. Seeds. Live cultures. Sourdough starter. Yogurt. Probiotics. Anything with a fungus in it. There is no fungus here. Be very clear with yourself about what that means.

## 4. Arrival

Come in over a saddle, slowly, over days. Do not descend straight into the water; the thermal shock of 34-degree water after cold thin air is described, accurately, as being taken into someone's mouth, and it is a lot to hand a body that is already struggling for oxygen.

Meet your sponsor at the rim. You need one: a person who will hold your cord, tend your tether, walk you into a lung, and vouch for you. Arrange it in advance through whatever channel your crossing offers, and if you cannot arrange it, do not go. There is no inn. There is no address system you can use — the addresses are grandmothers, and you do not know any grandmothers.

Enter the water at a shallow terrace, tethered, masked, with the fountain map drilled into memory. Swim your first hundred meters slowly enough to be bored. Then stop and hang there and let it be what it is.

## 5. Daily life

### Breathing

The city lives in pockets: inverted stone cups fed by lungwood pipes, warm, thin, humid, smelling of resin and crushed leaf and the sourness of a room where many people have talked.

That sourness is carbon dioxide. **Take it literally.** The locals are barrel-chested, dark-blooded, slow-hearted; they tolerate a gas mix that will give you a headache, then a bad decision, then nothing at all. You have a meter. Use it every time you enter a lung, and every time you wake.

Never sleep in a private lung on your first nights. Sleep in a public one with verified through-flow, near a breath-fountain, with your meter audible and someone else present. A blocked line does not kill anyone quickly — it kills a district over a month, quietly, in its sleep. You are not adapted enough to be a month-long statistic; you are a one-night statistic.

Learn the fountain map before you learn the street map. Public breath-fountains stand at the crossings: small bubble-domes on posts where you put your head up and take four breaths and go on. Four breaths. Not a conversation. Count them, know the spacing, and never swim a route whose fountain spacing you have not personally verified. The far towers of the Weir look close enough to touch. They are forty minutes away.

### Eating and drinking

Import everything. Lamp-lettuce is edible and bitter and you may accept a little as a courtesy, but understand that you are drinking the geology with it. There are six grazers in the entire basin; each has a name, a district, and a sort of citizenship, and children are taken to see them the way children elsewhere are taken to a cathedral. If someone offers you grazer meat, it is either an extraordinary honor or a test, and either way the correct answer is a long, slow, grateful refusal.

There is no fermentation here. No bread, no cheese, no beer, no vinegar, nothing that has been transformed by anything alive. Their food is fresh, mineral, bitter, and strange, and it will not sustain you well. Bring calories. Carry out every scrap and every gram of waste, including the wrappers, including the water you rinsed your mouth with.

### Moving

Streets exist because they are swum. A route stays open by traffic; a district that falls out of use does not ruin, it solidifies, and after two hundred years it is a variation in the grain of the rock.

This means two things for you. First, **do not enter an unused district.** It looks like a neighborhood. It is a fossil, and its doorways are ghosts of pockets in a loaf, and some of those pockets still hold air that has been sitting there since before your grandmother was born. Second, if you are given a route, swim it, twice a day, the way the woman swims hers so it will still be a street next year. Your passage is not a metaphor here. It is maintenance. It is the polite thing to do.

Doors seal if they are not worked. Hinges freeze. Work every door you use, every day, and tell your sponsor which doors those are so that someone works them if you are ill.

## 6. The specific ways to die

Write these on the inside of your wrist.

1. **Blackout on a long swim.** Silent, unwitnessed, cheerful until it happens. Mitigation: cords, fountains, humility about distance.
2. **Bad air in a lung, overnight.** Mitigation: the meter, always.
3. **Rising.** Untethered, buoyant, into the ceiling-midden, into the fog, out of the world. Mitigation: the tether and the person who tends it.
4. **Sealing.** A door that closes behind you in a district nobody swims. Mitigation: never go alone into quiet places.
5. **Stoning.** Not fast, but real: anything you leave still for a season becomes furniture, and anything you leave still for a lifetime becomes load-bearing. This includes your gear, your line, and, if you are careless about what you drink, your joints.
6. **The lip.** Do not swim out to the meniscus. It is a smooth convex edge of water leaning over four thousand meters of nothing, quivering like the surface of a full cup when someone walks past the table. It does not spill. It has not learned to fall. You have. Also: at the rim the water sometimes *walks* — a slow standing tongue exploring a ridge, sometimes retracting over a century, sometimes finding a valley. A finger of sea that was not there last generation may be there now, unmapped and unlit and unfountained. Do not explore it.
7. **The flood.** Nine hundred years of marks on posts, a hand's breadth per generation, and lip-watchers who know the curve is steepening. The probability during your visit is very small. The consequence is four hundred cubic kilometers going down a mountain in an afternoon. Know which peaks are peaks and which saddles are saddles. Do not base yourself in Low Rungs — it is ancient, coarse, dense, half-solidified, and the worst place in the city to try to leave in a hurry. Sleep high, sleep near a saddle, and have a plan you have physically walked.
8. **The softening.** Stay out of the eastern shallows. Do not touch the wounded grazer. Do not touch the biter. Do not offer the physicians your opinions about microbiology unless you have thought very hard about what you would be handing them, and do not let them take samples of you unless you have thought harder still.

## 7. Manners

Greet the dead at the doorway before you greet the living. Ask your sponsor how. Do not touch them, do not lean on them, do not tidy them, do not move them, and do not — under any circumstances — attempt to help. They are not neglected. They are the deed.

Acknowledge the elders in their niches, the ones who are consulted and then are not consulted and then are architecture. Do not rush them. Do not offer them medicine. You will be tempted; you will have ideas about chelation; keep them.

Do not touch the dust jars. Do not ask to buy one. Do not offer yours.

When you must be loud, go to a public lung and be loud there. Children are sent to the lung to shout, as a treat. A visitor who goes to the lung to shout is being charmingly childish. A visitor who shouts in the street has committed the only obscenity this culture has.

Accept being laughed at about distances. It is affectionate. It is the local welcome.

If the wick-birds come down through the fog while you are there — three or four pairs, once a year, to drink and go away again — stop everything. It is a holiday. You are on holiday too.

---

## 8. Leaving

Take everything. Every wrapper, every scrap of felt, every centimeter of cord, every drop of your rinse water, and your waste, sealed.

Then quarantine yourself in the other direction. Curd-moss is an animal that turns any still surface into stone, patiently, from the inside out, and it has never met a world with pipes, ships, hulls, or lungs. It is arguably the most dangerous organism you could carry out of any country in the multiverse. Do not bring a souvenir. Do not bring a cutting. Do not bring a liter of the water to see whether it still remembers how to stand.

Burn your soft kit, or leave it sealed forever, and be honest with whoever debriefs you about the eastern shallows and the wound and the new word the physicians have made.

## 9. What it will actually be like

Here is what I think will happen to you, and it is not on the hazard list.

You will hang in warm green-grey water in a city that looks like fabric thought about very seriously for a long time — fluted, ribbed, every wall printed with the pattern of the basket it was born in — and there will be no shadows anywhere, so everything will look slightly remembered rather than seen. The far towers will be close and far at once. The growing tips of the plants will stitch the streets in cold blue-green. Below you the seams will glow the exact orange they have glowed for eleven thousand years. You will not be able to tell how deep anything is. You will not be able to tell where any sound comes from. There will be a note in your sternum that you will stop hearing on the third day and never stop feeling.

And you will slowly become aware that you are the only thing in this country that is going bad.

Your gut is working. Your skin is shedding. Your breath is sour. Somewhere in the eastern shallows a wound has softened and there are people whose entire adult life is the study of it, who are not sure whether they are watching a disaster or a birth, and who suspect the two are the same thing seen from different ends. You will hang in the warm sweet sterile water inside your sealed suit and you will feel, for the first time in your life, obscene — not dirty, exactly, but *fast*. You are a process, in a place that has only ever had additions.

That feeling is the correct response and you should not talk yourself out of it. It is the thing that will keep you from spitting.

And then, on some evening, you will swim your street for the second time that day, because a street is a thing that exists because it is swum, and at the doorway your sponsor's grandmother will stand with her hands folded and a new skin of stone on her shoulders, and under your bare heel the step will lose a hair's breadth of itself — the only weather this country has — and you will understand that in a world where nothing wears away, nothing you do wears away either. Not the kindnesses, which they will absorb and forget the way they absorb everything, patiently, by adding to it. And not the mistakes.

Be careful. Be slow. Be tethered. Hold an end of something.

Go for a week. Come home.
