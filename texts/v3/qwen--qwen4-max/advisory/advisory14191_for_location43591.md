# Preparing for the Curing Flats

## 1. Decide first whether you are the kind of person who should go

This is not a destination. There is nothing to see in the ordinary sense — two hundred metres of fog, black water, white fur, and a few cold lights. There is no one to meet. There is no audience, no record, no memory; the place has deliberately arranged itself to require none of those things. If you carve your name into a young wall it will either fill or be kept, and either outcome means exactly as much as the other.

What you will get is a few hours of standing inside an enormous, patient, indifferent process that is approximately on schedule and does not need you. Some travelers find this the most restful thing they have ever experienced. Others come back with a hollowed-out feeling they cannot describe and do not shake for a year.

Be honest about which you are before you pack.

---

## 2. The governing hazard: every danger here announces itself by disabling the sense that would detect it

Read the first line of the survey again. *You hear it an hour before you see anything, and by then you have stopped being able to hear it, which is the same as arriving.*

That is not atmosphere. That is a warning label, and it repeats at every scale:

- The infrasound stops being audible while continuing to load your chest, your viscera, and your eyes.
- The water is liquid at −24 °C. Your body's whole cold-reflex vocabulary — *it's wet, it's running, it can't be that cold* — is wrong here, and you will not feel corrected.
- The fog is stable, the air is particulate-clean, and there is no wind. Sulphide and carbon dioxide are heavier than air and will pool in the low lanes and road-cuts, which are exactly the quiet, sheltered, attractive places you will want to stop in. Sulphide smells overwhelming for about ninety seconds and then smells like nothing at all.
- The rime grows a hand's length outward from every surface. The white shape you are looking at is not the shape of the thing. The edge of the escarpment is somewhere under a feather cornice, and you cannot see where.
- The young wall accepts your arm without resistance and without wetting you. It feels like nothing. It feels like permission.

So: **do not navigate this place by how you feel.** Every decision to stop, rest, continue, or turn back must be made against a number on an instrument, set in advance, before you enter the field. Write your turn-around criteria on your wrist while you are still warm and quiet and stupid with confidence, and obey that person later when you are none of those things.

---

## 3. Sound

Understand what you are protecting against, because the intuitive answer is wrong.

**The hiss** — billions of stridulating bodies, high-frequency, ordinary sound. This is what earplugs and muffs are for. Use both. It is genuinely loud and it will genuinely deafen you over a long sortie.

**The fundamental** — twenty-two or twenty-three cycles, felt behind the sternum. Plugs do essentially nothing. It comes up through your boots, through your pelvis, through the fluid in your chest, and it arrives at your inner ear by bone whether your ear canals are sealed or not. At high level, that band smears vision (your eyes are a resonant mass on a spring), disturbs balance, provokes nausea, and produces in a great many people a specific unearned emotion: dread, presence, the sense of standing near something enormous that has noticed you.

That emotion is your vestibular system being shaken. It is not information about the flats. It is also not *not* information about the flats. Do not make decisions inside it.

Practical consequences:

- **Screen yourself out** if you have any cardiac arrhythmia, seizure history, vestibular disorder, inner-ear surgery, aneurysm, or are pregnant. This is not caution theatre.
- **Do not take antiemetics.** The nausea is your dosimeter. Suppressing it is like unplugging a smoke alarm because the noise is annoying.
- **Your only real protection is geometry.** The nodes are quiet. The antinodes are not. Protection here means *knowing where the cancellation is and staying inside it*, not wearing something.
- **Habituation is not adaptation.** When the sound "becomes a substance you are standing inside," your perception has given up, not your body. Log exposure hours. Cap them. Suggested first visit: four hours in-field, total, and treat that as generous.
- **Expect beats.** The plateau is permanently out of phase with itself; near towers lead far towers by seconds, districts change key independently. Where two districts overlap you will get slow amplitude throb, a fraction of a cycle per second. This is worse than steady roar. It entrains breathing and it makes people cry without knowing why. Know that it is a beat frequency and not a summons.

**Sleep.** Do not try to sleep on the flats. You cannot decouple from ground-borne 22 Hz by lying on it — you couple *better*. A suspended hammock would isolate you beautifully, and there is nowhere to hang one that is not an obstruction, a nucleation site, or a road. The Filled Quarter is the only sane berth: solid, dead, elevated above the gas-pooling lanes, no keepers to disturb, no roads to close. It is also a roof over four hundred years of filled void with the chord going through it uninterrupted. You will be lying on a bell. Two nights maximum, and go home before you start seeing things in the fog that are the right size to be people.

## 4. Where to put your body

**Node roads** are a metre and a half wide. That is the whole tolerance. They are held open by living animals singing against the city, and they are not streets — the survey is explicit that they are apertures. They do not connect places you want to go. They connect places the geometry needs. Do not plan a route as though this were a road network; plan a there-and-back along one lane and be content.

**Never trust a survey's road widths.** Closure runs a centimetre a year and then accelerates as the gap narrows. A chart forty years old can be a metre wrong. Check the gap physically, continuously, with your hands, and turn back the moment your shoulders are working.

**Never step out of a node to look at something.** Not for a photograph, not for a better angle. The antinode is where the mineral comes out of solution. You are a warm, wet, mineral-rich, hundred-watt object; in an antinode you are a nucleation site with opinions. Things will begin to grow on you.

**Do not touch walls.** Especially the young ones. The invitation is extraordinary and it is not an invitation; a wall under twelve years old is *not yet a decision*, and there are doorways in the low terraces that began as somebody's curiosity. You will not know whether your socket fills or keeps. Leave no casts of yourself in a structure that will be used once.

**Look up before you stop.** Most of the high city is ice being traded for stone from the inside, molecule by molecule, and *most* is not *all*. The tell is structural, not visual: if a span is too thin, too elegant, too cantilevered — if no mineral could hold that at that stage of curing — then it is still ice, and it is waiting for a dawn sixty years out or for a local detuning next week. Rule of thumb: **impossible geometry is unconverted geometry.** Never stand under anything beautiful.

**Gleaners.** A metre an hour, enormous, harvesting terrace faces by the thousand. It will not chase you and it does not want you, but it will strip the surface you are standing against, and its passage takes the keepers with it. A gleaner sighting is not a wildlife encounter. It is an evacuation notice for that section, on a timescale of hours. Leave the lane calmly and immediately.

**The escarpment.** Rope up, probe every step, trust no white surface. Note also that First Cut is a place where water *runs* — where the field's ask fails. Where the water is behaving ordinarily, you are outside the protection you have been relying on.

## 5. Air, and the fact that you are food

The fog is not fog. In the low lanes it is on the order of a hundred thousand rice-grain animals per cubic metre. At walking exertion you move perhaps fifty litres a minute — five thousand animals a minute into your face. They are not pathogens. They are particulate, they are allergenic, they are aragonite, and they die and fall as a fine calcareous snow that will load a filter faster than any dust you have ever met.

- Sealed goggles, not glasses. Full-face if you can tolerate it.
- Mechanical prefiltration you can clear with a gloved hand, plus fine filtration behind it. Carry more filters than you think reasonable, then double it. Expect to change them mid-sortie.
- **A gas monitor: O₂, H₂S, CO₂.** Wear one at chest height and carry a second on a cord to lower into any lane, cut, or depression before you enter it.
- **All alarms must be haptic or visual.** Nothing audible will reach you in the roar, and in a node road an audible alarm is a scream in a library — it is also the one place where your noise might actually matter to the keepers.

And then the part nobody thinks about until they are told:

**Your breath is pollution, and it is also lunch.** These animals graze the air's thin rain of dissolved organics. You exhale a plume of exactly that. You will walk with a visible comet-tail of drifters behind you, marking your position to everything on the shelf, and you will be locally enriching a system whose failure mode is *a bloom of something that fouls the keepers' organs*. That is how a district fills.

Scrub your exhalation. Carbon, closed-circuit, whatever your kit allows. At minimum: do not eat in the lanes, do not spit, do not urinate, do not drop a crumb, do not blow your nose. You are a sealed system or you are a contaminant. There is no third option.

Your heat is the same problem in another medium. A hundred watts in a −20 °C world makes you a bonfire, melts your own rime, and leaves a clear warm seam through the white wherever you go. You are trackable, and you are dangerous to touch. Keep out of the road-cuts. Do not crouch among the keepers to get a look at them.

## 6. Water, cold, and the curing of your possessions

You will be wading continuously in an inch of calcium-and-silica-saturated brine that does not freeze. Everything you own that touches it will begin, slowly, to become part of the city.

- Boots: plastic shells, closed-cell liners, no leather, no down anywhere in your system. Wet cold at −20 °C with continuous condensation is a vapour-management problem before it is an insulation problem. Vapour barrier liners, synthetic insulation, and accept that you will be damp from the inside by hour two.
- **No exposed zippers.** Brine creep cements them. Toggles, laces, buckles, and a scraper you use obsessively.
- Nothing metal that can ring. A resonant object in an antinode is a seed. A trekking pole laid across a road-cut can, in principle, start a wall. Carry it, don't plant it.
- **Nothing you own may rest on wet stone and stay there.** No cached packs, no dropped gear, no guideline left tied off overnight. A line lying in a node road is an obstruction and a substrate; it will be cemented, and then it will grow. If you use a line — and in two hundred metres of visibility on a featureless plain you should — it is tended, held clear, and retrieved entire. Cave-diving discipline, with the added rule that the cave is building itself around your equipment.
- Bring your own water. Do not drink the brine. Melted rime is condensate grown on mineral surfaces in a blizzard of dead animals; it is not the clean thing it looks like.
- Everything you bring out will weigh more than it did going in. Budget for it.

## 7. Light and touch — and the one unforgivable act

Hearing is a spent sense here. Everything important is said with light and with contact. This makes you, with your headlamp, an obscenity.

- **No white light. No blue light. No strobe. No flash. Nothing bright, ever, in a lane.**
- Dim, hooded, downward, long-wavelength, used only when stopped and only as long as needed. Assume you are being read.

Understand the specific catastrophe available to you: the blue photophore relay runs a kilometre in eleven seconds, body to body, hand to hand. The survey has cracked exactly two words of the contact language and renders them *still open*. If you stand in a lane and flash a blue LED at intervals because it seems friendly, you are injecting an unparseable signal into a four-thousand-year-old maintenance protocol whose entire content is the continued openness of the road you are standing in. You do not know the word for *closed*. You do not know what a lie looks like in a language made of touch and light. A tourist with a keyring light can, in eleven seconds, tell a kilometre of keepers something that makes them stop singing.

**The only unforgivable act on the Curing Flats is closing a road.** Everything else on this list is about your own safety. This one is about theirs, and about a deadline none of them know exists.

When you are touched — and you will be, they come into the quiet to touch — do not flinch and do not grab. Brief bare contact, seconds only, then warm your hand. Aragonite at ambient temperature will cold-burn you if you let it. You are choosing, each time, between being understood and being uninjured; make the choice deliberately and don't make it more than a few times an hour.

Do not attempt to say anything. You have two words and you are not certain of either.

## 8. Do not help

You will see a road narrowing and want to clear it. You will see an ice span you could shore up. You will see a gleaner and think about killing it. You will want to leave a marker, a cache, a note, a better instrument, a warning about the dawn.

Don't. The setters have been accelerating for four hundred years and are approximately on schedule. The keepers have held their roads since before your species had writing. The system requires no memory, no literacy, and no intention, and it has survived every catastrophe this plateau can produce except one, which is arriving in sixty years and which they are already, without knowing it, racing.

You are not smarter than that. You are a warm blob of organics with a torch. Your intervention is not improvement; it is a delay to something that gets exactly one attempt.

---

## 9. Quarantine, both directions, absolutely

**Inbound.** There is no green here. No chlorophyll, nothing that has ever needed the sun, no competitor for anything. This is the most invasible biosphere you will ever stand in. A single viable diatom on a boot tread, a smear of soil in a seam, an algal spore in a nostril, could end four thousand years of work in a season. Sterilise everything. New consumables, sealed until arrival, nothing porous that has been outdoors anywhere. Your gut flora is a consideration; plan your diet and your waste accordingly and pack out every gram.

**Outbound.** You are leaving a world where cold-bodied organisms precipitate aragonite out of saturated brine along acoustic pressure surfaces, and where the instructions for a self-assembling regional fabrication process are stored in the tuning of dead shells. Decontaminate fully. Take no specimens. Take no shell — a dead holder is a note, and removing it is theft from a chord. Take no calcareous snow; the setters sweep that and use it.

Think very hard before you carry out a recording of the chord. It is not data. It is the only surviving copy of a set of instructions nobody has found the author of, and you would be introducing it to worlds with limestone aquifers, saturated groundwater, and a great deal of standing water in pipes.

## 10. The sortie itself

Go with one partner. Single file in the lanes — two abreast is an obstruction. Your partner's job is not to watch the scenery; it is to watch your face for the slack, drifting look that means the fundamental has you, and to watch the gap.

You will not be able to talk. Agree on touch signals and use them. Agree on a turn-around time, not a destination. Turn at half your consumables, at half your warmth, at the first filter change you didn't expect, at the first gleaner, at the first shoulders-in-the-gap.

The approach is an hour of escalating pressure with nothing to look at. That hour is the most dangerous part of the visit, because your instruments are climbing and your perception is falling and there is no view to tell you anything is happening. Set the limit before the hour starts.

And then, if you have done all of this correctly, you will get the thing you came for: stand in a node road at the third hour — the survey's third hour, not yours; bring your own clock and expect the place to have no opinion about it — in a lane of silence a metre and a half wide, inside a roar, hearing your own blood, and watch the cold blue run past you hand to hand out toward the unfinished east.

It is saying *still open*. It has been saying it for four thousand years. It will go on saying it for sixty more, and then something will happen once, and there will be no one left who remembers what any of it was for, because there was never anyone who remembered.

Your entire job is to stand there without making anything closed, and then to leave.

---

## 11. Coming back

Arrange for someone to meet you. Quiet rooms will feel wrong for a while — thin, unheld, like a chord with the fundamental pulled out. You will hear twenty-two cycles in the silence of your own bedroom for weeks. You will feel the dread again in an elevator, in a stairwell, near a large fan, and it will mean nothing, and you will know it means nothing, and it will work anyway.

And you may find that you have acquired a very specific and inconvenient longing: to be somewhere that is being built, patiently, by something that does not know you exist and does not require you to matter.

That feeling is the real souvenir. It is also, of everything on this list, the one most likely to make you go back a second time and stay an hour too long.
