# Preparing for the Even

## First, correct your threat model

Nothing there will hurt you.

There is no predator, no weather, no rockfall, no avalanche, no flood, no fire, no disease vector, no unstable ground. The plain is the most structurally reliable surface in any world you will ever walk on: nothing there has shifted in an age and nothing there will shift again. The animals do not bite. They do not move. You can put your hand on a hundred billion faces and not one of them will turn.

Every single thing that can go wrong on the Even, you will bring up the scarp yourself.

You are the only warm thing, the only wet thing, the only loud thing, and the only thing with a *before* and an *after*. You are a small travelling process in a place that has finished with process, and the local hazard is not that the place will act on you — it is that you will act on the place, permanently, and cannot take it back. There is no undo. There is no erasure, no settling, no healing, no fading, no forgiveness-by-time. Time is not doing any work there. Whatever you do will still be doing it when the sun goes out.

So prepare the way you would prepare to enter an operating theatre, or an archive, or a temple — not the way you would prepare for a mountain. The mountain part is trivial. The rest is not.

Four contaminations to manage: **your water, your noise, your order, and your process.** Everything below is one of those four.

---

## Before you go: the body

**Get your lungs and blood right first.** The scarp takes your breath at a third of its promise and the plain never gives it back. There is no wind to stir the air, no cloud below you, no weather to mix anything. You will be walking in thin, perfectly still air over a reflective white surface under full midday light. Ascend in stages, sleep low, spend real days acclimatising below the rim, and talk to someone about acetazolamide before you go, not after. Do not plan to "push through" the first day. Nobody pushes through anything on the Even; pushing is how you make a noise you'll regret.

**Do not go up sick.** Not a cold, not a tickle, not a cough you've had for a week and are sure is nearly over. A cough on the Even is a building. It hangs at the height you made it, at full wet startled volume, in a sphere two paces wide, forever, and if you make it at a junction it is a *false signpost* that will misroute keepers for ten thousand years. They will find it, cairn it, knot it, and route around it, and your name will be a permanent inconvenience in a world with only nineteen plants and a few thousand junctions to spare. Quarantine yourself properly before ascent. Ten days dry, no symptoms, and then go.

**Sort your teeth, your sinuses, your gut, and your periods out in advance.** Anything that leaks is a catastrophe there. Get the dental work done. Treat the nosebleeds. If you menstruate, consider suppression, and plan containment regardless. If you have a stress stomach, fix it before you go, because there is no latrine on the Even and there cannot be one — a latrine is a wet place, and you already know what a wet place does.

**Train the gait.** This is the thing nobody expects. The roads are the gaps between the animals: a hand's width, sometimes two. Ten to twenty centimetres. You cannot walk that normally. You will be walking heel-to-toe in a single line, toes forward, arms out, for every step of every day, like a person on a kerb. Practise it. Practise it for an hour. Practise it tired. Bring narrow shoes — approach shoes or fell shoes, not wide boots — and break them in on a chalk line, not a trail. Expect hip and ankle and arch pain of a kind you have never had, and prepare for it, because a blister is a wet place too and a limp is a permanent record.

You *can* step on the herd. They break with a thumbnail; it isn't forbidden, only pointless. But a trail of broken animals and gneiss flour behind you is a scar that will outlast the species, and it looks like a route, and someone will follow it and dead-end at a stack. Learn to walk the seams. It is the whole craft.

**Train the not-screaming.** The keepers are trained out of screaming before they are trained into anything else, and you should be too. This sounds absurd until you understand that you will be wading chest-deep through a suspended blizzard of dry winged bodies that brush your face and then freeze where you pushed them, in total silence, with no distance cues, and that at some point you will walk into a stranger's ten-thousand-year-old cough at full volume six inches from your ear. You will yelp. Everyone yelps. Practise the替代: hand to mouth, exhale through the nose, count four. Do it until it's reflex. Drill it with someone who jumps out at you. It is not a joke; it is the single most useful hour of preparation you can do.

**Train the silence and the deprivation.** Spend a day below without speaking. Spend an hour in a float tank or an anechoic chamber if you can get one. The quiet on the Even is the kind where you hear your own blood, and the mind does not tolerate that gracefully — it manufactures company. Expect auditory hallucination. Expect the sense of someone walking just behind you. Expect to feel watched by a hundred billion eyeless faces, which is not paranoia, it is just accurate description. Know in advance that these things happen, so that when they happen you don't narrate them out loud.

## The kit

Everything you carry must be **dry, countable, and tethered.**

**Nothing you carry may touch the ground.** Not for a second, not "just here while I adjust my pack." Anything set down stays exactly where you set it, forever, and becomes a landmark, and possibly a false signpost, and certainly an inventory item. Lanyard every object to your body. Hat, goggles, bottle, notebook, knife, snack wrapper — all on leashes. Practise handling your gear one-handed on a line until dropping something is unthinkable.

**The trailing cord.** The most important object you will own. Several kilometres of thin, low-stretch, brightly coloured line on a smooth reel, with your own knot signature pre-tied at fixed intervals. It pays out behind you and it always, always points home. Understand what this is: it is not a map. It is an *intention prosthetic.* When you have gone flat and cannot remember whether you are outbound or inbound, the cord in your left hand still knows which way is back. You will not be able to reason your way to that answer. Keep the cord in your hand. Not clipped to your pack. In your hand, all day, like a rosary.

Ask before you lay it. The keepers run their own knot-cord network on stone posts, miles of it, and it is infrastructure and it is inventoried. Either hire into their system or get leave to run your own, and take it up again on the way out if they want it taken up.

**A personal tally cord.** Short, hand-readable, one knot per crossing, different knot types for different kinds of event. Read it by touch. This is the local technology and it is better than anything you'll bring, because it works when you can't see, can't think, and can't remember what thinking was for.

**A pre-numbered log.** Numbered *before you arrive*, page by page, line by line, so that the order exists outside you and cannot be revised. Pencil, not pen — ink dries by evaporation and evaporation is a process and there are none. Graphite, stylus, wax, slate. Write each thing down as it happens, in sequence, and never backfill. Never write "earlier that day." You will not be able to check it.

**A hand-wound mechanical watch.** It is a small private gradient, like your breath, and it will probably run. Trust it as a *counter*, not as an authority. Log every winding as an event. There is no day and no night on the Even — the sky is dusk and the ground is noon and neither will move — so your circadian system will come apart within about thirty hours. Sleep by the watch, eat by the watch, and treat the watch's numbers as ritual rather than truth.

**A sequence canary.** Choose, before you go, something that is *pure order*: a tune you can hum, a poem you know by heart, the order of a shuffled deck you've memorised, the birth order of your cousins. Something where the content is useless without the sequence. Check it at fixed intervals — every hundred knots, say. If you can still produce all the pieces but cannot produce the *order*, you are going flat, and you turn back immediately, without discussion with yourself. This is the only test that works from the inside, because going flat is otherwise invisible: judging whether you are confused requires exactly the faculty that is failing.

**Bone-conduction headphones.** The best thing modern kit can offer this place. Private sound, delivered through bone, leaving no furniture in the air. A metronome for your gait. A recording of your own voice reading your log back to you in order. Music, when the silence starts manufacturing companions. All of it invisible and inaudible to the plain and to the keepers, and none of it permanent. Do not bring a speaker. Do not bring anything that clicks out loud unless you have leave and a reason.

**A fan.** A simple folding hand fan. There has never been wind on the Even and there will never be, except the small private convection of your own breath — and whatever you make with your hand. Air will not move heat off your skin there. Sweat will not evaporate; either it sits on you wet forever, or you stop making it, and the keepers' bodies suggest the latter. You can overheat in a cellar. Move slowly, rest often in the lee of a knob, strip layers before you need to, and fan yourself. It is the only wind you will ever feel there and you will be the one who made it.

**Glacier goggles, a brimmed hat, and a veil.** High, cloudless, thin air over a plain of grey-white mineral scale with a bloom of salt on it and mica leaves and polished gneiss knobs: the glare is severe and there is no shade anywhere but behind the knobs and under the stacks. The veil matters for a different reason — it keeps the suspended flying things off your lips and eyelashes, so that you don't yelp. Wear it for the sake of your manners.

**A dust mask** for the quarries and the stacked districts. That powder is the flour of a mountain: quartz, gneiss, silica. Your lungs clear themselves slowly and badly up there. The keepers knap flint for a living and they do not rot, which is not the same as not being harmed.

**A camera,** if you like, understood correctly: numbered frames are a tally that pictures. It will not help you judge distance — nothing will — but it will hold your order for you.

**Do not bring:** a dog, a child, a companion who cannot be relied on not to shout, a whistle, a speaker, a stove, a firestarter, a scent, a wet wipe, a gel, a pen, a glue that cures by drying, or water in anything but a sealed rigid double-contained vessel. Do not bring water as a gift. Do not bring a plant.

## The first law: your water

You are a thimble of water with legs.

There is no water on the Even and no process that can remove any. In the eastern district there is a spill three hundred years old and still wet, and around it, for two hundred paces in every direction, every animal faces the same way. That is the only direction on the plain. That is what a drop does there.

So: **containment is not hygiene, it is the entire moral law.**

- Rigid bottles, sealed, in a padded case, double-contained. No open cups, no bladders with bite valves that weep, no pouring, no sharing a bottle mouth-to-mouth.
- Drink sitting down, in one designated place, over a cloth, with the bottle inside the cloth. Never drink while walking. Never drink standing at a junction.
- Ration by count, not by thirst: a mouthful every so-many knots. Your water ration is a clock and your clock is a ration; use each to check the other.
- Sweat, urine, saliva, blood, tears: all of it contained and carried down. Sealed waste containers, enough of them, no exceptions, no "it's only a little." Do not spit. Do not blow your nose into the air. Do not weep openly — the keepers wipe their tears and *account for them*, and you should ask how, and do the same.
- If you bleed, catch it. Hemostatic gauze, a nosebleed kit, a bag. Then descend.
- If you spill: stop. Do not walk it around. Do not try to soak it up with dust and make it worse. Contain what you can, mark the place from outside, and go get a keeper, and tell them everything, exactly, in order. Then do what they tell you, which may be nothing, because there may be nothing.

And know the shape of the worst outcome. It is not that you'll be punished. It is that two hundred paces of faces will turn toward you, and they will not turn back, and you will have added a second direction to a world that spent an age abolishing the first. Every keeper who navigates by that ring for the next ten thousand years will be navigating by your mistake.

Never touch a keeper's thimble. Never ask to see it opened. Never offer them yours. It is not a supply; it is a vow.

## Noise discipline

Sound is made and stays where it was made, in a sphere about two paces across, at full volume, forever. Learn the consequences until they are instincts:

**You cannot be heard.** Not at three paces. Not by anyone. There is no shouting for help on the Even. A whistle is useless. A scream is useless — worse than useless, it's masonry. If you break your ankle and your partner is four paces off, they will not know, and they will not know until they happen to walk into the sphere where you are still screaming.

Therefore: **never travel alone, and never travel untethered.** A short line between you and your partner, tugged in a code you agreed below and rehearsed until it's automatic. Stay in visual contact. Agree hand signals. Check each other by count, not by call. The buddy system there is not a courtesy; it is the whole of the emergency services.

**You cannot be silent either.** Your heart, your breath, your gut, your joints, your clothing — all of it is made, and all of it stays. Walking lays down an unbroken ribbon of your own sound behind you, pinned at the heights you made it. This is the great gift and the great humiliation of the place.

The gift: **you can always find your way back by ear, and you can always find a lost companion.** Search for their wake and walk it, listening. Their sounds are where they were. The trail ends where they stopped. Practise this below with a partner in a dark field before you rely on it up there.

Also: the suspended flying things part for you and stay parted, so your passage is *cast in negative space* at head height, in the shape of your body, forever. Look back and you can see the corridor you came through. Read a companion's wake and you can see whether they were upright, whether they were carrying something, whether they limped, whether they crawled. The air is a forensic record. Conduct yourself accordingly.

The humiliation: everything you say is furniture. So decide, before you go up, what you are willing to leave. Do not say a name you will grieve. Do not say anything in panic. Do not narrate. Do not talk to yourself at the fourth clack — that is precisely the example the keepers use for what a stranger is.

**Talking requires standing close and standing still.** To speak with someone you must share a sphere: shoulder to shoulder, cheek to cheek, in a space about two paces wide. And that conversation will be there, at that volume, in that air, when the last keeper's grandchildren are sitting facing their doors. Speak rarely, carefully, and only in places agreed for speaking. Never in a seam you will need to walk again. Every hut door is hung to creak on purpose; the hinge is the address. Respect the addresses.

**Do not add signposts without leave.** The clacks are a shared, named, ancient system — Fourth Clack, the Widow's Tap, Three Together. A random noise at a junction is not litter, it is *corruption of the map.* If you want a private audible trail, ask, and agree a signature that cannot be confused with the local vocabulary: a distinct rhythm, a struck tuning fork of an unused pitch, something no one else has ever left. And accept that it will be inventoried, and named, and outlive you.

**The ring of cairns in the north encloses a scream.** You will not hear it until you are inside it. There is no acoustic warning of anything, anywhere, ever. Treat every marked enclosure as loaded. If you step into it: leave, do not cry out, count your paces out, report it so it can be knotted. Do not try to identify whose it is. It is not a mystery placed there for you.

## Sequence discipline: the actual danger

This is what the keepers exist to manage, and it is what will get you if anything does.

Spend a season on the Even and you will still know everything that happened to you. You will simply have lost the thread of *when*. Your mother's death and your first meal will sit side by side, equally present, equally unplaced. They call it going flat, and it does not feel like forgetting. It feels like everything being equally true.

You cannot fight it with memory. Memory is the thing that fails. You fight it with **hand tools and external order**, which is why the keepers have knot-cords and tally posts and a wall a finger high and forty miles long that says nothing but *and then, and then, and then.*

Your protocol:

1. **Cord in hand, always.** It answers the one question you will not be able to ask: which way is home.
2. **One knot per event.** Crossing, turn, rest, drink, sleep, decision. Read by hand. Do not skip because it seems obvious.
3. **Write decisions before you execute them.** "At Fourth Clack I turn left." Then turn. Not afterwards. Afterwards will not exist.
4. **A single tactile bit for direction.** A ring on the left hand outbound, moved to the right hand when you turn back. Nothing cleverer. Clever fails first.
5. **Check the canary on a fixed count.** Order intact: continue. Order gone: turn back, immediately, no debate.
6. **Cross-witness.** You keep your partner's tally; they keep yours. Two independent records, compared at every rest. When they diverge, believe the cords, not either of you.
7. **Hard turn-back thresholds fixed below, in knots and steps, never in feelings.** "I'll turn when it feels like half" is not a plan; there is no *like* up there.
8. **Sleep is an instrument.** Protect it. Eye mask, watch-scheduled, at a hut or in a plant clearing. Sleep debt accelerates flattening, and flattening is the thing that kills you by walking you past your own turn.
9. **Footprints are not evidence.** The seam-dust holds every print ever made and nothing blurs them. You will find your own prints on a route you have never taken. Do not conclude that you have been here. Do not conclude that you haven't. Do not navigate by prints, ever, for any reason. The same applies to the tunnels in the air: they are a map of *routes*, not of *history*, because nothing there looks older than anything else.

**Hire a keeper.** I cannot say this strongly enough. Do not free-solo the Even on a first visit. A keeper is a walking sequence-prosthesis who knows every clack in nine districts, who was trained out of screaming before anything else, and who will *count you*. Pay them in what they actually want, which is not goods: it is counted labour. Walk a cord and read it back. Lay your stones on the Long Tally in order. Carry a tally between districts. A day of your walking, honestly counted, is a fair price and an honest gift.

## Navigation, or: why looking is useless

There is no atmospheric perspective. The herd at the horizon is as crisp as the herd at your boots, and nothing gets smaller in the way things should. You can walk half a day toward a plant and have no evidence from the eye alone that you moved.

Understand the geometry of the landmarks before you rely on them:

- **The knobs** — thirty-foot polished stumps of the old peaks — are the only vertical things. They give you shade, a lee, a bearing, and a hard object to sit against. They are also slowly becoming animals, and the keepers notch a post to record it.
- **The nineteen plant clearings** are the only bare ground on the plain, ten or twelve paces across, and they are visible for a very long way — which is exactly the problem, because you cannot tell whether the one you're looking at is an hour off or three days off. They are also, as far as I can work out, the only places you can lie down without lying on the herd. Ask about sleeping in them. Do not assume.
- **Huts are much closer together than plants.** A district is four herds wide, a day's walk is eleven; there is a hut every few hours and a plant every few days. Plan your nights around huts and your emergencies around huts. Do not plan an emergency around a plant.
- **Shadows do not move.** The sun is a fixed small lamp. A sundial there is a compass. Check the shadow bearing at the scarp, write it down, and use it — it is the only free orientation you get.
- **The wet place** is the true north of the plain, and the keepers use it when their cords fail. Ask what is permitted. Looking from outside the ring is likely fine. Approaching is not. Asking why is not.

Navigate by **cord, count, clack, and shadow.** Sweep your head slowly as you walk, the way you'd sweep a detector, because the sound-spheres are invisible and pinned at the height they were made; a clack struck at hand height won't find your ear if your ear is somewhere else. Learn the local convention for striking height and put your ear in that plane.

Avoid the stacked districts. They dead-end, they overhang, they are a low cave of bodies with no sky, and no one has ever seen the bottom layer's feet. Do not go looking. That question is not yours.

## Injury, illness, and the fact that you will not heal

Nothing there decays, because decay is a difference between what a thing is and what it is becoming, and the Even has no differences left in stock. The keepers' bodies came up from below and have been drying for generations. They do not rot. They sit facing the door and are considered on duty.

Assume, therefore, that **your body's repairs will run badly or not at all.** A cut will not close, will not scab, will not fester; it will simply be a hole in you that you hold shut with tape. A blister will not resolve. A sprain will not settle. Bring mechanical closure — sutures, staples, steri-strips, tape — and not glue that cures by drying, because it won't. Bring a splint. Bring dry foot lubricant and a serious blister kit and use it at the first hot spot, not the second.

And adopt the rule: **any injury means descend.** Below the scarp, your body starts again. Above it, you are a slowly flattening object that has stopped negotiating. Do not test this. Do not see how it goes.

Cold: it is cellar-cool, settled, no bite, and it is the temperature of the rock and the air and the dust and, after an hour, the inside of your mouth. It will not feel like an attack. It will feel like a fact. Dress for still air at ten degrees, manage your exertion so you neither cook nor chill, and understand that you cannot warm yourself with fire. Fire is a gradient and would be an obscenity. Ask before you use even a chemical heat pack; the answer may be no, and the answer being no is not superstition, it is the local constitution.

Food: cold, dry, crumbless, and eaten over a cloth. Every crumb is furniture. Chocolate won't melt in a pocket that has cooled to cellar temperature. Nothing will taste of much, because your mouth will be the temperature of the stone. Bring salt and strong dry flavours and don't expect comfort from eating.

You will smell nothing but yourself, for the whole visit, because smell travels by diffusion and diffusion is a process. There is no getting away from your own body up there. Make peace with that below.

## Manners

They call a stranger *a new noise*. That is not an insult; it is a precise technical description, and it is also a warning about what you are about to become.

- **Do not hail anyone.** Approach to within a sphere, at the place agreed for speaking, and wait to be acknowledged. Silence is the courtesy. Being ignored is not rudeness; it is scheduling.
- **Ask before you add anything.** A clack, a cairn, a cord, a notch, a stone on the Tally. There is an inventory. There is a ladder leaning against nothing in the western sink which is on the inventory and which nobody moves. You will be on the inventory too. Your route will be named. Possibly you will be named for your first noise, so consider making your first noise a good one.
- **Report everything.** Every sound you made, every animal you broke, every drop, every wrong turn. The local sin is not harm — it is *unaccounted event.* Confession is the whole of politeness. When they cairn your cough and knot it and route the path around it, that is not a penalty. That is the only kind of immortality they have to offer, and they are offering it to you.
- **The previous keepers are on duty.** Do not move them, do not sit in their place, do not speak to them, do not react. Ask your living keeper what is owed to the ones on the bench and do exactly that, no more.
- **Take nothing.** Not a beast, not a scale, not a grain of the powder. That powder is the summit that stood twelve kilometres over your head; the mountain is not gone, it is distributed, and there is no more of it coming. A souvenir is a subtraction from a finished stock, and it will be counted as missing, and it is pointless besides. It's a fleck of gneiss. You can get gneiss anywhere.
- **Do not settle the argument about the twentieth plant.** It is four generations old. It is not a problem they have failed to solve; it is one of the few things on the Even that still has a *before* and an *after*, and they are keeping it alive the way you'd keep a fire. Bring questions. Do not bring answers.
- **Do not try to make the herd blink.** Do not try to make weather. Do not try to find the bottom layer. Do not enter the ring. Do not open the thimble. Do not move the ladder.

## Descent, and afterwards

Cap your first visit at a day or two. Not a season. The scarp is your decompression chamber, in more senses than altitude.

**Write your life's order down before you go up.** Dates, sequence, who died when, what came before what. Seal it below with someone who knows your story and can tell it back to you. Because you may come down with everything intact and unplaced, and find that you know your mother died but cannot say whether it was before or after you left, and no amount of remembering will fix it, because remembering is the faculty that was damaged. Some of it may stay unplaced forever. Do not go up in the middle of a grief you need to keep in order.

Come down slowly, sleep properly, eat something wet, and let someone read your timeline to you out loud. Expect to weep, and expect it to be strange — down there, tears dry.

---

## What to leave

You will leave something. That is not optional. Your footprints will lie in the seam-dust when the last keeper's grandchildren are sitting facing their doors; your wake will hang in the air in the shape of your body; your breath will have been the only weather in four hundred miles; and somewhere at knee height there will be a two-pace sphere containing the noise you didn't mean to make.

So choose it. Decide, below, on one deliberate thing: a single useful sound at an unmarked junction, agreed with the keeper, in a signature nobody else uses, inventoried and knotted and named. Or a day of counted walking. Or forty stones laid in order on a wall that says nothing but *and then*. Or, best and hardest, nothing at all — a visit that adds only knots, and a stranger who was, for two days, careful.

The mountain is standing at attention across four hundred miles, listening with a hundred billion faces for a sound that will not come. You are the sound. It has to last.

Be a good one.
