# The Unfinished Gorge: Preparation Notes for an Ordinary Traveler

## 1. Believe the rule before you go

Everything practical about this place follows from one sentence, and you must believe it in your body, not just your head:

**What repeats, stays. What happens once, goes down the fall.**

You are a once-thing. You arrived. The water is already reading you. Nothing you bring, nothing you improvise, nothing you notice for the first time will survive contact with that room. The only durable material in the gorge is habit, and you are made of some habit and a great deal of everything else.

So the whole art of visiting is this: **arrive already worn.** Not clever, not equipped, not brave. Worn.

## 2. Decide honestly whether you should go

The gorge is not dangerous the way a cliff is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a warm bath is dangerous when you are tired. Nothing here is cold, nothing is crisp, nothing hurts, and the roar does your thinking for you. The people who stayed did not fall. They relaxed.

If you are grieving, do not go. The pool makes faces you know, dozens a minute, hands coming up open as if offering. It is not a message. It is turbulence with a spare second of intention. Grieving travelers reach out, and reaching is a new gesture, and the water takes the soft new edge of them, warmly, without malice.

If you are the sort of person who travels for awe, do not go yet. Awe is novelty. The single most beautiful thing in this place — a swift turning gold as it crosses a caught evening — happens eleven thousand times a day and is, locally, a rut. If it strikes you as a revelation, the revelation will be the part of you that dissolves.

## 3. Build your liturgy at home, weeks in advance

This is the actual preparation. Everything else is logistics.

Choose a small, closed, repeatable act and do it until it is older than your visit. Suggestions from people who came back:

- Eight paces, a turn, eight paces, a turn — your own circuit, on your own patch of floor.
- Four touches on the beat: brow, cheek, chin, sternum. This doubles as the skin-inventory check you will need in blood-warm water.
- Your name and one sentence from home, spoken aloud, at a fixed interval.

Practice it daily until you could do it feverish, weeping, half-asleep. You want it grooved *before* you arrive, so the fall reads you as something with a history of repetition rather than a fresh sentence.

Two cautions.

**Do not borrow a resident's rhythm.** The crab walks eight and turns; the fern cycles at forty seconds; the door opens about twice a minute. Pick your own interval — the boulder's eleven seconds is the safest, since the boulder is a habit and not a life. Travelers who accidentally match a resident's period sometimes become its second iteration, like the swift briefly doubling. When the loop un-doubles, one of you is the once-thing.

**Make the ascent part of the loop.** Do not plan to "decide to leave." Deciding is novel; by the time you want to decide, you may not have the equipment for it. Design your circuit so that it *includes climbing*: down a few hollows, your eight paces, your four touches, up a few hollows, down again. Then leaving is not a choice, it is simply the next repetition, and eventually the loop carries you out onto the grass. People who go up are almost always people who made going up habitual.

## 4. Arrive bored

Read every account. Look at every image. Rehearse the descent in your imagination until it is tedious — the grain of the mist, the mouth-warmth, the smell of struck flint, the boulder going by at chest height, the bird going gold. Do it a hundred times. Wear the wonder out at home where wonder is cheap.

The ideal state on arrival is a mild, respectful boredom, like a man returning to the same stretch of riverbank he has fished for thirty years. Pilgrims survive here. Tourists are sediment.

## 5. The plateau: camp, cache, and the person who stays up

Ordinary physics resumes above the lip. The boundary of the gorge is the only thing down there that is not up for negotiation, and it works both ways: your transit kit, your notebook, your boots, your food, your medicine — all of it is a novel arrangement and none of it should cross the rim.

- **Camp well back.** The grass leans inward because that is the direction things go, and the lip is being cut. Do not pitch on the edge, do not sleep with your feet toward the hole.
- **Cache and mark everything.** Write your report *before* you descend: name, date, intended duration, what you expect. You may come out unable to remember any of it, since a memory of a single visit is exactly the sort of once-thing the water eats.
- **Leave a written inventory of your small daily habits.** How you take your tea. The phrase you say to the dog. Which side of the bed. The way you check the door twice. You will lose one of these on the way out and you will not know which. The list is how you find the hole afterward.
- **Leave someone up top if you can.** An anchor: a person who keeps saying your name and your routines in the ordinary air, and who can re-teach you afterward. What you relearn will be a new habit, not the old one. That is the best available outcome and you should make peace with it in advance.

If two of you must go down: no conversation. Conversation is novel. Walk in step, keep the same interval, touch the same shoulder at the same beat. Understand that you are coupling your survival to a shared loop, and that if one of you breaks it, the other becomes singular instantly.

## 6. What to wear, what to carry

Nothing manufactured. A buckle, a lens, a hinge, a battery, a lamp — one-time assemblies, eaten first.

There is a reasonable theory, unproven, that things *made of repetition* last longer: rope, braid, woven cloth, a knot you have tied the same way ten thousand times. A rope is nothing but the same twist again and again. Some guides allow a plaited cord anchored far back on the plateau. Do not anchor to the walls; the walls calve away hourly.

Otherwise: the oldest garment you own, the one worn so long it has taken your shape, or nothing at all. Nothing here is cold. Skin is a habit; skin does fine.

Do not carry a light. There is a caught evening on the eastern side and it is not yours to add to.

## 7. The descent

There is no threshold, only a gradient. You will be inside before you notice, which is why your turn-back condition must be a count and not a feeling.

**Breathing.** The coarse water goes into the lungs and satisfies. Your animal body will disagree. The first inhalation triggers every reflex you have about drowning: gasp, clamp, panic. Do not fight it and do not hold your breath — holding is a novel act. Exhale long, let it come in, keep your interval. It tastes of the inside of a shell, mineral and faintly sweet with an iron edge. If you can, practice slow deliberate exhales for a week beforehand.

**Warmth.** Everything is the temperature of a mouth. You will lose the boundary of your skin in the deeper drifts. This is what the four touches are for. Do them on the beat, every beat, and do not skip them because you feel fine.

**Buoyancy.** Wrong in a way that takes an hour to notice. Hair floats. Dropped things descend at the speed of a thought. Your reflexes are calibrated for a world that isn't here, and a reflex is a novelty unless you've trained it. Do not drop anything. Do not throw anything to test a depth. Do not swim.

## 8. Navigation: the wear is the only map

The rock is volatile; the plan of the room changes hourly; new alcoves open raw and hot and are gone by evening. The *habits* are the fixed furniture. Navigate by wear, never by wall.

Known worn volumes: the hundred and four heel-hollows on the west (the stair); the sill polished by forearms; the kneeling bowl near the south; the groove at head height where a hand steadied itself going down in the dark; the north ledge with its four identical moss patches; the boulder's beat at chest height near the east.

**Where nothing is worn, nothing has survived being walked.** Three paces from a breathable drift there is a column of the same stuff that will drown you standing up, and there is no way to see the difference. Walk where the wear-marks walk. Put your heel exactly in the hollow, your forearm exactly on the polish, your palm exactly in the groove.

If a shift leaves the stair hanging in open water with no stone beneath it — step in it anyway. The pressure is remembered. It will hold. This is the single hardest thing to make yourself do and it is the reason people die on the west wall.

## 9. Time

The sun crosses once, so the sun barely exists. There is no day in there. Use the tolling: the same boulder falling every eleven seconds, the room's only reliable object because it is not an object.

Count aloud, on your interval. A hundred tolls is about eighteen minutes. For a first visit, that is plenty; two hundred is generous; three hundred is how the sill gets fresh polish.

Do not touch the boulder to stop it, ride it, or take it. Put a hand on it as it passes, on the beat, and let it go. It is the clock, the handrail, and the metronome, and it has been doing this since there was an *here*.

## 10. The specific hazards

**Hushes.** Pockets of silence, fist-sized to bed-sized, drifting through the roar like fish. They are the only places a new thought can be thought, and they are where you dissolve. Your alarm is your own blood: the moment you can hear your pulse, or hear a thought *finish*, you are in one. Move, and get loud — your name, your sentence, your count. Never be quiet in a quiet place.

Note the trap: the moss lives in the hushes. The north ledge is the softest, stillest, prettiest ground in the gorge and it is the worst place in the room for you. Moss survives there because it is pure iteration, the same patch four times. You are not.

**Do not plug your ears.** The roar is load-bearing; it holds the mist up and packs the drifts into walls you can lean on. You also need your hearing to detect a hush. Expect to receive the low hum in your teeth and sternum rather than your ears; expect to hear squalls cross it. Let it become the baseline. Do not fight it.

**Drafts.** Foam shoulders finding arms, faces with the particularity of a face you know, hands offering, a four-legged thing running three strides before it forgets legs. Do not answer. Do not reach. Do not name them. Keep your hands in their fixed position. And do not try to *teach* one a rhythm — you would not be resurrecting anyone. You would be installing a loop that wears your grief forever. The crab is not a crab; it is eight steps and a turn.

**The residents.** Four lives in a volume the size of a church. Do not lift the crab. If you interrupt it, it becomes a once-thing and is gone before it hits the surface, and there is no crab for a while. Do not touch the fern. Do not stand in the swift's loop. You are a guest in a place with almost nothing alive in it; do not reduce the inventory.

**The doorway.** A rectangle of calm near the south wall, slightly cooler — the only cool thing in the gorge, and therefore very tempting. It opens twice a minute; nothing opens it; the hurry persists. Do not step through, and do not stand in the swept quarter-circle of its swing. A habit with force behind it will not stop for a once-thing.

**The caught evening.** Between two wet walls on the east, apricot going bronze, about a candle at ten paces, lighting a fifth of the chamber where it is permanently six o'clock. Do not sleep in it. Do not set your body's clock by it. Do not stand still in it long enough to become part of it — when the walls change, the bounce breaks, and whatever had joined that evening goes down the fall.

**Fresh alcoves.** Hot, raw, smelling of struck flint, arrises you could shave with. Do not enter. A cut here does not behave like a cut; healing takes duration and duration is what the water takes. You may not bleed. You may simply be less.

**The lip.** The overflow leaves at the low corner. That is a way out for water. Anything that goes over it becomes a once-thing on the fan.

## 11. How staying will feel

It will not feel like a trap. It will feel like being *held*. Warm grain over the shoulders, walls you can lean on that lean back, no cold, no crispness, no obligation to be interesting. The roar thinks so you don't have to. You will notice that your eight paces have become pleasant, then effortless, then the only thing you can imagine doing. You will notice that your name has stopped being a word and started being a groove. That is the moment. It is a very comfortable moment, and the groove will be you, and you will be as durable as the boulder and about as various.

Some of the hollows on the west wall are quite recent. The polish on the sill is warm — warmer than the stone.

## 12. Coming out

Climb the hollows. Hand in the groove where the hand goes. Haul out into thin grass and loose handfuls of rain and stand there deaf, ringing, dripping, appallingly singular. The deafness passes in hours or days. The singularity is the point.

Then check your list. Something small you used to do daily is gone and will never come back — not stolen, *left*, still being performed down there perfectly, forever, without you. You may catch the shape of it in the roar if you stand at the rim too long. Do not stand at the rim too long.

**Do not go back.** A second visit is a repetition of the first, and repetition is exactly what makes you durable there — which is to say, what makes you belong there. The people who go down twice to listen for their own lost habit are the ones whose heel-shape is fresh in the stone.

If someone offers you a souvenir from the fan below the mountain — miles of tumbled smooth single sunrises, first sentences, the exact way a particular door was closed on a particular afternoon — decline. It is the largest thing in the region and every piece of it is somebody's unrepeatable moment. It will mean nothing to you, or far too much, and either way you will have taken a stranger's one time.

## 13. The card

Memorize this until it is rote. Rote is the only thing that works here.

> Walk where it is worn.
> Heel in the hollow, palm in the groove.
> Breathe it; it satisfies.
> Touch brow, cheek, chin, sternum.
> Count the boulder; eleven; aloud.
> Loud in the silence.
> Hands folded; the faces are foam.
> Nothing new; nothing once; nothing mine.
> Up is part of the loop.
