# Preparing to Visit the Sorting Basin

## 1. First, seriously consider not going in

The most useful fact in the whole description is the one offered almost in passing: *you can see all of it from where you stand, and from every other point in it you can see all of it too.*

The bowl has total sightlines. There is nothing hidden behind anything. From the dry ring at the sill you can see the footing rings, the eleven steps, the cup in its seam, the bench, the boots, the tally, the indicator, the plant mats, the columns, and the thing on the plinth. A cheap pair of binoculars will resolve the glyph in a leaf's venation from thirty paces. A phone camera on a long exposure will show you the braided lanes better than crouching in the fog will.

So entering buys you almost nothing that seeing does not already give you. It buys intimacy. And intimacy is the hazard here — not metaphorically, mechanically. The field recruits what stays.

Go to the rim. Sit on ordinary rock outside the frost line with your boots on ordinary schist, and look. Most people who have made this trip well made it exactly that way, and came home with the same photographs and their hearts still arrhythmic in the good old fashion.

Everything below is for the case where you have decided to go in anyway, which is a want and not a need, and should be admitted as such.

## 2. What will actually hurt you

In order of likelihood:

**The mountain.** The last hour is ordinary alpine rock in thin cold air. Cold, UV, a rolled ankle, a storm on the rim, acute mountain sickness. More travelers are ruined by these than by anything the basin does. Acclimatize properly, sleep low, carry the usual kit, turn around for the usual reasons. The frost rind on the rim sits all day a few paces from a floor at blood heat; that gradient is a hypothermia trap for anyone who gets wet and then climbs out.

**The stone.** Fracture faces sharp enough to open a palm, no rounded edges anywhere, hidden cut sockets full of water, knee-high fog that hides all of it. Gloves on, always. Stiff soles. A pole. Never step into a socket, never wade, never assume a wet flat is shallow.

**Scald, without warning.** Because heat does not conduct sideways through the water, there is no warm-up gradient. A pool at 60° and water cold enough to ache sit an inch apart with the boundary holding. You will not feel it coming. Sixty degrees takes skin off in a few seconds. Carry a pocket non-contact infrared thermometer and read every wet surface before you commit a hand or a foot to it. This one cheap tool changes the whole risk profile of the visit.

**Dehydration you cannot feel.** More on this below, but understand it now: in the late afternoon the basin claims your sweat and your breath. You will not notice losing water. You will simply become badly thirsty, all at once, already behind.

**Regularity.** The slow one. The one that feels wonderful.

## 3. Why your body is a component here

Diffusion is off in the working fluid. Your body is a bag of water that runs on diffusion — every cell, every gradient, every ion channel, the film in your lungs where oxygen crosses, the gut where water is taken up, the kidney where the sorting happens. Inside the bowl, that physics is withdrawn from you as impartially as it is withdrawn from a bead on stone.

You will keep working. You will keep working by *pumping harder* — active transport, pressure, muscular effort, the same job the plant mats do with root pressure and transpiration. This is precisely the job the basin has niches for. It will not attack you. It will find you useful.

And the subjective experience of becoming useful is not pain. It is clarity. Tidiness. A sense of everything being alphabetised. Your heart settles into a rhythm with no variance in it at all, and you feel *superb*, and then you die, because a living thing is a jitter that persists.

Burn this in: **the symptom is feeling good.** Not dizziness, not fear. Serenity, efficiency, and a strong preference for staying.

A second consequence, less lethal but more far-reaching: with no diffusion, nothing dissolves and nothing dilutes. Spit stays a thread for an hour. That means every molecule you shed inside the bowl stays coherent, keeps its identity, follows a lane, and arrives at the plinth. Your sunscreen, your blood, your dandruff, your antidepressant metabolites, your ink. There is no "small" contamination. There is no washing it out. A thing that has been precipitating for forty-one years or two hundred, at a hair's breadth a season, to tolerances below the reach of hands, will incorporate you into itself, exactly, permanently, at a twelfth of its completion.

You are not a visitor who might make a mess. You are a feedstock injection.

## 4. Screening: who should not go

Fitness is the easy part. The real screening is temperament.

Someone sat on that bench long enough to wear two dishes into stone. Someone cut thousands of tally marks in patient groups of five and stopped with three in the last group. The boots are still laced. The basin does not trap the careless; it recruits the devoted. It selects for people who find peace in order, who like a long quiet task, who have been keeping a tally of something.

Do not go in if:

- You are in grief, or in love with something you cannot fix, or exhausted in the way that makes stillness feel like mercy.
- You have a strong contemplative practice. Counting breaths, mantra, rosary, prostrations, walking meditation — every one of these is the wrong tool here. This site inverts the protocol of every sacred place you have ever visited. **No meditation. No breathwork. No liturgy.** It is a cathedral with no echo and it will accept your devotions as a calibration signal.
- You are on beta blockers, sedatives, or anything else that flattens heart-rate variability. No alcohol before or during.
- You have a cardiac arrhythmia, a paced heart, or autonomic dysfunction. Get a physician's opinion, not a guidebook's.
- You are pregnant, or considering bringing a child, or considering bringing a dog. Bodies that cannot consent, cannot self-monitor, and cannot be argued out of sitting down do not go into the bowl. A dog's fur is a contamination event and its panting is an intake port.

## 5. Timing

**Never overnight. Never a full day. Never a season.** "Do not stay past a season" is advice for whatever used to work here. For you the limit is hours, and honestly, minutes.

The day's shape gives you one window:

- **Dawn — stay out.** Intake. The columns organize. Watch them from the sill: eight or nine vertical wrist-to-waist-thick standing pipes of fog, cut off flat at four metres, casting ordinary shadows. It is the best sight in the basin and it costs you nothing. Do not be inside a column, and above all do not be *standing still* in one.
- **Mid-morning to early afternoon — the window.** Columns thin, floor steams in sheets, lanes slow. Enter after the columns have gone slack; be out before the sorting starts. Two hours is generous. Thirty minutes is plenty. Cycle out to the cold rim to dump heat, and vary the interval.
- **Late afternoon — out.** The tell is unmistakable and it is already late: beads on flat stone begin to travel uphill in single file, the pools develop a grain like muscle, and the sweat on your forearms stops falling and stands up in ranks and walks away. If you see your own sweat queue, you have overstayed.
- **Dusk — out, and not on the bench.** The columns lean, the deposit comes down with its held-breath sound, the frost cracks like a shot in a room with no echo. It is the most beautiful ninety seconds this world offers and it is the exact posture in which the last keeper was consumed. Watch it from outside, or don't watch it.

And keep an eye on the sky. When real weather comes over the rim, the basin **rejects**: everything reverses for about a minute, films retract uphill, beads climb stems, pools shrink inward, columns collapse into the walls, and the surplus is walked out across the sill in a sheet and dumped over the lip. If you are in a lane when this happens your trouser legs will try to go somewhere. If you are on the sill's outer edge you may go with the sheet. Know where the overflow runs and camp well away from it.

## 6. Kit

**Wear:**
- Smooth, tightly-woven, non-shedding shell layers. Taped seams. No fleece, no wool, no cotton, no loose knits — every fibre is a capillary and a capillary is an invitation. Elastic cuffs, gaiters, nothing dangling, nothing flapping.
- Hood or head covering. Hair tied and contained. Trim your nails before you go.
- Thin gloves, plus a spare pair. Palm-cut risk is constant.
- Stiff-soled boots with smooth uppers, not fabric.
- Wraparound eye protection or sealed goggles. **No contact lenses** — a lens floats on a tear film that will be claimed, and it will dry onto your cornea.
- A close-fitting mask. It works both ways: it keeps your droplets out of the medium and keeps some of your breath's water yours. It feels worse at altitude. Accept the trade.
- Wide brim and full coverage instead of sunscreen. **No sunscreen, no repellent, no balm, no perfume, no aerosol of any kind.** Do lip care outside.

**Carry:**
- More water than you think, plus electrolytes. Three or four litres per person for a short visit. All drinking, eating, and urinating happens outside the bowl.
- Infrared thermometer. Pole. Headlamp. Binoculars.
- A chest-strap heart-rate monitor that logs beat-to-beat intervals, not just an average. Establish your baseline over several days on the ordinary mountain first, so you know your own normal spread — cold, altitude and dehydration narrow it too, and you need to read a trend, not a number.
- **One die.** Genuinely. See below.
- A rounded river stone from somewhere dull. Hold it often.
- Sealed bags for everything you take off. Everything you bring leaves with you, including your clothing.
- Nothing lubricated, nothing greasy, nothing that sheds, no tape you might leave behind, no drone (downwash is unscheduled turbulence and you will trigger a rejection).

## 7. Discipline: nothing in, nothing out

Clean-room habits, adapted:

- No spitting, no nose-blowing, no coughing uncovered. Altitude makes people cough; a cough is a spray of droplets that will be filed and delivered. If you cough into the bowl, leave.
- No bleeding. Cover every existing cut before you enter. Dry-air nosebleeds are common at altitude — humidify at camp, and if one starts, pinch and walk out and accept that you may already have marked the thing.
- No tears if you can help it. This is harder than it sounds at that bench.
- Never dig. The fifteen mats are one clone running along the fracture lines; you would be cutting a pump. Never take a leaf.
- Do not move the dead. A moth lies intact for weeks and then comes apart bead by bead at dusk and goes up a column. It is not litter, it is queued. Don't pocket it. Don't tidy it.
- Take nothing: no stone, no leaf, no water, no cup, no moth, no photograph of yourself touching anything.
- **Leave no mark of any kind.** No cairn inside, no flag, no note, no name, no scratch. And do not add a mark to the tally. Leave the last group at three.

If the leave-no-trace rule feels fussy, remember what the medium does. A note left in this bowl would lie perfect and dry for weeks and then be carried, sorted, and precipitated into an object whose tolerances are below the reach of any tool that touches. Your handwriting would become a feature of it. Be irrelevant instead. *It does not care that you have come* — make that literally true.

## 8. Conduct inside: be the moth

The moth flies a jittering, drunken, deliberately erratic route all day and never lands to feed. It is not drunk. It is doing the randomness the process still needs, because bodies are cheap and self-repairing. The amphibian's gills stir constantly and that stirring is the only randomness permitted in the water.

That is your job description and your survival strategy. **Supply your own jitter, out loud, in your body, continuously.**

- Walk wrong. Change lead foot. Scuff. Stop mid-stride. Never fall into a cadence, never breathe in step, never march. If you are with someone, deliberately walk out of step with them.
- Talk. Irrelevant, stupid, badly-organized talk. Name things inaccurately. Tell a shaggy story. Miscount on purpose. Laughter is the best protective act available to a human being in this basin: spasmodic, whole-body, unpredictable, and impossible to schedule. **A funny companion is safety equipment.**
- Do not count anything. Not steps, not breaths, not the mats, not the marks. If you must measure the plant spacing, do it once, badly, and write it down outside.
- Carry the die and roll it when you notice you have settled — to decide where to look next, whether to move, when to go. Not on a schedule; that would just be another rhythm. Roll it when you catch yourself being calm.
- Keep returning to the dry ring at the sill and drop a pebble and *listen to it bounce and roll and make a noise*. It is the most reassuring sound in the world here. Do it at irregular intervals. Then go back in, or don't.
- Do not sit on the bench. Stand at it. Look at the two dishes and the laced boots for as long as you need to, and then walk away, and notice how much you don't want to.

**Boundary tests.** The edge of the field is audible. Clap: outside it rings, inside it comes back flat and close, like talking in a wardrobe. Your footsteps stop making noise. Your voice loses its room. The frost line is the visual marker; the heat through your soles is the tactile one. Use the clap rather than the pebble when you're unsure — a pebble dropped in the wrong place is a permanent donation.

**Read the animals.** They are the instruments, and they are better calibrated than you. If the amphibian's gills go still, if the moth's flight smooths out, if the swift leaves its port — the field is deepening and you leave immediately, whatever your monitor says and however good you feel.

## 9. Water and heat

The floor sits at blood temperature, so the ground gives you no cooling. Midday is an open oven. Your only heat dump is sweat, and the basin takes sweat. You cannot use a wet cloth inside — it becomes a lane. There is no evaporative cooling available to you in there.

So: cool from outside. Cycle up to the frost rim, sit on cold ordinary rock, drink, eat, pee, change, laugh, come back. Vary the interval.

The three unmarked sockets by the south wall are an emergency ration, not a supply. Verify *unmarked* properly — light, loupe, look for the three strokes and the hook. Note that the water "tastes of nothing at all" because in a no-diffusion regime water cannot take up anything: it is effectively distilled. It is not mineral water, it is aggressive water, and litres of it will pull electrolytes out of you rather than put them in. Add your salts outside the bowl. Bring your own and treat the sockets as a backup that nobody has cancelled.

Treat thirst as late evidence, not as a signal.

## 10. The built things, and the thing

Six flush footing rings full of water; whatever stood on them was removed and the process runs without it. Eleven steps up a wall, ending at blank rock. A cup in a seam. A bench. An indicator that changes about once a season, in units you will not recognise.

Do not climb the stair looking for a door. Do not put anything in the rings. Do not drink from the cup. Do not touch, cover, clean, warm, shade, or reset the indicator. Do not attempt to read a meaning into its units on site — photograph it and argue about it at home, where arguing is safe.

At the plinth: **do not touch it, and do not follow the edge with your finger.** An edge that returns to its start having turned you slightly is not a curiosity, it is an orientation hazard, and you have a descent to make on sharp rock in thin air. Keep a fixed landmark on the rim in view. Do not complete circuits of it. Look for seconds, look away, look at something ordinary — the shadows here are cut-paper sharp and the sun is the ordinary sun, which means shadow-and-bearing navigation is reliable and you should use it constantly.

Don't stare. Looking too long makes your eyes water and the water is taken. Photograph it and review the images later, outside, briefly. Some travelers report the turning persists in images; don't spend an evening with it.

And do not help. It is a twelfth finished, at a hair's breadth a season, and nobody is coming to check. Do not contribute water, or blood, or effort, or intention. Do not remove the dead moth to "clean" the substrate. Do not finish the tally. The single most respectful thing an ordinary human can do at that plinth is fail to affect it in any way whatsoever.

## 11. Partners and extraction

Two inside at most, one on the sill watching, one at camp. Never solo. Sightlines are total but sound is not — your partner will not hear you. Agree visual signals: raised arm, a bright cloth, a mirror flash. Give the sill watcher **explicit authority to call you out and your prior agreement to obey without discussion.** You will not want to obey. That is the condition being treated.

The sill watcher must also keep moving. Waiting is stillness, and stillness is the mechanism.

If someone goes exact — serene, slow, radiant, pulse like a metronome, entirely reasonable about why they should stay a little longer — do not argue. Take their arm and walk them out. Don't let them sit. Don't let them watch the dusk. Get them below the frost line, then descend: warmth, salt, sugar, noise, irregular stimulation, oxygen if you have it, and a real medical assessment, because loss of beat-to-beat variability is a cardiac event and not a mood.

## 12. Afterwards

Expect a hangover of order. Watch for: sleeping too well; waking at exactly the same minute; a new distaste for mess, noise, interruption, other people's chewing; a feeling that you were *clearer* up there; a strong desire to go back and sit down.

Treat it deliberately. Go somewhere with weather and crowds and dogs and badly timed music. Eat something with your hands. Let a river do its careless mixing at you. Be with people who interrupt. Make no large decisions for a week, take up no discipline, start no tally. If you catch yourself counting, stop mid-number.

Do not go back the same season. Do not go back at all if you felt wonderful.

And do not listen to your own recordings on headphones at night. There are six sounds a minute in that bowl that are not you, and the rest is your heart and your knees and the wet click of your tongue, and it is the most persuasive audio you will ever own.

## 13. What to bring home

A rounded stone from a dull river, and the sound of a pebble bouncing and rolling and making a noise.

Down on the ordinary mountain the rejected rain runs off downhill in the usual careless way — spreading, mixing, forgetting, going nowhere in particular. That is waste. That is turbulence. That is the whole of what you are, and what the moth is doing on purpose, and what the basin has spent two hundred years eliminating in order to build one curled-person-sized thing that will not be finished while anything you love is still alive.

Go and look at it, if you must. Be moved. Then get out, and drink too much tea, and be late for something, and let your heart stagger the way it should.
