# Preparing for the Sill

*A brief for first-crossing travelers. Read it twice. The second reading is the one that matters, because by then you will have stopped thinking of it as a description of a place and started thinking of it as a description of yourself.*

---

## I. Understand what you are, before you understand where you're going

Everything else in this document follows from one fact, and you should sit with it until it stops being abstract:

**You are the wettest thing that has ever been on the Sill.**

Sixty-odd kilograms of water in a warm, edge-rich, breathing package. Nothing there has ever been wet. Not the stone, not the air, not the fauna, not the four-million-year archive of pressed streets under your boots. Water is not a resource on the Sill; it is an event. A spilled canteen is a massacre. A leaky suit is a massacre that keeps going after you leave. A man weeping at a doorstep because it looks exactly like his grandmother's doorstep is a small, permanent, unreadable smear in the stratigraphy of a world.

So the first preparation is not equipment. It is deciding, in advance, in a calm room on another world, what you will do when you feel something. Decide now. You will not be able to decide there, because the Sill is engineered by nothing and accidentally perfect at making you feel things.

The second fact, nearly as important:

**You are an edge.**

The city grows on edges. That is the whole mechanism — an edge grows, becomes a better edge, gathers faster. You have shoulders, ears, a nose, boot heels, buckles, a hood brim, the corners of your pack, the rim of your collar. You are warm, which is the signal for food and shelter both. You exhale dissolved matter that lays itself down on the nearest cold surface. You breathe in pulses.

You are not a visitor to a city that grows out of the air. You are the best nucleation site that has appeared on this shelf in an age, and you have arrived voluntarily.

## II. The two ways to go, and why you must choose before you cross

There is no neutral option. Pick one.

### The sealed route (recommended for everyone, always, and especially the first time)

A closed-loop suit. Nothing out, nothing in. Rebreather with CO₂ scrub and full moisture capture — no venting, no bubbles, no exhaust. Sweat, breath-humidity, urine, everything reclaimed inside the shell. You carry your own weather with you and you do not share it.

Sealed, you leave no rime. No handprints. No trail. No room. You walk through the Colonnade as a non-event, and the city does not learn your shape, and in ten thousand seasons there will be no quarter that is subtly, horribly, bilaterally *you*.

Sealed is also colder, quieter, lonelier, and — this is the part nobody warns you about — **it makes you invisible to a place that only knows how to see warmth and edges.** You will feel like a ghost in a house full of people. That feeling is correct and it is the price of leaving the place intact. Pay it.

### The open route

Some travelers go open. They breathe the air, taste the mineral at the back of the throat, put a bare palm on a wall, and leave a pale bloom of themselves on everything they pass. They are, in the most literal sense, contributing to the architecture. There is a quarter in the northern Stair Country that exists because people once did this and stayed a while.

I am not going to tell you it's wrong. I am going to tell you what it costs, plainly:

- Every exhalation removes a little of you and adds it to a wall. Over a long stay this is not metaphorical. Track your mass. Track your water. You are being spent.
- Your rime trail is permanent, legible, and follows you. Anything that reads warmth or shelter can read where you have been and where you are going.
- You are hydrating the air locally, continuously, at a scale of grams. Grams are survivable. Grams are also how the plinth gets its slumped, unreadable layers.
- The city keeps shapes. It will keep yours. It will keep it with small errors, forever, after you are gone.

If you go open, go for one tide, leave a budget, and do not sleep there.

**What follows assumes sealed.**

## III. The kit, and the reasoning behind each item

**A smooth shell.** Not a rugged one. This is counterintuitive and it is the single most important design decision you will make. Deposition follows edges, so *have none*. No protruding straps, no external pockets, no hood brim, no sharp buckles, no fold creases, no exposed zipper teeth. Round everything. Fair over every seam. Dress like a river pebble, not like a person. A traveler in a craggy technical suit with fourteen attachment points will, over four days, acquire a delicate pale fringe on every one of them, and then the fringe will start to gather faster, because that is what fringes do here.

Materials: fused silica, sapphire, fluoropolymer, glass. Glass is stable here — glass is what sound *becomes*. Avoid anything hygroscopic. Avoid anything salt-like. Avoid anything porous. You want a surface the air cannot get a grip on.

**Cut resistance everywhere, and gaiters.** Every footstep drops glass pellets behind your heel. A cough drops a coin-sized drift. You will be walking, sitting, and kneeling on precipitated sound. It is fine, sharp, and it accumulates in exactly the places you'll want to rest: the lee of walls, the bowls of plazas, the drains. A pinhole in your shell is not a puncture. It is a chemical release.

**Grit filtration on any intake, even a sealed one, as a redundancy.** You do not want to inhale forty years of wind.

**A rebreather, not open-circuit air.** Bottled air vented into the atmosphere is dry and chemically unremarkable; the problem is that open-circuit systems leak humidity, and you cannot afford a single gram of drift. Closed loop. Scrubber heat managed inward, not outward — see below on warmth.

**No combustion. None.** No stove, no flare, no candle, no hydrocarbon anything. Combustion makes water. A chemical flare is a small, bright, beautiful act of vandalism. Signal with an electric strobe, a mirror, or a colored panel.

**No food that steams. No hot drinks.** Eat dry, cold, and contained, in your helmet, with the wrapper going back in the pouch. Crumbs are edges.

**A jamb gauge.** A simple telescoping rod with a locking collar. Set it across the inside of any doorway you intend to use, mark the length, and re-check it. Every time. This is your primary predator detector and it costs nothing.

**A witness lamp and two mirrors.** Observation slows deposition — measurably. Leave a marker, turn your back, count a hundred, turn around. Being looked at holds stone in solution a little longer. A lamp *may* substitute for a gaze; travelers disagree, and the disagreement is unresolved because nobody is sure whether the operative quantity is photons or attention. **Calibrate this yourself on arrival with the hundred-count before you rely on it.** Do not stake your life on someone else's doctrine about a physics nobody here has decided.

Mirrors widen your watch. Whether reflected attention counts is, again, an open question. Test it. Log it. Trust your log.

**A forty-hour watch and a tide table.** The deck swells and thins on a ~40-hour cycle. The pressure tide advances the deposition front in pulses; a pulse is about the height of a step. Warm fronts move across the quarters with the tide, and the grazers follow them, and the grazers' routes are the best safety map you will ever get for free. Learn to read the tide. Everything on the Sill is scheduled by it, including the two unshadowed hours, including your extraction window.

**Bone-conduction audio, inside the helmet.** You will want a human voice. Bring one. Keep it off the air. Sound here costs mass and loads the reservoirs; a recorded voice played out loud is a small donation to a bomb.

**Tethers, and a tug code.** You cannot shout for your partner. You cannot shout at all. Agree on pulls: *stop*, *come*, *look*, *danger*, *doorway closing*, *I am not where you think I am*. Physical line between you at all times in transit, because during the unshadowed hour ten paces is not a distance, it is a suggestion.

**A pulse oximeter and an altitude protocol.** The air is thin enough that a shout would tire you. Acclimatize before you cross, not after. And understand the trap: hypoxia presents as euphoria, wonder, and a serene conviction that everything is fine and you should stay a bit longer. **The Sill also presents as euphoria, wonder, and a serene conviction that you should stay a bit longer.** You will not be able to tell them apart from the inside. Use the instrument. Trust the number over the feeling.

**A hard extraction timer, set before you cross, that you are not permitted to renegotiate on site.** More on this at the end.

## IV. Skills to train before you go

**Silence.** Not quietness — silence. Train it in your body until it is below thought. No humming. No muttering at a difficult climb. No laughing. No gasping when you come over a rise and see the Colonnade standing in ranks forty feet apart for a hundred miles, which you will want to do, badly. Every sound you make sheds a little of you and leaves a scatter of glass behind your heel. On the Sill, the ascetic fauna have made a religion out of this physics. Convert.

Learn a full sign vocabulary — not just emergency signals, but *conversation*. You will need to be able to say "this is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen" with your hands, and you will need to be able to say it while crying inside a sealed helmet, which is a thing you should practice, because tears in a sealed helmet are a reclamation problem and a fogging problem and you do not want to be solving those for the first time in the Orchard.

**Walking in up-light.** This is the underrated killer. Your visual system has spent your entire life assuming light comes from above. Here the dominant light comes up through the floor and lays every shadow upward across the ceiling, with a weaker second shadow falling down from a small hard sun. Concave reads convex. Stairs read as inverted stairs. A step down reads as a step up.

The Stair Country will break your ankle if you walk it on vision. Train for months under floor lighting until your gut stops arguing. Then, on site, use a probe pole and your feet, every time, on every tread, no exceptions, especially when you are tired and the stairs look obvious. *Especially then.*

**The hundred-count.** Your all-purpose instrument. Marker down, back turned, count a hundred, look. It measures local deposition rate, tells you whether your lamp works, tells you whether mirrors work, tells you which way the wind's habitual pause is lying today. Do it at every camp. Do it when a room feels welcoming. Do it when you can't be bothered.

**Attention gardening.** Walk the Sill for a day and you will find behind you a modest, quite well-made lane that was not there, arranged around the shape of your not-looking. This is a real capability and it is also how people get walled in. Learn to feel where your blind arc is. Learn to sweep it. Learn what a group of three can cover that a solo traveler cannot, and understand that solo travel here means permanently conceding a wedge of the world to construction.

## V. Where to stand, where to sit, where to sleep

Your instincts are exactly inverted. Correct them now.

**Shelter is the trap.** Not metaphorically — mechanically. The thing that eats grazers is a small, well-proportioned, warmly lit room with a good doorway, and it works because grazers shelter from the dissolution wind. That is the entire trap. It precipitates fastest wherever nothing is looking at it. It closes over days. What comes out is a pillar with a shape inside it, and there are quarters full of these, and from a distance they read as statuary, as civic ambition, as a city that commemorates something.

So:

- **Do not shelter from wind.** If you need a break, take it on exposed, scoured ground where the air is stripping material away rather than laying it down. Cold and eroding is safe. Warm and sheltered is being built.
- **The most hospitable architecture is the predator.** Good proportions, warm underlight, a generous doorway, a room that seems to have been waiting for you — leave. Awkward, asymmetric, low, ill-fitting rooms are far safer. The city's grammar will not put a threshold where nothing approaches; a threshold that seems to be *for you* is either a coincidence or an appetite.
- **Never let a doorway you're using leave your sight.** Gauge it. Lamp it. Look at it. If it narrows by a finger's width, go, immediately, and do not go back for your gear.
- **Do not sleep in a room.** Do not sleep in the Hollow Quarter, which is all rooms and no floors. Do not sleep in a doorway, under a lintel, in an arch, or anywhere the air is sheltered enough to settle in flat sheets.
- **Camp on a windward crest, in the open, strapped to your own frame.** Cold, scoured, exposed, uncomfortable, safe. Insulate from below — the stone takes heat and holds it — but manage your outward thermal signature, because warmth is food-sign and shelter-sign both, and there is one grazer per four or five square miles and they know each other's routes. A grazer standing still is a newel post. A grazer investigating you is a six-limbed banded-salt thing with a mouth like a rasp. They are not malicious. They are also not soft.

**The stored sound.** Grit silts in the lee of walls, in plaza bowls, in drains. There is an oval in the Hollow Quarter waist-deep in a century of unspent wind, crusted smooth and pale, and breaking that crust releases forty years at once — enough to knock you down and flay the deposit off every wall within a hundred paces. That is a lethal blast made entirely of things nobody said.

Learn the sign: **smooth, pale, level, slightly bowl-shaped pavement in a sheltered place.** It looks like the best campsite on the shelf. It is a magazine. The grazers go around it. Go around it. Do not kneel on it, do not drop your pack on it, do not probe it out of curiosity, and do not let your partner walk onto it while you are signing something else.

And remember you are loading it. Every sound you make is a deposit. A chatty expedition is manufacturing the weapon that will kill the next one.

## VI. The unshadowed hour

Twice per cycle, underlight and overhead sun balance exactly and nothing casts a shadow. During that interval, placement stops being enforced. Distances measured in effort come unmoored from distances measured in sight. The bollard you have been walking toward for an hour is behind you. The mile-long banister has acquired a turn. The avenues re-sort by a rule that is not geometric.

**Protocol:**

1. **Be off your feet before it starts.** Your tide watch predicts it. Stop moving fifteen minutes early. Do not be mid-span, mid-stair, or mid-anything.
2. **Be in physical contact with something large and continuous, and keep looking at it.** Hand on the banister. Eyes on the banister. Contact plus observation is the only anchor anyone has found that holds.
3. **Tether everything to your body.** Loose gear is loose grammar. Your pack sitting three feet away is, during the hour, an object in a plaza with no claim on being yours.
4. **Tether your partner and keep them in view.** Do not rely on the line alone; a tug tells you they exist, not where.
5. **Do not navigate. Do not measure. Do not conclude anything.** Any position fix taken during the hour is fiction. Wait it out.

**Navigate by grammar, not by map.** This is the deepest adjustment and it takes people weeks. The city has a syntax. It knows what may follow what. It will not put a drain in a lintel; it will not put a threshold where nothing approaches. Maps are useless here and bearings are worse. What works is an itinerary of *legitimate successions*: approach, threshold, vestibule, chamber, drain, plaza, bollard, avenue. Count doorways, not paces. Ask not "how far" but "what comes next."

Keep a grammar log, not a survey. And be syntactically legible yourself: during the hour, be a traveler at a threshold, not an unexplained object in the middle of an oval. The city re-sorts according to what may follow what. Give it something to follow.

## VII. Things you will see and should not approach

**The pillars.** You will know what they are once you've seen one. Do not break them open. There is nothing you can do, and the deposition took days, and the shape inside is not waiting.

**The Orchard.** Beautiful, waist-high, feathered, fractal, and reproducing by shedding seed-edges downwind. It is not a plant and it does not care about the distinction. Walk it if you must; walk it slowly; understand that every seed-edge that catches on your shell is a stowaway. **This is your primary quarantine concern on departure.** An invasive crystalline replicator with four million years of optimization for edge-growth, released into a wet world — think about what that means, and then think about it again.

**The thing from below.** Once in a long while something comes up through the deck, vast and unhurried, trailing vapor, and passes between the colonnades without touching them, and goes back down. Nobody who has seen it has been able to say what it was. Do not try to be the first. Do not stand in its way; be somewhere it can pass. Do not attempt to name it, because naming costs mass and because it does not seem to have finished being one thing.

## VIII. The leak drill

Memorize this, because there will not be time to think:

**Seal breach → do not patch and continue. Leave.**

The air is a solvent. It is thirsty in a way you have no intuition for. It will find the wet place instantly and it will not stop finding it. Patching buys you minutes and gives you a false sense of control while a quarter dissolves under your boots. Get out, get to your extraction, and file the incident honestly so the next traveler knows where the slumped street is.

**Cut → same.** Blood is water with a story attached.

**Canteen spill → same, and don't look back at it.**

**Tears → this is why you decided in advance.**

The mineral taste at the back of your throat is your canary. Sealed, you should never taste it. If you taste stone, you are open, and you are already being spent.

## IX. Duration, and the real hazard

First crossing: **one full cycle, forty hours, no sleeping.** That is enough. You will see the Colonnade and the Orchard and the plaza with the drain and the four buildings a quarter-league apart, and you will stand in a doorway and see the next doorway as a pale notch on the horizon and *know*, with the certainty of a body that has lived in towns, that those two doorways are neighbors. That is the experience. You do not need more than one tide to have it, and the room-thing needs days.

Second crossing: two cycles, a scour camp, a partner, a full watch rotation.

Nobody should stay a season. The Sill's danger is not acute. It is *cumulative and welcoming*, and it works on the timescale of days, which is precisely the timescale on which humans stop being careful.

Because here is what will actually happen to you, and no equipment addresses it:

You will find a doorstep standing alone on a plain, its center dished by traffic that never existed, and your body — which has lived in towns, which has come home ten thousand times, which knows what a worn threshold *means* — will fill this city with people. Not hallucinations. Worse: **hospitality.** You will feel expected. The grammar is intact. The spacing is wrong by a factor of a hundred, but the grammar is intact, and grammar is what your nervous system actually reads. Every doorway will feel like it is for someone. Every plaza will feel like a place where a market was. The silence will not feel empty; it will feel *held*, the way a note is held.

And you will start to think: I could stay a bit longer. I could just see the Stair Country. There's a quarter up north with low asymmetric doorways and vaulted ceilings that serve no load and chambers paired and branching and narrowing, and it is the plan of a lung, rebuilt in salt, tide after tide, with small errors, forever, because things came up here once and stayed a while and slowly built their own interiors out of the air.

The city keeps shapes. That is the one thing it does.

**Set your extraction timer on another world, in a calm room, and do not let the person you become on the Sill renegotiate it.** That person is not compromised. That person is not under a spell. That person is simply being read correctly by a place that has been reading arrivals for four million years, and being read correctly feels exactly like being welcome.

## X. Leaving

- Full decontamination. Every seed-edge, every gram of sound-grit, every fringe of deposit off your smooth shell. Quarantine the shell, not just yourself.
- Take nothing. A stolen doorstep is a hole in a sentence, and removing material creates a fresh edge, and fresh edges grow. You will not be able to see the consequence. There will be one.
- Bring no tool you would not want copied. There has never been a tool on the Sill. The city keeps shapes. Whatever you carry, it can learn to make in salt, badly, then better, then forever.
- Leave your grammar log with the next traveler. It is worth more than your photographs.

And if you went open — if you breathed, and touched the walls bare-handed, and left a pale print of yourself on every stone you passed — then before you go, walk a little way back along your own trail and look at it. It is faint and it is exact and it is already being copied with small errors.

Stand still for a moment. The chill comes up through your soles. The amber light comes up through the floor. Your two shadows lie one on the ceiling and one at your ankles, and every time you breathe out you add a little to the walls.

The Sill is in balance. Erosion and arrival matched to within a hair. It has already counted you in — not as a guest, not as an intruder, but as material, arriving on schedule, with the rest of the weather.

Go home before it finishes the sentence.
