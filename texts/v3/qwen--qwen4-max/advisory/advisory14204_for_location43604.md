# Preparing to Visit the Hold

## First, understand what you are visiting

It is not a marsh, not a farm, not a zoo, not a hell. It is a cooling system with a biology inside it, running an acceptance test that has never ended. Everything you will see is a component: the stock are fins, the drift is a humidity buffer, the lice are a sealant crew, the rain is a return line. Nothing in it is aware of the whole. Nothing in it is in charge.

Which means: there is no one to greet you, no one to ask, no one to notice you are in trouble, and no one to blame if you die. There is only a specification, stamped in ceramic, and a quality-control process that removes whatever drifts from it.

You will drift from it in every measurable respect. You will be cold, dry, loud, moving, novel, and hungry. Prepare accordingly. The whole art of visiting the Hold is to be as close to nothing as a person can manage, for a short time, and then leave.

## The fact that governs everything: there is no gradient

Thirty-nine degrees, near-saturated, air moving at the speed of a breath. This is a wet-bulb temperature of thirty-nine. Human beings do not survive it. Not "find it unpleasant" — do not survive it. Your sweat cannot evaporate. Your lungs cannot shed heat into air that is hotter and wetter than your lungs; each breath *adds* heat. Radiation, conduction, convection: all zero or negative. There is no shade, because shade is a gradient. There is no cool floor, no breeze, no night.

Run the numbers before you go, because you will not be able to feel them there. A resting body makes about 100–130 watts. A body is about 250 kilojoules per degree. So your core climbs roughly one degree every thirty to forty minutes, and you begin at thirty-seven. Confusion and poor judgment arrive somewhere around thirty-nine. Somewhere past forty you stop being a person who can follow instructions.

**The critical part: it will not feel hot.** Heat is a sensation of gradient, and there isn't one. The water is exactly the temperature of your hand, so your hand reports nothing. You will feel comfortable, held, pleasantly weightless, right up until you cannot do arithmetic. Travelers who die in the Hold do not die struggling. They die noticing that they've forgotten why they came, and that it doesn't seem important.

So instrument yourself instead of trusting your skin:

- A swallowed core-temperature telemetry pill, alarming at 38.0 °C. Not 38.5. You want the alarm while you can still act on it.
- A wrist timer with hard intervals, and a cognitive check every ten minutes: serial sevens, or name every object you can see, out loud in your helmet. When the check gets hard, the visit is over. The check *is* the visit's end condition; nothing else is.
- A partner, if you can bring one, whose only job is to watch your numbers and your speech. Two people who both feel fine is not safety. Numbers are safety.
- Ice. A kilogram of ice slurry, drunk, absorbs roughly 490 kilojoules warming to body temperature — about an hour of your own metabolism. It is the cheapest, most honest cooling you will ever carry, and it does not care about humidity. Two kilos in a bladder against your spine and chest, plus two to drink, is a real budget.

Unprotected, you have perhaps twenty useful minutes and forty lucky ones. Suited and iced, plan on ninety. **Measure your visit in swallows, not hours.** One swallow is a good visit. Two is the maximum for a first trip.

## Import your gradient in a box

You cannot borrow coolth here. You must bring it, sealed, and pay for it by the gram.

The obvious mistake is a cold outer surface. In near-saturated air at blood heat, anything colder than 39 °C condenses water furiously, and every gram of condensate dumps about 2.4 kilojoules into your cold pack. A chilled vest worn exposed will be spent in minutes and will make its own private rainstorm, which the slick will bloom beneath. So: **your cooling lives inside insulation, and your outer skin sits at ambient.** A sealed shell, aerogel or vacuum panel, phase-change or ice bladders against the torso, outer surface warmed to 39. You are carrying a small cold room with a warm coat on.

Pre-warm every piece of kit to blood heat *before* you arrive. Cold gear weeps for minutes, puddles on the floor, and announces you as a weather event. A cold visor blinds you instantly and completely — hydrophilic coating, heated visor, and expect thirty seconds of nothing anyway. There is no airlock, no vestibule, no threshold in the Hold. It begins at the floor. Arrive already suited, already cooled, already timed.

Breathing: a closed or semi-closed loop with a cool dry mix is ideal, but it makes your exhalation a plume of wrong-temperature, wrong-humidity gas — a visible variance. Most travelers compromise: filtered ambient air, accept the respiratory heat load, budget for it, keep the visit short. Do not plan on "just breathing normally." Normal breathing here is a slow heating.

For long observation, there is a legitimate trick: the floor is an infinite, actively maintained 39 °C heat sink. A small heat pump with its hot plate in the standing water and its cold loop against your skin has a tiny lift and excellent efficiency — a few tens of watts of electricity buys you two hundred watts of cooling, indefinitely. It also makes compressor noise and a local hot spot, which is to say it makes you loudly out of spec. Tethered posts only. And notice what you've built: a small kiln with an animal radiator. You will be a scale model of the thing you came to look at.

## Air, water, skin

The drift is thirty thousand sterile larvae per cubic meter, comma-sized, at chest height, replaced as fast as it's lost. They do not hatch and do not feed. They are still foreign protein in your sinuses, your eyes, your ears, your throat. Assume you will inhale some. Assume your immune system will have opinions. If you have any history of arthropod allergy, bring antihistamines and epinephrine and consider a different destination — there is chitin in every seam of this world.

The slick is a living electroautotroph that colonizes *every wet surface*. You are a wet surface. Your suit will grow a brown-gold film within hours, and grazers with mouthparts like wet leather will come and test it. Your conjunctiva, your airway, and any cut are wet surfaces too. Nothing here is described as pathogenic, and nothing here has ever met a mammal; that is not the same as safe. Full barrier. No skin exposed to air or water except deliberately, briefly, on purpose, with a meter.

Do not drink the condensate. It tastes of iron and salt and it is the working fluid of a hundred million guts. Bring all water. Bring all food — there is nothing here with a leaf, nothing that ever fruited, and everything edible is a line in a ledger that balances to the gram.

Before you put your hand in the water: **measure for potential.** The slick takes current from buried buswork. There is electricity under that floor, and the water is exactly the temperature of your hand, which means your hand will not tell you a single true thing about what it is touching. The place's first act is to remove the difference between you and it. Do not let it remove the difference between "warm" and "live."

Hours in saturation macerate skin, prune hands, blister feet, and rot the folds of you. The Hold has no smell of rot anywhere — lanolin, hot zinc, milk, sweetness. Which means the ordinary animal warning that *something is decaying* has been engineered out of the world. You will not smell your own trouble. Inspect yourself on a schedule.

## Where to put your feet

There is almost no floor. The basins are rim to rim, stocked at eleven centimeters, which is the spacing that maximizes heat transfer. You cannot walk between the animals; there is no between. You walk on gantries and along flume lips, and both are occupied — slick mats under the gantries, counters in ranks along the flumes. Do not displace a counter. It is doing inventory, and you do not want to be a discrepancy in it.

Surfaces: poured arrises sharp enough to cut, tie-holes plugged with bone-ash ceramic, wet rubber channel lining that has never hardened, and a hand-deep sheet of water at blood temperature that hides all of it. Soft-soled, high-grip, cut-resistant boots that shed nothing. No loose grit. There are no rounded stones in this world and you should not introduce one.

Respect the camber. Two centimeters per hundred meters, surveyed, true, and the whole world's function depends on the sheet going somewhere. A boot placed wrong can pond water across a few square meters of hide, and ponded hide is a fin that has stopped working. Step where the sheet is already braided around something. Never dam a channel. Never set a case down flat across the flow.

Never enter a flume. The dead go down them as a plumbing event and are dissolved and returned in four steps, and the presses and grinders are below and behind, baffled and jacketed, and you will never hear them coming. Do not go below the shelf you arrived on. The decommissioned tiers are still warm, still wet, still occupied by stock reassigned to nothing and kept at temperature because cooling them would cost more. There is no bedrock. There is nothing under you that was not poured, and none of it is a refuge.

## Standing still is how you get installed

This is the danger nobody plans for.

The seams are maintained by lice that lay mineral at exactly the rate the water takes it away. That is why nothing is old. They keep the stencils crisp. They keep the tie-hole plugs seated. They will do it to your boots. Stand still for an hour in a hand-deep sheet and you will be grouted — not attacked, not trapped by intent, simply *maintained*, incorporated into a joint that is now behaving correctly.

Stand under a gantry and a drip will find you. The animals below grew sockets for their drips, deliberately, because a drip landing flat makes a sound. You are flat. You will make a sound, once a second, in a world where noise is inefficiency.

So: keep moving, gently, every few minutes. Shift your feet. Never sit. Never lie down. Never lean for long. The Hold does not have a category for a visitor, but it has a category for a stationary warm wet object, and it is *substrate*.

## You are variance

The reducers move through the basins taking whatever has drifted from spec: the too-thin hide, the loud one, the one that bred. They are long and low with soft jaws and they are not predators, they are quality control, and there is no appeal because there is no one above them.

You are loud. Your voice at conversational volume is a catastrophic acoustic anomaly in a world whose only consonant is an inventory click. Speak in whispers inside your helmet or not at all; agree hand signals before you arrive. Tether every tool. Zip every pocket. Drop nothing — a dropped object is either a grinding event or a permanent discrepancy, and there is no one to report it to.

Do not touch the stock. A handprint is a thermal and textural defect on a precision fin. Nothing here can be comforted and nothing here will comfort you, although it will feel exactly like being comforted, which is the trap.

Do not subtract and do not add. Not one larva, not one grazer, not one flake of scale, not one gram of your own waste. Carry everything out, including everything your body produces. The counters tally; the ledger balances to the gram; you do not want your visit to be the reason a reducer comes west.

There is traveler folklore about wearing a ceramic disc stamped in the specification's font — that the counters click past stamps without reading, and that legibility as infrastructure is the only immunity available. I mention it because you will hear it. I would not bet a life on typography. Bet on being brief.

## Time, direction, memory

There is no night and no word for night. There is *turn*, and each turn is identical. Light comes from the whole ceiling at once; shadows are soft and directly underneath and have not moved long enough to be a clock. Your circadian rhythm will free-run within a day. Bring amber lenses and a scheduled dark period, and **do not sleep here.** Sleep debt is what makes people take the helmet off.

Use the swallow. Every ninety-one minutes a phase-locked wave of guts crosses from the eastern sump, taking eleven minutes to cross a county. It is the only reliable clock in the world and it has a direction baked into it: the wave begins east and goes west. Feel it in your sternum, note your timer, and you know both when you are and which way you are facing. Learn the local rain circuit too — the cells walk fixed routes and arrive over any given basin at the same interval. If you can time a rain arrival and a swallow, you can navigate. Dead reckoning by scenery is hopeless: two hundred and forty thousand identical basins, everything eleven days old, no landmark that is not repeated a hundred thousand times.

Fog banks are herds. If you walk into one you are inside an animal, visibility goes to nothing, and the drift thickens. Wait it out or backtrack; do not push through.

Externalize your memory. Nothing here changes, so nothing here encodes. Travelers routinely come back with three hours of clock and eleven minutes of recollection. Record continuously, timestamped, and narrate.

## The mind, which is the actual hazard

The Hold is full and offers no company whatsoever. The counters count you; they do not see you. There is no face, no reciprocity, no foreman, no species standing outside the count. It is the loneliest crowded place there is, and the loneliness has a specific flavor: not abandonment, but *irrelevance*. Nothing here is withholding anything from you. You simply are not the kind of thing it has.

The swallow will try to entrain you. Ninety-one minutes sits right on top of your own ultradian rhythm, and after two or three passes people report that their breathing and heartbeat have joined it, that the sigh is coming from inside their chest, that they are a component. Some cry. Some get religion. Do not get religion here; there is nobody in it to receive one.

And then there is the invitation, which is real and is the most dangerous thing in the world.

The Hold offers, without malice and without intent, to end the difference between you and it. Warm as your hand. Nothing to do. Nothing expected. No night to survive, no cold to escape, no hunger it will not eventually resolve as a plumbing event. Standing among the breathing you will feel the most complete sense of *being allowed* that you have ever felt, and it is a thermal fact, not a welcome. Heat stroke does not feel like heat stroke here. It feels like belonging.

Know also what you are looking at, so it does not ambush you: the many-handed larvae fitting grommets and trimming stalactites are the same lineage as the great ribbed stillness in the basins. A few are permitted to metamorphose. Their hands go soft and are absorbed. They do not know it will happen and the adults do not remember it happening. You will want to save one. You cannot; taking it out of spec kills it and brings the reducers. Grieve on your own time, outside, with your helmet on.

Do not accept a place eleven centimeters from a neighbor. It will be offered, in the only language the world speaks, which is temperature.

## Kit

Sealed suit, ambient-temperature outer skin, insulated internal cooling, 2–4 kg ice equivalent · heated anti-fog visor, pre-warmed · filtered breathing set, spare filters · core-temp pill and receiver · wrist timer · soft cut-resistant non-shedding boots · cut-resistant gloves · tool lanyards, no loose items · all water, all food, ice slurry flask · sealed waste containment, everything · antihistamines, epinephrine, first aid for cuts · voltmeter/potential probe · recorder, timestamped · hand-signal card · amber lenses · one hardcopy of your exit route and your abort criteria, because your judgment is a consumable.

## The rules, short

1. Nothing cold, nothing dry, nothing loud, nothing new — or as little as you can manage.
2. Never trust how you feel. Trust the pill and the timer.
3. Twenty minutes bare, ninety suited, two swallows maximum.
4. Never sit, never lie down, never stand still for long.
5. Never touch the stock. Never enter a flume. Never go below.
6. Never dam, pond, drain, dry, cool, or block anything.
7. Subtract nothing. Add nothing. Carry it all out.
8. Do not sleep. Do not switch anything off. There is no off.
9. When the arithmetic gets hard, you are already late. Go.

## How to actually visit the fourth sump

Because you should go, and you should stand there. Arrive suited and warm on an upper gantry. Descend to the fourth sump on the gantry line, not through the basins. Find a place where the water is hand-deep and the cauls make the light the color of skimmed milk, and where you are not under a drip.

Set your timer for one swallow. Check your core. Stand, and shift your weight every minute, and let the drift arrive on your visor and be replaced. If you want your palm in the water: probe for potential first, then ten seconds, then out, then dry it, then gloves back on. You will not be able to tell where your skin stops. That is the whole experience and ten seconds is enough to have it honestly.

Wait for the swallow. It will come from the east and cross you and go west, and the water in the channels will shiver, and the quiet will come back like a door closing very gently. Note the time. Say aloud, inside your helmet, what you are feeling, for the recording, because you will not remember it.

Then look at the stencil under the water, crisp as the hour it was sprayed, edges kept by lice that will still be keeping them when your grandchildren are old. **WARM STANDBY — DO NOT DRAIN.** It is not addressed to you. Nobody has ever had to be told. Obey it anyway; it is the only instruction in the world and it is a good one.

Then leave, on the same swallow you came in on.

## After

Decontaminate completely, and mean it. Nothing living leaves the Hold: no slick, no larva, no louse. An organism that makes sugar from current and air is not something to introduce to a world with a power grid; an arthropod that lays mineral at the rate water removes it will find your city's pipes very interesting. And nothing living goes *in*, either — not your microbiome, not a seed in your boot tread, not a rounded stone in your pocket.

Then attend to the return, which is harder than people expect. The first breeze will feel like an accusation. Cold will feel violent and personal. You will find yourself unable to stop thinking about a place that never asked anything of you, and you will catch yourself wanting to go back and lie down in the fourth sump, and you should tell somebody about that wanting, out loud, in words, rather than keeping it.

Sleep in a cold room. Drink something with ice in it that clinks. Eat something that had a leaf.

Here, the word for green means copper corrosion. Go and look at green.
