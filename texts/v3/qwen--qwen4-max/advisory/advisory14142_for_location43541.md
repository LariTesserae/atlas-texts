# Preparing for Surge Hall Nine

## First, understand what you are actually entering

It looks like a room. It is a cave.

Sixty-one meters of perfectly legible, brightly lit, warmly comfortable water, and **no open water anywhere in it**. That combination kills people. Your eyes will report "well-lit factory floor, I can see the far wall," and your body will believe it can go up whenever it wants. It cannot. Above you, at every point except one, there is forty meters of packed leaf, ceramic rack, conduit, brace and tension rod. There is no direct ascent. There is no surface to blow up to. There is a door near the summit and a seventy-centimeter lane between you and it, and the lane has a rod across it every third meter.

Treat this as an overhead-environment penetration dive in warm bright water. Redundant gas. Thirds rule. A tender at the lock. Never alone. The clarity is a gift and a lie: it lets you *see* safety from anywhere without letting you *reach* it.

The one exception is the Eye. That six-meter empty cylinder on the axis is the only unobstructed vertical corridor in the hall, and it runs from the throat to the dome, and the door is at the top of it. **The Eye is your ascent shaft, your muster point, your reference, and the only place two people can hang side by side.** Go there first. Learn the geometry from there. Then work outward radially, bay by bay, and never let yourself get farther from the axis than you could comfortably return — and then keep a much larger margin than that.

## Before you go: decide that you are the contamination

Nothing in that hall has a mouth. Nothing in it eats. That is not a detail; it is the load-bearing fact of the entire world you are visiting. Eleven years of leaves lie in the low corners a meter deep, still green, because no decomposer has ever been admitted. That is a metastable condition. It is eleven years of held breath.

You are a bag of mouths. You carry a food web on your skin, in your gut, in the seams of your gear, in the mud in a stitching from a lake in another universe. One copepod egg. One nematode. One rot bacterium that finds a drift of undecayed leaves and discovers an eleven-year banquet with no competitors and no predators and thirty-one-degree water. If that happens, the oxygen budget inverts, the drifts go black, the sulfide comes, and Bay Eight — redundant Bay One — turns out not to be redundant enough. Behind that door is all the life for forty kilometers. You could end it by being careless with a boot.

So:

- **Everything you bring is new or sterilized.** Autoclave, gamma, whatever your transit culture offers. Nothing that has ever touched soil, fresh water, a plant, an animal, or a human mouth.
- **Nothing organic.** No cotton, wool, leather, wood, paper, wax, natural-fiber line, no wooden-handled knife, no felt padding, no beeswax seal. Synthetics, titanium, steel, glass, ceramic.
- **No aluminum, no zinc, no galvanizing, no brass fittings.** The water is weeping lime; it is strongly alkaline. Aluminum in caustic water generates hydrogen and heat and eats itself. Your gear will dissolve in a place where nothing else corrodes.
- **Understand that the water is hungry.** Bay Two strips metals for a living. This is warm, alkaline, oxygen-drunk, metal-starved water — chemically the most aggressive *clean* water you will ever put your body in. It will take ions out of your fittings and oils out of your skin. Leave the ring at home.
- **Closed circuit only. No bubbles, ever.** Open-circuit exhaust injects your oral flora directly into the water column and puts a gas pocket under the dome. A rebreather with a contained scrubber vents nothing. Bring redundancy in the loop, not a bailout that you plan to breathe.
- **No food. No waste. No bare skin. No loose hair.** Full encapsulation, sealed hood, sealed gloves.
- **Do not spit in your mask.** The oldest habit in diving is here an act of vandalism. Use a bonded hydrophilic film or a cured synthetic defog applied and finished outside the lock.
- **No cuts, no cold, no congestion, no dental work in progress.** You must not bleed in there. Blood is the first food that room has ever been offered.

And accept the honest limit: you will not be sterile. You will be as close as you can get. Contamination risk is a function of time, which is one of the several reasons your visit should be short.

## Kit

- Closed-circuit rebreather, dual redundancy, altitude-corrected computer, no aluminum anywhere in the gas path.
- Thin exposure protection only. Thirty-one degrees is blood-warm; you will overheat in a thick suit while threading, and you will slowly chill if you hang motionless in the Eye for an hour. A sealed skin with a modest thermal layer you can vent is right. Plan hydration; you will sweat inside a sealed suit and cannot drink.
- **UV-blocking visor and full skin coverage.** The light train is *stacked quartz*, not glass. Quartz passes ultraviolet. That shaft is gathering thin high-altitude sun and delivering it attenuated but honest, and "honest" includes the part of the spectrum you cannot see. In a room with no shadows there is nowhere to get out of it. Photokeratitis and underwater sunburn are real possibilities in the upper gallery. Do not press your face to the lens. Do not stare up the shaft. Do not linger in the bar of gold because it is beautiful.
- Cut-resistant gloves, low profile. The arrises will open your palm; the weld beads stand up in their original ripples; the mill scale is intact; nothing has been rounded by use. Everything in that hall is still sharp.
- Two cutting tools, secured short, and the discipline to know what you are cutting (below).
- **Nothing dangling.** No console, no octopus on a hose, no loose strap end, no lanyard, no long hair, no camera on a wrist coil. Streamline until you feel absurd, then streamline more. In seventy centimeters of lane threaded with rods and sampling lines, a dangling gauge is how you die.
- A powerful lamp — not because it is dark, but because light is the only thing that works there for signaling. Radio dies in water; acoustic comms die in Bay Seven. Hand signals, lamps, and a sealed electronic slate. No wax pencil, no graphite: nothing that sheds.
- High-visibility magenta or orange. Not green. You want to be the one thing in the room that does not look like a plant.
- A rinse kit for after: clean fresh water, and a mild acid — dilute citric or acetic — for skin, hair, ears, and gear.

## Getting there

The cone is sixty-one years old and has not learned to be a mountain. No soil, no lichen, no rounded slope, no gullies. That means no path, no handhold that isn't a blade of glass, and loose unweathered scoria on a steep angle. It is minus four and the wind has teeth, and it is high enough that the sun is thin. Helmet, eye protection against wind-driven glass, real cold-weather kit, boots you will leave in the vestibule, roped party, acclimatization beforehand. Altitude also means your decompression obligations at the far end of the visit are worse than the tables in your head.

There has never been a footprint on that slope. Think about where you put your feet. Step on rock where you can. Your boot is also carrying the outside in — and the outside is sterile glass dust that will scratch a two-meter lens and shade forty thousand blades. **Blow down and strip in the vestibule. Do not let the mountain follow you through the door.**

The door is the only door. Do not prop it. Close it behind you.

Time your entry to the pulse. Every forty seconds the regulator sends a pressure wave up from the throat; you will feel it as a hand laid flat on your chest. A hatch may be stiff against the surge and easy in the trough. Learn the rhythm in the lock, breathe through it, and count it: it will become your clock in a place with no other fast events.

## In the water

The first ten seconds are all wrong in the pleasant direction — cold air to blood-warm water, and your numb hands come back. Give yourself five minutes in the lock. Then:

**Depth and gas.** The hall is forty meters tall; the floor is five bar. The water is deliberately oxygen-rich — that is the entire product of this facility. Any extraction membrane or gas-equilibrium system you use will hand you a higher oxygen fraction than you expect, and at depth that becomes a convulsant. Keep your loop under a sane oxygen partial pressure and know that the deepest, most oxygen-saturated, most suction-dangerous volume in the hall is the one you most want to visit: the throat under the floor grate.

**You do not need to go down there.** You can see it perfectly from twelve meters. You can read the plate on the far wall from anywhere. That is the gift of this place: it lets you look directly at the dangerous thing from a safe distance. Take the gift. Stay in the upper third. Hang in the Eye at mid-height and look down.

**Equalize early and gently,** and keep equalizing on the forty-second cycle. Never breath-hold. A pressure transient that reaches your chest can reach your sinuses and your middle ear.

**Alkalinity.** Do not drink it. It tastes of new stone and chalky sweetness at the back of the throat and you will want to; that taste is lime. Keep it out of your eyes, your ears, your mouth. Do not hug the walls — the white haze clinging there is the freshest and most caustic water in the hall, and an hour of skin against it is a chemical burn on top of a cut from an arris that has never been worn smooth.

**If you find a gas space under the dome, do not breathe it.** The dome sweats condensate, so there may be a film of gas at the crown. It is not a refuge. Breaking into it changes the hall's chemistry and puts your flora in the one place the light comes through.

## Moving

Seventy centimeters, and a rod every third meter. Turn sideways. Hand over hand on structure, never on leaves. Move at about the speed you'd use in a museum full of glass. Nothing here is urgent, and your ordinary human tempo is the fastest thing that has ever happened in this room.

Learn the difference between a **tension member and a sampling line** before you touch either. Taut lines hum; slack lines drift. Bay Three grows rope with a nine-tonne breaking strain around a nylon core, and the plants have taken up their tension rods as they grew — you can hear them doing it, small dry clicks like a knuckle in another room. A structural member under that kind of load will whip when it parts, and it will take your face off, and then it will drop a plant into the water column that was never meant to be there. **Cut only what you are certain is not structural.** If you are snagged and unsure, stop, breathe, signal, and let your buddy solve it. Do not solve it with a knife.

Do not use leaves as handholds. Bay One's forty thousand blades are serial-numbered in enamel along the midrib; they are the oxygen budget. Bay Six is a thermometer — bruise it and you make the hall lie to itself. Bay Two's rust-tipped leaves are a hazardous-metals concentrator; do not touch them with a torn glove and absolutely do not put them in your mouth. Bay Four's gaskets may be an allergen you have never met.

**Leave the drifts alone.** The meter-deep layers of perfect green leaves in the low corners are the strangest thing in the room and the most fragile. They are also stratigraphy: eleven years in order, a book nobody has opened. Your fin kick will open it, hang a white cloud in the water for days, and leave a hole that will not close in your lifetime — the hall's water moves at the speed of a minute hand's shadow. Under those drifts the bacterial films work at their own patient pace; do not assume the water in there is the water out here.

## The machines

The gantry travels a centimeter an hour. That is twenty-four centimeters a day, and it ratchets one tooth a minute, and it trims to the drawing, and it does not know you exist. It reads leaf color; it does not read people. It is the slowest lethal thing you will ever meet: if you are wedged, or unconscious, or asleep, or simply resting in a lane, it will find you and it will not stop and you will not feel it moving until you cannot get out. The brass brushes pollinate one flower every eleven seconds and will brush your eyeball with the same indifference. The macerating grates handle death.

**Never rest in a lane. Rest in the Eye.**

The ring of intakes that holds the Eye empty is a suction boundary. Keep fingers, hair, and straps out of it. And understand the plumbing: anything you drop is gone. It goes to a grate or down the penstock, fourteen hundred meters, into a turbine hall nobody has entered in nine years, where something is either turning or isn't. Your dropped knife becomes an offering to a machine that does not answer. Carry nothing you cannot afford to lose forever.

## What it will do to your head

Prepare for this part properly; it is the part people are not ready for.

**The silence is not peaceful, it is subtractive.** Bay Seven was planted to eat echo and it works. In a sixty-one-meter room you will hear your own body first and loudest: heart, blood, breath, joints, the wet click of your own swallowing. Then the forty-second pulse, more felt than heard. Then the gantry's tooth. Then stems taking up tension. After an hour, some people start hearing things that are not there — the mind manufactures sound when the room refuses to provide any. Know that this is normal and let it happen without chasing it.

**The pulse will try to become your heartbeat.** People come out breathing on the forty-second cycle without noticing. Count it out loud in your head, on purpose, so it stays outside you.

**The legibility is its own vertigo.** No shadow deep enough to keep a secret. Every serial visible. You can read the far plate. There is nowhere to be private, nowhere to be small, nowhere to look that isn't fully explained. Combined with the acoustic void, this produces a very specific dissociation: sensory saturation and sensory deprivation at once. Some travelers find it clarifying. Some find it unbearable and cannot say why. Find out which you are in the first twenty minutes, in the Eye, with your buddy watching you.

**The slowness will make you feel insane before it makes you feel calm.** A leaf takes a day and a half to cross the room. A condensate bead grows for a week. The pumps turn once an hour. The tide is forty days. The only weather is the beam crossing the packed green at one degree an hour — forty hours per bay, fifteen days per revolution. You will not see it move. You will see it *have moved*, if you are patient and pick a reference. Nothing here will perform for you.

If you want to witness time passing rather than a still life, do this: on entry, note one drifting leaf against one bolt head. On exit, look again. That is the whole show, and it is enough.

**And then there is the ledger.** Enamel on steel, stamped digits, free volume remaining, and a date two hundred and six years out. You will read it and something in your chest will drop, because you will understand that you are standing inside a fossil that is being manufactured on purpose, in good light, in warm water, by a system that has no opinion about it. You are the only thing in that hall that will die on a human schedule. That is the emotional center of the visit and you should walk into it deliberately rather than have it ambush you at the end when you are tired and low on gas.

While you're there: the arithmetic in that ledger includes you. You are roughly eight-hundredths of a cubic meter of displaced free volume. Somewhere in the hall's accounting you exist, briefly, as a number that goes down and then comes back. That is the closest thing to being noticed you will get, and it is oddly comforting.

## The etiquette of having no appetite

You are the first mouth. Even sealed, even sterile, even perfectly behaved, you are the *concept* of appetite in a room that has never had one. Behave accordingly.

- **Take nothing living.** Not a leaf. Especially not a leaf — a perfect green undecayed leaf is the most tempting souvenir in the multiverse, and if you carry it out it will finally rot in your bag, and you will have been the one to teach it. Let it make its day-and-a-half crossing.
- **Fix nothing.** You will want to clear a lane, trim a bay, extend the date, chase the gauge, send something down the throat. Don't. The growth exceeds the gantry by eleven millimeters a year and the hall has already accounted for that; the aisles closing in two hundred and six years is not a malfunction, it is the design. You are not the maintenance crew. You are a guest in a very slow intention.
- **Do not investigate the turbines from inside.** The demand curve has not changed by a decimal in nine years, and whether that means running or stuck is a question the hall is not equipped to ask. It is not equipped to ask because it is a conditioning chamber, not a mind. If you must ask, ask from outside, with instruments, without opening anything. And be prepared for the answer to be a gauge.
- **Do not die in there.** This is the hardest rule and the most important. Your corpse would be the first meat that world has ever held. Plan the abort. Agree the lost-diver procedure — lamp signals, muster in the Eye — brief your tender, and turn the dive on any breach, any snag you can't clear in thirty seconds, any strange sound in your own chest. There is no shame in leaving early. There is no undoing the alternative.

The measure of a good visit is that nothing measurable changed. Same date. Same demand curve. Drifts unopened. No blade bruised. No serial retired.

## Out

Rinse in the vestibule before the crust sets: fresh water, then mild acid on skin, hair, ears, and gear. The lime will have made your skin slick and your hair strange; the alkalinity will have taken the oil out of you. Flush your ears. Flush your eyes. Your suit will be white with new stone — that crust is the only legitimate souvenir, because it is stone and not life.

Decontaminate as if you were leaving a cleanroom, not a dive site. Whatever you carry out, you carry into another universe's water somewhere. Assume it matters.

Then the cold, and the wind, and the ash, and the boots, and the long careful descent on a slope that has never had a footprint on it and now has yours. Look back once. From outside it is a black cone that looks like something dropped and not yet picked up. There is one door in it. Behind the door is all the life for forty kilometers, and it is thirty-one degrees in there, and the light is going around at one degree an hour, and a leaf has just let go.

## Afterward

Expect the pulse to stay in your chest for a few days. Expect to notice rot everywhere you go — the fruit bowl, the leaves in the gutter, the smell of a forest floor — and to understand for the first time that rot is *appetite*, that the whole ordinary world is being eaten continuously and that you have visited the one room where it isn't. Expect to dream in gold-green with no sound.

Expect, some weeks later, to catch yourself wanting to go back and *help*. That feeling is the real hazard of Surge Hall Nine, and it does not show up on any checklist. The hall knows the number and has no opinion about the number. You will have opinions. Carry them out with you and leave the water alone.

5. Take the lime crust, the photograph, and the number. Leave the leaf.
