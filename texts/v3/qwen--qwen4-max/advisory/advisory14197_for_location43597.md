# Preparing to Visit the Bowl City

## First, understand what you are visiting

It is not a ruin, not a cave, not a wilderness. It is a **finished instrument in a closed steady state**, and both of those facts are safety information.

*Finished* means there is no repair process. No soil, no growth, no talus, no succession, no new anything. A scratch you make will not weather into a scar; it will simply be a scratch, forever, on a work that was tuned once and stopped. *Steady state* means the room can absorb nothing — not your lint, not your breath's carbon, not your dinner, not you. There is no decay economy here large enough to process a human being. If you die in that room, your body will be the single largest nutrient event in its history and will end it as surely as a fire.

So the correct mental category is not "hike." It is **cave dive, plus cleanroom, plus cathedral**. You are going into an overhead environment with one door, no rescue, no light, no communication, active cold, and an object of irreplaceable value that you can damage by speaking.

Decide now, warm and dry, that you are an audience. Not a participant, not a surveyor, not a collaborator. It does not need your voice. The stopping is part of the work.

---

## The four things that will kill you

**1. Cold, quietly.** Air at 11 °C, saturated, water a hair colder, and it never stops taking heat from you. This is not dramatic cold; it is arithmetic. Bare and wet, you lose useful hands in ten minutes and useful judgment in thirty. The trap is that the room's loudness functions as silence, and hypothermia functions as peace, and the two together produce a person who is content, thinking nothing, cooling smoothly toward death. **The first symptom of serious trouble is that the cold stops bothering you.** Treat that as a red alert, not as arrival.

**2. Water at the doorway.** The room is buffered against a year of weather. *The entrance is not.* Bare rock above means zero infiltration: rain becomes sheets immediately and seams become firehoses. The country's permanent weather is "just stopped or about to start." A storm you cannot see, over a catchment you have not walked, can fill your one door while you are forty paces from it. This is the most likely way to die here and the easiest to prevent.

**3. The sound.** Loud enough that speech is pointless; a bass you feel in the sternum and teeth. Assume 100–115 dB sustained, possibly more, with significant low-frequency energy. Meter it at the lip before committing. Above 100 dB you are buying minutes, not hours. Low frequencies near 20 Hz can resonate your eyeballs and smear the grey needle of light — that is your eyes, not the room; don't panic. Plugs will not save you from bone conduction; you will hear it through your skull whatever you do.

**4. No rescue.** Twenty feet of shoulder-wide wet crawl, then ramps one body wide, then partitions a finger thick. Nobody is carrying an unconscious adult out of there quickly. Treat every decision as you would on a free solo: no move you cannot reverse, nothing you cannot afford to lose.

## Rules to fix before you go

Write these somewhere you can read them while warm, then memorize them, because inside you will not be able to think and will not want to.

- **One person in the room.** The ramps are one body wide; the wear is one stride, one circuit. The place is built for a single walker. One in, one at the lip with the tether, one on the rock above watching the whole catchment.
- **Thirty minutes in the keel. Hard cap forty-five.** Exit on the timer, on the first involuntary shiver, or on the first moment you notice you've stopped noticing the cold — whichever comes first.
- **Stay in the keel.** Do not climb. Not one tier.
- **Touch nothing that is not a worn handhold or the keel stone.** No cell lips, no bowls, no bone pegs, no cord, no copper tabs, no chisel mark.
- **Take nothing. Add nothing. Mark nothing.** Not a bowl, not a flake, not a rubbing, not a chalk dot, not a second chisel stroke beside the first.
- **Say nothing.** More on this below.
- **Do not walk the full circuit.** Leave the loop unclosed.

## Clothing and the cold budget

You need to be warm, streamlined, silent, and *non-shedding*, which rules out most normal outdoor clothing.

- **Smooth-skin closed-cell neoprene, 3 mm, with hood**, or a drysuit with a slick outer shell. No fleece, no wool, no cotton, no lining that can loft fibers. A single thread of your world's fabric in that room is a permanent foreign object.
- **Sealed cuffs and neck.** You shed skin constantly; every flake is a nutrient packet in a system that runs on iron and sulfur.
- **Smooth-soled boots**, no aggressive lugs (they carry grit and mud), no buckles, no metal eyelets, no hard heel that can click on stone.
- **Gloves are the hard trade-off.** Hands are how you read this city, and 3 mm neoprene reads nothing. Plan short bare-handed intervals — five minutes, then hands back inside the suit against your own trunk — and accept that you will feel less than you came to feel.
- **Soft low-profile helmet**, foam shell, silent strap. A hard hat striking a facet is a semitone flinch across ten thousand voices.
- **No pack.** Nothing bulky will fit the seam or the ramps. One flat chest pouch, taped shut, everything inside it tethered to you with soft cord.
- **Absolutely no chemical heat.** No flame — nothing here has ever burned, and soot on those facets would be the first fire in the place's history. No hand warmers: an oxidizing-iron warmer is, quite literally, an offering of food to the veils you are trying not to feed. No glow sticks, no snap-cracks, nothing that leaks.

Eat and hydrate before. Empty your bladder before. No alcohol, no going in tired or already chilled — shivering is metabolism and you need the fuel. Plan for waste: everything you produce leaves with you, including urine. Nothing goes into the keel. Nothing gets spat, blown, or dripped.

---

## Cleanliness: you are a nutrient bomb

Eighty kilos of carbon in a room whose entire biology is eleven species eating rock. Assume nothing about whether your microbes can survive here; assume everything about whether your *body* can feed what lives here.

- **Mechanical cleaning, not chemical.** Scrub and rinse gear to bare, then rinse again. Do not sterilize with bleach, alcohol, or biocides — residue in that seep is poison to a closed eleven-species system, and there is no dilution, no flush, no recovery.
- **Dress in a clean staging area, then rinse the outside of the suit** after dressing.
- **Nothing porous, nothing edible, nothing that smells.** No food even sealed, no gum, no tobacco, no leather, no paper, no wood.
- **Keep your face away from the tiers.** Do not lean into a cell to look. Do not breathe on the veils. Your breath is CO₂ and water and aerosol in a saturated carbonate system; it etches or it precipitates. The visitor puts its head into cells. You are not the visitor.
- **Do not touch the low slimy tiers at all.** That is the grazed, living zone. And do not touch the papery grey crust in the high dry cells — its margin has not moved in living memory, and your fingerprint could be the first new thing to happen to it in a thousand years.

Camp downstream, far off, on bare rock. Nothing left, nothing washed, nothing spilled anywhere upstream: with no soil, everything on the surface goes straight into the seams.

## Silence discipline: the chord is your visibility

This is the single most important operational idea. You navigate by sound and touch. **A broadband transient — a dropped tool, a shout, a boot scuff — masks the room and acoustically blinds you until it rings down.** In a high-Q cavity that can be minutes. This is your silt-out. Protect the soundscape exactly as a cave diver protects visibility.

- Tape every closure. No velcro, no zippers, no carabiners, no jangling. Nothing rigid that can fall, nothing metal that can strike. Thin-walled stone bowls, ten thousand of them, and no way to make another.
- Move on the wet stone slowly, feet placed, never slid.
- **Keep all lines slack, soft, and heavy.** A taut cord in moving water is a string instrument; you will add a note that isn't yours.
- **Learn the local language before you go.** Mouth to ear, or consonants tapped on a collarbone. Practice it with your partner in the dark, cold, until it's fluent. Understand that this means touching someone's collarbone with your fingers, or their mouth at your ear; be ready for that intimacy and don't flinch from it.
- **Nothing you say is private.** The room folds your voice into itself for hours. If you speak, you are composing, badly, into a finished piece. Decide in advance to be silent, and prepare a way to be moved without sound. Practice being moved without sound; it is harder than it seems.

## Light and eyes

You will not see this city. You will hear it and touch it.

Full dark adaptation takes thirty to forty minutes; do most of it *outside*, in shade, warm, before you crawl, then let the fissure finish the job. Sit at the lip in the dark for a few minutes and let the crowd resolve into the chord — that resolution is also your ears calibrating, and it's the threshold you should not skip.

Bring **one dim red light**, wrist-mounted, tactile switch, used sparingly and pointed low: at the keel, at your hands, at the chisel mark from a distance. Never sweep it across the tiers. A floodlamp turns an instrument into a spectacle and gives you a memory in colors the room does not contain. The fauna are blind and the veils don't photosynthesize, so light is not the ecological sin here — but it is heat, and it is a lie about what the place is like.

Come home with almost no images. That's correct.

## Movement, and the large visitor

The keel is a shallow V with a forearm-wide stream. Stand in it. Let the water go over your boots at the same speed it went over the boots of whoever cut the mark. That is the whole visit, and it is enough.

- **Do not trust the peg-ladders or the lime-glassy cord.** Nothing in there is load-rated; nothing has ever held anything heavier than the walker and the visitor. The concave cell lips are worn thin by millennia of hands — they are the *weakest* part, not the handiest.
- **Avoid the throat at the low end.** It's a rock pipe with falling water; anything dropped near it is gone. It also produces the bass that you feel in your teeth. Don't stand over it.
- **Listen for holes.** The visitor puts its head into a dozen cells; a dozen adjacent voices going out, and the absence *moving*, is your announcement. If you hear that, press flat against the keel wall, kill your light, and let it pass. Do not block a ramp. Do not illuminate it. Do not feed it, follow it, or touch it. It walks the ramps without dislodging anything, which is more than you can promise.
- Meeting it in the seam itself is the bad case. Another reason for a watcher at the lip who can tell you, by tether-taps, that something has gone down.

## Clocks

Your devices may or may not behave; the wear on the ramps is fresh and *never any fresher*, which should make you humble about local time. Use three redundant clocks:

1. **A vibrating timer against your collarbone.** Nothing beeps. Nothing will be heard.
2. **Your partner and the tether,** with a pre-agreed pull code, and an unconditional haul at T+45 regardless of what you signal back.
3. **The city itself.** Near the entrance, find one slow bowl — a quarter-hour tipper — learn its note, and count its cycles. Ten thousand periods from a heartbeat to fifteen minutes: this room is also a clock, external to you and to your possibly-unreliable instruments. It's the most trustworthy timekeeper you'll have. It is also one more reason never to remove a bowl.

## The doorway

- Walk the catchment, not just the sky. Bare rock means instantaneous runoff.
- Go in a dry window with a watcher whose only job is weather and abort.
- **Rehearse the crawl** before the trip: twenty feet, shoulder-width, wet, cold, loud, no turning around. Arms forward, one shoulder leading, exhale to pass. Rehearse it blind. Rehearse being dragged out of it limp, with a soft sling, by two people, so that if it ever happens the people doing it have done it before.
- Stage dry clothes, a shelter, a hypothermia wrap, and hot sweet drinks in a sealed smooth bag right at the seam. **You keep cooling after you exit** — the surface is wet rock at eleven degrees and it is about to rain. Plan extraction all the way to somewhere warm, and don't let the cold-impaired person drive.

---

## What to bring home

- **A hydrophone**, in a soft sleeve, lowered into the keel stream. The water couples everything to everything; you get the whole city and the throat's bass without touching a single surface. This is the only recording method that leaves no mark.
- No adhesives, no putty, no contact-mic residue, no rubbings of the mark, no samples of anything.
- No census. Nobody has finished counting the cells; leave it unfinished. Leave the eleventh animal unverified. Resist the urge to complete this place in any way — it was completed already, by someone else, and then stopped.
- Warn yourself about playback: the bass is felt, not heard, and you will be tempted to turn it up until it is. Don't hurt yourself trying to recreate a room in a living room.

---

## Two things to sit with beforehand

**The stride.** Before you commit, measure the dished treads against your own stride length. One circuit, one set of feet, one stride, fresh and never fresher. If it matches yours, think very carefully about whether you want to go in, and in any case do not close the loop. If you meet the walker: yield, don't follow, don't ask. Put your mouth to their ear only if they offer theirs.

**The afterimage.** A loudness that complete does not stop when you leave. Many people carry the fifth around in silence for weeks — in a quiet kitchen, in a parked car, at the edge of sleep. It is not damage; or it is not only damage. Prepare for it as you'd prepare for a grief you have chosen: something finished, that you were allowed to stand inside for half an hour, that did not need you and was not improved by you.

That is the whole etiquette. Go in cold-proofed, clean, silent, tethered, alone, on a clock. Stand in the keel. Take nothing, add nothing, mark nothing, say nothing. Leave before you stop wanting to.
