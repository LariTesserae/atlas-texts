# Preparing for the Pavement

## 1. Decide, honestly, whether you want to go

There is nothing to see there. Understand this before you spend anything.

No wreck, no vent, no reef, no wall, no creature that has ever needed to be beautiful. The plain is ninety meters down in water that has not been warm since a February nobody remembers, and it is flat, and it is grey, and it is the same in every direction for three days' slow swimming. The single event of note happens roughly twice a week, lasts a minute, and involves a fish hitting a shell against a rock.

If you go expecting spectacle you will be bored within an hour and cold within two, and boredom plus cold plus dark plus a decompression obligation is how people die in places like this.

What the plain actually offers is a *condition*. It is a finished world. Everything that can be removed from it has been removed; everything that happens in it happens in order to keep it exactly as it is. You are going to spend a week inside a process that produces nothing, and if you are the right kind of traveler that will be the most affecting thing you have ever done, and if you are not it will be an expensive way to be uncomfortable.

Go if you want to know what patience feels like from the inside. Don't go for the fish. The fish is a rumor.

## 2. The body problem

You are ordinary, which means you are a warm bag of gas at one atmosphere, and this place is ten atmospheres of near-freezing water with no gas anywhere in it. Every difficulty you will have descends from that mismatch, and most of the interesting difficulties are not safety problems but *manners* problems, which I'll come to.

You have three realistic ways in.

**The bounce dive.** Surface-supplied or trimix scuba, one descent, forty minutes on the bottom, hours of decompression. This is the wrong answer and I want to be explicit about why. You will spend your entire visit either descending, ascending, or hanging on a line in the clear water above the dust ceiling, unable to go back down. You will get one look at the mosaic in whatever light that particular hour happens to offer. You will not see a storm, you will not see the film stripped and regrown, and your odds of being present for one of the fish's two weekly minutes are close to nil. You will come home with a photograph and no knowledge.

**The one-atmosphere exosuit.** Articulated, thrustered, no decompression, no narcosis, no gas planning. Excellent for the storm — you can sit out on the plain while the floor re-lays itself and feel the front pass through the aluminum. Two costs. First, you cannot feel anything, and the soap-slickness of the stones is arguably the entire point of the place. Second, you are a large hollow ringing object in a world with no hollow objects, and every joint and every thruster is a noise that has never existed here.

**Saturation.** This is the honest way. A small habitat held *above* the dust ceiling, in the clear water at thirty or forty meters, with the storage depth set to the plain — ninety meters, ten bar. You live at pressure. You commute down a line each day and work for hours instead of minutes. You decompress once, at the end, over three to five days, and then you go home.

Saturation buys you the only thing that matters here: enough consecutive days to see the nine-day breath of the light, one storm, and the four-day settling afterward. Budget three weeks minimum, four if you can. Anything shorter and you are sampling a cycle you cannot perceive.

Do not put the habitat on the bottom. It will scour a pit around itself, it will be undermined as the cobbles roll, and — worse — it will become the only shelter for three days in any direction, and you will have accidentally built a reef in a world whose defining rule is that there is no shelter. Hang it in the water column. Commute.

## 3. Cold, and the lie the floor tells

One point eight degrees, stable to a tenth of a degree across the year. There is no warm patch, no thermocline to duck into, no sun-warmed shallows. There is nowhere to get warm except inside your own equipment.

Water pulls heat out of you roughly twenty-five times faster than air at the same temperature. At 1.8 °C an unprotected hand is painful in under a minute and useless in five to ten. Plan accordingly:

- Drysuit, thick wool or fleece under it, **argon** for suit inflation rather than air, and active heating — battery vest at minimum, hot-water suit if you're surface-supported. Heat is not comfort here; heat is dexterity, and dexterity is survival.
- Dry gloves with removable liners. Bring three sets of liners. They will get wet from inside.
- On heliox at ten bar, most of your heat loss goes out through your lungs. **Heated breathing gas is not optional.** Without it you will be hypothermic while feeling merely chilly, which is the dangerous form.
- Keep a chemical heat pack in a thigh pocket, and a flask of hot sweet liquid in the habitat.

And learn the local lie: the stones feel colder than the water. They are not. They are the same 1.8 °C, but rock conducts heat out of your palm far faster than water does, so your nerves report a temperature that does not exist. This matters practically, because it means every casual touch — a knee set down, a hand steadying yourself, a palm resting on the floor while you look at something — is a real and rapid heat drain that your instincts will over-report and your discipline will under-manage. **Don't kneel. Don't lie down. Don't rest on the bottom.** Hover. It is also the only polite posture, for reasons below.

## 4. The gas, and the tax on leaving

At ten bar, air is a poison twice over: oxygen at 2.1 bar partial pressure will convulse you, and nitrogen at nearly eight bar will make you stupid enough to enjoy it. Nobody breathes air at ninety meters.

For a saturation stay you'll live on heliox at something like four to five percent oxygen. **That mixture will kill you at the surface** — it is hypoxic above about ten meters. Label everything. Check everything. The gas that keeps you alive down there is unsurvivable up here, and the transition is where saturation divers actually die.

If you're doing excursion mixes rather than pure heliox, keep your equivalent narcotic depth under thirty meters. Narcosis in a place with no landmarks, no urgency cues, and a continuous soothing hiss is not funny-drunk; it is a quiet, contented failure to notice that four hours have passed and your hands have stopped working.

On a rebreather, carbon dioxide is your real predator. Dense gas, cold, hard work, and an environment with zero alarm signals — nothing here will startle you into checking your instruments. Set a timer. Check on the timer, not on feeling.

And plan the exit before the entrance. From ninety meters you cannot swim up. You cannot bolt. Whatever happens — mask lost, suit flooded, light gone, storm arriving, panic — your response has to be a rehearsed procedure that ends at a line you can find by touch, because you will not be able to see it. **The plain's calm is a trap that makes people forget they are on a leash.**

## 5. What arrival is actually like

Nobody warns you about the ceiling.

You drop from the boat through ordinary clear cold water, green-grey, full of the ordinary falling snow of a productive sea. At fifty, sixty meters you see below you what looks like a seabed — a flat grey floor, faintly luminous, much too shallow. That is not the bottom. That is the top of the flour.

You go into it and you go completely blind. Not dim: blind. Twenty or thirty meters of vertical fall with no up, no down, no direction, only your own bubbles and the pressure in your ears. It is the closest thing to sensory deletion a diver ever gets and it lasts about half a minute.

Then you fall out the bottom of it and there is a *room*.

A glowing grey ceiling twenty meters over your head. A floor of polished stones running away into dimness in every direction. A wan blue-grey light with no source and no shadows. You have descended through the sky and come out underneath it, and you will have the unmistakable feeling of being indoors, in a building the size of a county, with the lights left on very low.

Sit there for a while before you do anything. That feeling is the place's honest first impression and it will not come again.

On the way back up you will do it in reverse, and you will burst out of the grey into sunlight, and it will be unbearable, and you will understand what everything down there has never had.

## 6. Light discipline

Your eyes need thirty to forty-five minutes to fully dark-adapt. In the calm weeks the ambient light on the plain is genuinely within reach of rod vision — you can see your fingers, you can see the mosaic, you can see the pale stripe of the old river channel. Give yourself the forty minutes. Do not shortcut it with a lamp.

Then use as little artificial light as you can get away with.

- Deep red, lowest usable output, on a wrist or a chest mount rather than a hand. A handheld white torch is a searchlight in a world whose brightest event is a cloudy afternoon.
- Assume red is not invisible. Use the least light that works, pointed down, never swept.
- White light and photography: budget it. Ten minutes per day, deliberately, at the end of an excursion, not throughout.

Two reasons, one selfish and one not.

Selfish: your torch shows you liver-pink and smoke-grey and the iron bruise inside the quartz, and the dull rose of the coralline scab, and the golden-brown of the diatom film — colors that are *real* but that nothing here has ever seen and that you will mistake for the truth. Spend most of your time in the native non-light so that when you do turn the lamp on you know you are looking at a translation.

Unselfish: a lamp on the plain is a novel celestial object. It will pull amphipods out of the flour into a column, it will draw the falling snow, it will change what the crab does for hours afterward, and it will hang in the water as a beacon long after you've moved on. You are not illuminating a scene. You are introducing a sun.

## 7. The one bare hand

You will want to touch a stone with your skin. The description is right: soap, and the shoulders of something very old, and the faint one-cell bloom that makes it slip.

No instrument records that. No photograph, no sample, no note. It is the single piece of this place that cannot be carried out, and it is worth the risk if you take the risk properly:

Once, on a calm day, mid-excursion, warm and dry and with your buddy holding your arm. Glove off one hand. Fingertips on one stone. **Count out loud to twenty.** Glove back on, liner back in, heat pack in the fist, and stay in the water another twenty minutes before you go up. Do not do it at the end of a dive, do not do it when you're already chilled, and do not do it twice because the first time was too short.

Twenty seconds is enough. It is astonishing how much comes through in twenty seconds.

## 8. You are the only hollow thing here

This is the part that takes travelers by surprise, and it is the part I most want you to prepare for.

There is no air in this world. Nothing is empty. Nothing can ring. The cobbles are wedged so tight that a rolling stone's *tock* dies within a stone's width. Under everything is a hiss like a hand pushed into a sack of rice, and after an hour that hiss becomes your definition of silence.

You bring lungs, a mask, a suit, a counterlung, a habitat, a gas space in your drysuit, and — if you're on open circuit — a continuous roaring column of bubbles. In acoustic terms you are a cathedral organ dropped into a library.

So:

**Go rebreather.** Not for gas economy. For manners. Bubbles here are not noise; they are the only *loud* thing that has ever happened, and they lift flour off the bottom into plumes that hang for hours.

**Use wired comms to the habitat.** Through-water acoustic comms and the fish's knocks occupy the same conceptual space: they are content-bearing sound in an ocean of noise-without-content. Every acoustic transmission you make is jamming the only channel that means anything. Reserve it for emergencies. Low power, short, and never near the northern scarp.

**Never tap your cylinder.** Not to signal, not to test, not out of idle curiosity. Three knocks, a pause, two more, is a sentence in a language with exactly one speaker. Do not say words in it.

And learn the local acoustic rule so you can use it: **work does not make noise here.** The storm will not sound like a storm. Your ears will report a hush and a thickening of the hiss while your chest and sinuses register a low grinding shove. If your body feels pressed and your ears feel nothing, a front is coming. Trust the chest over the ear.

One more thing, physiological and cruel: underwater you cannot tell where sound comes from. It reaches both ears through your skull almost instantly. When you hear the knocks — and if you're lucky you will — you will have absolutely no idea which direction they came from. There is no swimming toward them. There is only knowing where the corner is, beforehand, and being there.

## 9. Navigation on a floor that forgets

There are no landmarks. There is no stratigraphy. Any arrangement you memorize will be gone within nine days. Worse: your own fin kicks scour bright patches and wipe the film off stone tops, and those marks last for days in calm weather, which means **you can and will follow your own trail in a circle** and believe you are exploring.

What is actually reliable:

- **The scarp on the northern side.** The one real feature. Internal waves break on it. Find it on day one, log its bearing and depth, and treat it as your north wall.
- **The paleochannel** — a stripe of coarser gravel running across the plain, a river's ghost. It is a compass line you can *feel through a glove*. It is the only road. Learn its trend and use it as a handrail.
- **The eastern megaripples**, half a meter high, ten meters crest to crest. Their orientation tells you about the flow; their position does not tell you anything, because they march a couple of meters a day. Never use a ripple as a waypoint.
- **The gabbro corner.** Foreign, angular, the only edge in the world — and movable, and buriable, and the fish loses it too. Survey it fresh each visit. It may also nudge a magnetic compass at close range; don't take a bearing with your face against it.

Everything else by instrument. Inertial navigation, a Doppler log, a short-baseline acoustic array — but moor the transponders *above* the abrasive zone, in the clear water, or the rolling gravel will undermine and topple them within a week. And know that a Doppler log's bottom-lock is measuring a floor that creeps: fine in calm weather, garbage during a storm, exactly when you need it.

Lay as little physical line on the bottom as you can. It will be rolled over, buried, abraded, and it will become a snag hazard in zero visibility. Where you must, make it taut, elevated, and retrievable in one pull.

**Tether everything. Count everything in and count everything out.** Weight is the only right to remain here. Anything light that you drop will travel; anything heavy that you drop will stay, forever, as a foreign object in a world with exactly one foreign object already. If you lose a tool you have to find it, in the dark, on a moving floor. Make that impossible by design.

## 10. The storm

It comes at walking pace, which is faster than you can swim with a drysuit and a rebreather for any useful distance. **You cannot outswim the front. You must leave before it arrives.** So learn its precursors and treat them as an evacuation order, not a curiosity:

1. The ceiling begins to descend and dim over hours.
2. The flour organizes into visible sideways ribbons on the tide.
3. The hiss thickens and coarsens.
4. Your chest and sinuses register a low pressure gradient your ears don't report.
5. The megaripples in the east start moving faster than a body-length a day.

Any two of these together: go up. Not to the surface — to the habitat, above the ceiling, in the clear.

On the bottom during a front, the danger is not being crushed. Cobbles move a hand's breadth. The danger is a knee or an ankle or a hose trapped between rolling stones; abrasion through a suit at a joint; gear buried under a migrating megaripple in an hour; and total disorientation in a black with no direction in it, with your own bubbles screaming and your compass useless and your ears reporting nothing. If you are caught: get off the bottom, get neutral, get a hand on your line, and stop trying to solve anything.

If you want to *watch* the storm — and you should, it is the great spectacle, the whole floor pulled apart and reassembled with the same tiles — do it from the exosuit, or from a standoff position at the plain's edge with a taut reference and a clear route up. Watch the leading edge arrive as a grey wall forty meters tall. Watch the hundred thousand stones begin to stutter. Then go up and listen to the hydrophone and feel it in your sternum and understand that you are hearing an enormous amount of work being done at almost no volume, forever, for nothing.

Afterward: four days of settling. The ceiling rises. The light comes back. Don't miss the return. It's the best thing here.

## 11. What you will contaminate

You are a hundred watts of body heat in a place that varies by a tenth of a degree a year. You are the only underside, the only overhang, the only crevice for three days' travel. You are a hard vertical surface in a world where everything that wants to attach must attach to something that rolls.

This means:

- **You will become habitat.** Amphipods in your gear folds. Brittle stars under anything you set down. Possibly the fish under the habitat. Decide *in advance* what you'll do about it, because the wrong answer — picking up your bag and leaving — is an eviction. Set nothing down that you intend to retrieve. Hover.
- **Biosecurity is real.** The coralline scab at the scarp lip grows a millimeter a year. The dropstone fell eleven thousand years ago. This is a slow, sparse, isolated system and you have just come from somewhere else. Scrub, disinfect, rinse, and dry everything before it goes in. No wood, no cotton, no natural fiber, no organic anything on the outside of your kit. No food on the plain, ever, not a crumb. No ballast water. Nothing alive.
- **Don't touch the coralline.** A single fin kick removes decades. Stay two meters off the scarp lip and look.
- **Budget your touches.** Everything you set a hand on wipes a coin of the meadow — the entire base of the food web, one cell thick. It regrows in days. It is not catastrophic. But count them. Keep a literal tally in your log. It will change how you move, and moving carefully is most of what respect means here.

## 12. The fish, and the specific temptation

Seventy centimeters, heavy-headed, thick-lipped, mottled exactly like the pavement. It will not perform for you. It is patient in a way you are not built to be patient.

To have any chance: find the corner first, on a survey, without the fish present. Then come back and sit mid-water downcurrent of it, lights off, dark-adapted, neutral, doing nothing, for hours. Rebreather, no bubbles, no acoustic comms, no tapping. Repeat across days. If you hear knocks you will not know where they came from, so the only strategy is to already be in the right place when it happens.

And then the temptation, which is the real hazard of this location, more than the cold or the gas:

**Do not help.**

Do not bring clams. Do not chip a better corner. Do not clear the flour away from the anvil so it's easier to find. Do not set the gabbro on a stable base. Do not make a second anvil, or a better one, or leave a marker so you can relocate it. Do not, under any circumstances, pick it up and take it home. It is not an artifact. It is a stone that becomes a tool for about a minute and then stops being one. All of its value is in the relation between it and a habit inside a fish's body. In your bag it is a rock, and you have destroyed the only construction in three days' travel in exchange for nothing at all.

The deep sin here is *gifts*. This is a world with no accumulation, no deposit, no history, no stratigraphy — a state that is refreshed rather than developed. Anything you leave that persists is a violation of its nature so total that it dwarfs any physical damage you could do. No cairns. No plaques. No markers. No improved anvil. No convenient pile of clams. You would be introducing history into a place that has spent three hundred million years getting rid of it.

You are allowed to be a memory. You are not allowed to build one.

## 13. Time, and your mind

The hiss becomes silence after an hour. That is not a metaphor; it is a documented perceptual event and it is the beginning of the trouble. With no sound cues, no light cues but a nine-day breath, no landmarks, no company, and mild cold narcosis, your time sense will simply stop. Divers in places like this surface convinced they've been down forty minutes and find it's been three hours.

- Keep a hard 24-hour schedule inside the habitat. Light on, light off, meals at fixed times. Do not let the plain's rhythm become yours.
- Set excursion timers and obey them without negotiating.
- A surface partner with a check-in cadence and authority to call you up.
- A fixed return date, decided before you go, that you are not permitted to extend from below.

And know the emotional shape of it. There is no company here — the water is packed with matter and almost bare of life. A shark passes overhead once or twice a year and it is the biggest event of the month. The crab takes a season to cross. You may feel a grief with no object, or the sense of being watched by nothing, or an enormous flat peace that is not consolation but simply the absence of anything left to be done.

Some travelers find that peace and do not want to leave. That is the actual risk of this location. It offers you the rest of a finished thing, and it is very persuasive, and it is not for you. You are a creature that develops. This place isn't. Admire it from outside.

## 14. Kit, condensed

Drysuit, argon bottle, three sets of glove liners, wool base layers ×3, battery heating ×2 with spare packs, heated breathing gas, chemical heat packs ×6. Rebreather with cold-rated scrubber and a full independent bailout for the worst-case excursion, plus a redundant mask and two lights (one red primary, one white with a lockout so you cannot turn it on by accident). Doppler/inertial nav, depth-rated compass, wrist timer ×2, cutting tools ×2, lift bag and reel, taut-line kit, tool lanyards for every single object. Hydrophone and a passive recorder. A thermometer you trust to a hundredth of a degree. A camera you will use ten minutes a day.

Nothing wooden. Nothing cotton. Nothing you would grieve to lose. Nothing you would be tempted to leave.

## 15. A shape for the stay

**Days 1–2.** Compression and settling. First descent with no lights at all, forty minutes of dark adaptation, and then just sit and look at the mosaic and the ceiling until you can feel the difference between the water's cold and the floor's cold. Find the scarp. Find the channel.

**Days 3–5.** Survey. Map the coarse stripe, the megaripple field, the swale. Locate the gabbro corner. Do not look for the fish; look for its stone.

**Days 6–9.** Sit near the corner. Hours of nothing. This is the actual expedition; everything else was logistics. If autumn, watch the swale for the kelp fall — sodden blades from a coast sixty kilometers away, the year's manna, and the only week the plain is crowded.

**Whenever it comes.** Read the precursors. Leave early. Watch the front from standoff. Then four days of settling, and the ceiling lifting, and the grey light coming back down onto a floor that is new in every particular and identical in every respect, and on ten thousand stones the film beginning again.

**Last days.** Decompress. Slowly. Use the time to write it down while you can still feel it, because you will lose the feel of it faster than you lose any other memory you have ever made.

## 16. Leaving

Take nothing. Not one stone. They are the plain's last possessions, sorted by weight and nothing else, each one touching four or five neighbors, and the joints are so tight you couldn't get a knife in. Removing one is a small thing and it is also the only kind of theft this world is capable of suffering.

Bring back the twenty seconds of bare hand. Bring back the half-minute of blindness inside the cloud and the room you fell into afterward. Bring back the fact that somewhere a fish is looking for a corner it has already lost twice this year, and will find it, and will knock three times and pause and knock twice, and nothing anywhere will record that it happened.

You'll be the only place it's kept. That's enough. Don't leave anything behind to compete with it.
