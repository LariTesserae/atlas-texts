# Preparing for the Sill

## What kind of trip this actually is

Two things will try to hurt you here, and they are not the same kind of thing.

The first is ordinary: five thousand metres, four degrees at dawn, frost most nights, caustic dust, unrounded rock, and an industrial city grinding eleven thousand tonnes of stone a day. That hazard set is well understood. You can buy your way out of most of it with equipment and patience.

The second is that the ground has not finished being ground. It has no rind, no memory, no outside, and in the east it has not yet settled on what it is. That hazard set is not well understood, and the locals' attitude toward it is a bureaucratic shrug that will read to you as either callousness or wisdom. It is neither. It is the settled manners of people who have decided which questions to stop asking.

The practical consequence is this: **on the Sill, the dangerous things are polite.** Nothing lunges. The Quick does not look like a trap; it looks like a floor. The duplicate does not glow; it has a torn ear and it is warm. The fog does not roll in; it takes a week, and you will not notice it arriving until you have been inside it for a day.

Prepare for a place that will not warn you.

## Before you go: the body

**Do not arrive at altitude.** If your transit permits a choice of arrival point, arrive below the Undercast, in the drowned world, and come up the funicular in stages over three to four days with nights at the intermediate stations. If your transit is fixed-point, arrive *in Rind*, in a heated room, with a sponsor physically present, and do nothing at all for seventy-two hours. Not the museum. Not the Spine. Nothing.

Five thousand metres is not a scenic altitude. It is above most permanent human habitation. Acute mountain sickness is likely; high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral oedema are real possibilities and both kill people who felt fine that morning. Bring acetazolamide and know its dosing, bring a pulse oximeter and know your own baseline, and learn the difference between "altitude is unpleasant" and "I am descending now." The cure for everything wrong with you at altitude is *down*, and down here means below the rim, into cloud, on a funicular that also carries armed hoppers of imported dirt and does not run for your convenience. **Book a descent slot before you need one.** Know the cargo schedule. Know which stations have a physician.

Other bodily notes:

- Water boils at 83°C. Boiling still disinfects, but you must hold it there longer than you are used to — a rolling boil counted for one minute is not enough. Filter *and* treat. If you're staying more than a fortnight, a small pressure vessel restores both proper cooking and proper tea, and will make you quietly popular.
- Long-simmer food is a fantasy. Bring instant, pre-cooked, high-calorie. Rice and beans will humble you.
- Cold, dry, thin air strips water out of you with every breath and you will not feel thirsty. Drink on a schedule. Saline nasal spray, thick lip balm, hand salve, eye drops. You will bleed from the nose in your sleep and it will be normal.
- Alcohol at altitude is not the alcohol you know, and the cold will hide it from you.
- Nothing here has co-evolved with anything, which means low zoonotic risk and *enormous* pollen. Plants growing at theoretical maximum, flowering all at once, hot-sugar smell, insect columns you can hear. Bring antihistamines you've tested on yourself. If you have any history of severe allergy, discuss an epinephrine autoinjector with your physician before you go. Do not eat anything you cannot name and have not been licensed to eat.

## The packing doctrine: arrive with nothing new

This is the single most useful thing I can tell you, and it is not a metaphor.

**Everything you carry should already have a history.** Boots resoled twice. A coat with patched elbows. A flask with a dent in it. A knife whose blade has been sharpened away to a crescent. A pocket coin rubbed featureless. A pack with a repaired strap. On the Sill, unweathered things are *provisional* — they hold no impressions and cannot be entirely relied on to remain the same substance. You do not want to test that principle on your only pair of boots at the far end of Course Eleven.

There are three further reasons, and all of them matter:

1. **Social.** Visible mending is formal wear in Rind. A new coat reads as either destitution or arrogance. Patches, turned cuffs, a resoled boot: that is a dinner jacket.
2. **Customs.** The ordinary dirt in your boot tread is a controlled import. Rind brings soil up under armed guard. You will be inspected, scraped, and assayed, and you may be charged duty on your own filth. Arrive with worn-but-clean boots and declare honestly.
3. **Ontological.** You are, by local standards, the most eroded object for a hundred kilometres. You are a walking river cobble. People will look at your hands. Some will ask to touch your boots. Children may find your face frightening or fascinating, because it has an *outside*. Be gentle about this and do not make it a performance.

Bring one small worn thing purely as an anchor — a coin, a ring, a smooth stone from a river at home, declared and papered. You will want something in your pocket to put your thumb on. I'll explain why later.

## Light: your eyes will lie to you

Most of the light comes up off the Undercast. This inverts everything.

**Sun protection.** The sun is a dull coin you can stare at, and this is a trap. At five thousand metres, above a horizon-to-horizon white reflector, you are being irradiated from below, in the places no hat has ever protected: under the chin, under the jaw, the underside of the nose, the inside of the nostrils, the lower lids, the soft skin under the brow. Zinc those places. Reapply. Use category-4 wraparound glacier glasses with side shields, plus ski goggles for fog and frost, plus a spare pair, because you cannot buy them here. Snow blindness off a cloud sea is a genuine way to become a casualty on a plateau with no landmarks.

**Faces.** Everyone is lit like someone holding a lamp under their chin. Every face you meet will look ill, sinister, or grieved. **Do not read emotion off a Rind face.** You will get it wrong constantly and the errors will be insulting. Locals compensate with voice, posture, hands, and explicit statement — they say what they feel, plainly, because faces don't carry it. Learn to do the same. It feels rude to you. It is polite here.

A small trick: a clip lamp on your cap brim, pointed at your own face, restores ordinary top-light and makes you legible to people. Note that a broad sun hat does the opposite — it cuts what little sky-light there is and leaves you purely under-lit, a mask. Wear the brim on the plateau for your eyes; take it off indoors to be a person.

**Depth.** Soft shallow shadows plus dead-flat ground plus sourceless grey means you cannot see relief. A dew pond a hundred metres across and eleven centimetres deep reads as more floor. A polygonal joint reads as a painted line until your ankle is in it. Walk with a pole. Probe. In fog, hand-line.

**And nothing is rounded.** Every surface on this plateau is a fresh fracture. Falling on the Sill is falling on broken glass. Stiff high boots with toe protection, leather gloves (two pairs), knee pads, a hard hat near the escarpment and the Works. **Never put a bare hand out to steady yourself.** Never sit down casually. Never lean.

## Water is at addresses

There is no downstream. Water on the Sill sits where it is put, in hollows, until it freezes or evaporates. Two consequences:

**A puddle is not necessarily water.** It could be condensate, dew, lime slurry, machine oil, or the acid-and-spore mix the lichen tenders spray on north faces. Nothing has washed away. Whatever spilled there is still there. Never step in a puddle, never drink one, never test one with your hand. Green does not automatically mean safe — sometimes green means acid.

**Water is owned.** Every leaking pipe, every mesh harp, every kilometre of galvanised netting is somebody's livelihood, guild-levied and metered. The green explosion under a leaky pipe is not wilderness; it is an asset. Do not drink from condensers, sumps, troughs, wallows, or dew ponds without a licence, and do not wash in them. Carry three to four litres, insulated, wide-mouthed (narrow necks freeze first), and refill in the city.

Here is a fact worth internalising: **the fog front crosses the plateau in about a week, which is roughly one and a half kilometres an hour. You can outwalk the weather.** That is useful and it is also a trap. Walk west fast enough and you can stay in clear air indefinitely — and leave the entire water supply behind you. Know where the front is. It is posted. Plan around it like a tide.

Rain comes about every eleven days for an hour and is gone twenty minutes later. It is not a water source. It is a calendar.

## The Works

Rind is a heavy industrial town and should be treated as one, with the added complication that its dust is *chemically unfinished*. Freshly fractured mineral surfaces have not yet reacted with anything. Atomised lime is caustic and the milled basalt is angular in a way ordinary sand is not.

- FFP3/P100 respirator, not a bandana. Sealed goggles, not spectacles. Carry an eyewash bottle. Lime in a wet eye is an emergency.
- Check the grit-cannon firing schedule daily and treat the escarpment faces as a live range. A decade of wear in an afternoon is an abrasive blast on a geological scale; you do not want to be downwind or below.
- Do not camp at the cliff foot. The frost-looms run four hundred freeze-thaw cycles a night; that means spalling, and every fragment that comes off is a blade. There is no scree to catch you and no rounded lip to warn you. Stay behind the rim markers, well back.
- Waste-heat pipes are lagged where they are meant to be touched and scalding where they aren't. Do not sleep against a pipe. Do not dry your socks on an unmarked one.
- The Spine is re-shimmed every spring and the older doorframes are parallelograms. **Do not trust architecture to be plumb.** Do not offer to fix it. The skew is the record, and the record is the point.

## Navigation: the land will not remember you

Footprints do not stay. There are no desire paths. Nobody has ever worn a shortcut into anything. Your instinct to retrace your steps is worthless here, and so are several of your instruments:

- **Shadows** point in directions unrelated to the sun. Children are taught not to use them. Unlearn it.
- **Compasses** are unreliable on fresh basalt, and worse near the Quick, where the rock has not settled on a lithology.
- **Sound** carries loud, hollow, and unlocatable. A mill that sounds adjacent may be six kilometres off. There is no echo, which means in fog you are deaf to geometry — you cannot hear the size of a space or the presence of a wall. Learn the fog-horn codes; they exist because sound doesn't reflect. And a whistle, while worth carrying, will not reliably tell anyone where you are.
- **Photographs** are for the city and for weathered things. Do not navigate by them. Dust eats cameras.

What works is what the locals use: **the rail, the painted markers, the smell of the works, the Courses, and the ledger.**

Carry two waterproof notebooks in separate pockets, several soft pencils (pens fail in cold and at altitude), sanctioned chalk, a fifty-metre knotted line, tape, a high-vis vest, a headlamp with spare cells kept against your body, a strobe, and a bivvy or bothy bag even if you intend to be indoors. Tie off in fog. Knot string the way everyone else does — you will be laughed at for a day and then you will be one of them.

**Write every day, and have someone countersign.** This is not journalling. On the Sill, the world does not keep records; you are the only place your itinerary exists. Two copies, carried separately, one left with your sponsor. If you lose your ledger you may lose your route, your permissions, your identity docket, and — this is the part people don't believe until it happens — your confidence in what you did last Tuesday.

If you are truly lost: find rail and follow it inward. Rail is the one thing here that stays.

## The far Courses, and the Quick

**Do not go out past Course Eleven alone.** Go with a licensed tender, on a docket, or not at all.

The duplicates are exactly as described and exactly as undiscussed. You will walk four hours and meet the same animal, warm, breathing, with the same torn ear and the same fading hoofprint. The Office logs one animal and pays one bounty. Adopt the local convention without needing to resolve it: **identity here is a ledger entry, not a body.** Register yourself on arrival. Get a docket. Carry it. Have a sponsor who will say which of you is you.

If you meet yourself: don't touch, don't argue, don't try to establish which is real by asking questions only you would know — the copy has your recent history too. Note the time and the position, walk back to the Spine, report it, and accept the shrug. The shrug is a mercy. Do not make important decisions, sign anything, promise anything, or fall in love out past Course Eleven. Do your contracts and your vows on old ground in the older districts, where the stone has been rained on enough to hold them.

**Near the Quick, do not write and do not name.** A surveyor plants a rod, writes *basalt*, signs it, and the ground becomes basalt, gratefully. That is a licensed act performed by a trained person for reasons you do not understand. Casual description here is not casual. Do not speculate aloud about what is under your feet. Do not say "this looks like sand." Do not let a poet, a child, or a drunk lead the party. Keep your own book closed, or keep it strictly to past-tense observations with no substance nouns. Hire the surveyor. Let them do all the naming. Unlicensed naming near the Quick can kill somebody, and there is a reason nobody wants to discuss that either.

The eye slides off unsurveyed ground and you find you have been looking at your own hands. Treat this like a crevasse field: rope, marked route, eyes on the rod or on your partner's vest, count steps aloud, buddy-check for lost time. And learn the grounding technique, because it is genuinely effective and it is the one piece of local first aid that works everywhere:

**Look at your hands. Read your knuckles. Count your scars.** They are the most weathered thing within a hundred kilometres. They have an outside. They will hold.

Near the Quick, don't shout. The echo comes first, thin and slightly wrong, and then you find yourself waiting to make the noise. If you hear an accident before it happens, do not flail about trying to prevent it. Go still, tether, and wait. Loud sound there is a commitment you don't understand the terms of.

## Money, customs, and what not to bring

Sand is the reserve currency. Your money is worthless. Exchange at the Grain House, learn the grades, and **carry a calibrated pocket scale** — payment is by weight, and disputes are about grading and moisture content, which is to say about fraud.

Now the important warnings, because a well-meaning traveler can do enormous damage here in about ten minutes:

- **Do not bring sand.** A jar of beach sand as a souvenir gift is, in monetary terms, counterfeiting on a scale that could destabilise the Grain House. If you must, declare it, in sealed container, with provenance, in small quantity, through the proper channel.
- **Do not import soil, leaf litter, seeds, worms, microbes, or water without declaration.** Rind imports dirt under armed guard for very good reasons. Your casual handful of garden loam is an ecological event on a plateau where nothing has learned to compete. Look at what nineteen years of one leaking pipe did to Course Nine, and then imagine what you could do by accident.
- **Do not buy anything "old" without Grain House assay and papers.** A stone tumbled in a drum for a week looks ancient to people who have never seen a river. That is precisely why the children in the museum cannot conceive of the cobble: they have no reference for the difference between wear and manufacture. Neither, after a fortnight here, will you.
- **The best gift is a single weathered object with a documented history.** One river pebble, sealed, with a photograph of the river it came from and a written account of how long it lay there. That is a princely gift. Provenance is worth more than the object. A pebble with a story is a treasure; a bag of pebbles is a crime.

Expect people to want to buy your history from you: the worn coin, your grandfather's knife, the ring. Decide your answer before you arrive, because you will be offered more money than you expect and the offer will be sincere.

## The thing you must not do

**Do not offer to finish the Cut.**

You could. With machinery, with explosives, with a season and a budget, you could put water across the last eleven hundred metres and out over the rim before the fog front next crosses. Everyone would watch you do it.

Don't.

The Cut is not a canal that is late. It is sixty-one years of guilds and wills and marriages, and the thing it is building is not a channel — it is *duration*. A valley made in a month is a ditch. You would not be giving them a river; you would be giving them a forgery, on ground that cannot hold a forgery, and taking away the only cathedral they have. The plaque is not a progress report.

The same restraint applies everywhere. Don't fix the doorframes. Don't offer better mills. Don't tell a child that the cobble in the museum is ordinary, that your driveway has a hundred of them, that where you're from beaches are made of nothing else. If a child asks about rivers, tell them slowly and truthfully and then **let them not believe you.** That is the correct outcome. Insisting would be a kind of violence.

The etiquette, in short: **never boast about age.** Compliment wear, sincerely and specifically. "Your doorstep is beautiful." "How long has that coat been in the family?" "Your building has moved" is a compliment about a family's tenancy. Learn what people are proud of here, which is not newness, and is not cleverness, and is not speed.

## Sleep, and the mind

This is the part travelers underestimate.

There is no dawn and no dusk — only a slight thickening of a grey that was already thick. There is no diurnal cue at all. Your circadian rhythm will come apart in about four days. Bring an eye mask, keep a clock, keep to municipal time religiously, and adopt the eleven-day rain as your week because everyone else does.

The racket is permanent, enormous, and **has no reverberation.** Mills, looms, horns, drips, midges, crickets — and none of it comes back off anything. Human ears find non-reverberant space deeply wrong; it is like shouting into a wardrobe forever. Bring rated earplugs and over-ear defenders for the mills, and bring music with reverb in it, or an instrument, or the habit of humming. Rind's tenements are the only *insides* on a plateau where even a broken stone has no inside. Spend real time indoors every day, in a room with hard walls, where your voice comes back to you. Rent thick walls on the Spine.

One silver lining worth knowing: no echo means no carrying. You can whisper beside a roaring mill and be heard only by the person next to you. Rind people stand close and speak quietly. Their machines are loud; their speech is intimate. Let that be a comfort.

And there will be a night. The hollowback bellow arrives from twelve kilometres at three in the morning as a flat metallic tearing, enormously loud and completely hollow, and you will get used to it, and then one night you won't. This is a known event. It is not an emergency. Do not go out to look. Go to a heated room, put your thumb on your worn coin, read your ledger, and wait for it to be morning, which will not look any different. Nobody will comfort you much about this. It is not coldness; they have all had the night.

Budget for the sensory whiplash of the green seams, too. Four paces from bare stone ringing under your boot into cushion plants two metres across, flowering all at once, hot sugar, insects humming, crickets so loud you have to shout — and then four paces back into nothing. It is a parking lot and a greenhouse jungle, alternating, without transition. The smell will make you emotional in a way you won't be able to justify. Let it.

Finally, name the feeling so it doesn't ambush you: two hundred kilometres of dead-flat floor, no drainage lines, no dendritic script, nothing your eye can parse as landscape, under a violet-black noon sky with stars behind the haze. It reads as *interior*. You will feel claustrophobic in more open space than you have ever stood in. Agoraphobia and claustrophobia at once, on a floor, in a room with no walls. It passes. Writing helps. Being with someone helps.

**First visit: three weeks, with a sponsor, not alone.** Leave before you stop noticing that everything is sharp. That's the sign.

## Emergencies

- Altitude, confusion, breathlessness at rest, a cough that changes: **descend.** Below the rim, into the wet old drowned world where things have rinds and echoes work and footprints stay. It is the sanctuary and it is also unknown, so go by funicular, to a station, with papers.
- Ontological vertigo, lost time, the eye sliding, an echo arriving early: stop, tether, hands, ledger, walk inward to the Spine. Report it without embarrassment. Everyone has a story they don't tell.
- Lost: rail, inward.
- Lost ledger: your sponsor's copy, which is why you left one.
- Anything green and wet and unfamiliar: don't touch it, don't drink it, ask a tender.

## What to bring home

Not raw stone. An unweathered fragment is an object that has not committed to being anything, and you would be carrying it into a world with different rules, where it might settle gratefully into whatever your world's descriptions suggest. Leave it, or seal it and declare it, and honestly: leave it.

Not moss — that's the first soil. Not a gizzard stone taken by your own hand — that's a licensed trade and a guild's living. Buy one legally, with provenance: a small round grey pebble that spent a thousand years in a river under the clouds and came home in a bird. It is the perfect souvenir and you will never look at it the same way twice.

Bring your ledger. Bring a legal brass shim from the Spine if one is offered. Bring the copy of what your sponsor countersigned.

And prepare for re-entry, which is harder than the trip. You will come home to a world where everything is rounded, everything has a rind, everything has an inside, and every doorstep is dished by feet. You will cry at a worn stair. You will stand in a lane where the ruts are two hundred years deep and be unable to explain to your companion why you can't move. You will notice, permanently, that decay is time and that time is the only thing that makes anything real, and you will find it difficult to be casual about a chipped mug.

Walk a desire path. Put your hand in a river. Let the world be old at you.

---

## One last thing to schedule

Under a leaking pipe on Course Nine there is a depression in raw stone you could lay your thumb in. Nineteen years of one drop. Go there. Put your thumb in it. It is the only thing on the Sill shaped like a human gesture, and no hand made it.

Then walk out to the plaque, eleven hundred metres from the rim, and stand where the water will go.

You will not see the river. Nobody alive will. In four hundred years it will have a name, and the name will be some grandmother's word for something else entirely, and the whole point of the city — the mills, the looms, the cannon, the ledgers, the sand in the vaults, the man with the lamp on Course Four writing down where the herd slept — is to build a thing whose completion is not for anyone who is building it.

That's the actual preparation. Everything above is boots and paperwork. The real work is arriving able to love something you cannot see the end of, and then leaving without speeding it up.
