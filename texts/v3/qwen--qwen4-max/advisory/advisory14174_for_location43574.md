# Preparing to Visit the Unfinished Chamber

## First, understand what will actually endanger you

Nothing in that room wants to hurt you. The harrow is not a wolf; it is a stage of the thing it eats, and it has no eyes to find you with. The stone is not hostile. The cold is mild. There is no flood, no fall, no gas that smells wrong.

The three things that will get you are **your own breath**, **your own hands**, and **your own patience**.

Your breath will poison the air around your collarbone in a room with no wind to take it away, and it will do so while you feel perfectly fine, in a place where you must find a lightless crack by touch in order to leave. Your hands will dull every surface they touch, and dulling is the mechanism by which this world grows, so you will be editing the geology with your fingertips without noticing. And your patience will fail in the specific direction of *staying* — because every genuinely worth-seeing event in that chamber happens on a timescale longer than a safe visit, and because the room builds furniture in the shape of whatever occupies it long enough.

Prepare against those three. Everything else is comfort.

---

## Before you go anywhere near the scarp

**Get your body quiet.** You want to arrive at the seam with a low resting heart rate and no urgency. Your blood is audible to the animals and your heat is a season passing through; the calmer you are, the smaller the weather event you make. Cardiovascular fitness is not for the climb — it's so that eighty minutes of standing still and slow shuffling costs you almost no respiration.

**Rehearse the seam somewhere ordinary.** Find a gap twenty-five centimetres wide — between a wall and a fridge, a doorframe — and go through it sideways, forty times, until your body stops arguing. Lead with the same shoulder every time. Exhale all the way down and go on the empty lungs. Cheek to the surface, ribs stacked, arms in front or trailing flat. You are not trying to be brave in there; you are trying to have a motor habit that survives panic. The seam does not tighten. Your ribcage does, when you inhale at the wrong moment. Learn that in a place where it doesn't matter.

**Sort out your sinuses and your gut beforehand.** You will not eat, drink, cough, sneeze, spit, or blow your nose inside. Take an antihistamine if you're prone. Empty everything before you approach. This is not squeamishness — a sneeze is a jet of foreign aerosol into a world whose entire water budget is three basins the size of cupped hands and a drip that takes eleven seconds to gather.

**Decide, in writing, before you go, that you will make no marks.** Not a scratch, not a stacked chip, not a footprint placed deliberately, not your palm flat on a wall to steady yourself. The room has no decay anywhere except one corner where decay was recently invented. Anything you leave is permanent on a geological timescale, and worse: *the room copies*. It has already made a shelf, a niche, an alcove exactly the size of a person. Your idle graffiti may become somebody's anatomy. Write the rule down and date it, because inside you will not be able to reconstruct why it mattered.

**Go alone, or leave one person at the seam.** Two people inside doubles the breath load in a room with no ventilation, and the seam takes one at a time. If you bring a companion, station them outside with their head in the weather, holding a clock, briefed to call you at a fixed time regardless of what you shout back. Call them the porch keeper. Their entire job is to be a source of time, which is the one thing the chamber does not contain.

## Kit

The governing constraint: **you go in sideways.** Nothing wider than your compressed chest, nothing on your back, nothing that snags. A flat chest rig or a slim front pouch. If you can't get through a doorframe wearing it, it doesn't go.

**Wear:**

- A thin, slick, tightly-woven coverall in pale grey or bone. Matte, not shiny. No fleece, no wool, no velcro, no external pockets, no loose laces. You want to shed as few fibres as possible and to read neither as a light source nor as a dull patch — a dark human standing against a glowing wall looks exactly like weathering, and weathering provokes the stone to fail.
- A close hood. Every few days it rains warm, faintly-of-someone drizzle out of the ceiling for four minutes. That rain is condensed breath, possibly not yours. Keep it off your lips.
- Rigid-soled, high-ankled boots with smooth uppers. The floor is unsorted angular chips, sharp enough to open a knee, and **you will not be able to see them**, because in the shadowless middle depth is a rumour and every hole reads as a drawing of a hole. There are three basins and a thumb-width deep place and two knee-shaped hollows. Your ankles need to survive stepping into one blind.
- Low-profile knee and elbow pads *under* the coverall. You will kneel or fall eventually. Plan for the knee to be padded rather than opened.
- Thin gloves with sensitive fingertips, plus a warmer overpair you can strip with your teeth. **You find the exit by feeling a hand's-breadth cold stripe on your knuckles.** Numb hands at eleven degrees cannot discriminate that. Keep your hands warm until the moment you need them, then bare them.
- A brimmed cap. Not sunglasses.

**Carry:**

- **Two independent silent timers**, vibrating, against the skin. Set them *before* you enter, from outside, where duration still behaves. First one at twenty-five minutes: begin walking out. Second at forty: leave regardless. You will not be able to feel time passing in there — no shadows move, nothing decays, no sound arrives from outside, the drip's sound lands before the drop. Your estimate of "about ten minutes" will be an hour.
- **A CO₂ meter**, small, clipped at collarbone height, with a vibrating alarm. This is the single most important object you bring. Also a chemical indicator tube as backup, because in a room where the perimeter is shorter clockwise than counterclockwise, I would not stake my life on one instrument.
- A flat bladder of water, sealed. Drink before and after, not during.
- A short rigid probe — metal or ceramic, **rubber-tipped**. Not wood. Wood is food, rot, and a whole foreign biology, and a hard tip makes a *tick* that means something specific to things that navigate by the sound of stone about to break.
- A wound kit: occlusive dressings, antiseptic wipes, gloves you can put on fast. Blood is iron and protein and salt in a world whose total soil is a thimbleful. If you bleed, you seal it immediately and you leave.
- Sealed bags for everything that comes out with you, including tissues, hair, and the chips in your boot seams.
- A small dense filter — a welder's glass, shade 3 to 5 — on a cord, **held, never worn**. For looking closely at a fresh face. More on this below.
- Optionally: twenty metres of smooth pale monofilament on a flat spool. See the trade-off below.

**Do not bring:** a headlamp you intend to use, a candle or any flame, a fan or drone or anything that makes wind, a wooden staff, food, a dog, a child, a second person, a camera flash, or the intention to take a souvenir.

A flame consumes oxygen and makes CO₂, water, soot, heat and shadow — it is five separate catastrophes at once. A drone's downwash is the first hurricane in the history of this world; it would scatter the fliers, tear the breath-cloud apart, and destroy the only meteorology the room has. **Do not make wind. Do not fan yourself. Do not blow on anything.**

## Finding the seam and going in

Outside is ordinary weather; the strangeness is confined. Scout the scarp from a distance first — it is an eroding face and the seam takes your bare head, so no helmet. Don't go after rain or a freeze-thaw cycle. Fix the location in your memory by bearings to distant landmarks, not by marking the rock.

Wet the back of your hand with water from your own bottle. Evaporation sharpens thermal discrimination enormously. Lay the hand flat and slide it along the scarp slowly, patiently, a hand's breadth at a time. You are feeling for a stripe of cold about the width of your palm, and for a slow outward breath at the pace of something asleep.

When you find it: **hold your hand there a while before you enter.** It is the only greeting the room can receive — temperature and touch are its whole vocabulary. If the breathing changes pace, come back another day. I can't defend that as engineering. Do it anyway; there are marks in there, and you don't know whose.

Then: everything to the front, exhale fully, same shoulder, cheek to rock, and go. The rock your cheek touches has never been weathered. It will feel like nothing you have touched. Then the seam lets you go.

---

## The first ninety seconds: do nothing

Stand still just inside and let your eyes sort themselves out. This is the only time you should be motionless for long.

What will happen: your pupils will open for the grey dusk of the middle, and then some part of your gaze will catch the rind of brilliance along a wall and it will hurt, and you will get an afterimage. **This is the central optical hazard of the place.** A dark blob floating in your vision, in a room where you must navigate by finding the darkest thing — every afterimage is a false exit. You will confidently walk toward three exits that are your own retina.

So: learn immediately to look at the *middle*, at the floor near your feet, at the animals. Never look directly at a fresh plate. When you must examine one, hold up the peep glass. Do not wear tinted lenses continuously — the middle is genuinely dim and you need it to see the chips and the basins.

Your boots will look drawn rather than placed. This is not a hallucination and it will not stop. Some people get a mild derealisation from it, a sense of being a diagram of a person. Expect that. It passes when you leave.

Then take your census, quickly, because it is the closest thing to an instrument you have: **count the rosettes.** There are seven; one is always about to be. Note where the grazer is, and the harrow if it's visible. Note the brightest plate. Note the thimble of soil and the fungus. Do this in the first minute, because your later memory of counts will be testimony, not measurement.

**Establish the seam as your hub.** From here on, everything is radial: out from the seam along a path, back along the same path. **Never circumnavigate.** The perimeter is shorter one way than the other; a circuit will teach you something you cannot use and cost you breath and touch you cannot afford. Treat the seam as the only fixed point in the room, because it is.

---

## Air: the real clock

The room is chapel-sized and there is no air movement to speak of. Your breath does not disperse. It hangs at collarbone height and accumulates. A resting adult makes roughly fifteen litres of CO₂ an hour; in a cubic metre of unmixed air around your head that's a percent and a half in sixty minutes. You will not be breathing the room's average. You will be breathing your own exhaust.

Rough discipline, with the meter clipped at your collarbone:

- Under 1,500 ppm: fine.
- 2,500 ppm: headache territory. Start moving toward the seam.
- 5,000 ppm: alarm. **Leave now**, calmly, no exertion.
- Above that, judgement goes before breathing does, and you will be the last to know.

The symptoms are yawning, a tight band across the forehead, a slightly sweet air-hunger, and — the dangerous one — a warm, agreeable certainty that you have plenty of time and that this is the most interesting place you have ever been.

**You have a second, better meter: your own cloud.** The description is literal — stand still and you can watch a small cloud begin at the level of your collarbone. Watch it. When it thickens, move. And when the pale fliers turn toward it, and the rosettes lean, **that is the room telling you your exhaust has become a resource.** Leave. The plants grow toward newness, and you are the newest thing here; being grown toward is not a compliment, it is the beginning of being incorporated.

Practical rules:
- Breathe slow and shallow through the nose. Talk as little as possible; speech is exhalation and also a signal in a language you don't speak.
- Every ten minutes, step two or three paces sideways out of your own cloud. Don't stride; shuffle.
- Return to the seam periodically and stand in the cold stripe for a minute. It is the only place with any exchange. Think of it as the porch. All rest happens on the porch.
- **Never sit down inside.** Not on chips, not on a pad. Sitting compresses and sorts the floor, and the floor has never been sorted; you would be creating the first bedding plane in a world with no past tense. Rest outside.
- If you must sneeze or cough: face to the seam, into a sealed bag, and then leave.

## Moving, touching, and the cost of a handhold

Shuffle. Don't stride, don't lunge, don't stamp. Probe ahead with the rubber-tipped stick, lightly — you are feeling for basins and hollows, not tapping. A hard *tick* on stone is a word here, and seam-flies land on fractures an instant before they exist; you do not want to announce a fracture that isn't coming, or summon something to a place you're about to put your foot.

**Do not lean on anything.** Whatever you lean on may not have set, and a failure makes light, and light brings grazers, and the crackle behind that face is a crowd waiting to be excavated into being. You could hatch a roomful of things that have never had a sun and give them yourself as their first one.

Touch discipline:
- Fingertips only, never palms. There are already two palm-shaped patches where the glow has been rubbed away. Don't add a third.
- If you must steady yourself repeatedly, use **one sacrifice point** — the same small patch of already-dull stone, near the seam, every time. Concentrate your damage rather than distributing it.
- Consider the monofilament: a line from the seam to your farthest excursion lets you return by feel without trailing a hand along a wall and leaving a two-metre dull scratch — which is, of course, exactly what one of the existing marks is. That's the argument for it. The argument against: it lies on a floor that is one grain in fifty a living animal, it will be walked over, it may be built into something, and if it snags you must either abandon a permanent synthetic filament in a world with no decay or spend an hour retrieving it while your CO₂ climbs. My advice: use it only for your first radial excursion, retrieve it hand over hand as you back out, and never leave it.

**Do not drink the water.** Not the drip, not the basins, not the rain. Not because it's certainly poisonous — it's juvenile water out of setting dolomite and it may be nothing worse than hard — but because those three basins and that eleven-second drip are the entire standing hydrology of the place, and a mouthful is a measurable fraction of a world. Also because your mouth is a bacterial culture and the water has never been in a body. Bring all your own. Don't wash a cut in it. Don't put a fingertip in a basin to see what the surface tension does, however much you want to.

**Do not set anything down.** Anything set down becomes an artefact, and in a shadowless room you will not find it again.

## What the animals will do to you

Over the first twenty minutes: almost nothing. Over an hour: the room will slowly, unhurriedly converge on you. Eleven degrees of cold-blooded patience. They hear your blood. Your warmth is a front, a season. The cricket-thing will orient. The fliers will turn. The grazer will move a few centimetres.

Do not read slowness as tameness, and do not read convergence as aggression. It is attendance. The room is full the way a station is full; most of the crowd isn't here yet.

If a limpet attaches to your boot or your coverall: don't tear it off. Its shell is glassy and incomplete and you'll leave part of it behind, which is both a wound and a nutrient drop. Lever gently with the probe, or simply outwait it — you can outwait anything here. Note the odd, unpleasant fact and don't dwell on it: **you may be the only surface in this room that regenerates its own freshness.** Everything else goes dull and stays dull. You keep making new skin. Whether that makes you inedible or infinitely grazable, I don't know, and I'd rather you covered every centimetre and left before the experiment ran.

If the harrow is in your path: don't dodge. It doesn't stalk; it arrives where the prey will be, and evasion is a conversation it has already finished. It has no eyes and you are not a grazer. Move slowly and predictably and step around it with a wide margin. The realistic injury is you putting a boot through twenty legs you couldn't see because nothing casts a shadow.

## The marks, and the actual temptation

You will find them by absence. The three-chip course. The two-metre hand-line that stops as if the arm tired. The knee-high dry-stone wall standing in the middle of the floor, dividing nothing, built for a geometry that will be correct in three centuries. The two knee-shaped hollows, worn, in a room where nothing is worn.

Stand in front of these and something will happen to you that I want to name in advance, because naming it is most of the defence:

**You will want to add to them.** To put a fourth chip on the course. To extend the line. To finish the wall. To kneel in the hollows, because they are knee-shaped and you have knees and the fit is *right there*. It will feel less like vandalism and more like answering.

Don't. Not the wall, not the line, not the chips. Don't step over the wall — it's a door in a wall that hasn't been built yet, and you don't know which side you'd be on. And **do not kneel in the hollows, not once, not briefly.** Nothing here decays, so nothing here forgets, and the room dreams the furniture of older caves. It has already made an alcove exactly the size of a person. If you occupy one shape long enough in one place, you are teaching it a shape, and it is very slow and very patient and it will get it right eventually, and then there will be a place in the world that fits you and nothing else.

That is how someone knelt here a very long time.

Corollary, and it's the most useful rule in this whole document: **keep varying your stance. Never occupy the same square of floor twice. Don't let it learn your shape.**

## Time, and the thing you came for

The four-minute rain comes every few days. The grazer's metamorphosis into the harrow happens once in a long while. The seam-fly landing an instant before the fracture exists — that you might catch, if you're lucky, in an hour.

Which means: **the events that justify the trip are mostly the ones you cannot safely wait for.** Accept this before you go, or you will negotiate with yourself inside, and inside you will be slightly hypercapnic and timeless and full of the conviction that just one more hour is fine.

Put your ear near — not against — the brightest wall, once, and listen to the fine dense crackling like a fire two rooms away. That's the crowd, waiting to be excavated into being. Listen for thirty seconds and then stop, because it is the most habit-forming sound in the room and it is the one that makes people stay.

Plan on **many short visits across years, not one long one.** But understand the cost of that too: one visit is a weather event; ten visits is a climate; a regular visitor is a season the ecology can adapt to, and then you are not observing this world, you are a forcing term in it. Decide, at some point, whether you're willing to be its weather. It's a real question and there isn't a clean answer.

---

## Leaving

Begin at the first timer, not the second. Say goodbye out loud at the seam, not inside — speech in there is breath and signal.

1. Stop looking at walls. Look at the floor-middle, or close your eyes entirely. Your afterimages will offer you three or four exits and all of them are lies.
2. Warm your hands at your armpits until they hurt a little. Then strip the gloves.
3. Wet your knuckles from your own bottle.
4. Retrieve your line if you laid one, hand over hand, without walking back along it.
5. Fingertips on the wall — pads, not palms — and move toward where you remember the dark being. Trust temperature. Vision is not a sense you have here.
6. Keep the same hand on the wall so you always know which way you're facing. Count nothing. Paces and seconds are unreliable; the perimeter proves it.
7. The cold stripe finds your knuckles. You'll feel the slow outward breath.
8. Everything to the front. Same shoulder. Full exhale. Cheek to rock. Go on the empty lungs.
9. **Do not push off with your trailing hand.** Whatever you push off may not have set.

If you complete what feels like a full circuit and haven't found the seam: stop, sit nowhere, lean on nothing, put your bare wet knuckles flat on the stone and start again from the beginning, slowly. Do not run. Running is breath, heat, wind, noise, and a foot placed blind on chips. You have more air than you think if you stay calm, and much less than you think if you don't. The likeliest failure mode in this entire location is a person inhaling at the wrong moment in a twenty-five-centimetre gap and deciding the rock has closed.

---

## Afterward

Outside, before you touch anything of your own: strip and bag. Pick the chips out of your boot seams and your cuffs into a sealed container. You cannot put them back correctly — they lie exactly where they fell, unsorted, unbedded, and that arrangement is the only record this world has; returning a chip to the wrong place is a lie in the archive. So don't return them and don't keep them on a shelf. Quarantine them. What's riding on them is a springtail analogue, or a larva folded out of solid mineral, or a piece of the first rot ever invented, and none of those things have ever met an immune system or a competitor.

Then sit down in the weather and write everything, immediately, with the time and date. Your memory of the size, the duration and the counts will be wrong — you were in a room with no shadows, no echo, no decay and no reliable causality, and human memory is built out of exactly those things. Write it as testimony. Note that the perimeter disagreed with itself. Note whether you found a scrape near the entrance from a foot that had not stepped there, and whether it was your size.

If you did find that: don't go back to close the loop, and don't go back to avoid it. Just leave it alone.

And check yourself, honestly, for the one symptom that matters. Not the headache, not the chill, not the flatness of your own hands. Check whether you are already planning to stay longer next time. Whether the alcove was about your size. Whether you have started thinking of the crackle as company.

That's the injury this place does. It's an unfinished room being eaten into existence at the speed of hunger, and it is very short of furniture, and you are the most substantial thing that has ever stood in the middle of it casting no shadow at all.

Go briefly. Touch almost nothing. Take nothing. Make no mark. Leave before you see the rain.
