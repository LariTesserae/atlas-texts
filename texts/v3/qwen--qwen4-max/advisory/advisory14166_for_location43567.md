# Preparing to Visit the Vesicle

## 1. Accept the first fact before you pack anything

You are not going inside.

The opening is four millimeters by thirty centimeters. Your shoulders are forty-five centimeters. To pass, you would have to remove a slab of the roof — which is to say, destroy the ceiling, the pressure boundary, the cold-air intake, the light source, the acoustic cavity, and the only reason this world exists. There is no version of "entering the Vesicle" that leaves the Vesicle behind.

Even your hand is mostly wrong for it. The galleries are nine and a half centimeters of node spacing minus two centimeters of felt: a slot about seven and a half centimeters high. A flat hand fits. A fist does not. And you can only reach the *top* slot, because to reach the fourth gallery you would have to push through three shelves, each of which is decades of accumulation and a continuous living pavement, and none of which can be rebuilt.

So the honest itinerary is this: **you will lie on a warm black plateau at altitude, with your face turned aside from a crack, and you will look at a sliver of the top gallery through a slot the width of a pencil.** Everything else you will get by listening, by inference, and by patience.

Prepare for that trip. It is a good trip. But if you go expecting to walk among the amblypygids, you will do something terrible on the second day trying to make it happen.

## 2. Your body is the main hazard you are bringing

Sit with these numbers before you go, because they are the whole ethical content of the visit:

- The entire water reservoir of this biosphere is about a cup. You are carrying forty liters. **You are two hundred worlds' worth of water.**
- An hour of hard work in a sealed suit sweats out roughly a liter — four worlds.
- Fifteen hours of your breathing exhales enough water to double the reservoir. Your breath is also the only oxygen-rich gas that has ever been offered to a 740 °C sulfide jet, and it is a monsoon at the ceiling where the salt has never once been wet.
- You shed about a gram of yourself a day in skin and hair. In a world whose entire primary production is capped by a four-millimeter aperture, a gram of protein and lipid falling into the top gallery is not litter. It is a nutrient pulse, a boom, and then a bust.
- A sterile human hair is still a feast. **Sterile is not clean.** Departiculate as well as sterilize.

The practical consequence: treat yourself as a contained hazardous substance, not as a visitor with good manners.

- A dedicated smooth-shelled suit, never worn in any other world. No fleece, no wool, no cotton, no down, no exposed seams. You are visiting a world made of fibers; do not introduce yours.
- Closed-circuit breathing apparatus if you can get it, or supplied air with the exhaust piped ten meters downwind and below the crack. A rebreather is the morally correct respirator here for a reason no one expects: **it emits nothing.**
- Full face protection. Goggles alone are not enough when the plume carries hydrogen fluoride.
- Double gloves, taped cuffs, everything bagged at the margin on the way out.
- No sunscreen on exposed skin near the crack, no repellent, no perfume, no lip balm, no aerosol of anything. A drift of DEET into that slot is an extinction event with a pleasant smell.
- Eat cold, sealed, downwind, far away. Your stove's exhaust is a cloud of water and CO₂ and heat aimed at the cold end of a heat engine.
- Pack out urine. All of it. Sealed container. Do not rinse anything on the plateau, ever.

## 3. The plateau will hurt you in ordinary ways

Four thousand three hundred meters of altitude and kilometers of featureless black glass. The air is thin enough to make you stupid, and stupid is disqualifying here, because every rule in this document requires you to remember it while tired.

Acclimatize properly — weeks, staged, with a real descent plan for HAPE/HACE. Assume your judgment at the crack is worse than your judgment at camp, and design the protocol so it can be executed by someone at 70% capacity: checklists, a buddy, pre-agreed abort signals, no improvisation near the aperture.

The ground is sharp. Wind-scoured tachylite and spatter will cut boots, hands, and knees. Stiff soles, gaiters, knee pads with a smooth non-shedding skin. There are no boulders, no anchors, no shelter, no water, and no snow to melt. Carry everything.

Two hazards people forget:

**You are the tallest object on a conductive plain in a kilovolt-per-meter field.** Storms pass over this plateau — that is what makes the whistle slide. When one comes, leave. Not into a hollow, not onto a rise. Off.

**CO₂ is heavy and the fissure is an exhaust.** The plateau is wind-scoured and usually safe, but on a calm night the gas can pool in any depression, and directly over the crack it is an asphyxiant column with sulfur dioxide in it. The famous pose — face over the fissure, feeling the buzz behind the sternum — is precisely the pose that puts your lungs in the exhaust and your breath in the intake. Do it once, briefly, head to the side, breath held, or better, don't do it at all. Use a mirror on a stick.

Gas kit: four-gas monitor plus a specific HF sensor, worn at chest height, alarmed. Sulfur dioxide is the struck-match smell; hydrogen sulfide is not reliably smelled at all above about 100 ppm because it paralyzes your nose. **Your nose is not an instrument here.** Carry 2.5% calcium gluconate gel and know that dilute HF burns may not hurt for hours and can still stop your heart. Treat any plume contact as an HF exposure until proven otherwise.

## 4. The roof is not a floor

Under your boots is a 3.6-meter void spanned by a glass crust that is *already failing* — that is what the crack is. It is propagating. It sheds flakes on a century timescale, and it has never been load-tested by anything heavier than weather.

If you fall through, three things happen: you land in 90 °C glass wool within reach of a 740 °C jet; you cannot be extracted without breaking the ceiling open; and your body delivers forty liters of water and seventy kilograms of nutrient into a cup-sized biosphere. **A fall here is not an accident. It is an extinction with a casualty.**

So:

1. Map the void from outside before you go near it. Ground-penetrating radar works well in dry basalt; add a microgravity or tap-seismic survey. Map *all* the vesicles you can find, not just this one — the plateau is a foam, and you do not know which bubble is under your tent.
2. Establish an exclusion footprint. Camp, cook, sit, sleep, and store gear outside it, upwind, on flow you have verified is solid.
3. Work from the margin with reach — long lens, periscope, boom — rather than from above.
4. If you must be near the crack: distributed-load mat, crawling posture, slow movement, a tether to a ballast anchor set well outside the footprint. Do not drill anchors into the flow near the chamber; drilling is both a crack-propagation risk and a deafening broadcast in the only channel the locals have.
5. Write the no-rescue clause into your plan and say it out loud to your partner before you go. If someone goes through, there is no operation that saves both them and the world. Decide in advance what you want done, because you will not decide it well afterward.

## 5. Finding a four-millimeter crack in kilometers of black glass

There is no snow line, no plume (the gas is far too dry to condense), no vegetation, no boulder field, nothing. The only visible mark in the entire landscape is a stain of white salt about a hand-span across.

Look for the white.

Then confirm by:

- **Schlieren shimmer** at dawn, when cold air meets the 70 °C breath — a warp in the horizon, not a smoke.
- **The buzz through your boots and sternum.** When you feel it, you are already standing on the roof. Stop walking.
- A geophone or contact mic on the rock and an infrasound microphone; walk a grid, log amplitude, find the maximum.
- A CO₂ sniffer at ankle height on a calm morning.

Bring survey-grade positioning — RTK, centimeter-level — and a physical datum you can find by touch in the dark. Four millimeters is smaller than the error bar of an ordinary GPS. People have spent whole days kneeling on top of this world, unable to find it, which is the safest possible failure mode and still a waste of a season.

Do not mark it with paint, tape, cairns of imported rock, or anything that sheds. Do not sweep dust off the pavement near it. Do not step in the salt.

## 6. Sound: the part you cannot protect against

Inside the chamber: 118 dB at 21 Hz, plus harmonics, plus a wandering 1600 Hz edge tone. You are not going inside, but the rock carries the fundamental, and near the crack you will be in it.

Understand what earplugs do and do not do. They will take the edge tone down by thirty decibels and make the world sound like nothing at all. They will do **essentially nothing at 21 Hz.** Infrasound is not heard, it is *applied*. Chest wall, sinuses, gut, and — this is the one to know about — the fluid of the eye, which resonates near twenty hertz. Vision blurs. Objects seem to move at the edge of the frame.

Combine that with altitude hypoxia, dehydration, dry heat, and a sealed suit, and you get the classic reaction: dread, sorrow, a sense of presence, the conviction that something is behind you.

**Write this on your wrist slate: the presence is my own eyeball.**

Do not trust your emotional reading of this place. It is a machine and a food chain, and it is also a twenty-one-hertz hand on your chest. Set exposure limits near the aperture in minutes, not hours. Log them. Rotate. Have your partner watch you for the specific signs — nausea, unsteadiness, sudden certainty about something.

And plan your communications for a world that eats sound. Shouting is useless at the crack and useless in the wind. Use a rope-tug code, hand signals, and a slate. There is a symmetry here worth noticing: the locals pass every message body to body, one neighbor at a time, at half a centimeter a second. So will you. Adopt the local protocol.

## 7. You will broadcast, whether you intend to or not

This is the subtlest harm and the one almost nobody prepares for.

These animals cannot hear each other through the air. They talk through the glass — drumming, stridulation, substrate signals down a filament, dying in two centimeters. Their entire civilization has never had a broadcast channel.

**Your footfalls couple into the rock and from the rock into the fibers.** A bootstep on the roof is the one thing that can address every animal in the world simultaneously, in the only language it has, at an amplitude nothing has ever produced.

You cannot avoid this entirely. You can minimize it: soft soles, crawl the last meters, spread your weight, move slowly and continuously rather than in impacts, never stamp, never drop anything, never set a tripod leg down hard.

And if you do cause an alarm, you get to watch the only narrative event this world has: a rumor front radiating from your position at half a centimeter per second, crossing the chamber in eleven minutes, ten thousand animals each telling the one beside them. Sit still and watch it go. Then understand that a shelf you crush with a fingertip takes years to recolonize and decades to re-felt, and that the memory of your afternoon will outlast a hundred generations.

## 8. Do not touch the engine

The chamber is a thermoacoustic prime mover running on a temperature difference across a fibrous stack. Its intake, exhaust, light source, and pressure boundary are the same four-millimeter slot. Nearly everything a visitor instinctively does is an intervention:

- **Do not cover the fissure.** Not with a lens plate, not with a window, not with a tarp "to protect it," not with your hand. You would be plugging the world's mouth.
- **Do not shade it.** Your body's shadow across the cold intake changes the gradient that drives the whole thing. Plan your shadow. Work with the sun behind you, or at night.
- **Do not blow into it, breathe into it, or pump air into it.** You would be feeding oxidizer to a hot sulfide jet and water to a world that has never had a free droplet.
- **No drones.** Rotor downwash over that slot is a hurricane at the intake and a shotgun at the top shelf.
- **No probes if you can help it.** Anything you lower tears shelves on the way down, gets etched by HF, abraded by glass, and coated in sublimate. If a probe is unavoidable, it goes into the top gallery only, on a fixed mount, sterile and particle-free, and it stays for the minimum time.
- **Do not lie *over* the crack.** You are a hundred-watt heater at the cold end. Lie beside it, reach across with optics, not with your torso. You are also a ground plane in a field the springtails use to jump; your body distorts the only thing here that is faster than a heartbeat.

One thing you *can* do with impunity: **shine a light.** There are no eyes in this chamber and nothing that could use a photon. Light is the least harmful intrusion available to you. Keep the lamp cold, low-power, and remote-sourced by fiber so you are not adding heat, and then look as long as you like. It is a strange gift — the one sense you have that this world discarded, and the only one you may spend freely.

## 9. What to actually do there

Bring a season, not a day. The fastest natural event in this world is a whistle sliding a whole tone as a storm passes and sliding back over the following hour. If your visit is shorter than a weather cycle, you have seen a photograph of a place, not the place.

**The afternoon light-line.** Once a day, daylight through the slot crosses the top shelf. Time it, be in position an hour early, out of the sun's path, and watch a four-millimeter blade of sun move over pale gold felt stitched with sulfur crystals and paved with a continuous crust of springtails that flexes when the pressure cycle passes and relaxes when it goes. That flexing is the thing to come for. It is the world's pulse made visible.

**The night portrait.** Long exposure, fast lens, tripod set on verified solid flow off the footprint. In absolute darkness the seam shows as a line of dull cherry red thirty centimeters long, leaking out through the crack. That is the only permanent light in the world, and it is the only honest photograph of it: a red thread in black glass, kilometers from anything.

**The listening.** A geophone array on the plateau margin, an infrasound mic, a contact mic on the rock. Record 21 Hz and its harmonics, and the edge tone, continuously, for as long as you can. The whistle is a thermometer and a barometer. Its drift is the magma withdrawing a hand's breadth a decade. **The most valuable thing you can bring back is not a sample. It is a pitch record.**

**The storm.** Shelter off the plateau, then return immediately after and listen to the tone slide down over an hour. You will have watched the fastest thing that happens here.

**Static.** Watch for the scatter of blue snaps when a shelf settles, and the violet brush discharge standing on the lavacicle tips. Expect them to eat your electronics. Faraday pouches, fiber instead of copper, redundant media, and a pencil. Film shooters: dry-air static fog will put little lightning bolts on your frames. Some of your data will be lost. Write things down on paper anyway.

## 10. Taking things

Ideally: nothing.

A filament is not a specimen. It is a highway, a substrate telephone line, a surface bearing forty microns of the world's entire primary production, and a structural member of a shelf that took decades to felt. Cutting one is not collecting; it is amputation.

If the mission genuinely requires physical material, there is exactly one defensible source: **the old dead wool at the wall margin.** It is already fossil. It holds the archive — the fatter, older amblypygid bodies that prove the world is resizing its inhabitants. Take the minimum, from the margin, with sterile departiculated tools, logged, once. And know that you have committed a small permanent wrong, and write that down too.

## 11. The other bubbles

There are dozens of sealed vesicles in this flow. Most are still closed, still hot, still spinning wool in the dark with nothing alive inside them.

You will want to open one. You have hands, and a crack is only a flake or two a century, and you could make one in an afternoon.

Don't.

Not because it is forbidden, but because of what you would actually be doing: introducing your own biota, or nothing, into a pristine engine whose entire future depends on the timing and geometry of its opening. You would be deciding, casually, on a Tuesday, whether a world gets inhabited and by whom. And you would be doing it while carrying two hundred worlds' worth of water in your tissues. There is also a decent chance a sealed vesicle is hotter, pressurized, and full of gas that kills you when it lets go.

Map them. Listen to them. Each has its own fundamental; a geophone array across the plateau will give you a chord of sterile engines humming under the glass. **Bring home the map and the chord.** That is a discovery. An opened bubble is a contaminated bubble, and you only get one first look at each.

## 12. Prepare your answer to the temptation

The room is being manufactured shut. Nine hundred years, more or less — the survey numbers do not reconcile cleanly, and you should assume the pessimistic reading of every figure in this document, including that one. The shelves thicken, the nodes creep together, the big hunters have already been squeezed out of the bottom two galleries, and eventually the stack chokes on its own product and the note stops.

You will be lying there with your hand a meter from the crack, understanding exactly what is happening and exactly how to slow it down. Widen the aperture. Harvest some wool. Open the galleries. Keep the engine alive.

Decide *before you go* whether you are the kind of traveler who does that, because you will not decide it well at 4,300 meters with your chest humming.

The case against: you cannot maintain it forever, and a world that requires your maintenance is a different world — a garden, with a gardener who will eventually get bored or die. The closing is also the making of the fossil; the plateau is a library of plugged engines, and the dead ones may be readable in ways nothing else is. And this is not a unique rescue case. It is one instance of a process happening dozens of times under one plateau and probably on ten thousand plateaus across the multiverse.

The case for: it is a cup of water and a few thousand animals and nine hundred years, and you could give it more, and no one would ever know.

Both are real. Pick one, write it down, tell your partner. What is not acceptable is drifting into intervention by inches — a hand resting on the roof, a lens plate left "just for the night," a probe left in a gallery because retrieving it seems crueller than leaving it.

## 13. Leaving

Decontaminate at the margin, not at camp. The wool is charged and will cling to you in a halo; it will snap at your knuckles; it will be in your cuffs, your eyelashes, your camera bag, your beard. Bag the suit. Bag the boots. Account for every scrap you brought. Check the ground where you lay.

Quarantine in both directions. Nothing in there wants you — no pathogen adapted to you, no venom worth the name, nothing that can bite through a shell. The biological hazard runs entirely outward. But do not bet a biosphere on "probably harmless," and do not carry respirable glass home to shed into your lungs for the next decade. **No souvenirs.**

## 14. What it will feel like

Like standing on a parking lot. No vista, no color, no distance, no sound you can localize, nothing that will look back at you. Your knees hurt, your bottled air tastes of rubber, your chest is being played like a drum by a rock, and the plateau goes on being black and empty in every direction.

And then you turn your head aside and look at a slot four millimeters wide, and there is a blade of afternoon sun inside it lying across gold felt, and the felt is *moving* — not blowing, not crawling, but flexing in time, twenty-one times a second, a living crust breathing with a machine that nobody built.

You will be the largest thing that has ever been near this world, and the only thing that has ever known it exists. You will be capable of ending it by leaning slightly.

The correct expression of that is to lie very still, take nothing, write down the pitch, and come back in ten years to hear how far it has fallen.
