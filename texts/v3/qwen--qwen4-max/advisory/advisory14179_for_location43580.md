## First, understand what you are entering

It is not a cave, and it is not a building. It is the inside of an organ that has been alive for four hundred years, and you are going to be a foreign body in it — a soft new lump in a membrane that has memorized every millimetre of itself.

Everything about your preparation follows from that. The Cote is not fragile. It has survived seven collapses, a slump, and four centuries of being wholly rebuilt brick by brick. It can survive you. What it cannot easily absorb is *novelty*: a new sound, a new smell, a new shape in the acoustic map, a new idea. The ledger balances. Your whole job as a visitor is to enter without adding a line to it.

And be ready for the thing nobody warns you about: it will be **calm**. Bright as an overcast noon, blood-warm, wet as a mouth, deafening, and utterly, unbearably peaceful. Visitors weep at the boom and can't say why. It's because nothing in there is anxious. Four hundred years of nothing going wrong is a pressure you can feel on your chest, like the wing-thrum. Some people find it the most restful place they have ever stood. Some people have to leave in twenty minutes and sit on the grass shaking. Find out which you are on a short first visit, above ground hours, with an easy exit.

## The body: this is the part that can actually kill you

**Heat plus saturation is the real hazard.** Blood heat and "wet with it" means your sweat will not evaporate. You cannot cool. A healthy person can do light work in those conditions for a while and hard work for almost no time at all, and the failure mode is not discomfort, it's collapse. The keepers move slowly for a reason; their slow pace is not rustic charm, it is a thermodynamic treaty.

- Plan on **twenty minutes of walking or standing, then a cooling spell up the throat**, for your first several visits. Do not be proud about this. The keepers will not be impressed by your endurance; they will be alarmed by your red face.
- Drink salted water constantly, more than you think. Bring your own salt. Do not drink from the drip-pots (see below), the sump, or the sweat course, however tempting the sight of birds drinking off the brick.
- If you stop sweating, or get gooseflesh in that heat, or start feeling clever and cheerful and slightly detached, you are already in trouble. Say so immediately, into someone's ear, and go up.
- Do not go at all if you are pregnant, immunocompromised, asthmatic, or have any lung condition. This is not a legal disclaimer. The air is a warm aerosol of guano, feather-meal, lime and fungal spore. There is a pneumonia that lives in exactly this kind of place, and it is a weeks-long illness with a real mortality rate. Ask your physician about it before you go, not after. The keepers have a cough. You will notice it on the second day. They stopped noticing it in childhood.

**Airway.** Wear a properly fitted fine particulate respirator for the harvest, for the nine-day turning, and for your first visits generally. Accept that it muffles you and hides your face and makes you look like a coward; the keepers will find it funny and let you keep it. Negotiate this in advance so it isn't an insult. Later, if you're spending real time there, you'll do what they do — but understand you're taking on a chronic exposure that they took on before they could consent.

**Eyes and skin.** Everything you own will fog instantly. Glasses are near-useless at that humidity; bring anti-fog, or contacts, or accept blurred vision and move slower. Lime dust is alkaline and will chap your hands and sting your eyes; the ammonia will make you water for the first hour and then you'll stop noticing, which is not the same as it stopping. Gloves for turning. Nothing scented — no perfume, no scented sunscreen, no flavoured anything.

**Clothing.** Cotton or linen, thin, long-sleeved, close-fitting, washable, and *dull*. Specifically:

- **No wool, no down, no silk, no feathers, no leather trim.** Carpet beetles eat keratin. You will bring them home in your jacket and they will eat your grandmother's quilt.
- **Nothing that rustles.** No crinkly synthetics, no jangling zips, no clinking buckles. In a room that navigates by clicking, a rustle is a foreign utterance. Wrap or tape anything that ticks.
- **A close cap, no brim.** A brim makes you acoustically bigger than you look. Nothing dangling: no scarf ends, no camera strap, no loose laces. The far galleries are strung with orb-weavers and you will be walking through web; you want to be a smooth object.
- **Soft-soled dedicated cote shoes, left there.** Smooth tread, so you carry no seeds in or out. You will learn the keepers' shuffle and you will need it: the stair risers are nesting niches. A normal confident stride puts your toe into a throat with a bird in it. Step toe-first, look at every step, never take two risers at once.

**Kit that survives the climate.** Paper goes limp, ink runs, electronics sweat and die. Pencil on waterproof paper. Any optical gear must acclimatize sealed in a bag with desiccant for an hour before you open it, or every lens will be a white fog. Do not bring anything with a fan.

## The ears: protection is a trade, not a free good

The hiss is twenty thousand clicks a second, much of it above your range; the wing-thrum is pressure; the boom is a struck drumhead you feel in your teeth. It is genuinely loud and you should protect your hearing.

But **silence is the alarm**, and full occlusion makes you deaf to it. So:

- Musicians' filters, not plugs. Flat attenuation, low reduction. You must still be able to hear the counting-chant, the drip-scale, and a stop.
- **No noise-cancelling anything.** Not for sleeping, not for relief. Taking your ears out of the room is like blindfolding yourself on a cliff path, and it is rude in a way the keepers will feel before they can explain it.
- Learn the **visual** alarm before you learn anything else: what it looks like when eleven thousand heads come out at once, and what the keepers' hands do when the choir drops. Ask to be shown. Practise it.
- **No ultrasonic emitters of any kind.** No sonar rangefinders, no ultrasonic autofocus, no motion sensors, no pest repellers, no drone. You would be screaming into their eyes.

## Entering: the actual protocol

1. **Approach across the grass first.** Stand on the ordinary surface and look at it: the shutter, the catwalk, the four selenite slabs, the grated swallow, and in cold weather the column of steam the surface people call the cote's ghost. Understand that you are standing on a roof made of twenty feet of made ground and seven failed worlds. **Do not dig. Do not take a core sample. Do not land anything heavy. Do not walk on the catwalk or go near the slabs.** A shadow across the oculus is an eclipse, and an eclipse down there is an event.
2. **Ask the family. Be counted.** Everything in the Cote is in the ledger, including the cats. Ask to be written in. Give your name; accept a number. Learn the numbers before you learn words, exactly as the children do — the counting-chant is a low flat drone in a band the birds don't use, and it is the only channel you have. Learn the hand-signs. You will speak into ears, low and close, all day. Never shout. Never whistle.
3. **Go in with empty pockets and no bag.** You are walking into a vault: the saliva nests glued into the pot-backs are the entire wealth of this family, graded and counted and carried up. Sewn or empty pockets, nothing you could palm a nest into, and submit cheerfully to being counted on the way out. This is not an accusation. It's manners.
4. **Time your first visit to the empty half of the day.** Arrive after the eleven-minute exodus, mid-morning; leave well before dusk. Be nowhere near the throat at either shift-change. That four-second knot has never produced a collision *because nothing new has ever been in it.* You would be the first new thing. Watch it from the landing, later, with a keeper's hand on your arm.
5. **Let them introduce you.** Enter slowly, then stand still in the middle of the floor for a full minute and let the flock re-map you. They will click you intensively — you'll feel it on your face and hair, a dry attention, like being examined by sand. Don't flinch. Don't wave. Then walk the circumference once, at their pace, four minutes, so the room learns your shape at every angle.
6. **Nothing touches the wall. Ever.** This is the first commandment. Every hand's-breadth is a nest. Do not lean, do not brace, do not steady yourself, do not set a bag down against the brick, do not rest a hand on a gallery lip. Walk with your hands folded or holding the reed they give you to sweep the webs from your face. If you stumble, fall inward, into the floor, and take the compost. A crush of nestlings is a hole in the choir, and a hole in the choir is the alarm, and the alarm is the only thing anyone here is afraid of.
7. **Keep off the middle of the floor.** It is a working compost and it is too hot to hold your hand in. Stay on the walkway. Know which sector is being turned on the nine-day cycle and stay out of it; that's the day of maximum dust and maximum disturbance, and a poor day to visit.
8. **Give way to Aunt.** She is a colleague, not a specimen and not a pest. Do not handle her, feed her, photograph her at close range, or step over her. If she's on the walkway, wait. Learn how the keepers acknowledge her and do that. Do not remark that she is a different snake from the last one. She isn't.

## The forbidden acts

Most of these are not obvious, and all of them are serious.

- **Do not make music.** Do not hum, tap, whistle, clap, drum on a rail, or strike anything. The drip-pots are a tuned instrument and the five-note phrase is *language*. A descending figure means *nothing is wrong* and has meant it for four hundred years. Play it wrong and eleven thousand heads come out at once. Do not ladle the pots unless a keeper puts the ladle in your hand and stands beside you.
- **Never play a recording inside the Cote.** Not the choir, not the drip-scale, not your own voice. Playback is a lie in a language you don't speak.
- **No flash, no strobe, no torch, no sudden light or sudden dark.** Dawn here is a wooden shutter on a rope pulled by a woman. You do not get to make dawn.
- **No flame of your own.** No lighter, no match, no candle, no stove, no smoking, no vaping. There are a hundred rush-lamps in fat, straw everywhere, and a compost floor. Fire is the one thing that could end this place in an hour. Ask, on your first day, what the fire signal is and where you are to stand.
- **No soap, no aerosol, no repellent, no insecticide.** The whole food web is indoors: crickets, guano-flies, beetles, woodlice, worms, geckos, spiders, fish, snakes, cats. Poisoning the insects starves the birds. Eat above ground; bring no crumbs.
- **Bring nothing living and nothing new.** No seeds in your tread, no feather from another world, no bird product, no culture, no soil. Scrub and quarantine your kit. Eighty years ago a vine came down uninvited and they still cut it to exactly two arms every spring, because they like it and will not have it. That is the whole policy toward novelty in one sentence.
- **Do not offer improvements.** No LEDs, no stainless spatula, no polymer pots, no better lime, no fan, no antibiotic, no scheme. Nothing here is original and nothing is new, and those are the same fact. Every brick has been replaced twice; the wear outlives the material; each new brick is laid to the old worn line. The place is finished the way a river is finished. Efficiency is a wound. If you want to help — and you should want to help — carry straw down in the usual bundles, turn the chaff with a wooden rake, whitewash a course on the rotation, trim nothing, count.
- **If you break something, say so at once.** There is a repair rotation older than the family. Your crime is not the broken pot; it's the concealed line in the ledger.

## The two sins peculiar to your kind of traveller

**Do not compare.** Do not mention the Cote in the next branch over with the same eleven thousand and forty throats. Do not mention the one that is dark, or the one where the greys died, or the one where they use a machine. Do not say *interesting, ours has stone.* This place's identity is a number, a worn line, and a phrase. Comparison is not conversation here; it's a solvent.

**Do not offer the dead.** Once a year at midday they close the shutter and hold the room quiet for a minute for everyone who died when the seventh cote came down. You will be able to think of a world where it didn't. You may even be able to go there. **Do not say so. Do not offer. Do not bring anyone back.** Grief that has been kept perfectly for four hundred years, in a jar, in the light, is not a problem awaiting your technology. If you are present for the minute: stand still, say nothing, take no picture, and let yourself hear the dome settling one grain at a time and your own blood going round. Your stomach will be embarrassingly loud. So is everyone's. That's the point of it.

## Sleep, time, and the long hazard

There has been no darkness in the Low Cote for four centuries. Your body does not know what to do with that. Sleep above ground for the first weeks; bring a real eye mask and use it in a surface room; keep your own day rigidly even when the room insists it is always workaday noon. Do not ask for the shutter to be closed for your comfort.

And be honest with yourself about the seduction. This is a place that converts matter into pattern — bricks into a worn line, snakes into a name, people into a rotation. Stay long enough and it will begin converting you: you'll count before you speak, you'll dream the drip-scale, you'll shuffle, you'll stop noticing the cough, and one day somebody will call you by a role instead of your name and it will feel like home. That is not a metaphor and it is not necessarily bad. But decide *before you go down* how long you're staying, write it on your hand, and keep one original thing about yourself that you do not lay to the old worn line.

## What to bring, briefly

Salt. Water bottle. Flat-attenuation ear filters. Fitted particulate respirator. Thin cotton, no wool, close cap, soft dedicated shoes. Anti-fog. Pencil and waterproof paper. Gloves. Desiccant and sealed bags. A reed for the webs, or the manners to accept one. Straw bought on the local surface, carried down in the usual bundle, as your gift — identical, invisible, a line the ledger can absorb.

Leave behind: bags, cameras with flash or autofocus sonar, wool, scent, flame, food, souvenirs-in-waiting, opinions, and every version of this place you have already seen.

## Leaving

Plan on **a day of surface for every day below.** Sit on the grass in the weather nobody down there thinks about. Let the ordinary wind be boring. Expect the hiss to follow you into quiet rooms for a week; expect ordinary silence to feel like an alarm, and expect to wake once or twice with your heart going because a room somewhere stopped.

Don't take a pot. A pot is nothing — it's been replaced twice, and the matter was never the point. Take the depth of the threshold hollow, measured with your own finger. Take the five notes written down, and understand you are carrying a key: never sound that phrase in another world's Cote, because you would be telling eleven thousand animals that nothing is wrong, and you would not know whether it was true.

The right souvenir is the opposite of a taking. You walked the circumference in four minutes, several times a day, in soft shoes, and you sanded the worn line by a fraction of nothing. The place used you a little. That's the only honest way to have been there: to have added wear, and no lines.
