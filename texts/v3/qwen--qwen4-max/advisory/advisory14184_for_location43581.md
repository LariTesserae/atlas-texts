# Before You Go: The Plain That Makes Things

## The one fact that organizes all the others

This is not a hostile environment in the ordinary sense. Nothing here hunts, nothing here resents you, nothing here will notice you at all. It is a **manufacturing process** — a vapor-deposition reactor forty kilometers wide, running unattended, with a seismic problem and no lights.

And you are the coldest thing in it.

Read that again, because every practical decision follows from it. The vapor plates onto anything a fraction cooler than itself. The pavement is blood-warm. The fresh towers are cherry-red. You will be trying to keep the inside of your suit at something a mammal can survive. To this plain, you are not an intruder. You are a substrate — the most attractive one that has ever been offered to it. Everything in the air is looking for somewhere to become solid, and you are the best offer for forty kilometers.

You are also the only water in the world. Not the only *free* water — the only water, anywhere, in any form. Your breath, your sweat, the liter and a half sloshing in your gut: this is an anomaly the plain has never had to deal with.

So prepare not to *survive* a landscape. Prepare to enter a hot cell without being plated, poisoned, cooked, cut, buried, or copied — and to leave without introducing weather to a place that has never had any.

## Measure five things before you commit to anything

Do not plan this trip from a description, including this one. Send a probe, or spend your first hour at the rim on a tether, and get numbers.

**1. Pressure.** The description "almost all the atmosphere lies below this pavement" is a warning, not a figure. Below about 6 kPa your saliva boils at body temperature and no mask, no filter, no amount of toughness will help you; you need a pressure garment. Bring a dropper of water and watch what a bead of it does on a cold plate. That single test tells you which category of visit this is.

But note: it almost doesn't matter what the answer is, because of number three. **There is no version of this visit that involves exposed skin.**

**2. The local vapor temperature, one centimeter off the crust.** This is the most important number you will take, because it defines the plating threshold, and the plating threshold defines your entire suit.

Here is the intuition. Deposition here needs only a *fraction* of undercooling. That means the way to become invisible to the process is not to be clean, or sealed, or slick — it is to be **hot**. A surface at or above the local vapor temperature has nothing offered to it. It is not a candidate.

So the correct architecture is counterintuitive and you should make peace with it early: **a hot skin over a cold person.** Heated window, heated joints, heated antenna, heated boot shells, insulated interior, and a cold plate against your back. Run the outside above the local vapor temperature and the plain will simply not see you. Run it cold and you will spend the whole visit being slowly turned into a statue, which is exactly what the casts around you are the record of.

If the number comes back at 90 °C, this is an engineering afternoon. If it comes back at 400 °C, this is a serious vehicle and you should probably visit by remote presence instead. Get the number first.

**3. What is actually in the vapor.** "Metals riding on carbon monoxide" is not a poetic phrase. It is a description of metal carbonyl chemistry — the same trick by which industry plates pure nickel and pure iron out of gas. Mirror-bright iron appearing on cool surfaces is what that process *looks like*.

Which means you should assume, until proven otherwise, that you are standing in nickel tetracarbonyl and iron pentacarbonyl vapor, along with CO, sulfur species, and halogen acids.

Nickel carbonyl is one of the more unpleasant substances a person can meet. It is dangerous at concentrations you cannot perceive, it crosses skin, and — this is the part that kills travelers — **it makes you feel fine.** The pulmonary edema arrives twelve to twenty-four hours later, in your bunk, after you have already told everyone the trip was uneventful.

Therefore:
- No filter cartridges. No "I'll just hold my breath." No cartridge is rated for this and no cartridge tells you when it has failed.
- Closed-circuit rebreather, metal-sealed, no elastomer you haven't checked for permeation. Nothing with an intake. Nothing that vents.
- Full dermal barrier, including the neck, including the wrists, including the two millimeters of gap you didn't think about.
- Two independent CO detectors and one carbonyl-specific monitor, worn, alarmed, logged. You cannot smell your way out of this: CO is odorless, and the sulfur will fatigue your nose in minutes anyway. **Your nose is not an instrument here. Nothing smells at a distance, which means nothing warns at a distance.**
- A mandatory 48-hour post-visit observation with someone who knows what to look for, and a low threshold for hyperbaric oxygen. "I only have a headache" is the sentence this place kills people with.

**4. Deposition rate on witness coupons.** Hang a rack of small plates at a range of temperatures on a pole, two meters up and at ankle height, and time them. Photograph them. You want to know how many minutes of cold exposure it takes to (a) fog an optic, (b) seize a bearing, (c) weld a boot to the floor. A metal film only needs to be a hundred nanometers thick to be opaque. Given that a boot depression fills a centimeter in ten minutes, do not assume you have long.

**5. The surge period.** Every few hours a throat quickens and a forest goes up in a quarter hour. You need the interval, the phase, and the precursor. Watch the fizzing at a seam, watch the lid's glow, count new orange per minute. Then set your abort at the *first* sign of quickening, not at the surge itself, because the walk out takes as long as it takes.

## Heat: the light is a thermometer, so use it as one

The single most useful thing about this place is that **brightness is temperature and temperature is age.** Dull cherry means roughly six or seven hundred degrees. Black means older than about a quarter hour and probably touchable.

Turn that into a rule a tired person can follow: **walk on black. Never step on orange. Never approach orange.**

Understand what you're looking at when a field of spires goes up glowing. Those are not pretty. Those are forty-meter radiators at furnace temperature, and in air this thin you cannot shed heat by convection — the air "carries almost nothing," which means it carries almost nothing *away from you*. Your suit's normal cooling strategy does not exist here. Radiant rejection and consumable coolant are all you have.

Budget the coolant honestly. Sublimating water absorbs a bit under three megajoules per kilogram; call it 700–800 watts per kilogram per hour. Walking, plus your own metabolism, plus radiant load from anything glowing nearby, and you are spending **one to two kilograms of ice per hour**, and none of it can be replaced locally, because there is no water here, at all, anywhere.

Your visit is not measured in hours. It is measured in kilograms. Write the number on your wrist and turn around at half of it.

Two further notes on heat:

- Aluminized outer layers are the right idea and will degrade. Rough deposited needles are not a mirror. Assume your reflectance falls through the sortie and pad the budget.
- Do not sit. Do not kneel. Do not lie down. The description of this place will tell you to put your palm flat on the pavement and feel the world arrive through your bones, and it is *right* that this is the most extraordinary sensation available here, and it is also a way to cook your hand, cast your hand, and weld your glove to the ground in the same gesture. There is a better way to do it. See below.

## The listening staff

Sound doesn't travel in air here, but it travels beautifully in stone, and it arrives *early*, because stone is faster. A collapse four hundred meters away is silent to your ear and unmistakable through the ground.

This is your only early-warning system and it is a good one. Build your kit around it.

Carry a **staff**: a stiff rod with a hard ceramic foot, a contact microphone at the tip, and a bone-conduction pad at the top where you hold it against your jaw or the mastoid behind your ear. Plant it, and the plain speaks into your skull. Boot soles with contact pickups give you a second and third channel, so you can triangulate.

Then *drill*, before you go, on recordings and on a simulated floor, until you can tell without thinking:

- the long grinding roar — a collapse, inbound, and you have seconds;
- the fizz — a seam, close, and you are standing in the plume;
- the tick and ping — local spall, harmless, and you will learn to stop flinching at it.

The staff also does your other essential job: it probes the floor. Which brings us to the thing that will actually get you if the chemistry doesn't.

## The floor is not a floor

The pavement is a crust over a slow hot mass, and it drinks. Fragments dimple it and go down over an hour or a day. A terrace has already foundered somewhere out there and gone soft.

So: **the crust is a lid, and you do not know its thickness.**

Rules for walking:

- Probe every step in the dark. Every step. Make it a gait, not a decision.
- **Convexity means void.** A low blister inflating is a bubble under you. The description's test is the right one: look away, look back, and if it has changed, it is not ground. Never cross a swelling.
- Avoid lips, rims, and rings. A raised collar of layered salt around a hole is a throat, and throats surge, and you do not want to be the coolest object within a hundred meters when one does.
- Avoid anything freshly loaded. Where a tower came down, the crust is now carrying a new weight and beginning to swallow it. The debris field is the *least* stable ground, not the most.
- Never shelter under an arch. The fused pairs are accidents, not structures. They stand because they haven't finished failing.
- Keep three times a feature's height away from it. A forty-meter stovepipe folds at the knee and reaches farther than you think, and it reaches you at six hundred degrees with edges that have never been rounded by anything.

And the counterintuitive comfort: **the emptiness is the safe part.** Two kilometers of bare, unmarked, faintly glossy crust is the best ground on the plain. Four or five presences per horizon means most of this world is a place you can walk. The features are the hazards. Sightsee from a distance, with optics, from the rim. Do not go and stand in the forest of hollow spires. The forest is the worst place here, and it is the place you will most want to go.

## Seeing in a world that only shows itself in flashes

Your eyes will be nearly useless in the ordinary way, and you should stop asking them to be.

Ambient light is ox-blood, from below, dim. Then a fracture flash goes off — hard white quartz-light — and takes your dark adaptation with it. You cannot maintain scotopic vision in a strobe. Accept this and design around it.

**Infrared is your primary eye.** Everything here is thermally legible: new growth blazes, old crust is dim, and — crucially — the rising plumes show up as bright columns you can see and route around. In visible light a plume is nothing until it's coating you. In IR it is obvious. The beautiful shimmering column is the thing that will cook and plate you; learn to read it as a wall.

**Use the flashes rather than fighting them.** A flash-triggered camera that catches a still and *holds* it on a display gives you the world the way the world offers it: a mosaic of stale photographs, updated whenever something breaks. Navigate on the mosaic with inertial dead reckoning between. Understand and accept that every image you are steering by is out of date, and that in fifteen minutes the terrain in it will have finished being replaced.

**Treat the visor as a shutter, not a window.** This is the conclusion people resist and then thank you for. If a bare cold window silvers over in seconds to minutes, you cannot look through it continuously. So: heated window above the local vapor temperature if your measurements allow it; a fast electrochromic or mechanical shutter to save your eyes from the flashes; a stack of tear-off films, more than you think is absurd, and the habit of pulling one on a schedule rather than when you notice you've gone blind. Plus a wiper. Plus a *second* independent optical path, because going blind on a foundering crust in a silent collapse field is the specific way this place kills a competent person.

Drill blind walking on a tether before you go. Ten minutes of it, at home, in the dark, with a staff. It will change how you pack.

## Navigation when nothing keeps still

There are no landmarks here. Not "landmarks are hard to see" — **landmarks do not persist.** A spire you steered by is black in fifteen minutes, down in an hour, drunk and reissued as something else by tomorrow. The map you made on the way out is wrong on the way back.

What is fixed:

- **The seams.** They are plumbing, they are structural, they are where the fractures are. Map the seam network, not the towers. This is the only map worth drawing.
- **The rim.** A hard baseline. Get a bearing on it in a big flash and write it down with a timestamp.
- **Your inertial log and your tether.** Physical tether for the first hundred meters, always, even if it feels insulting.
- **Your own trail — which rises.** This is the strange gift of the place. Your boot depressions fill preferentially and stand a centimeter proud, bright then black, and last about a day. Your backtrack is a raised ridge you can *feel with the staff in the dark*. It is a better guide than anything you can see. Use it.

Which means every position fix should be written down with a time, and you should trust the log over your memory, because your memory is of a landscape that no longer exists. The plain has no history. You have to bring your own.

## The stain

A film a few microns thick, spreading at a slow walk, that makes fresh mineral grow in courses, in bond, in right angles. Stairs. Sills. Thresholds. A doorway framing more plain.

It is not a ruin and it is not made. It is a habit of crystallization that propagates by contact — a pattern that survives the destruction of every one of its instances. It is the closest thing here to life, and whether it is alive is not a question you need to settle in order to handle it correctly.

Handle it as **a contact-transmissible architecture.**

- Do not touch it. Do not walk through it. Do not sample it with anything you intend to keep.
- If you contact it, abandon the item. Not decontaminate — *abandon*. You have no water, no solvent, and no biocide that means anything to a thing that isn't alive. Heat-soak what you can above its stability range; leave what you can't.
- Nothing from this plain enters your habitat, your vehicle, or your home world in an unsampled, unannealed state. This is the rule that matters beyond your own life. A pattern that copies itself by contact into any accreting mineral is not something to introduce to a universe with limestone in it.

And one thing to watch for, which I offer as a caution rather than a claim. Your boot tread is a repeating right-angled pattern pressed into fresh accreting mineral, and your trail is a course of them laid across the crust. If you ever look back and see your own tread pattern *continuing past where you walked* — propagating outward, coursed, in bond — leave immediately, leave the boots, and do not return with the same equipment.

## You are a water event

There has never been anything wet here. Nothing has been weathered, nothing has been rounded, no edge has been softened, there is no stain of long exposure anywhere on this plain.

You are carrying five liters of the most reactive substance this world has ever encountered, plus a sweat gland system, plus a leak risk.

- Absolute closed loop. Rebreather, no venting, no exhaled moisture to the outside. No spitting, no urinating, no rinsing, no "just a little."
- Sweat is contained or it is a spill. Design for it.
- If you breach — suit tear, coolant line, drinking valve — you abort, and you also *log the site*, because you have just created the first wet patch in the history of this province and someone should know where it is.

Two more consequences of your own chemistry that people miss:

**Your oxygen is a fire hazard.** The ambient is CO and sulfur with essentially no oxidizer, so nothing here burns. Your suit exhaust is oxygen leaking into hot CO over hot finely-divided metal. Do not vent oxygen near a seam. Closed circuit, again, for the third reason.

**Your suit may catch fire in the airlock.** Everything that plates onto you will be freshly reduced, atomically fine metal — the kind of iron and nickel powder that ignites on contact with air. Strip the outer layer in vacuum, purge inert, passivate slowly with dilute oxygen, and *never* repressurize a plated suit with breathable mix as your first step. This is the hazard that gets people who did everything else right.

## The mind, which is the part you will underestimate

Nothing watches. Nothing flees the collapse. Nothing here has ever held an intention.

And there are stairs. Eight of them, rising to a landing that supports nothing, standing alone two kilometers from anything, glowing faintly at the risers because they were finished eleven minutes ago.

You are a pattern-completing animal in a dark strobe-lit place full of doorways. You will see architecture, and then you will see *address*, and then you will find yourself walking toward it. That is not a mystical risk; it is the ordinary behavior of a human nervous system in these conditions, and it is the reason to make rules now, while you are warm and rational and reading this.

- **Never go through a doorway. Never climb a stair. Never enter an arch.** Not because something will happen, but because you will start believing something is meant.
- Do not build anything. Do not stack stones. Do not knock, tap in patterns, or leave a mark that could be read as a message. You will not be answering anything. You will be starting a conversation with yourself that you cannot end.
- Do not name the features. Names imply persistence and this place has none.
- Keep two columns in your log: *what I saw* and *what I thought*. Read them at the turnaround. The gap between them is your actual condition.
- A partner, a voice on a schedule, and a hard clock. The strobe, the silence of great events, and the intimacy of the small sounds together produce a state very like trance. People stay for one more flash. Then another. The plain will happily keep you until your ice runs out; it will not even record that you were there.

Because that is the last thing to prepare for, and it is not a hazard exactly. Your footprints will rise, brighten, blacken, and be drunk back down inside a day. Everything you touch will be reabsorbed and reissued as a needle or an antler or a threshold. Nothing here writes anything but addition, and destruction is only the middle of a sentence. You will not leave a mark. There is no kind of mark this place is able to keep.

Some travelers find that unbearable and some find it the whole point. Know which one you are before you spend the ice.

## Kit, compactly

Pressure garment, metal-sealed, closed-circuit rebreather, no exposed sliding surfaces, no zippers, no fabric that sheds. Hot outer skin with the temperature set by your measurement, not by your comfort. Insulated standoff soles. Cut- and puncture-resistant everything: every fracture surface here is a first surface, and the silica hairs are respirable, biopersistent, and have never been weathered soft. Heated shuttered visor, absurd number of tear-offs, wiper, second optical path. IR camera, flash-triggered still camera, inertial logger. Listening staff with contact mic and bone-conduction pad; boot geophones. Two CO monitors, one carbonyl monitor. Ice, weighed and budgeted. Witness coupons. Tether. Inert purge and a vacuum strip bag for the return. A 48-hour medical plan and someone at home who knows the words "delayed pulmonary edema."

Nothing with an intake. Nothing that vents. Nothing you are unwilling to abandon.

## Abort triggers, decided in advance

First sign of quickening at any throat. Any monitor alarm. Any visor failure. Any suit temperature outside band. Any tear, any leak, any wet. Any blister underfoot. Half your ice. Any moment you catch yourself walking toward a doorway.

## What to actually do when you get there

Walk out onto the black crust, into the emptiness, well away from anything glowing. Plant the staff. Hold it against your jaw. Stand still — but shift your feet, keep shifting, don't let the plain start making a record of your ankles.

Wait.

Something enormous will come down somewhere out in the dark, and you will hear nothing at all, and the whole world will arrive up through your boots and the rod and into the bones of your face as a long grinding roar. Then you will lift the staff and there will be silence, and a flash will show you a plain that is already not the one you were looking at.

That is the visit. That is the entire reason to go. Do that once, on good ground, with your ice half spent, and then turn around and follow the raised ridge of your own footsteps home.
