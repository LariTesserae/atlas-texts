# Preparing for The Bell

## 0. Reframe the trip

Do not plan a visit. Plan a **dive**.

Everything about this place is a limited-consumable problem: your air is finite, your cold is finite, your silence is finite, and the environment is not hostile so much as *utterly uninterested in whether you survive it*. There is no predator here, no malice, no weather that means you harm. There is only a chemistry that has never once had to accommodate a wet, oxygen-breathing, fast-moving animal, and a civic architecture with no locks because nothing has ever needed one.

You will be the first thing in the history of this world that can hurry, take, and leave. Behave accordingly.

---

## 1. The air: you are bringing fire into a world that cannot make it

There is no oxidizer in the Bell. That is not an inconvenience; it is the load-bearing fact of the entire civilization. Their fluid is made from hydrogen and sour gas. Their kilns are lowered into hot seep-throats on bast cord and left for a season because *reduction is the only firing there is*. Their blades stay sharp forever. Their windows last a hundred thousand years. All of it rests on the absence of the thing you carry in a bottle on your back.

Therefore:

- **Closed-circuit, zero-vent, no exceptions.** No open-circuit exhaust, no purge valve that dumps into ambient, no "just a little bleed." A hydrogen atmosphere plus your oxygen leak plus a dry-dust static spark is a detonation in a sealed pressure vessel with a self-supporting roof arch that has been in balance for a million years. You would not be a person who had an accident. You would be the end of a world.
- **No flames, no strikers, no chemical heat packs, no oxygen candles as spares.** If your kit list contains anything that produces heat by oxidation, leave it on the plain. Your emergency heat is not needed anyway; your emergency *cold* is.
- Treat the ambient as immediately lethal: sour gas at seep concentrations drops you between one breath and the next, and the almond fraction should be assumed cyanide-class until you have personally assayed it. Your nose is useless here — sulfide olfactory paralysis is fast, and anyway the whole city smells like almonds to them and like nothing at all to a sealed suit.
- **Antistatic everything.** Conductive outer shell, dissipative boots, a trailing bond strap, no fleece, no rapid doffing. Dry salt dust with no adsorbed moisture is a triboelectric nightmare. Ambient won't ignite without oxidizer — but your suit's vicinity might, and their sap-lines are full of things that would rather not meet a spark.
- Materials: assume the oil attacks every elastomer you own. It crawls, it does not bead, it does not evaporate cleanly, it *varnishes*. It will seize your joints, film your visor, clog your regulators, and glaze your radiator fins until your cooling efficiency drops by half. Bring spare seals, a solvent that is safe in a closed loop, and a scraping tool. Plan to be cleaned hourly.

That last point matters more than it sounds. **You will need to be tended, and tending is their form of intimacy.** A wright's hand-fan is ten thousand tendrils of hook and abrasive working at a speed you'd need an hour to confirm. Letting one scrape the lacquer off your knee joint is the closest thing to a handshake this world has. Accept it. Sit still for it. It will take an afternoon and it will be the first time anyone touches you.

## 2. The water: you are a solvent with a heartbeat

You are roughly forty liters of the one substance that has never existed here.

Understand precisely what that means. Their blades never dull because nothing can dissolve them. Their windows are cleaved mountain, clear for a hundred millennia, and would weep and slump in a wet world within a year. Their streets, their stairs, their bones, their bones' bones — all halite, all anhydrite, all soluble. A single drop of your sweat on a hundred-thousand-year window etches a permanent flaw into the oldest artifact in the world. A nosebleed on a plaza defaces a chronicle that masons read like a history book. A leaking suit is not a personal tragedy; it is an industrial-scale chemical attack on a city that has no concept of an enemy that isn't shade.

So:

- **Double containment, and a suit that drinks its own breath.** Your exhaled humidity must be condensed, captured, and re-consumed inside the loop. Nothing out. Not vapor, not condensate, not a drip from a glove cuff.
- No spitting, no crying where it can fall, no wet wipes, no open water, no drinking in the open, no damp clothing, no "it's only a little."
- Sterilize and dry the exterior before descent. Your skin biome is a wet ecology in a bag; a breach seeds a world that has never had a bacterium with a flagellum.
- **Blunt kit only.** No knives, no picks, no crampons, no sharp buckles, nothing that can nick a sealed sap-line. Their entire civilization is pressurized plumbing that never pools; a puncture is a hemorrhage, and the spilled cup leaves a black ring that stays for ten thousand years. You do not want your name in the floor.
- If you are injured: contain, then ascend. Bleeding on the floor is the worst thing you can do here, and it is worse than anything you can do to yourself.

There is a related discipline of the mind. **Do not demonstrate.** You may tell them, slowly and honestly, what you are made of, if asked in a way you understand. But never show them what water does to salt. Their word for *enemy* is a word for *shade*. Do not give them a second word.

## 3. Heat: your bottom time is a cold budget

Ambient is not the flat blast of the plain; it is patient, saturated, ancient heat, and the crown is worse than the floor because heat climbs and pools against the roof slab. In a sealed suit you cannot sweat, you cannot radiate to walls that are hotter than you, and you cannot convect. Your only sink is **conduction into cold mass**.

Which means: **the cold-salt trade is your life support.**

The dense blocks hauled out hot and hauled back cold, the porters and sledges and cold-storage slots — that is not local color, that is your gas supply. Before descent, contract for cold. Learn where the cold slots are on every terrace you intend to enter. Carry a block on your back and a spare in your loop, and treat the swap interval as your hard turnaround rule, exactly as a diver treats thirds.

- **Never go below your last cold slot with less than a full return margin.** The floor near the vents cooks a hand laid on it; you will be lying on it to talk.
- Do not go up. The crown is hot, crowded, poor, stunted, and sheds roof. It is also not your business.
- Wear a hard hat regardless. The arch has been falling flake by flake for a million years, and occasionally a slab lets go and takes a long time to cross the gulf.
- The once-a-day coin of true daylight is a *wound*, focused by salt. Know the pillar-calendar before you know anything else. Be behind cleaved-halite shutters or up the throat when it lands. Your suit is black and your cooling budget is finite; the coin will find you if you let it.

There is a temptation here you should decide about now, on the plain, in the cold. You can carry a cold block from the night-side wall to a rich district in ninety seconds. Their porters take a season. You could be the wealthiest entity in the history of the Bell by lunchtime, and you would wreck the plumbing trade, the porter guilds, the district hierarchy, and the meaning of wealth in a single afternoon. **Do not sell your speed.** Carry your own cold. Carry a neighbor's cold once, as a gift, with permission, and let it be a story rather than an industry.

## 4. Sound: go deaf on purpose, and listen with your hands

The seep choir is thousands of whistles retuned twice daily by the breath. The wet hour is a hundred thousand tuned drip-cups struck by condensed sap-fog, and it is the loudest thing in this world. Under it, at the pressure turn, the Bell booms below hearing and the note comes back off the far wall in eleven seconds.

- **Serious hearing protection, worn always, from the Mouth down.** Not because the noise is unpleasant but because it is unrelenting and you will be here for weeks.
- This costs you nothing socially, because **nobody talks through the air.** Speech is percussion through the lattice. Grammar lives in the intervals. Deafness is the normal condition of a visitor and hearing is a luxury.
- Build a **root-plate prosthesis**: a hard flat pad, couplant, on a stalk or a hinge so you can lift it clear of the stone. Palm, forearm, sternum, knees — you will read best through bone. Practice at home until you can distinguish interval patterns without counting.
- **Decouple at the pressure turn.** The boom goes through the trunk of you; infrasound at that amplitude is nausea, chest resonance, visual smear, and a specific dread with no object. Lift the plate. Sit on something dead — a vacated trunk, a salt block, a bast pad. Then set the plate down again and count the eleven seconds, because eleven seconds is a unit of time and also the length of a sentence, and you will need it as a metronome.
- Never drum on a switchboard slab. Forty conversations pass through one plaza and none of them touch; a stranger's random knocking there is not noise, it is forty simultaneous obscenities. Find a dead slab, a buttress root, an empty trunk floor. Ask first, by knocking on the *approach*, not on the board.

## 5. Light: be an outline, not a lamp

Light costs sap. Three cups in a private room is scandal. A person of consequence is a person you can see, and the great plazas are dark.

- **Do not shine anything.** A torch is heat, extravagance, obscenity, and a small version of the wound. If you must have photons, use the dimmest diffuse red you can navigate by, pointed down, and expect to be sealed against.
- Bring **passive light amplification** instead of emission. It changes nothing about the room and everything about your ability to survive it.
- Then do the correct thing: **paint your edges.** Luminous film on every seam, cuff, heel, shoulder, helmet rim — matched to their green, tended, re-fed, scraped and renewed. The Bell at rest is a diagram of itself, a black volume with a hundred thousand faint green lines hanging in it. The middles of things are invisible. If you arrive as a lit blob you are a scandal; if you arrive as a faint green outline of a person you are *legible*, and legibility is the whole of politeness here.
- Navigational corollary: **assume every dark rectangle is a window until proven otherwise.** The oldest halite is so clear that looking through it is like looking through nothing. You will walk into a hundred-thousand-year artifact at shoulder height and crack it, and your suit will be wet inside, and you will have committed the two worst acts available to you in one second. Read the edges. The edges are the map.

## 6. Movement: you are weather, and your shadow is an assault

Nothing here has ever hurried. Their word for enemy is *shade*. A slowly advancing shadow is a siege; a flickering shadow is a barrage. You, walking, are a strobe of assault on every blade you pass.

- **Keep to the void.** The rind is property; the gulf is commons. Two hundred paces of hot dim nothing between lit quarters is not a street and never needed to be one. It is also your road, your only road, and the reason a visitor can exist here at all. Cross the dark. Never pass between a wright and its vent, its lamp, or its cool wall.
- **Give three paces of berth, minimum.** The hand-fan is body, tool, and life's work at once. Brushing past is amputation and the destruction of forty years of lamina.
- **Slow to the speed of the room.** Practice stillness before you come: an hour motionless is the minimum unit of being noticed. Nothing here will look at you faster than that. There are no eyes; there is attention, and attention is measured in hours.
- The floor dust has no cohesion and behaves like a liquid: it ripples, slumps, dunes across the lower steps at every breath. You will sink. Stay on pavement where you can, use three points of contact, and tether on terraces — the drops are unlit and ninety paces of gulf is a long way to fall in a suit.
- **Do not walk on the sweepers.** The cilia carpets are living creatures passing dust hand to hand toward the collection slots, a season to cross a square. Cross at the slots. Ask for a route.
- **Walk the same path every time.** There are no tracks in this city and there never have been; the only floor-marks are ten thousand years of spilled-oil rings, and the masons read them as a chronicle. Your footprints will be the first tracks anyone has ever made here, and they will be read for centuries. Leave one clean line, not a scribble. You are writing the first entry in a history that has never had a walker in it.

## 7. Time, lodging, and the shape of a conversation

A sentence is eleven seconds. An introduction is an afternoon. A negotiation is a month. A stair takes forty years and is not a large project. To go elsewhere, a citizen grows a runner and buds a new trunk over decades.

- **Budget a season, not a day.** Anything shorter and you will be a smear in the record: a thing that came, made noise, and left before anyone finished turning toward it.
- **Lodge in a vacated trunk.** When a wright transfers itself, the old trunk is left standing, empty, furnished, and eventually let to a stranger. This is the guest-house, the inn, the spare room of the entire world, and it is the only architecture in the Bell shaped like you. It is also a good thermal address, a good acoustic address, and a place where your presence is a normal category rather than an emergency.
- Bring patience drills, not entertainment. Your entertainment is the outline, the chord, the ember hour, and the slow green tide.
- Prepare for a specific loneliness: **nothing will ever respond at your speed.** You will feel like a ghost in a photograph. The dangerous symptom is hurry-sickness — the urge to *make something happen*, to finish a sentence for someone, to unplug a vent, to fix a forty-year siege in an afternoon. That urge is the single greatest threat you pose. Name it in advance and give it a rule: *I do not complete other people's decades.*

## 8. Politics: a world with no locks and no immune system

There is no predation, so there is no fear, so every door is a valve against weather and nothing else. You are the first thing that could take and run. Their entire moral architecture has no defense against you and never needed one.

- **Bind yourself publicly.** Announce your constraints in intervals, slowly, to whoever will hold still. Let yourself be sealed against. You *are* weather: warm, wet-seeming, wrong-smelling. A district closing a valve on you is not an insult; it is the correct response to you.
- **Graft, don't sign.** Treaties here are anastomoses — a shared conduit that is the agreement, so that breaking it means cutting a pipe that feeds you. You cannot grow one. So plumb one. Bring tube, couplings, a tee. Tie your cooling loop into a district's radiator run so that your waste heat feeds something of theirs and their cold feeds you. **Make yourself cuttable.** A visitor who can leave at no cost is a visitor who cannot be trusted; a visitor whose life support is a shared pipe is a citizen of a kind they can parse.
- **Do not take sides.** Their wars are shading, root-girdling, sour exudates along a boundary, a vent quietly plugged and a crime discovered forty years later as a smell. You could end any of these in a minute. Don't. Don't unplug anything, don't carry exudates, don't accept a cold-haul commission from a party in a boundary dispute. You would be a weapon that arrives before the declaration.
- **When a quarter blooms**, it converts to spore-tissue, roars its sap up as fog, and dies back; the quarter is sealed and kept dark for a decade, the neighbors harvest the tube-wood, and the mourning is indistinguishable from the carpentry. Do not call it death. Do not try to save anyone. Do not take a piece of tube-wood as a souvenir: a dead neighbor becomes stairs, and you would be robbing a grave and a load-bearing member at once. Sit in the dark with them for the decade's first hour. That is the funeral.

## 9. What to offer, and what to refuse

Their currency is moved heat, sap, fired amber pots, tube-wood, and labor-time. Almost every gift you can imagine is an economic weapon: ceramics from a kiln-world would collapse the value of the most precious objects in existence; a cold-courier service would gut the porter trade; a bright lamp would make light meaningless.

Refuse all of it. Offer instead the two things only you have:

**Be an eye.** They have no word for the plain except *the place where the spores go*. Nobody has ever crossed it; there are no tracks anywhere and never have been. You can go out, and look, and come back, and say what is there. Where the yellow snow lands. How far. Whether anything at the far scarp is green. This costs nothing material and it is a gift beyond price, and it comes with a duty: report honestly, including the almost-certain answer that almost all of it dies. Do not manufacture hope to be liked.

**Be a black box.** Twice in recorded history a stranger came down the throat, grew wrong, smelled wrong, and was not removed — because twice the strangers survived a gas spike that killed everything else. You are, genuinely, the only thing in the Bell that is indifferent to its atmosphere. When the seeps go sour and a district dies, you can walk through it and remember it. Offer that. It is honest, it distorts nothing, and it is the most valuable thing a fast wet creature can give a slow dry city.

It also costs you: you must be willing to be present at a catastrophe and do nothing but witness. Decide now whether you can.

And refuse the last temptation, which will come to you quietly on the plain afterward: **do not carry spores elsewhere.** Their entire reproduction is a lottery of yellow flour dying on salt. You could end that. You could also seed a dozen worlds with a chemistry that has no water and no predators and a hundred thousand years of patience. That is not yours to fix.

## 10. The three things to actually do

1. **Sit for the ember hour.** Twice a day the deepest cracks go dull red and the floor is a low sky of embers, and everyone who can afford to stops working and looks. Stop working. Do not drum through it. This is the closest thing to a festival in a world with no festivals, and being idle together is the whole of the ritual.
2. **Watch the coin from behind the shutters, with the priesthood.** They keep it as a calendar and fear it as a fire. You are from up there. Let them see you flinch at it. Nothing will make you more comprehensible than sharing their fear of your own sky.
3. **Visit the thin one.** There are outer quarters folded like cloth by creeping salt, rooms narrowed to the width of a hand, still maintained, still lit, a green thread of lamp-film down a slot — and inside, a wright who has been getting thinner for eight thousand years and who still drums, perfectly clearly, on the hour, about the weather.

You are the only thing in the history of this world that can cross the gulf and go to it. Bring your plate. Do not promise rescue: the salt is inexorable and cutting it free would break the arch. Do not offer to widen the slot. Just lie in the dark with your palm on the stone and **answer on the hour, about the weather,** for as many hours as you can stand.

That is the visit. Everything else is logistics.

---

## 11. Getting out, and the rule about dying

- Time your ascent to the inhale, when a thin cold thread goes in past your face. The exhale is a hot toxic blast up a sixty-pace slant and will cook your suit and your budget together.
- **Knock at the Mouth on the way in, and wait.** Lie at the slot with your ear over the hole first — a long time, longer than seems reasonable — and drum on the lintel, and let the answer take a day if it takes a day. Do not simply walk into a city that has never been walked into.
- The plain will kill you as competently as the Bell: no features, no horizon that matters, shimmer standing up like crowds of people who dissolve when you look, and wind-streaks that mean nothing. Instruments, bearings, a buddy topside, a hard abort clock, a beacon. There are no tracks to follow home, including your own.
- **Quarantine on exit.** You will be varnished amber and dusted with yellow flour. Nothing alive comes home with you, and no water goes down.
- And the last rule, the one to write on the inside of your helmet: **leave alive, or leave nothing.** A human body is forty liters of solvent and a wet ecology, and the unknotting filaments that dissolve dead structure back into sap and gas would try to eat you and might learn something they cannot unlearn. Your survival is not a personal preference here. It is the whole of your ethics.

## What it will actually be like

You will lie on hot stone in the dark, deaf on purpose, hands flat, and feel a sentence arrive through your wrists. Around you the city will be a diagram — a hundred thousand faint green lines hanging in a black volume four hundred paces across, doubled in varnish, the middles of everything invisible. You will not be able to see the person talking to you. You will be able to feel them, and the whole lattice carrying forty other conversations that do not touch, and under it the boom going through your chest and coming back in eleven seconds.

Nothing will look at you. Nothing will hurry toward you. Your shadow will be an insult you have to keep apologizing for. You will be cold-broke by the third hour and terrified of your own sweat, and you will spend an afternoon being scraped clean by ten thousand tendrils, and it will be the tenderest thing that has ever happened to you.

And then the cracks will go dull red, and the drums will stop, all of them, everywhere, and a city that has been talking for longer than there have been eyes will fall silent for an hour to look at the floor.

Sit still. Don't say anything. You'll be understood.
