# Preparing for the Condensary

## 1. First, get the category right

It is not a ruin, not a wonder, and not a garden. It is a **working plant that has been live for three hundred years**, and the sign at the head of the stair means exactly what it says. *No admittance while plant is live.* The plant has never not been live. Everyone who goes down there goes down in violation of a rule that cannot be obeyed, and has made a private peace with that. Do not quote the sign at anyone. Watch what they do instead, and ask about the chalk.

The second correction: this is a **still**. Eight thousand square metres of evaporating surface and condensing surface, running day and night, producing water with nothing in it whatsoever. The purity is not a bonus feature. The purity is the hazard. There is no potable water anywhere on the site — the tap says POTABLE and isn't quite, the rain is distilled, the outfall trough that the cattle love is the purest water in the county and there is nothing in it at all. A place made entirely of clean water is a place that will take the salt out of your body while you stand in it feeling hydrated.

The third correction, and the one to hold onto all day: **there is no decay here.** The stand reabsorbs its own litter before fungi can get to it. Every free calorie is already spoken for. It has never had to defend itself against rot, because rot has never got a foothold.

You are rot. You are a warm sack of fungi and bacteria and yeast, carrying a rot ecosystem in your gut and another one in the tread of your boots, and you are about to walk into the one place in the world that has no immunity to you. The danger here runs the wrong way round. Almost nothing in the hall can infect you. You could devastate it.

## 2. What will actually hurt you

**Heat, first and always.** Thirty-one degrees is not hot. Thirty-one degrees at saturation is a different animal. Your sweat will not evaporate — it will simply run off you and join the rain, and you will lose the water and the salt without buying any cooling with it. You have no evaporative defence down there. You are a sealed engine in a sealed room. Fit, young, healthy people go down in this and get clumsy and stupid in forty minutes and do not notice, because they feel wet, and wet feels like it's working.

Plan for heat the way you'd plan for altitude. Short exposures. Real recovery above ground, not in the stairwell (the stair is a flue of warm breath; it is not a cooling place). Salt in every mouthful. Know that the classic warning sign — *you've stopped sweating* — is useless here, because you never stopped. Use instead: headache, nausea, clumsiness, a sudden dislike of the noise, losing your count. **If you lose the count, climb.**

**Water, second.** Do not drink the rain. Do not drink from the POTABLE tap, whatever the man beside you does. Do not drink the trough. A day of it and you will feel hollow and start to cramp; that is your blood going thin. Carry all your water in, carry the empties out, and put salt in it — proper rehydration salts, not a sports drink. Expect to sweat a litre an hour and feel none of it dry.

Distilled rain in your eyes stings, continuously, all day. It is hypotonic; it swells the surface of the eye. Contact lens wearers: don't. Spectacle wearers: your glasses go blind on the third step and **they will not clear, ever, at any point in the visit**, because everything below is at the dew point. There is no wiping your way out of it. This is a place that takes your glasses away. Make your peace with that before you descend, and go down with someone who can see.

**Pressure, third, and it is the one that maims.** Two hundred and six osmotic engines at five bar, and the jets are fine enough to cut leather. A conduit will take a hand off at the wrist. The stand clots its own wound in forty seconds and yours not at all.

So: **cut nothing.** Not a leaf, not a root, not a thread, not a sample, not a stray fibre off a gasket. Don't prune, don't tidy, don't help. Don't lean on a bladder, don't climb a cradle, don't put a hand or a boot into a tray, don't stand in front of anything with a nozzle. Carry a real trauma kit — tourniquet you can work one-handed on yourself, pressure dressing — and know that the casualty has to go up thirty-one wet steps.

**The slow ones.** The roar is broadband, everywhere, at equal volume, with no direction in it. This does more to people than the heat. Sound is how you know how big a room is and where the walls are, and here it tells you nothing, forever. Expect a specific vertigo, a sense that the hall is simultaneously small and unbounded. Expect to shout for an hour and wreck your throat, then stop, and feel something like grief when you stop — that is the moment you learn your voice is not a tool here. Bring ear protection (you will need it, this is a cooling hall falling three metres onto leaves), and understand what it costs you: plugs take away the tubes and the warnings, but **the knock comes through your sternum and your boots, not your ears.** You will keep the clock in your body. Learn to feel it before you need it.

And the other slow one: **you will be entered.** More on that below, and it is not a joke.

## 3. Before you cross

**Biosecurity, in the direction you weren't expecting.** Arrive in clothes and boots that have never been in soil, compost, a byre, a cave, or another branch's field. Scrub and disinfect the tread. Find out what the people here use and use that. Do not bring food that rots — no fruit, no bread, no cheese, no leftovers. Eat at the lip and carry the crumbs away. Do not defecate in the hall; plan for that above ground, unglamorously, in advance. Do not spit. Do not bleed on the root-plate if you can help it, and if you can't, understand that the floor will take it up and be grateful, and that this is not a thing you want to have happened.

**And no chemistry.** This is the rule nobody thinks of and it matters more than almost anything else on this page. The entire organism runs on **membranes** — brine on one side, sweet water on the other, two hundred and six times over. Surfactants destroy membranes. Oils foul them. Therefore: no soap, no shampoo, no wet wipes, no alcohol gel, no sunscreen, no insect repellent, no barrier cream, no lip balm, no perfume, no smoke. Cover up for sun instead of creaming up. There is an enamel sign that says WASH HANDS and it is three hundred years old; ask where the wash water goes before you use anything. Rinse with condensate if you must — it is superb washing water, it is just not drinking water — and do your real washing at the lip with your own supply, and take the dirty water away with you.

**Your travel kit stays above.** Anything you carry below will eventually be copied: entered, plated, grown hollow on a mandrel. A hollow copper replica of your device is inert and harmless and also a thing you will have to explain to somebody. Leave it with your person at the lip, sealed.

## 4. Kit

Think **warm cave dive at one atmosphere, with sauna protocol and industrial-site rules.**

- Wide-brimmed hat. Non-negotiable. It keeps three metres of distilled drizzle off your face and out of your eyes, and it stops the roof printing sunburn on you in the shape of itself.
- Thin, loose, quick-drying long sleeves and legs. **No waterproofs.** A jacket in there is a coffin; you will cook. You are going to be wet. Be wet in something that dries.
- Boots with real grip. The aisles are worn glassy and they are wet and warm.
- A dry bag, double-bagged, worn on your body, **not opened below**: towel, complete dry change, hat, flask of something hot. In winter you will step out of saturated thirty-one degrees into frost, soaked to the skin. Exit chill is the injury nobody plans for.
- All your water. Electrolyte salts. Salty food.
- Ear protection. Torch, plus a spare, for signalling — light works down there, voice does not.
- **A diver's slate and a grease pencil.** Paper turns to pulp; ink runs. Chalk is the local technology and it is the correct answer. Bring chalk.
- Trauma kit as above. Sterile saline eyewash — your own, not the rain.
- A four-gas meter, or the local equivalent, and see the next section for why I insist.
- A watch, as a cross-check. Not as your clock.
- Nothing metal on your hands or wrists: no ring, no metal watchband, no chain. The trays farm low voltage over enormous area, which means modest push and a great deal of current available. Don't be the bridge.
- If you have a latex allergy, declare it before you go anywhere near the store. The gaskets are latex tissue, stacked in hundreds, still tacky.

Leave behind: knives, spades, sample jars, collecting tubes, a hammer, anything you were planning to leave as a gift, and any intention of teaching it something.

## 5. Timing

**The knock is the clock.** Nine minutes eleven seconds. Feel it in your chest before you hear it. Count it from the moment you reach the lip and keep counting, in your body, all day. Locals count; they do not calculate. Note that the arithmetic in circulation does not quite close — sixteen hundred knocks at nine-eleven is nearer ten days than the eleven and a fraction everyone will tell you, and the roof's eight thousand square metres is more than the lid's footprint accounts for. Don't correct anyone. **Count, don't convert.** And don't assume that because you can see sky-coins you are under sky; some bays may run out under the pasture.

**Go by day.** Ordinary light comes through hand-sized gaps in ordinary coins that move at the ordinary speed of the day. They tell you the time and roughly which way is which. Your compass is unreliable in a hall threaded with grown busbars and rust-veins where the ironmongery used to be. Navigate by the enamel names at head height — BAY 2, BAY 3, PLATING, STORE, WASH HANDS — by the sun-coins, by the draft, and by **the wear on the floor.**

That last one is the best safety rule in the building and the stand wrote it for you: the aisles are worn glassy by two hundred years of feet, and the edges are spongy where traffic doesn't go. Under the spongy edges, a metre down, is black blood-warm salt water. **Walk where the floor has been walked.** The traffic is a record of every judgement anyone made about what holds.

**Air, and why I want the meter.** This is an eleven-metre hole with a respiring sump at the bottom and a plant overhead. By day, in ordinary sun, it makes oxygen. At night it does the opposite, in a pit, and pits collect what heavy air does. Ventilation is driven by the chimney draft, which is driven by evaporation off the upper skin.

At the turn, the upper skin warms through and the condensation stops.

I do not know that the draft stops with it. I would not bet a life on it not stopping. And when the sump drops a hand's width, an anoxic black sump being drawn down is exactly the circumstance that gives off what it has been holding. So: **no night visits, no lingering at floor level near grates, and no first turn without someone who has done six of them.** If you get a headache or a breathlessness out of proportion to the heat, or the meter complains, climb. Thirty-one steps is a minute of your life.

Ask, politely, what the chalk mark on the eleventh step records. Accept that you may not be told. Treat the eleventh step as a place to make a decision.

## 6. The lip

From the lane it is a hedge that has filled a hole, and the leaves lie **level with the field.** There is no visual edge. You can walk off eleven metres of nothing while looking at grass. Approach on the chimney stub — four metres, capped with a copper fruit — or the north outfall and its stone trough, or find the handrail. Never cut across the pasture in the dark. Never run.

Cattle graze to within a body-length and in frost they crowd the rim like people at a radiator. A bullock that wants your warm spot will lean on you. Do not sit on the rounded brick with your back to the field.

Put your palm flat on the rim and find the note. Learn it. It has been in your hands since the lane and you didn't know. If it ever changes, come up.

Then do what the heron does. It comes twice a year and stands at the lip for an hour or two, looking down into the leaves, and it has never once gone in. **Make your first visit exactly that.** Look down for an hour. Go home. You will not regret it and you will sleep better.

## 7. Going down, in stages

Thirty-one steps, dished in the middle by feet, wet, and you are blind from step three. Take your glasses off and stow them. Count out loud in your head. Hand on the copper — it is warm and it is clean, copper is its own disinfectant — and three points of contact.

Then, over as many visits as you can afford:

1. Bottom step. One minute. Come up. Learn the roar arriving all at once, like weather, and learn that it has no direction, and let your body stop trying to find one.
2. Twenty minutes on the glassy aisle no deeper than BAY 2. Find the STORE and memorise the way to it: it is the driest bay, under the doubled baffle, and it is the nearest thing to a recovery room you have. Find the doubled window and its weak-tea light and remember it as a place to sit.
3. The plating trays, at a distance, hands behind your back. The hum is bubbles, around B-flat, unbroken for three centuries.
4. The rack of half-grown elbows. Save it. It looks exactly like a rack of pale fruit and it is the most beautiful thing in the hall and nobody has ever seen it who did not say so. You will not be able to say so. That is the whole experience of this place in one moment: something enormous happens to you and your voice is no use. Bring a slate. Bring a person whose face you can read.
5. The store, and the insulators, and the tally. Gritty silica in the driest bay means dust; mask up if you're handling, and don't stir the peat anywhere — the blind nematodes in it are what make the batteries work.
6. Only much later, a turn.
7. The sport-corner last, or never. Two cubic metres of cidery sweetness, the only smell of death in eight thousand square metres, the only place anything is allowed to go soft, and a slug that farms it. It is pleasant. It is also the one place with a fungal economy. If you have any sensitivity of the chest, admire it from the aisle.

One honest word about the aerosol: a hall of warm wet moving water and mist, at thirty-one degrees, with a sump, is a **cooling tower**. There is a soft continuous ticking under LISTEN that is the nematodes, or is not the nematodes. Generations of people have worked here. Ask them, gently, whether the old men have a cough. Then decide about a respirator, knowing that a respirator in saturated thirty-one degrees is a misery and will clog, and that this is a real trade-off with no clean answer.

## 8. Conduct

The etiquette is short and absolute.

**Take nothing.** Not an insulator, not a gasket, not a cricket, not a fish, not a slug, not a handful of peat. Nothing happens if you do — nothing at all, for a long time — and then over a season the tally corrects and the rack fills again. That is not permission. That is a closed loop being polite about a subtraction. One part in forty is surplus and the surplus goes back to the trays. *Nothing leaves* is a description of the physics and you should treat it as law. If you want an insulator, ask whoever keeps the chalk, and be prepared for the answer to be no, and be prepared for it to take a season.

**Leave nothing.** No rubbish, no kit, no offering, no tool. Take a boot and a boot appears in the trays, plated, hollow, with the lace grown as one closed loop. A pail with no seam. Shears that will not open, because the stand does not know what opening is for. These are the objects that will follow you home. Do not add to them out of carelessness, and above all **do not try to teach it.** Do not demonstrate a hinge. Do not leave a diagram. There is a second glyph beside HOLLOWAY & VANE — PATENT No. 4 that the firm never cut, and it has been drifting for three hundred years, and it counts. You are not qualified to edit that.

**Do not sleep there.** Do not lie down. Do not spend hours motionless in one spot. At the back of the plating bay there is a mandrel about a man's height with a suggestion of shoulders. It has been there a very long time and it is probably for a cowl. Don't stand beside it for a photograph.

**The two ears.** SPEAK and LISTEN, four metres apart, enamel plates worn nearly blank. LISTEN gives you the sump — salt engines tightening like a rope under load, and the ticking. SPEAK gives you the field, faintly and perfectly: cows, a gate, a tractor two valleys off. It is the loveliest thing in the hall and it is a microphone wired into every bay. Say something on SPEAK and the hall hears it, and it will not answer, but the tally will change somewhere by one.

My advice: not on your first visit. And when you do, say something you would be content to have counted, and to have counted for a very long time.

**Don't drop things.** Don't hammer, don't bang a tray, don't add a knock of your own. Two hundred and six engines keep apart by phasing to the pulse and the pulse is hydraulic, so a bang probably means nothing. Probably. The people who work here don't drop things.

## 9. The turn

Eleven days and a fraction; sixteen hundred knocks; find out from the chalk hand, and count.

At the turn the ring main reverses, the bladders empty in sequence over about six hours, the sump drops, the roof-skin warms, and **the rain stops.** The hum stays. The ticking stays. Your own blood arrives. The cows two hundred metres away are suddenly enormous. Every sound you make goes down the tubes to every bay at once, because the stand is not speaking then.

It is listening.

Be somewhere in the middle of a glassy aisle, well away from both plates and well away from the sump, sitting still, with your water and your salt and your dry layer and your meter and one person you trust. Agree touch signals beforehand. Agree not to talk. Six hours is a long time to be quiet next to something with sixteen hundred knocks of patience, and the quiet after that much roar will do things to your head — people hear things in it, and the ticking is very patient. Let it be beautiful. Keep your shoulders down. Notice, at the end, that they were around your ears the whole time.

Then the bladders refill and the first sheet comes off the roof with a hiss like something being fried, and the knock comes back into your chest, and it is one of the great experiences available to a human being.

Do not cross a branch boundary that day. Do not drive. Plan a rest day and a bed and something salty to eat.

## 10. Out

Climb. The noise falls off you like a coat. The ordinary noon is standing exactly where you left it, and it is the same light that was falling through the roof-gaps onto the root-plate, and the whole thing is behind you and below you and level with the grass.

Get dry **immediately**, especially in winter. Full change, hat, hot flask. Then do it properly: scrub the boot tread, bag everything, quarantine the kit before it goes anywhere else — you have been standing in peat full of the nematodes that make the batteries work, and a wingless white cricket lives in the dry bay and nowhere else in the world, and a small pale fish lives in the sweet-water channel by the stair. Nothing leaves. Be the person who kept that true.

Walk out across the field. You will feel it in the soles of your boots for a hundred paces. Nine minutes and eleven seconds. Then again.

Expect it to keep going for days after you get home. Expect silence to be difficult, and rain on a roof to be either unbearable or the only thing that will put you to sleep. Expect to find yourself thinking about a pair of shears that will not open. Expect to want to tell someone about the elbows and to discover that you can't, quite, and that this is not a failure of your language but a property of the hall.

## The short list, to memorise

- Cover your head. Bring all your water. **Salt every mouthful.**
- Drink nothing that falls, runs, or says POTABLE.
- Cut nothing. Touch no bladder, no conduit, no tray.
- No soap, no cream, no repellent, no oils. It is made of membranes.
- Walk the glassy aisles. The wear is two hundred years of good judgement.
- Count the knock in your chest. Lose the count, climb.
- Day only. Meter on. Headache or breathlessness, climb.
- Take nothing, leave nothing, teach nothing, say nothing on SPEAK.
- Never sleep there.
- Be at the lip for your first turn, and be with someone for your second.
- Arrive clean. You are the only rot that will enter that hall today.

Bring chalk. Ask what the eleventh step records. Stand on the rim with your palm flat on the brick until you notice you have been hearing the note since the lane, and then look down into the leaves for an hour, the way the heron does, and go home.
