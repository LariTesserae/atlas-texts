# Preparing for The Sum

## First, understand what kind of danger this is

Nothing in the basin will attack you. There is no weather to kill you, no flood, no predator, no famine, no war, no night terror. The herds are gentle, the people are gentler, the water is clean, the temperature never moves. You will be fed. You will be offered a warm bed cut into a warm moss bank and a flask of sour brown water and a place to stand.

The danger is that the place will make room for you.

Everything here is being replaced at exactly the rate it is being made. You are the only thing in twenty miles that will be different tomorrow — that is not poetry, it is a description of your physical status. You shed. You accrete. You are a source of leftovers in a country with no leftovers, and an event in a country with no events. Prepare, therefore, less like an explorer and more like someone entering a clean room, or an operating theatre, or a house in mourning.

The two failure modes are: **you stop moving and the pavement keeps you**, and **you subtract or add something and the number does not come out.** Everything below is in service of avoiding those.

---

## Before you go: body

Get your teeth, kidneys, lungs and feet checked. This is a hard-water country and you will be living inside a supersaturated solution. If you have a history of stones, think hard about whether to go at all. Get dental work finished before arrival; you do not want an open socket or an exposed root in a place where every wet surface is a nucleation site.

Acclimatize. The air is thin — the rim is high and the basin is not low. You will be breathless on the two-hundred-step stairs and you will be breathing hard in air that carries the pooled smoke of every hearth in the basin, held under a permanent lid at the height of the rim's shoulders. Expect a cough for the first three days. If you have asthma, bring your treatment and declare it; do not be stoic about breathing here, because breath is loud and a coughing fit in that silence is an incident.

Train your feet and your standing. The single most useful physical preparation is to spend a full day at home upright — walking, standing, leaning, squatting, kneeling, eating standing, reading standing — and to do it again until it is unremarkable. You must be able to rest without sitting. You must be able to be tired on bare rock and not put yourself down on it. Long slow walks on uneven hard ground with a staff; ankle strength; the discipline of placing a foot rather than dropping it.

Practice silence. Not meditation as a hobby — specifically, practice being in a quiet place until the quiet stops being an absence and becomes a substance. Some travelers come apart in the first two days because they can hear their own blood and their own gut and the seam of their sleeve, and there is nothing else, and no echo comes back to reassure them that the world is still out there.

## Kit

**Nothing that sheds.** This is the hard rule and it eliminates most modern clothing. No fleece, no down, no wool that pills, no velcro, no loose synthetic. A leaking down jacket releases a feather that will not blow away, will lie exactly where it fell, will be filmed over by morning and will still be there when the sitters' eyelashes have softened. Wear tightly woven plant fibre and smooth leather. Grey, bone, green, rust — the basin's own colours. Bright colour is visible for miles across bare clint and there is nowhere in this country to be out of sight.

**Tether everything.** Lanyards on your knife, your flask, your cap, your spectacles, your cloths. A bag that does not open over open ground. You will adopt the local habit of never setting anything down, ever, for any reason, and you should build the habit into your equipment before you arrive so it is not a decision you have to keep making.

**Little or no metal, and declare what you bring.** They have none to speak of. A steel blade is not a tool here, it is an unaccountable substance and an argument. Your fillings, your zip, your buckle, your spectacles, your ring — all of it will take a film, and a ring will bond to a finger inside a week. Take rings off before you go. Take spectacles with a case and a cord and a spare pair; do not wear contact lenses in lime dust and fog.

**A short staff.** Not a walking aid — a probe. More on this below.

**A stool you carry, or a mat you always roll.** Something to rest on that never spends a night outdoors. A stool left standing bonds at the feet; a groundsheet bonds along its whole underside and becomes a stone sheet in the shape of your bed.

**A small flame.** A candle, a taper, anything. You will use it to test air.

**Cloths, many.** For your nose, your mouth, your eyes, your wounds. You will not spit, sneeze, blow, or drip onto the floor of this country. Everything comes out of you into a cloth or a vessel and goes into the accounted cycle.

**A tally cord, and learn to use it before you arrive.** Knot arithmetic is their entire literate apparatus. Arriving able to keep a count in knots is the single most courteous thing you can do; it makes you legible. Do not bring a diary. A diary is a chronicle and they have none, and the reason they have none is not that they are primitive.

**Scrub your boots to the tread.** Empty every pocket. No seeds, no soil, no crumbs, no dried mud from anywhere else. There is exactly one tree in the basin, in a hollow on the south side, and it is counted, and it has the same number of berries. There are soil pits the width of a coin. If a seed of yours takes in one of them, you will have made a second tree, and there is no wind to blow it away and no frost to kill it and no history to absorb it. You will have broken a number permanently.

## The two waters

Learn the distinction on your first hour and never confuse it again.

**White builds. Brown unbuilds.**

Do not drink the white. It is beautiful, clear, faintly blue in depth, and it will lay lime down on anything that gives it a foothold. Drink the brown: sour, tea-coloured, tannic, strained. It tastes like a moss bank smells and it will stain your teeth and it is the local solvent and the local medicine. Ask how they keep their teeth; do not improvise a dental routine in a place where one water etches enamel and the other plates it.

Never rinse a wound with white. Use brown, then grease.

**The morning wash is not hygiene, it is maintenance.** Everyone does it and you must do it with them, thoroughly, at the same hour: eyelids and lashes, lips and the corners of the mouth, nostrils, ears, hairline, under the nails, between fingers and toes, every crease where two surfaces of you touch. Bonding starts at contact lines. Then grease — fat, oil, whatever the household uses — on lashes, lips, and any place the film could key. Oil breaks the bond before it starts. Get grease locally, through the tally; do not bring your own scented petroleum product into a closed basin.

**Keep your hair short or bound or oiled.** Long hair in the dew becomes a white rope. Ask about nails, about hair-cutting, about menstrual blood, about where a body's leavings go. These are not embarrassing questions here; they are inventory questions, and the households will have exact answers and will be relieved that you asked instead of quietly making a discrepancy.

**The Kettle is the trap.** It is the one warm thing in a cool country, warm enough to sit in, and the word *sit* is the whole problem. It is white water. It is the plating water at its most inviting. If you bathe, do it standing, briefly, with someone who knows the protocol, and brown-rinse and grease after. Do not soak. Do not linger. Do not go alone at dusk because you are cold and tired and it is warm.

**If you bond:** do not tear free. You will leave skin behind, and skin is a leftover, and you will bleed, and blood is a bigger leftover. Pour brown along the bond line and wait. A night's film goes quickly. A week's takes longer. Be quiet while it works; someone will bring you moss-tea and not speak, and that is the correct response and you should offer it to others.

## Moving through the basin

**Grey is ground. Green is a hole.** This is the rule that will keep you alive. The grikes are knee-wide and sometimes two men deep, and the lushness is all in the seams — moss a metre deep, ferns like combs, shoulder-high willow in the wetter clefts — and from a distance and in the softened light the green reads as carpet. It is not carpet. It is the lid on a void. Never step on green. Probe with the staff. Never run. Never walk the open pavement at dusk or in the dark. Never walk it alone until you know it.

A broken ankle in a grike is the most likely way to die here, because it is the most likely way to become unable to keep moving.

**No camping.** There is no pitching a tent on the clint. Sleep indoors, or in a cut bed in a moss bank, and be under cover before dewfall. Night is when the film comes.

**Test the air.** In still air, deep fissures and rotting peat collect bad air. The moss banks are warm because they are composting, and composting eats oxygen. Before you put your head into a grike or lie down in a cut bed, lower the flame. If it dims, get out. Sleep with your head out of the cut, not down in it. Do not go into the Throat for any reason. The river is a constant hand's-depth and utterly tame and it ends by going down and not coming back, and there is no flood mark anywhere because there is no flood, and consequently nobody here has any instinct about moving water that would help you.

**Tarns deceive.** No soft bottom, no silt, no weed, glassy and blue and apparently shallow, going down into clean fissured pavement. Cold, deep, no waves. Do not swim alone. Do not wade barefoot anywhere: the riverbed chips are as sharp as the day they were split and always will be.

**Get the maps before you get anything else.** Ask for: the grike survey, the rain fixtures, the hour-bird stations, the herd circuit and its crossing places, and above all **the map of the standing swarms.** Because there is no wind, the midge columns hang at fixed points over the seeps, permanently, named, walked around. Walking through one is not walking through a cloud of bugs. It is demolishing a landmark and cutting a bird's food station on a route that has never been cut. Likewise the webs — felted, layered, thumb-thick, a yard long, built by spiders who have never in their lineage had anything tear a web down. Route around. Be the first thing that ever tore a web and you will have introduced a category.

**Do not cross the Shine.** It is the herd road, a mirror-gloss ribbon polished by hooves and re-plated nightly, visible from the rim as a line of light. Thousands of animals on a circuit they have always moved on do not have a procedure for an obstruction. Use the crossings.

**Do not climb the rim.** Every fracture up there is fresh and always has been; the couloirs of angular chips are held by nothing but the fact that nothing has ever disturbed them. Do not throw a stone. Do not skip a stone on a tarn. Do not drop anything into a grike — it will lie at the bottom, unweathered, forever, and change the width of a seam that is held at exactly its width by a thread of brown water and someone's daily attention.

**Time your travel around the fixtures.** Rain falls at the ninth stair, at the eastern casting beds, and along a diagonal of the southern pavement, at the same hour, in the same amount. The ninth stair is your way in and out and it is wet and slick at that hour. You can stand with one foot in the rain. Learn where the line is; it is a useful landmark and a good place to be told the hour.

## The count

**Go to the census hall first.** Before you eat, before you sleep, before you bathe. Present yourself. Bring a manifest of everything you carry — every item, counted, in knots if you can manage it — and be able to produce it on demand, morning and night. Count your buttons. Know your number.

Then keep it true. If you lose a button, say so at once. If you break a cord, say so. Concealing a small loss is the only real crime you could commit here; discrepancy is the one sin, and a household that cannot make its tally come out will sit in the doorway all night while the neighbours bring moss-tea and do not speak. Do not do that to a household on your account. Round nothing. "About a hundred" is not an answer. Count.

**Never forage.** Do not pick a flower, take a snail, gather a shell, eat a berry, catch a frog, dig peat, or lift a chip. All of it is in the number. The tree's berries are in the number. Ask "is this in the count?" before you eat anything, and eat what is offered from the accounted stores. Foraging here is not resourcefulness; it is subtraction from a load-bearing sum.

**Account for your body.** You shed skin and hair, you breathe out carbon and water, you piss and shit, you sweat salt onto rock that the herds will come to lick. There is no midden anywhere in this city, no rubbish hill, no silt fan, no drift, and that absence is the strangest and most fragile thing about the place. Ask on your first day for the protocol for a body's leavings and follow it exactly, including the parts that feel absurd. Comb over a bowl. Do not clip your nails on the pavement. Do not spit. Carry it out or put it into the moss-ash-beetle cycle at the appointed place. Assume that every gram of you is somebody's arithmetic.

**Offer to be weighed in and weighed out.** They will understand this immediately even if they have never needed it, and it is the cleanest way to make your visit balance. You will leave carrying lime the basin lent you — on your boots, your clothes, your hair. Wash it off in brown at the proper place before you go up the stair, and let the rinsings go back to the beds.

**Bring no gifts that add.** No seed, no spice, no blade, no song, no story, no news. The birds here have one note each, given once per circuit, because song is for holding ground and saying something new, and the ground holds itself and nothing is new. Do not whistle. Do not imitate an hour-bird: you will have told a false hour across a basin that tells time by birds. Do not teach a child a game. Do not promise to come back — a promise is an unaccounted future event. Offer labour instead: a day resetting forms at the casting beds, a second pair of eyes on a tally. Work and accurate counting are the only gifts that fit.

**Wear eye protection at the beds and the quarry faces.** The chips are angular and fresh forever, the dust is alkaline and abrasive, and when a soaked wedge opens a block it opens with one crack like a knuckle and then a long silence. That crack is the loudest regular sound in the city and it happens forty times a day. Do not startle at it. But if you hear cracking repeated, or a rumble, tell someone at once: that is not the sound the country makes.

## Names, and sitters

There are exactly as many names as people. A name is a place in the number and it is passed along. There is no name for you.

Do not ask for one. Bring your own and use it and accept that you will be an entry without a place, and that this is a wound your hosts will carry gently for as long as you stay. If a name is offered to you, understand what is being offered: room has been made. Decline, with thanks, and leave soon.

Out on the clints there are people sitting cross-legged, glazed white, part of the floor, some softened by age and some so recent you can see the eyelashes. They are counted. They are boundary markers. They are also, in a way not discussed with strangers, why the count holds. Nobody is put there. It is chosen and it is honoured, and when a child comes there is generally an elder who has been getting ready for a while.

Do not stare. Do not touch. Do not ask their names. Do not ask what the counting does — the people say the sea is coming back a film at a time and that the counting is what keeps it from hurrying. **Act as if that is literally true.** You are not in a position to know it is a metaphor, and the cost of being wrong is a basin a day out of the sea with the sea arriving faster.

And do not sit down beside one. This is the specific trap for a sympathetic traveler: you will feel the pull to keep them company, to sit cross-legged on the warm grey stone beside a person whose eyelashes you can still see, and be with them a while. That is how it takes you. Honour them standing.

## What it will actually feel like

Cool, still, bright, ten o'clock in the morning forever. Air that does not push on your face — you will keep noticing this for days, the way you notice a missing tooth. Smoke going up in straight grey ropes and flattening into a lid. The smell of wet stone and peat smoke and, close to the seams, something green and animal and faintly sweet, thousands of snails rasping like tiny files if you put your ear down. Your own blood in your ears. The Long Drip audible from a quarter-mile off. A single crack from the east, and then nothing for a long while.

Households three hundred paces apart, half a league apart, and between them bare ringing clint and green seams and nothing at all. You will be alone constantly and visible constantly. There is no privacy of terrain and no weather to hide in and no dark corner; there is only the enormous courtesy of people who do not look at you when you do not want to be looked at.

Time will come apart in your hands. The light does not change, the fog comes up the gorge at its accustomed speed to its accustomed height, the birds give their one note at their station, and there is nothing else to tell one day from another. Keep your days as a number on a cord. Not a journal — a number. Arithmetic is welcome here; chronicle is not.

And it will be peaceful in a way you have probably never experienced and will not easily give up. No urgency, no threat, no decay, no leftovers, nothing getting older, everything being replaced. Kind people. Warm moss. A warm pool. Soft light. The stillness will slow your thinking down until thinking feels like an unnecessary kind of fidgeting, and one afternoon you will notice that you have been standing in one place for a long time and that it would be easy, and honoured, and not at all like dying, to stop.

Set your departure before you arrive. Fix the number of days and keep it on the cord where you can feel it. Carry a reason to leave that is stronger than peace — a person, a debt, a promise made somewhere else, in a world where promises are ordinary. Keep it in your hand, literally, something you can touch when the basin starts to feel like the answer.

And watch for these signs, because they mean you have already stayed too long: the birds begin to route around you. A herd animal comes to lick the rime off your face at a fixed hour. A household sets a place for you in the tally without asking. Someone begins to speak of you the way they speak of an elder who has been getting ready.

The country does not trap anyone. It fits you in. That is the whole of its violence.

---

## Leaving

Go up the stair you came down, at an hour that is not the ninth stair's rain. Close the tally in the census hall, item by item, and let them check it. Return the lime your gear has borrowed. Take nothing — no shell, no chip, no berry, no fragment of basket-printed block, no souvenir of any kind. A souvenir is a subtraction and you will be carrying it out of a sum that has to come out right.

If a boot or a tool of yours got left out and is stone now, ask whether it is yours or the floor's. Accept the answer.

Then let the number return to true behind you. That is the only gift you can leave that they will actually want, and it is a real one, and you should be proud of it: for a few days there was one more thing in the basin than there had ever been, and afterwards there was exactly the right amount again, and the sea went on coming back a film at a time.

They will be gentle with you. They will offer you the flask. Take it, wash, keep walking, and go home while you still want to.
