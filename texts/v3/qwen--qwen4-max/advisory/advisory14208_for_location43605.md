# Preparing for The Standing Water

## 1. The one fact that organizes everything else

There is nothing in this basin that can hurt you on purpose, and almost nothing that can hurt you by accident. No predators, no current, no surf, no cold, no rot, no tide. It is the least hostile environment most travelers will ever stand at the edge of.

It is also a place where the fastest natural event is a bubble leaving bark.

You are the fast event. You are, by several orders of magnitude, the most energetic, most chemically active, most microbially loaded, loudest thing that has ever entered this water. Every hazard here runs in both directions: what the place can do to you, and what you can do to the place. The second list is longer, and the damage lasts longer, and nobody will see it happen.

So prepare the way you would prepare to enter a room where someone has been asleep for a thousand years and you have been asked to bring them a glass of water.

---

## 2. First, seriously consider not going in

The best visit to the Standing Water is made from the eastern saddle, lying on warm rock with your face above the film and a passive hydrophone in the water on a sterilized line, listening through headphones.

This is not a consolation prize. Sound is this place's only long-range channel. In an hour of listening you will hear a snail — an actual snail, half a kilometer away, rasping lime off a column — and you will hear it more clearly than you have ever heard anything alive. You will hear the tick of venting bark from the far margin. You will hear your own heartbeat and be annoyed by it, and then you will learn to subtract it, and then the basin will open underneath the subtraction.

From above you can also see the seams as shelves in the blue, count the gas domes under the leaves, read the trunk bands, watch the noon whiting turn over, and photograph fixed points that you will photograph again in ten years. That last is the only way to actually *see* this place. Its motion is a meter a year. A human visit is too short to perceive it and too long to be innocent. Instruments and return trips are how you watch a forest become a mountain.

If you do all of that and never wet your shoulder, you will have had the visit. Going in adds one thing — the dissolution of your skin boundary — and that one thing is worth having, and costs more than you think. Decide honestly which visit you came for.

## 3. The air above the water

**Altitude.** 3,500 m. Roughly two-thirds of sea-level pressure. You will be at perhaps 88–92% saturation sitting still, lower when you walk, much lower when you sleep. Arrive at intermediate altitude for two nights minimum, three if you can, before you go near the rim. Do not plan to do the approach on the day you arrive.

Understand the specific trap: altitude reduces your breath-hold, degrades your judgment, and does both *without telling you*. The water is warm and forgiving and you will feel excellent. Hypoxic euphoria and thermal bliss are the same feeling and they will hold hands.

**Carbon dioxide.** The young rock that heats the water also exhales. CO₂ is heavier than air, invisible, odorless at lethal concentrations, and pools in hollows on still nights and mornings. This basin is full of hollows: talus pockets, the lee of rim lips, the underside of overhangs, the trough below the saddle where the overflow sheet degasses. Carry a personal CO₂/O₂/H₂S monitor. Wear it at chest height. Check any hollow at knee height *before* you put your head into it. Never lie down in a depression. Never sleep below the rim. Two people have died in worlds like this one sitting down to rest in a nice sheltered spot.

**Sun.** Thin air, mirror surface, clear water. You will burn from above and below simultaneously, and you will burn *through* the first meter of water. You cannot use sunscreen — see §7 — so cover physically: light-colored, smooth, full-body, hood, brim, face. No exposed skin between 0900 and 1600, including underwater.

**Cloud.** Some mornings the deck lies below you and the basin sits in sunlight on a white floor that isn't the ground. Then cloud comes over the saddle and lies on the water. In that moment you lose the rim, the horizon, the sun, and the surface — because a warm still water under fog gives you *no visual boundary at all*. You will be floating in a white nothing at body temperature with no up. This is not mystical. It is how people drown three meters from shore.

**Standing rule: if cloud comes over the saddle, you leave the water immediately.**

**The exit.** You will step out of 34°C water into thin, dry, moving air at altitude, wet, vasodilated, with no thermal warning system left. The chill will arrive as a surprise and then as shaking. Have a dry robe, a warm hat, and a sheltered changing spot on rock, staged before you enter. Do not plan to "warm up walking."

## 4. What the water does to your body

**You will not feel wet.** Humans have no wetness receptor. We infer wet from cold and from pressure. This water is at skin temperature and does not move. Both cues are absent. After some minutes you will not feel wet, or warm, or cool, or bounded. You will feel like a shape being held by nothing.

That is the famous effect and it is real and it is worth the trip. It is also the mechanism by which people die here, because:

**You cannot dump heat.** At 34°C there is essentially no gradient between your skin and the medium. At rest, with a core of 37°C, you can shed metabolic heat slowly and stay comfortable for a long while. Start swimming and your heat production goes up five- to tenfold with nowhere to go. You will not sweat usefully. You will not feel hot — the water is *exactly* the temperature at which your thermal senses stop reporting. You will simply become progressively stupider, then nauseated, then unconscious, while feeling more at home than you have ever felt.

This place will kill you by agreeing with you.

Hard limits, for an ordinary acclimatized adult:
- **Floating still, at the surface, in the top lens: 40 minutes maximum.**
- **Any active swimming: 15 minutes, then out.**
- **Below 5 m: not for you.** (See §6.)
- **Never alone. Never without a timer that vibrates and a person on the rock with a stopwatch.**

**Dehydration.** Immersion diuresis will make you urinate repeatedly; the dry thin air above will take water off you invisibly; the warm water will take more. You will lose two liters in a session and notice nothing. Dehydration worsens altitude illness, worsens heat stress, and thickens your blood. Drink before and after, on a schedule, not on thirst. Four liters per person per day, carried in.

**Do not urinate in the basin.** Not for decorum. Immersion diuresis is a certainty, so plan for it: exit on a schedule, or use a containment system. A liter of human urine introduced into a water column that does not mix will sit in a lens for a very long time, and this is a nutrient-poor system that has never had a nutrient pulse in its life. You would be fertilizing a seam.

**Finding your body again.** If the dissolution starts to frighten you, move to the cold margin, where the crystal snow falls and the water is not 34. Your skin will come back. Do it with your head level and both ears equally immersed — asymmetric cooling of the ears produces caloric vertigo, and spinning underwater is how you lose your up.

## 5. Your senses will resign, in specific ways

Prepare for these individually. They are not vague.

**The surface becomes a mirror from below.** Look up steeply and you see the sky through Snell's window, a bright cone overhead. Look up at any shallow angle and you see total internal reflection: the forest, upside down, green and lit. Add mirror-bright gas domes held under leaves, and the doubling at every seam, and you are inside a hall of mirrors that all look like *somewhere to go*. There is no reliable visual up in this basin. Accept that now, on land.

**The snow lies.** At noon the microcrystals drift *upward* on the warm columns in the center and *downward* at the margins. If you use suspended particles to judge which way is up — and you will, involuntarily, because that is what brains do — you will be wrong in the middle of the basin, exactly where you most want to be.

**Your only trustworthy up is a number.** Wear a depth gauge. Watch it. Ascent is toward lower pressure, not toward the brighter direction. If you have no bubbles and no gauge, you have no up.

**Sound will come from inside your skull.** Sound travels ~4.4× faster in water; the interaural timing cues your brain uses to localize are compressed almost to nothing. Everything will seem to originate at the center of your head. The tick half a basin away will feel like a thought. Combined with the loss of your skin boundary, this is a reliable recipe for depersonalization. Some travelers love it. Some panic. Know which you are before you find out at four meters.

**Countermeasure:** a thin tether to the entry point, or a buddy's bright hood, or a fixed physical landmark you memorized from above — the fork of a particular drowned crown, a specific white column. Choose one *before* you enter and re-find it every few minutes. Not as a safety line. As an anchor for the sentence "I am a person in a place."

**The note.** Twice a year the cooling rock makes a sound below hearing, felt in the sternum, lasting a minute or two. Infrasound at sufficient amplitude produces dread, nausea, a sense of presence, sometimes vibration of the eyeball. If you are in the water when it comes you may experience unprovoked terror or unprovoked revelation. Both are the mountain.

**Rule: during the note, hold still, breathe, and make no decisions.** Do not surface in a panic — ascent is the dangerous part. Do not resolve to stay. Do not propose anything to anyone. Wait the two minutes out. It will pass and you will feel foolish and that is the correct outcome.

## 6. The breath under the leaves

You will find a dome. It will be a body's length across, silver, held under a leaf like a coin under a table, and it will be exactly the size of the thing you want.

**Do not breathe from it.**

Four separate reasons, any one sufficient:

1. **You don't know what's in it.** This is gas that has sat in heartwood for decades and then under a leaf for days. "Oxygen-drunk" is a description of how it feels, not an assay. Tree metabolism and vent chemistry can produce anything from oxygen-rich to nearly anoxic, high-CO₂, methane, ethylene, sulfide. Three breaths of a hypercapnic or hypoxic mix is a way to lose consciousness with your face in a bubble. If you must know, sample it with an analyzer on a pole from the surface. Sample, don't sip.

2. **Barotrauma.** A dome at 10 m holds gas at two atmospheres. Fill your lungs there and ascend without exhaling continuously and you will rupture them and put gas into your arterial circulation. This kills people who are otherwise having a lovely day. Any breath taken at depth obligates you to a slow, continuously-exhaling ascent, which is precisely the discipline that a person entranced by a magic air bubble does not have.

3. **It ends.** Three or four breaths and the dome shreds and goes up. Now you are at depth, startled, with no gas, in a mirror-world, with your body boundary dissolved and your judgment cooked. That is a drowning sequence, not an emergency.

4. **It isn't yours.** Those domes are the forest's air supply. It made them over decades and it is *waiting* on them. You would be drinking someone's held breath.

**Depth policy for the ordinary traveler: stay in the top lens.** Skin-dive to three meters, five at the very most, after full acclimatization, never after hyperventilating, never alone, always with a buddy watching from directly above. The floor at forty meters is five atmospheres and is not a place for an untrained human at altitude, and the lower lenses have their own chemistry that you have not measured. The second lens with its copper leaves and white filaments is beautiful and you can see it perfectly from above, because everything here is perfectly visible. That is the gift of this place. Let it be the gift.

If your travel body issues you closed-circuit equipment and technical training, note the additional irony: a rebreather in 34°C water gives you a hot scrubber, elevated CO₂ work of breathing, and no way to shed heat. Silent, bubble-free, and it will cook you faster than open circuit.

## 7. Invisible chemistry, invisible life

**Test before you touch.** pH, temperature at three depths, dissolved gases, sulfide. Water that drops calcite this fast is heavily supersaturated and often alkaline; geothermal systems range from benign to acid. You are going to put your eyes and your mucous membranes into it.

- **Goggles or mask, always. No contact lenses, ever.** Alkaline corneal injury is fast and initially unconvincing.
- **Nose clip. Non-negotiable.** Warm freshwater at 33–34°C is the ideal habitat for pathogenic thermophilic amoebae. In a world with no fish and few grazers and no decay, assume the microbial ecology is unfamiliar and uncompetitive. Nothing up the nose. Not a sniff, not a splash, not a rinse.
- **Do not drink it. Do not rinse your face, mask, or wounds with it.** Carry sterile saline for eyes and wounds.
- **Never put a hand or foot into a vent.** Wrist-wide shimmering columns rising from fresh fracture: the bulk water is 34°C because it is *mixed*. The source is not. You will not see the temperature.
- **Cover your skin.** Thin, smooth, full-body, dedicated to this world. Cuts here are made by fresh calcite with an edge like a blade, and a wound in supersaturated water is a wound that starts to become a surface. Tweezers and irrigation in the kit.
- **Expect to lime.** Hair, mask skirt, suit seams, any crease you leave still. It begins during your visit. This is not harmful in itself; it is a warning that everything you leave behind will also begin.

**Biosecurity, both directions.** You are a vector between worlds. This basin is warm, sunlit, predator-free, competition-free, and does not rot its dead. It is the most invasible habitat imaginable. A diatom in a suit seam, a seed in a boot tread, a skin flake, a gut organism on an unwashed hand — any of these could rewrite the whole system, and because nothing mixes, the rewrite would start as a stain on one shelf and spread at the speed of tissue for a hundred years.

Therefore: gear that has never been in another world, or full disinfection; nothing porous, no wood, no cotton, no felt; new or bleached footwear; nothing scented; hands washed before entry; all gear sealed on exit; and a post-visit health quarantine for yourself, because you also don't know what lives in there.

## 8. What you may not introduce

This is the part travelers find hardest, because the individual acts are so small.

**Nothing mixes here.** That is the whole physics of the place. Warm mineral lenses stack under lighter water and the boundaries hold for kilometers and for years. Pollen drifts along a seam for *years*. A seed stops at a boundary and germinates sideways.

Which means: anything you shed does not dilute, disperse, or flush. It arrives at a density interface and *stays there*, in a sheet, at exactly the depth you shed it, essentially forever, in a system that has never had a foreign molecule.

One drop of sunscreen becomes a permanent lens on a boundary that a forest's pollen has been riding since before your grandmother was born. One fin kick across a seam mixes two waters that have never met and can precipitate a whiting cloud that smothers every leaf beneath it — a white scar visible from the rim for decades. One soap residue, one drop of blood, one skin oil fingerprint on a leaf underside: permanent, at human scale, and *visible*, because everything here is perfectly visible.

**Therefore:**
- No sunscreen, no repellent, no soap, no deodorant, no lubricant, no oil, no "biodegradable" anything.
- No fins. They shred leaves and stir seams. Move with your hands, parting rather than passing.
- Cross seams vertically, slowly, once, at a chosen spot — or better, don't cross them at all. Swim parallel.
- Do not touch the leaves. Do not touch the filaments. Do not touch the root curtains.
- **Never cut or pull a root.** Those roots are holding a buoyant trunk with decades of stored gas against its will, taut as rigging. They are also being dissolved grain by grain from below by the mining mats and encased in lime from above. You do not know which one is about to let go. A trunk that releases accelerates upward through the canopy with the mass of a truck. Do not linger above a root plate. Do not add your weight to it.
- Take nothing. Not a crystal, not a shell, not a snail. There are perhaps a few hundred snails in the entire basin. You would be taking a percent of a species.

**And do not make a wave.** There has never been a wave here. The fetch is long and the thin wind has no grip and the surface at noon is a hard bright plane. The film on that plane is a habitat; the striders' dimples are the only residents of the surface itself, and there are so few of them that their marks look like accidents. A single careless entry sends a ripple across the whole basin, breaks the film, and slaps rims that grow a finger's width in a decade.

Enter by substitution, not displacement. Slide in at the saddle over the course of a full minute, without a sound and without a ring.

## 9. Kit

**Body:** dedicated smooth full-body cover, light-colored, hooded; gloves; soft boots inside, hard boots outside; nose clip; low-volume mask; no contacts.
**Numbers:** wrist depth gauge; vibrating timer; thermometer; pH and gas probes; personal CO₂/O₂/H₂S monitor for above water.
**Buoyancy:** a ditchable weight system, weighted to be **positively buoyant at the surface in the top lens**. Note the trap: mineral-heavy water below is denser, so you get *more* buoyant as you descend, not less. Weight yourself for the shallow and accept that the deep will refuse you. That refusal is a safety feature. Do not defeat it.
**Water:** four liters drinking, plus separate rinse water, plus sterile saline. Carry the rinse runoff out in a container. Yes, really.
**Warmth:** dry robe, hat, shelter, staged on rock before entry.
**Medical:** wound irrigation, tweezers, eyewash, a plan for a hyperthermic casualty (shade, cool carried water, evaporation, evacuation — and note there is no cold water here to cool them with).
**Instruments:** passive hydrophone and headphones; camera; fixed photopoint notes. A hydrophone is the single best piece of equipment you can bring to this world.
**Not brought:** fins, sunscreen, soap, scented anything, sample jars, a knife you intend to use, and the idea that you will stay a while.

## 10. Approach, entry, exit

**Approach on rock, not talus.** The splintered slopes lie at the exact angle they fell. Nothing there has been tested by weather; no block has been settled by a thousand rains; there is no soil, no rounding, no interlock. Every step is on a first arrangement. You will be hypoxic while doing this. Take ridges and continuous faces. Test nothing with your weight.

**Do not camp in the staircase.** The eastern saddle is a travertine dam holding forty meters of water above two kilometers of sharp stepped pools. Natural dams fail. Rims there give way once a season somewhere, with a dry crack and a long hiss. Camp above the rim, on high rock, out of every drainage line, upwind of every hollow.

**Do not enter a terrace pool.** I know. Warm, clear, waist-deep, green floor, no fish, a private world with a lid of light on it, ten thousand of them stepping down into cloud. They are the most inviting objects in any world you will visit. Their rims are blades and their rims fail and if you are standing in one when it goes you will travel two kilometers down a staircase made of knives. Look at them from the rock. Walk on the old thick calcite, never on the young white lips. Every footprint you leave on a growing rim is a decade of somebody's work.

**Enter at the eastern saddle**, where the water and the world are at the same level, slowly, in silence, over a full minute, with your gear already rinsed and your buddy already on the rock with the stopwatch running.

## 11. A visit, in practice

- Three acclimatization days minimum. Nothing in the water on day one.
- Day one: the rim, the hydrophone, the photopoints, the trunk bands, the seams from above. Two hours of listening. Learn one landmark by heart.
- Sessions in the water: **one per day**, forty minutes floating or fifteen swimming, timed by two clocks, with a watcher who has agreed a signal and has authority to end your visit without discussion.
- Abort immediately on: cloud over the saddle; any headache, nausea, or confusion; any thunder; the note; a rim crack anywhere; loss of your landmark; the thought *I could just stay a bit longer*.
- Between sessions, drink, eat, write. Write on the rock, dry, in words, while you are still a person with edges. The written record is the only part of this visit that will survive your own memory of it, because the memory will be of a feeling and not of a place.
- Go back next year. And in ten. The fixed photopoints will show you a root curtain that has advanced a meter and a meadow that has closed a gap and a shoreline that has climbed. That is the actual visit. The forty minutes was the introduction.

## 12. What to do with your minutes

Float on your back at the surface with your ears under and your nose clipped and your eyes open, and do nothing at all.

Look up through the window in the surface at a sun that has no wind to blur it. Look sideways and see the forest upside down. Watch a silver thread leave a lenticel and climb and break and spread into a dome. Watch a seed stop at a seam and sit there and begin, patiently, to germinate sideways. Find a trunk's three belts — aerial bark, swollen pale breathing skin, true aquatic tissue — and read the water's whole history off it like a tide gauge. At noon, watch the whiting: look away, look back, and confirm that the entire volume is turning over in a glitter too slow to see.

Listen for the snail. Give it the full hour. When it comes it will arrive from nowhere, inside your skull, perfectly intact, and it will be the smallest sound you have ever traveled to hear.

That is the whole itinerary. There is nothing to reach and nothing to summit and nothing to collect. The place is not offering you an experience. It is offering you a *rate*.

---

## 13. Afterwards

Rinse with carried water, over a container, and carry that out too. Seal your gear. Quarantine yourself per your travel body's protocol. Get warm before you get moving. Check your ears, your eyes, your cuts. Drink.

Then expect the following, and do not be alarmed by it:

For several days you will have trouble locating your skin. Showers will feel violent. Rooms will feel loud and fast and full of things insisting. You will miss the basin with a specificity that surprises you — not its beauty, which you can photograph, but its *lack of insistence*, which you cannot.

Understand what happened. You were held at exactly your own temperature, in still water, in full light, with no gravity to fight, no cold to resist, no predator to watch for, no sound but a snail, at a mild altitude hypoxia, possibly with infrasound in your sternum. Every system you have for detecting where you end and the world begins was given nothing to report. Your body concluded, reasonably, that there was no boundary.

The description is right that this is not a failure of perception. It is accurate information about the place: it *has* been at this temperature, this light, this silence, longer than anything in it has been alive, and it does not distinguish between a tree and a stone and a hundred years and a minute. You felt that correctly.

But you are 37 degrees, and a metabolism, and a deadline, and the place is none of those. Both readings are true and you must hold both, because the failure mode here is not fear. It is agreement. Some travelers do not come out, and they are not overcome by anything — no cold, no current, no beast. They simply stop finding a reason to be a separate object, and they are warm, and they are not thirsty, and there is air under the leaves if they want it, and nothing in the whole basin is in a hurry.

Bring a timer. Bring a watcher. Bring a landmark and a notebook and a second visit already scheduled.

Come out on the rock, shivering, dry, loud, hungry, and unmistakably one thing among many — and then look back down through forty meters of perfectly clear water at a forest that is turning into a mountain one finger's width at a time, and let it go on doing that without you.
