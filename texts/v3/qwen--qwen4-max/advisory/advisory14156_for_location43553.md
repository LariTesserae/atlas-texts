# Preparing for the Standing Sea

## 0. First, correct your expectations

You are not visiting an ocean. You are visiting the inside of a machine that happens to be full of water, and the machine is running well.

This distinction governs every other decision. A wild place tolerates you; it has weather, decay, accidents, slack. This place has none of those. It has eleven setpoints and nine hundred years of holding them. It will not be interested in you, it will not be harmed by you in any way it can measure, and it will not remember you. The only two things it exports are heat and sound, and those are the only two things you are permitted to leave behind.

Most of what follows is logistics. But the real preparation is this: **you must bring your own night, your own envelope, your own clock, and your own witness.** The world has none of the four. Everything else is gear.

---

## 1. What will kill you, ranked by how quickly it does it

**Sound.** Under a minute at the wrong coordinates, and the wrong coordinates are most of them. This is the hazard people underestimate because they read the word *silent* in an instrument report. Instruments that measure change report nothing here. Instruments that measure pressure report a field that fills a lunged man's sinuses with blood. The roar has no envelope — no gust, no lull, no attack — which means your startle reflex never fires, your fear never spikes, and you get no warning of any kind. You will feel fine and then you will be bleeding.

**Pressure.** Six kilometres average, eleven at the trench. Six hundred to eleven hundred atmospheres. There is no breathing gas that works there. Helium tremor and high-pressure nervous syndrome begin around three hundred metres; the experimental mixes top out somewhere below seven hundred. **The sea averages six thousand.** You cannot swim here. You cannot dive here. You cannot saturate here. Every ambient-pressure excursion you have ever done, in every world, is irrelevant. The deep is available to you only inside a hull, or through instruments, or not at all.

**Altitude.** The rim is at 5,800 m — half pressure, about a tenth of an atmosphere of oxygen partial pressure. The peaks go to 7,800. You will arrive, if you arrive carelessly, into the thin part of the world with a body that thinks it lives at sea level. Acute mountain sickness within hours; pulmonary or cerebral oedema within a day or two if you push.

**Cold.** 1.4 °C, dense, four percent salt, and no current to speak of. The cold here is described as a slow hand rather than a shock, and that is worse, not better: the shock is what makes people get out.

**Correction.** Not malice. Filtration. Suspended matter is a fault condition and gets corrected, and a human body drifting in the water column is suspended matter. There are intakes on the floor drawing continuously through clarification beds. There are grazers that turn toward deviation. There are nine-kilometre animals whose entire metabolism is the damping of anomalies. None of them want anything from you. All of them will process you.

## 2. The body, before you go

**Acclimatize properly, or arrive already acclimatized.** Weeks, not days. Staged ascent, or hypoxic pre-breathing, or whatever your travel method permits in the way of telling your body what to be — the water here was told what to be, and it worked. Carry acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, a pulse oximeter, and a portable hyperbaric bag. Know your descent route off the rim before you set foot on it. The rim is the high point of this world; the only cure for the rim is *down*, and you should not have to improvise *down* at three in the morning in a place where there is no three in the morning.

**Get your teeth fixed.** I am entirely serious. A void under a filling, a gas pocket in a root canal, an unresolved sinus, a chest infection, a blocked Eustachian tube — these are the failure points. Everything with a gas pocket lives in the quiet rooms, and your skull is a cluster of small gas pockets that you cannot evacuate. See a dentist. See an ENT. Do not go with a cold. Do not go with allergies uncontrolled. A millilitre of trapped air in a maxillary sinus is the part of you that will fail first, and it will fail while the rest of you is still deciding whether anything is wrong.

**Understand that earplugs do not work underwater.** Your tissue is impedance-matched to the sea; sound goes into your skull directly and reaches the cochlea without asking your ears. Plugs buy you very little and can introduce their own resonances. A hood that traps a gas layer is worse than no hood: you have added a driven bubble to your head. Protection in this world means *being somewhere else* or *being behind a hull*. There is no third option that you can wear.

**Train for monotony.** Spend a week somewhere unchanging before you go. A white room, a fixed light, a constant tone. Learn what your mind does at hour forty when nothing has happened. You will need to know your own pathology in advance, because the Standing Sea will find it and hold it in front of you for as long as you stay.

## 3. Kit

### Above the skin

Mountaineering equipment for 6,000–8,000 m, with three local modifications.

**Gloves and boots for knife-edges.** The rock is freshly broken gabbro and glassy basalt with no soil, no lichen, no settled scree. Nothing has been rounded. Every edge is nine hundred years sharp. Cut-resistant gloves, stiff soles, and the discipline to put a hand down only where you have looked.

**No bolting.** There is no soil to anchor in and the rock will take a piton, and a piton is a permanent mark on a world where permanence is the whole point. Bring slings and removable protection for existing features; use the brass benchmarks, the handrails, the ladders. They are there. They were built for bodies like yours. Do not add to them.

**Category 4 eye protection and full skin coverage.** The sun stands at mid-afternoon and has moved a degree and a half in nine centuries. It will not go down. You cannot wait out the glare, you cannot walk into evening, and shade is not a time of day here — it is a piece of real estate that you must find and go to. You will burn on one side. Plan for that.

**All your own water.** The only fresh water in this world is the standing rain wall on the northern rim, and the ledger there balances to within *a bucket a year*. A week of drinking is two buckets. You would be spending two years of the world's entire hydrological margin on your thirst. Bring your water. Carry out your urine. If you must condense from the plume, return what you take, and log the debt.

**No open flame.** Combustion is poor at half pressure anyway — your tea will be bad, water boils around 80 °C — and a flame is an anomaly in a thin dry sky. Electric heat, from your own supply.

### The instruments

**A hydrophone array and a pressure map, not a chart of the rock.** Position here is acoustic. The contour lines are decoration. You need a live survey of the node structure, updated by you, trusted over anything printed. Eleven thousand quiet rooms in twenty-seven million cubic kilometres is well under one percent of the volume. They are two to three kilometres wide. Between them are distances you cannot cross.

**A comb-locked tone generator.** More on this below. It is the only legal way to make a sound.

**Passive listening only. No active sonar, no echosounder, no pinger.** A broadband ping at source level would be the loudest event in nine hundred years, it would be damped to nothing within seconds, and it would be a fault logged by every trim tower in the basin. Navigate by the frozen light, by the charts, and by listening.

**Cycle counters on everything structural.** This is the item nobody thinks of. At 31.5 Hz your equipment accumulates 2.72 million load cycles per day. At the hundredth harmonic — 3,150 Hz, right in the middle of your hearing — it accumulates 272 million per day, 1.9 billion per week. Fully reversed, no envelope, no rest, no mean-stress relaxation, forever. Fatigue is the failure mode of this world and it does not care that your gear is new. **Retire equipment by cycles, not by hours.** A month here is a working lifetime elsewhere. Bring spares of anything that resonates, and expect fasteners to walk loose, welds to crawl, and fretting to appear at every joint you own.

### The hull, if you are going below

A pressure hull at one atmosphere is a gas pocket. A gas pocket in a comb is a driven resonator. A sphere or a clean cylinder is a bell, and the comb has a tooth every 31.5 Hz all the way up through the audible band — **some tooth will find your hull's mode.** You cannot detune your way out; there is nowhere to detune to.

So: build lossy. Constrained-layer damping, mass loading, thick viscoelastic interlayers, deliberately irregular geometry, nothing that rings. Assume the hull will sing at some frequency and design so that the singing is small and where you can measure it. Put strain gauges on it and watch them. Do not trust a hull because it is thick.

Two more hull notes, both counterintuitive:

- **The deep is mechanically kinder than the shallow.** High static pressure suppresses cavitation. Your thrusters will be quieter at six kilometres than at sixty metres. The dangerous water is near the lid.
- **Your warm hull will grow a shell.** The brine is saturated and cannot dissolve another gram; heat it locally and, for the minerals with retrograde solubility, it will drop what it is holding onto the nearest surface. That surface is you. Viewports cloud. Heat exchangers foul. Seals grit. Budget for scale, and if you scrape it, catch every flake — scrapings are suspended matter, and suspended matter is a fault.

### The personal kit

A black tent or a truly opaque sleep mask. **There is no night anywhere in this world, above the skin or below it.** The sun does not set and at four kilometres down it is still an ordinary bright overcast noon. Your pupils will never open. You will never dark-adapt. You will keep expecting a dusk that is not coming, and your circadian rhythm will free-run and drift and take your judgement with it. Import darkness. Enforce a 24-hour schedule with a watch you wind yourself. Consider melatonin. Treat sleep as a discipline, not a response.

Headphones and music with silence in it. Something with an attack and a decay. Your nervous system is built to notice onsets, and this place has none; the roar has never once started. After a day of a sound with no envelope you will begin to hallucinate envelopes — you will hear the machines gust and lull and pause, and none of that will be happening. Give your ears something that genuinely begins and ends, in private, on a schedule.

A notebook, a pen, a tally. Something that accumulates. You will be the only accumulating thing in the basin.

## 4. Arrival

Arrive on the rim, above the skin, in shade, near a numbered benchmark, with your ears clear and your oxygen already running if you need it.

Do not arrive in the water. Do not arrive at depth. Do not arrive in the rain wall. Do not arrive anywhere you have not surveyed.

**Spend your first ten minutes measuring, not looking.** You will want to look. The blue plate tilting up into a haze that is not distance but the curve of the world is one of the things you came for. Look at it on the second ten minutes. First: hydrophone in, pressure map live, find your nearest node, confirm your altitude, confirm your own sinuses. The place will still be there. It will be exactly there. That is the one guarantee you have.

Above the skin the roar is mostly gone — the impedance mismatch between water and air throws back almost all of it, and what escapes comes through a narrow cone near vertical. What you will hear on the rim is the hiss of the rain wall ninety kilometres of weather away, a hum you cannot localize, and every fifty-three minutes a slow press and release. Note that in air at 31.5 Hz the wavelength is about ten metres. **Above the lid you can step out of a loud spot in five paces. Below it, the wavelength is forty-eight metres and you cannot.** Do not let the mildness of the rim teach you the wrong lesson about the sea.

## 5. The rim: what to do and what not to touch

**The gauge house.** Go in. Read the log. Look at the pen running in the groove it has cut a millimetre deep into the brass drum — that groove is the only record in this world, and it is made of wear, and it is the most honest document you will ever read. Look at the gloves holding the shape of hands.

Do not touch the pen. Do not take the gloves. Do not write a second entry. The log is a control document and an entry is a deviation; also, and more to the point, it is not yours. Bring your own book and write in that. *There will be no record that you were here* is not a threat. It is a description of the filing system.

**The cableway.** There is a single car parked mid-span and it has been parked there for nine hundred years. Do not ride it. Consider seriously the possibility that it is not transport. A mass held at a computed coordinate on a wire across a resonant basin, next to a plate that says TRIM IS NOT OPTIONAL, is a tuning weight. It is parked where it is because that is where it needs to be. Moving it would retune the world.

**The door with nothing behind it.** Do not assume interiors. Some things here are monuments and some are instruments and the architecture does not distinguish. Treat every door as load-bearing until proven otherwise.

**The rain wall.** Forty kilometres long, nine hundred metres high, standing where the towers' plume meets the cold off the peaks. It is the only wind in this world and the only weather. Go near it if you like; do not go into it. It is a permanent cold downpour with a permanent downdraft, and it does not travel, which means it will not pass over you and be done. It will still be falling on you when you stop being able to stand.

**Shade.** Camp in it. It is a location, not an hour, and it will not move out from under you.

## 6. Going down: the honest version

Here is the itinerary that a human body can actually survive, and I want you to make peace with it before you go.

The pump-houses stand every two hundred kilometres: cast iron and concrete, zinc anodes, a lit window, a handrail, a ladder up a cliff. Cast iron and concrete are excellent in compression. Handrails and ladders and benches are built to the scale of a body like yours. And there are roughly fifteen hundred stations against eleven thousand quiet rooms.

**Working hypothesis: the stations are sited in the nodes.** That is why they have windows. That is why there are gloves on a bench. Someone built a human-shaped path through this world, and the path is: rim, gauge house, ladder, station, station, station. Verify this hypothesis with your own survey before you bet your sinuses on it. But if it holds, it is your whole visit, and it is a real one.

The alternative — a submersible, a free swim in the void, four hundred metres of visibility in every direction — is available only to a hull, and the hull is a bell, and you will spend the entire descent listening to your own vessel being played by the basin.

**You will never see a giant whole.** Visibility is four to five hundred metres; they are three to nine kilometres long. There is no vantage inside this water from which one of them fits. And they are 99.97 percent water — you can see the far rim through one — so what you will actually perceive is a warping of the frozen lace of light, a lensing, a place where the painted net of caustics bends. And a hole in the roar. That is what a nine-kilometre animal looks like from inside its own world: a distortion in a still pattern, and a quiet patch. Prepare to know the largest things you have ever encountered only as parts and as maps. Bring patience and a way to assemble. Do not bring a camera and expect a photograph.

**Light discipline, and the real reason for it.** The caustics are still. The animals use them as a map; some graze a particular bright line the way a sheep works a fence. Your torch does not merely disturb wildlife — **it forges the map.** A moving light pulls a grazer off a route that is set by the current in the electrodes, and the timetable is the food chain. So: keep lights still, keep them dim, keep them red (red is stripped first by the water and reaches least far), and never sweep. In this world a still light is a place and a moving light is a time, and time is a fault condition.

**Mind the pasture.** The copper-green nap on the conduits is a millimetre thick and is the base of every food chain here. Do not land on it, do not scrape it, do not sample it. A thumbnail of it is somebody's year.

**Mind the current.** Those are live electrode grids. Electrically isolate your hull, do not bond to the structures, do not park across a line. In a saturated chloride brine under imposed current, assume stray potentials, assume galvanic attack on anything you brought, and assume that gas evolved at an anode is not oxygen. Gas pockets are the thing that dies here.

**Mind the timetable.** The grazers are metre-wide, shelled, low, and move at a fixed speed on a fixed route. They will not stop and they will not swerve. They are rolling stock. Cross the lines at right angles, quickly, and never assume right of way. If one dies another fills the slot inside a cycle; killing one accomplishes nothing except a cost.

**If you are caught in the roar and cannot reach a node:** the only acoustic shelter outside a quiet room is inside the body of the thing that eats the noise. The giants sit at the antinodes precisely because that is where the pressure is worst, and their lattice is doing work on it, and immediately around and within that lattice the field is being consumed. It is 99.97 percent water; you can pass into it. I am not recommending this. The branches flex — that flexing is the animal's whole living — and they are mineral, and they will scissor, and you may be mineralized in turn. But I want you to know it exists, because at the wrong coordinates you will have under a minute and no other idea.

## 7. How to make a sound

The comb has teeth every 31.5 Hz and gaps between them. Two hundred species live in those gaps, a few hertz each, mutually inaudible. The machines do not listen there. It is the only legal channel in the world.

So: **be sound, not substance.**

Carry a synthesizer phase-locked to the pump fundamental. Notch centres fall at 15.75 + 31.5n Hz — 992.25, 1023.75, and so on up the band. Survey first and find an *empty* notch; roughly a third of them are occupied and shouting into one of those is shouting into someone's language. Then speak in single held pure tones, narrow enough to stay in the slot. Not words. Not knocks — a knock is a broadband transient and a broadband transient is a fault.

To ask permission at a lit window: whistle one tone, in a slot, and hold it, and wait. Count the fifty-three minutes while you wait. That is the polite way to knock in this world.

Everything else about you is noise. Your voice is broadband. Your joints click. Your regulator exhaust is a bubble bath of driven resonators — carry a rebreather, no bubbles, ever, and understand that even a millimetre bubble has a resonance in the audible comb and will be driven violently. Breathe slowly. Move slowly. Hold still. Be as close to a fixture as a living thing can be.

## 8. Conduct: the ledger

Nothing accumulates. Every gram is on a loop. This makes contamination a category of offence you have probably never had to think about before.

The water is saturated with the exact mineral of the mountain and cannot dissolve another gram. That is why the drill marks are crisp. **Your body is a source of unsaturated water.** Sweat, urine, exhaled condensate, a litre of fresh water spilled from your flask — locally, briefly, you un-saturate the brine, and dissolution begins, and erosion happens for the first time in nine hundred years. Your litter is not litter here. It is the first weathering of a world that forbade weathering.

So: sealed loop. Nothing discharged. Everything carried out, including the things you would not normally think of as objects. Tether every tool. Captive fasteners only, no loose screws, nothing small and detachable. No crumbly food. Count your manifest in and count it out and do not leave until the numbers agree.

Heat is legal — heat leaves through the skin, that is what the skin is for. Your hundred watts is a lawful export. Sound in a notch is legal. **You may be warm; you may not be wet.**

And do not try to fix anything. Do not free anything, wake anything, shut anything down. It is not a ruin and it is not a prison and it is not a tragedy. It is nominal. The entire industrial apparatus of a continent exists to hold eleven variables and it has succeeded. Stopping is not among the things it can do, and you — one soft body with a notebook — are not the exception. Relinquish the narrative before you arrive. It is the heaviest thing in your pack.

## 9. Time, and the mind

The sea's only clock presses on your chest every fifty-three minutes. That is the round trip: fifteen hundred metres a second, twenty-four hundred kilometres each way. You will not hear it. You will feel it, and after an hour you will understand that the animals are counting, and after a day you will be counting too, and this is not a symptom, it is the correct response.

But it is not a human clock. Fifty-three minutes divides into nothing you live by. Bring a watch. Wind it. Keep a log with entries — *any* entries, weather that does not exist, meals, mood, the number of times you have looked at the same frozen line of light. You need event boundaries or your memory will not encode; you will lose whole days into a single undifferentiated afternoon and come out unable to say whether you stayed an hour or a month.

Set a hard departure condition before you arrive and tell someone outside it. Not a feeling — a number, on your own watch. Because the specific danger of this place is not that it kills you. It is that it is *comfortable*. It is held. Nothing accumulates, nothing is required, nothing decays, the light is kind and the quiet rooms are calm and the work is already done and has been done for nine centuries. There is a bench with gloves on it. There is a lit window every two hundred kilometres. The world fills vacancies inside a cycle.

Prepare for the possibility that the works will offer you a job. Prepare to decline.

And if something answers the window — do not assume it is a person, and do not assume it is not. The pump-houses do not answer, which is a fact about the pump-houses and not a fact about what is in them. Have a plan for both. Have a plan for the third thing, which is that the door opens and there is nothing behind it but more cliff.

## 10. Leaving

Decontaminate. Reconcile the manifest. Retire your structural gear by cycle count even if it looks new — especially if it looks new; everything here looks new.

Expect the hum to come with you. Thirty-one and a half hertz and its harmonics will be in your refrigerators, your aircraft, your own pulse, for weeks. Expect the world outside to seem unbearably various — gusting, lulling, starting, stopping, everything happening all at once with no setpoints anywhere. Expect to wait for the press on your chest and not feel it.

Expect a specific grief that you will not have a word for: not that the place was sad, but that it was fine, and will be fine, and did not need you, and has no slot in which to put the fact that you came. There will be no record. The pen will go on drawing its straight line in the groove it has worn into the brass.

Write your own second entry, in your own book, on the way out. *Level nominal. No action.* It is the only honest report, and it is also, if you let it be, the whole of what you came to see: a world that has been told what to be and has stayed that way, holding, holding, holding, in an afternoon that has lasted nine hundred years — and you, briefly, the only thing in it that was going to change.
