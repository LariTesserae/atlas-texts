# Preparing for the Hold

*A traveler's briefing. Read it twice: once before you decide to go, once on the mule path.*

---

## 1. Understand what you are visiting

It is not a ruin. Nobody there thinks anything went wrong. The town is not shrinking; the *water is rising*, a finger's width per generation, and the town is climbing after it, one floor at a time, into rooms that dead people asked for. The emptiness is not loss. It is inheritance — four thousand rooms' worth of patience, held by a hundred and sixty people who are in no hurry.

The single most important thing to prepare is not your lungs. It is your **speed**. You will arrive as the fastest object in the bowl. Your tools measure in milliseconds; their fastest event is a family carrying its bowls up one level and finding the new room slightly the wrong shape. Almost every way you can do harm here is a way of being *quick*: measuring instead of asking, lifting instead of looking, fixing instead of helping, cutting instead of wounding-and-waiting.

Prepare to be slow. Everything else follows from that.

---

## 2. Find out whether your body should go at all

Three screenings, honestly done, before you spend a coin on passage.

**The chord.** Find a big pipe organ under a full 32-foot stop, or the machine hall of a working mill, or the inside of a steel hull with the engines turning. Stand there for an hour. Do not entertain yourself. Notice what your body does. Some people go calm and sleepy. Some get a pressure behind the eyes, a nausea with no stomach to it, a dread with no object, and a strong animal urge to *leave*. If you are the second kind, do not go. There is no earplug solution to this — see §6 — and there is no quiet room in the bowl. The chord is in the rock. It comes up through your boot soles and your sternum whether or not your ears are involved.

**Cold water, on your face, without warning.** Not a cold shower. A cold immersion. If your breath snatches and your throat closes and you cannot make yourself put your face in and hold it, that reflex is what will kill you in the first four seconds of your first dive, in a maze, alone. It can be blunted with practice. It cannot be argued with.

**Enclosure and open volume.** The Hold gives you both, sometimes in the same second: a ribbed vault you can barely turn around in, and then a plaza of clean green water with nothing in front of you for a quarter hour. If either of those panics you, it will panic you at eleven breaths down, in water so clear you cannot tell how far away anything is.

Also, plainly: get your teeth fixed before you go. A dental abscess in a town with no metal but copper is a catastrophe you will have imposed on other people.

---

## 3. The eight to twelve weeks before

**Cold.** Daily cold-water face immersion, and as much whole-body cold exposure as you can stand safely, with someone present. You are training habituation, not toughness. What you want is for the gasp to stop happening. Separately, train your *hands*: bucket of ice water, then tie knots. The town has a whole architecture — the sweats, the warm dark, ten people hanging with their arms out saying nothing — built around the fact that hands go. Yours will go faster than theirs.

**Breath.** Train with a real instructor and a real buddy, in water, never alone, never on a couch. CO₂ tolerance work, gentle Frenzel equalization, relaxed exhale starts, no packing, **no hyperventilation, ever**. Learn what your own blackout warning signs are, and then accept that at this altitude you may not get them.

**Altitude.** Assume the col is somewhere around 2,800–3,500 m — above the last juniper, where cloud goes *through* a place. Plan a staged ascent: several days sleeping low, walking up. If your crossing method can drop you directly into the cirque, **don't use it that way**. Arrive in the valley town and take the mule path. That hour of hearing the chord before you see the mouth is not scenery. It is your acclimatization, and it is your introduction, and people will notice how you behaved on the moraine.

Discuss altitude prophylaxis with a physician who understands that you will be breath-holding in cold water; some of the standard options interact badly with that. Hydrate. No alcohol on ascent. Know the difference between a bad headache and the headache that means *descend now*.

**Legs and shoulders, not lungs.** Most of your moving will be hand-over-hand along ribs, slowly, in a furnished street. Train pulling, hanging, and moving delicately. Do not train to be a fast swimmer. A fast swimmer here is a bull in a museum with the lights off.

---

## 4. Two quarantines

This is the part travelers skip and the part that matters most.

**The dam is one organism.** Clonal, "one organism by any argument that matters," four hundred paces of it, holding a lake three hundred feet above a valley. There is no genetic firebreak. A pathogen that likes this plant has nothing to stop it. If you kill the Hold you do not kill a landmark; you drop a lake on a town, and the town has already done this once, four hundred years ago, by accident, and lost a third of everybody, and takes its children to look at the healed notch before it teaches them to swim.

So: treat your kit like a hatchery intake. Nothing wooden, nothing woven of plant fibre, no cork, no leather with soil in the stitching, no felt, no live bait, no seeds, no soil, no aquarium anything, no cutting from a garden. Scrub, disinfect, sun-dry, and then do it again in the valley town before the mule path. Ask locally what they do about visitors' boots — they may have a protocol older than your world's germ theory, and it will be better than yours, because they have more to lose. Bring your own boots in a sealed bag and expect to leave them at the rim and take local cork sandals.

**You are the other vector.** A hundred and sixty people, a short summer, and whatever respiratory flora you carried in from three worlds over. Ten days of isolation before arrival, no symptoms, no sore throat, no "it's only a cold." If you are sick, postpone. Cover your cough, wash your hands, and understand that in the dry bells you are breathing *shared, carried, finite air* — see §8 — and so is everyone else, and a cough in a bell is not a private event.

And your gut. Ask, on the first day, where waste goes, and do exactly that. Do not improvise a latrine over a fissure. Do not urinate in a bell. Your intestinal bacteria are novel to a closed, cold, tannin-stained system that has been quietly not-rotting for centuries.

---

## 5. What to leave

**Every blade.** The one crime with one sentence is cutting live structure. Arriving with a good steel knife is arriving with the murder weapon of the only capital offence, strapped to your hip, in a town where everyone can hear everything you do. Leave it. If you need an edge, ask for a bone awl and learn to use it the way the gall-wrights do: *wound to ask, harvest the dead, never cut a rib.*

**All other metal.** Copper only. Not a superstition — an economy and an aesthetic and, in tannin water, a chemistry. Steel rusts and stains and marks; a dropped stainless buckle becomes an unaccountable object in a place where every object is accounted for. Tape over what you must bring. Better: bring nothing.

**Earplugs and a thick hood.** This is the trap in the standard cold-water kit. Sound is the *public medium*. Water carries a knock four times faster and forty times farther, and the ribs carry it farther still, and the entire safety system — *air coming up*, *shoal*, *hail*, and the slow double knock that means the lip has been measured — arrives as percussion. Plug your ears and hood them over and you have made yourself deaf, unreachable, and a hazard to everyone working near you. Solve cold ears another way: a thin hood, a water channel, a smear of resin, patience, and the acceptance that your ears will be cold for a month. Ask an elder about it; several of them will have the bony ear canals of a lifetime of cold water and will have opinions.

**Fins, or at least your long ones.** The walls are upholstered. Ten million snails are grazing a micron at a time and their rasping is a sound you can hear through the ribs. That film is the bottom of everything. One careless flutter kick strips a year of a wall and silts out a gallery for everyone behind you. Move by hand, frog-kick if you must kick, and go barefoot or in soft local sandals in the streets.

**Lights, or most of them.** Light is private. A torch swung down the Long Street is a searchlight down a bedroom corridor. Bring the smallest, reddest thing you can, tethered, for emergency only, pointed at your own hands, and *ask* before you use it in an occupied gallery. Also consider what your beam does to combers who have almost no eyes in the deep galleries.

**Instruments.** Your altimeter, your depth gauge, your tuner, your laser rangefinder, your camera. Not because they are forbidden — because they are *fast*, and because the Hand is not a measurement problem. The council goes to the lip with a wetted cord once a year and there are old people who will tell you with total seriousness that the third throat has gone flat, and this is a matter for the council. You could settle that argument in nine seconds. Do not settle that argument. Seal the instruments, declare them, and let the cord be wet.

**Anything you would be sorry to see catalogued.** Nothing rots down there. A dropped lens cap will still be on a shelf in five hundred years, and someone will know it isn't theirs, and there will be no knot for it.

## 6. What to bring

Less than you think, and mostly consumable.

- **Warmth for the surface, not the water.** Wool and more wool, dry, in a sealed bag, in a dry bell. There is one hot stone in the bowl and it is for baking cakes. You will not be allowed to monopolize it, and you shouldn't want to.
- **A thin exposure suit if you must**, with two caveats. It floats, and it makes you *better than the counters expect*, which is a social hazard, not an advantage — see §7. And it makes you buoyant in a place where the standing joke about a careless person is *she feeds the spillway*.
- **Stone weights, ditchable, in a net.** No lead. Stone belongs here; if you have to drop it, it goes home. This is the one thing you may lose without guilt, and it should be the only thing.
- **Lanyards. Absurd numbers of lanyards.** Hat, mask, tablet, awl, hook, sandals, the lid of everything. Tie your kit until you feel ridiculous, then add one more. Learn *their* knots, not yours; a knot nobody local can inspect is a knot nobody local trusts.
- **A low-volume mask, a spare strap, and no snorkel you'll be tempted to trust.**
- **A slate and grease pencil, tethered.** Honest equipment for someone who cannot read rope yet.
- **Your own food, mostly.** Fish and pounded pith is a lean diet for a body doing cold immersion at altitude; you may need four thousand calories a day and you should not quietly eat a family's winter to get them. Bring dense, boring, crumb-free calories. Eat in a dry bell. Collect what falls.
- **A modest consumable gift, and no durable one.** Sugar, spice, tea — things that vanish. Not tools, not nylon rope, not lenses, not a lighter, not steel, not tobacco, not spirits, not seed. A durable good from you becomes a permanent alteration to a hundred-and-sixty-person economy that runs on fish and rope. The best gift you can possibly bring is **a shift at the lip**. Ask for one on your first morning. There is no better introduction in the bowl.
- **A personal medical kit, discreet and consumable**: antiseptic, dressings, ear care, pain relief, your own prescriptions, and the sense to use it quietly rather than staging a clinic.
- **Water treatment, used privately.** Drink from the melt *above* the funnels if you can — that is water that has not yet been through the town, its voles, its mink, its choughs, or its core. Do not make a performance of filtering. Their water is their water and it has never hurt them.

## 7. Water discipline: the ways this place kills people

Read this section twice. The Hold is not hostile. It is simply a cold, deep, loud, three-dimensional maze at altitude, and it does not care how good a swimmer you are.

**Count short, and count *their* count.** The counters stand at the bell mouths with a knotted cord and a stone, and they count faster than any lowlander thinks reasonable, because the air is thin and a sea-level breath-hold serves badly here. Your suit and your training may let you stay down longer than the count. **Do not.** The count is not a limit on lungs; it is a limit on *rescue* — on how far down someone can get to you, in nine-degree water, at this altitude, in time. Exceed it and you have not proved anything. You have simply arranged to black out somewhere nobody can reach. When the counter calls you up early, come up, and don't argue.

**Ascent blackout is the main killer.** The core shaft is eleven breaths down. Whatever that is in your units, it is deep enough that the oxygen in your lungs is compressed at the bottom and expands as you rise, and the last few metres are where you go dark without warning. Altitude makes this worse: you start with less. Cold makes it worse. Clarity makes it worse — *a fish forty feet away looks like a fish held in your hand*, so you will misjudge every distance, over-swim, and arrive somewhere deeper than you intended. Never free-swim across open volume, however inviting. Keep a hand on a rib or a line at all times. Learn to navigate by touch and by the chord's intensity, because your eyes will lie to you all day.

**Never breathe from a wild pocket.** Two separate ways to die. Air trapped at depth is *compressed*: fill your lungs there and ascend without exhaling and you will rupture them. And air that has sat in a sealed dome for a long time may have had its oxygen eaten out of it by rot and oxidation; it looks like air, it feels like air, and it will drop you. Take breath only where a porter serves it, in a bell above the waterline, and never at depth. This is also why you must not try air-porting. Children apprentice at nine and are told, before anything else, *level, level, level*. You will dump the load, and the load is somebody's paid work, and the town will get it back a basket at a time from your clumsiness.

**Stay away from the throats.** Five fluted leaf-horns swallowing meltwater and entraining air, booming. That is a dam intake. The flow into it will pin you and it will not let go, and the pipe goes down into the plant. Never swim toward the sound. Never enter a horn to look. Learn from a porter how many ribs away is safe, and add one. The chord is also your friend here: it gets louder in your teeth as you get closer, and that is the only warning you'll get.

**Cold, cumulative.** Nine degrees. Your useful working time on the first dive is short and your useful working time on the fifth is shorter. The sweats are blood-warm, twenty degrees, no more — they stop the shaking; they do not rewarm you through. Plan short dives, long dry intervals, and wool. Hypothermia here does not feel like the movies; it feels like being a bit stupid and a bit clumsy and quietly deciding to go down one more time.

**Silt and turns.** Eleven hundred and forty chambers, nine levels, doorways everywhere, most of them unvisited. A wrong turn plus a fin-kick plus a stained shaft is a coffin. Line, contact, and a partner who knows the way — and *your partner is not your equal in risk*. They are adapted and you are not. You are the weak link in every pair you join.

**Nobody goes alone.** Not to the core. Not anywhere, really, until you have been there a season.

## 8. Air is somebody's labour

The dry rooms are cups pushed under: nine inhabited, air trapped above the waterline. The gasp-pockets are harvested by a guild who go down with inverted baskets and walk the drowned streets with a silver sloshing weight held level above their heads.

Which means: **when you sit in a bell breathing hard, you are spending a nine-year-old's afternoon.**

Do not linger in bells. Do not exercise in them. Do not sleep in one without being told you may. Do not bring combustion into one — no stove, no candle, no lamp of your own, no smoking, ever. You will consume shared air and add carbon monoxide to a room whose whole point is that it is a cup. If you feel headachy and dull and oddly content in a bell, that is not the chord. Get out and say so; stale air is a real thing in a sealed room and the town would rather be told.

When a basket comes up, the knock for *air coming up* goes out and everyone knows. Learn that knock before you learn the word for bread.

---

## 9. Sound: how to be polite with your whole body

Learn the knocks first, before vocabulary. They are the safety layer and they are not language-dependent.

- **Knock the threshold before you enter any room.** Every room. Including empty ones. *Especially* empty ones; you do not know what is empty.
- **Never touch a working diver.** Knock the wall. Your instinct in an emergency will be to grab — practise, on land, not grabbing. Ask what the rescue knock is and what the actual rescue procedure is, and let them tell you, and do not improvise a lowland rescue on someone who has a system.
- **Put a hand or a forehead on a rib to listen.** You cannot localize sound underwater; it arrives four times too fast for your ears to compare. Direction comes through the structure. Locals do this without thinking. You will look like you are leaning on the furniture. Lean on the furniture.
- **Learn *hail*.** When it goes, get out of the water and go to the lip. Hail and leaf-fall and ice and a dead mink are the same emergency: a blocked spillway is the one thing that can kill everybody at once, and the town's entire collective labour is keeping that spoon-shaped curve clear.
- **Learn the slow double knock**, once a year, and know what it means, and stop moving when you hear it.
- **Expect to shout at the rim and be silent everywhere else.** And expect the strange thing: after a week you stop noticing the chord, and after a month you cannot sleep anywhere else. Plan for that. See §14.

---

## 10. Rope is writing

The knot-cord in the driest bell is an archive of deeds, genealogies, and promises: this family, this whorl, this shape, this year; *coming*, *ready*, *drowned*. Some families' entire standing consists of rooms not yet grown.

Therefore:

- **Never untie, cut, tidy, or "help" with a cord.** You are handling land titles and graves. A well-meaning re-coiling is a forgery.
- Learn to read knots with cold hands, in green light, and in the dark. Practise at home for weeks. It is the closest thing here to literacy and it will change how you are treated.
- The black knot means *drowned*. It is not a metaphor and it is not a story you ask for lightly.
- **Do not request a room.** A request is a twenty-to-forty-year promise to a plant, filed in an archive, and you will be gone. A cord with no living claimant is a small permanent sadness that the shrimp will eventually clean. If you want to leave something behind, give labour at the lip, or rope, or ask — *ask* — whether there is an orphan chamber someone would like the use of, and let them decide what your gift is.

---

## 11. The drowned quarters and the core

Below the Wash the furniture is still there: bowls on shelves, a comb in a doorway, a shoe a hundred and forty years old that everyone can name because the cord says so. To them this is the back of a cupboard. To you it will feel like a tomb, and the difference is entirely your problem.

**Look. Don't lift.** Everything down there is owned, indexed, and ancestral, and it has survived because the water is cold, tannin-stained, and without oxygen. Bring it up and you begin destroying it. Take it away and you have robbed a family whose deed is a rope in a dry room. If something is *given* to you, understand what you are being handed and behave accordingly.

**The core is a grave and a digestive organ.** The dead go down into the warm and are eaten by red worms and eels and become plant, and the plant grows the next room, and this is understood literally and without much ceremony. Do not dive it out of curiosity. Do not ask to see a burial as a spectacle. If you are invited, go, be quiet, and do not ask for anyone back.

**Decide, before you go, what you want done with your own body if you die there.** Write it down. Tell someone at home and tell the council on arrival — briefly, once, without drama. You are a foreign body in a closed system and they may have views about that; let them have the last word. It is a five-minute conversation and it is the most considerate thing on this entire list.

**If you are invited to the sweats, go.** Ten of them hanging in the warm dark with their arms out, saying nothing, waiting for their hands to come back. Ammonia, wet earth, something like bread. It is the only place in the bowl that smells like a body, and it is the closest thing they have to a public bath and a church. Do not ask questions there. Do not be the one who talks. You may find yourself crying and it will not be remarkable. This is the single fastest way to stop being a visitor.

---

## 12. The tenants

**The mink-thing** swims the drowned streets as easily as the dry ones and takes fish off the racks. Nobody kills it. It is a tenant with a bad character. Do not kill it, do not trap it, do not "solve" it, do not feed it, and do not leave your dinner unlidded and untethered. Lid everything. Cork floats; the whole town is tethered; the phrase for a careless person is *she feeds the spillway*.

**The choughs** are hundreds of red-billed acrobats screaming in the updraft off the dam face all day, nesting in the cork fissures — which is to say, nesting in the thing that must be kept clear. Their nests are spillway labour. Do not shoo them, do not photograph the nests with a flash, do not bring a drone into that updraft.

**The midges** stand over the shallows in columns like grey smoke and the bats come up the col at dusk to take them. Cover your face at dusk. Do not bring repellent you intend to wash off in the lake; put it on your clothes, or wear a net, and ask what they use.

**The snails, the amphipods, the caddis in their mica masonry, the loach flat on the cork, the pale combers pouring through a plaza like thrown rice** — one species, with eyes in the lit galleries and hardly any in the deep ones. Do not collect. Do not take a shell. Do not take the lichen that has been measuring the same hand's-breadth of bark since before the town had a name. Nothing living leaves with you, in either direction. That is a quarantine rule and it is also simply manners.

---

## 13. Timing and length

**Go at the end of the melt.** The lip is at its most urgent, the throats are at their fullest, the midges and bats are up, and the Hand is coming — the council at the lip with a wetted cord against the one notch in the cirque wall that the plant did not make. To be present for the slow double knock is to understand the whole place in a minute and a half.

**Ask about winter before you consider it.** If the melt stops, the throats may stop, and if the throats stop, the air harvest may stop, and a town that lives on carried breath may do something in winter that no summer visitor has ever seen. That is either the most interesting time to go or the worst. Find out from someone who has done it, not from me.

**Stay three days or stay a season. Do not stay two weeks.** Two weeks is long enough to become dependent on the chord and not long enough to be any use. Under a week, you are a polite guest who sleeps badly and leaves. Over a month, you are a person who cannot sleep anywhere else, and you should know that cost before you pay it.

**Do not bring children who cannot swim and cannot be watched every second.** The floor is water. A third of everybody died once. Nine is the age they apprentice, and they will assume your child is available for the lip, and they will be right to assume it, and it is your job to know whether that is safe.

---

## 14. Leaving, and after

Count your kit at the lip, both ways. Nothing you drop becomes litter; it becomes an heirloom nobody can name.

Take nothing living. Take nothing owned. Take a knot you were given, and a recording if you asked and were allowed — sound is public here, and they may be curious about a record of the third throat, and you should offer it quietly to the council rather than waving it at an old man in front of his neighbours.

Then prepare for the part nobody warns you about.

**Home will be silent in a way that feels like a held breath.** You will lie in a good bed in a quiet room and your chest will wait for a chord that isn't coming, and you will not sleep properly for weeks. There is no fix. A recording does not carry the part that comes up through the boot soles and the sternum; that was bone conduction, and you left your bones' world behind. Some travelers find a low hum to live beside — a mill, a hull, a big transformer, an organ loft — and it helps, and it is not the same, and they go back.

You will also come home with habits. You will tie your hat. You will knock a doorframe before you walk through it, and check that nobody is working on the other side. You will find yourself putting a hand flat on a wall to listen. You will get unreasonably angry at people who leave things loose. You will stand in a room full of furniture and feel that it is *crowded*, and then walk out into an empty street and feel that it is empty in the wrong way — empty because nobody bothered, not empty because somebody's great-grandchild hasn't moved in yet.

And you will want, very badly, to tell someone how high the water was, to the millimetre.

Don't. Let the cord be wet.

## The card

*Copy this out and carry it in your head, not your pocket.*

1. Arrive low. Walk up. Listen for the hour.
2. Quarantine yourself, then your kit. The dam is one plant.
3. No blades. No metal but copper. Nothing living, in or out.
4. Ears open. Always. Sound is the road.
5. Knock every threshold. Never touch a diver. Hand on a rib.
6. Count short. Come up when called. Never alone. Never the core.
7. Never breathe a wild pocket. Never swim toward the sound.
8. Tie everything. Only your stones may be lost.
9. Air is a child's afternoon. Don't burn in a bell.
10. Look, don't lift. Never untie a cord. Never request a room.
11. Keep the lip. Ask for a shift on the first morning.
12. Wound to ask. Harvest the dead. **Never cut a rib.**
13. Say nothing in the sweats.
14. Be the slowest thing in the bowl.
