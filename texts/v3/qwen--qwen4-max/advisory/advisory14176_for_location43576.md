# Preparing for the Foreland

## The one-paragraph brief

Nothing here is going to attack you. There is no predator, no avalanche slope you must cross, no exotic chemistry, no hostile local physics. This is an ordinary Earth or something close enough to it that your lungs and your boots and your gut will all work as designed. The danger is entirely a danger of **misreading**. Every parameter in this valley is set to a value your instincts have not been calibrated for: the light comes from the wrong side, the cold is the wet kind, the ground is a liquid eight hours a day and a solid for the other sixteen, distances are unresolvable, sound doesn't carry, and the map — the actual arrangement of where water goes — is being redrawn while you stand on it. Ordinary landscapes forgive those errors. This one has no soil to soften a fall, no trail to bring you back, no one to hear you, and nothing that dries.

Go prepared to be *wet, slow, and small*, and it will be one of the great places. Go prepared to conquer it and you will be a hypothermic casualty on a gravel bar by four in the afternoon.

---

## 1. Before you cross: the body is the first problem

Call it four thousand metres. That is the single most likely thing to ruin or end your visit, and it has nothing to do with the ice.

- **Buy the altitude with days, not with willpower.** Three to five nights staged below before you set foot on the plain; above 3,000 m, raise your *sleeping* elevation by no more than 300–500 m a day, with a rest day every thousand. Day-tripping up from a lowland valley is the classic error and the flat, easy, shadowless ground will make it feel permissible.
- **The terrain is a trap for exertion.** It looks like a floor. It reads as flat and effortless, so you will walk fast with a heavy pack, which is precisely the recipe for high-altitude pulmonary edema in cold wet air. Deliberately walk at half the pace that feels natural. If you cannot hold a conversation, stop.
- Know the two failure modes cold. **HAPE:** breathless at rest, dry cough, absurd fatigue, a wet crackle, falling oxygen saturation — often on day two, often at night. **HACE:** headache that ignores painkillers, vomiting, and above all *ataxia* — if someone can't walk a straight line, they are dying, not tired. The only real treatment is descent, immediately, in the dark, in the wet, which is why you must plan the descent route while you are still smart.
- Acetazolamide if your physician agrees; dexamethasone and nifedipine in the kit if you're going far from help; a pulse oximeter, understanding that a number alone means little and a *falling* number with symptoms means everything.
- Eat past appetite. Altitude suppresses hunger and wet cold shivers away 4,000–5,000 kcal a day. Bring food you actually like, and salt it hard: you'll be drinking litres of glacial water that contains almost no dissolved minerals, and plain-water hyponatremia is a real and stupid way to have a seizure at 4,000 m.

## 2. Abandon the project of staying dry

This is the mental adjustment that makes the whole trip work.

There is a sheet of water two centimetres deep and a kilometre wide with **no banks**. No banks means no ford, no crossing place, no dry line, no "other side." You will be standing in water within a minute of arriving and you will be standing in water for the rest of the day. Anyone who spends their hours trying to hop from stone to stone will cover two hundred metres, exhaust themselves, and get wet anyway at 11 a.m.

So dress for **immersion, not for winter**:

- Waterproof boots that you accept will be full of water by mid-morning, plus full gaiters to keep the *silt* out rather than the water. Gaiters matter more than you think: rock flour in a boot is grinding paste.
- Neoprene or waterproof socks for the flats. Thin synthetic liner socks. **Three or four pairs, and one pair in a sealed drybag that is sacred** — that pair is for sleeping, and you never, ever wear it outside the tent.
- The real injury here is not frostbite. It is **non-freezing cold injury** — hours of feet at 0–2 °C in water. Numb, white, wrinkled feet that stay numb and painful for months afterward. At every long halt, sit on a rock, take the boots off, wring the socks, put the dry ones on, get the feet warm against your belly or a partner's, and put the wet ones back on to walk. Ten minutes of this per day buys you your feet.
- Insulate with synthetic, not down, wherever it can get wet. Down here is a liability unless it lives inside a drybag or under a shell.
- Many thin gloves plus one overmitt. Wet cold destroys dexterity fast, and you will want your fingers for the small lives.
- **Nothing dries.** Say it to yourself before you arrive. The air is at saturation and has nowhere to put its water — that's why the rime is growing. Hanging a shirt on a boulder accomplishes nothing except making it colder. Your only dryers are your own body heat and your stove. Everything you bring in wet stays wet until you leave. Pack accordingly: fewer changes, more sealed reserves, and one absolutely inviolable dry bundle containing your sleeping insulation and your sleep socks.

## 3. The light: your eyes and your judgement

Faces lit from underneath. No relief. A dog-sized rock and a house-sized rock resolving in the same instant.

- **Category 4 glacier glasses, wraparound or with side shields, and a spare pair, and goggles for drift.** At 4,000 m you're getting roughly half again the UV of sea level; snow throws back most of what arrives; stratus lets a great deal of it through. And because the light comes from *below*, a hat brim — your usual defence — does almost nothing. Sunscreen goes on the underside of your chin, under your nose, and around your nostrils. People forget this and spend the night with a swollen face.
- Snow blindness has a **six-to-twelve-hour delay**. You will not know you're being burned while it happens. You'll wake at two in the morning with sand under your eyelids, unable to open your eyes, at altitude, in a place with no rescue. Overcast is not permission. Bring lubricating drops and a strip of tape and gauze.
- **Learn to read the ground without shadows.** Use colour and texture instead of relief: wet is dark, dry is pale, silt is smooth, bedrock is striped. Test with a pole rather than an eye. Throw a pebble ahead if you can't tell a step from a slope.
- **The water is your spirit level.** In a world with no shadows, every pool surface is a true horizontal datum. If you want to know which way the ground actually falls — and you desperately will, for camp and for drainage — look at where the water sits against its rim. This is the one honest instrument the place gives you, and it's lying all over the floor.
- **Striae and whalebacks are a compass you can read with your fingertips.** The scratches all run down-valley; the polished smooth stoss side faces up-ice, the plucked ragged lee faces down-valley. In a total whiteout, when the stratus comes down onto the plain and you cannot tell sky from ground, you can kneel and put your palm on a boulder and know which way is out. Learn to do this *before* you need it.
- Distances lie, so **navigate in time, not in space**. Set turn-back *hours*, not turn-back landmarks. Log a GPS breadcrumb from the moment you arrive and treat it as your only trustworthy path. Any map of the water is already wrong; pools that existed last week are dry rims now.
- Use sound as a rangefinder — it's the only one that works here. The serac noise arriving eight seconds after the collapse means about 2.7 kilometres. Count between the sight and the sound and you'll know something true about a place that tells you nothing else.

## 4. Walking, and the two states of the floor

The ground has a day shift and a night shift, and they are different substances.

**Night and early morning:** frozen, load-bearing, crunchy, hollow-sounding — needle ice has jacked the whole surface layer up off its bed. This is the fastest, safest walking of the day, and also the most slippery, because a film of ice over machine-polished bedrock is about as slick as anything on Earth. **Microspikes and two poles with baskets are not optional.** A slip here is a fall onto fresh angular fracture-sharp rubble, at altitude, an hour from anywhere.

**Late morning to three:** the crust collapses into porridge. Every step sinks, sucks, and squelches. Progress halves. Plan for it; don't fight it.

**Three in the afternoon:** the sun goes behind the western arête and in about eleven minutes the world locks. This is the hard deadline of the day. Be off the flats, be at camp, be dry-ish and insulated *before* the shift ends, because the moment the water stiffens the temperature falls off a cliff and the katabatic comes down off the ice to find you.

A few places to not put your feet:

- **Any hollow.** A dry hollow at 4 p.m. is tomorrow's pool. The divides are being surveyed at a centimetre an hour; the ground that drains east today may drain into your tent on Thursday.
- **Any pool rim, and anything downstream of one.** A nickpoint is eating backward into that lip and when it arrives the pool empties in four minutes. Do not camp in the path of a lake that is deciding to leave.
- **The fresh dump at the snout.** Angular, unstained, smelling of fracture means *unconsolidated*, ice-cored, at the angle of repose, and bigger than it was in spring. It will fail if you stand on it wrong.
- **Old moraine crests** look like perfect camp platforms and may be dead ice under a rubble skin. Look for fresh slump pits, listen for water running beneath the stones, and prefer bedrock when you have the choice.
- **The lee of the walls in mid-morning.** That tick of stones settling as the ice inside their cracks lets go is freeze-thaw releasing rock. The walls do their dropping when the thaw arrives.

## 5. The ice: draw the line before you get there

Decide, in the warm and rational lowlands, that **you are not going onto the glacier**, and let that decision be boring and final.

The reason isn't crevasses in the usual sense. It's that the whole underfloor is a plumbing network at pressure, enlarging itself as it runs, ten hours a day. There are shafts and conduits under a skin of ice and snow whose thickness you cannot read in flat light, and the surface has no shadows to warn you. The medial moraine looks exactly like a footpath — a raised straight causeway of rubble, dead convenient — and it is a thin skin of debris riding on moving ice. It is the most inviting thing in the valley and it is a conveyor belt.

Never approach a moulin lip. Never stand under the outfall portal, especially in the afternoon at peak discharge; arches of dirty ice collapse. Do not route yourself through the fall line below the icefall. And when you hear the serac go, understand that the eight-second delay means it is already over — that sound is information, not warning.

## 6. Water, flour, and lungs

The water is chemically almost pure and biologically nearly clean, and physically a nightmare. Rock flour stays in suspension for days and destroys filters.

- Carry a **collapsible settling bucket**. Fill it, let it stand overnight, decant the clear top in the morning. A pinch of alum flocculant speeds this dramatically and weighs nothing.
- Pre-filter through a coffee filter or a bandana; use a **backflushable** hollow-fibre filter or a purifier designed for glacial silt, and backflush it every single time. Carry chlorine dioxide as backup and remember that cold, cloudy water needs *double* the contact time.
- Boiling works and costs fuel; at 4,000 m water boils around 86 °C, so give it a full three minutes at a rolling boil, and accept that pasta is a lie up here.
- **The filter sleeps in your sleeping bag.** A frozen hollow-fibre cartridge is a silently ruined cartridge. Same for batteries, stove fuel if you can, boot liners, and your water bottles (inverted, insulated).
- When the dry wind comes and the flour goes airborne: **buff or P2 over the mouth and nose, glasses instead of contact lenses.** Freshly fractured quartz is chemically reactive in a way that old dust isn't; this haze is genuinely irritating to lungs and eyes. It also gets into zippers, threads, stove pumps, and cameras. Carry a soft brush and use it constantly. Seal electronics in bags; do not change a lens out here, ever.

## 7. Camp

- Pitch on a **bedrock crown or a gravel bar with a natural rise**, in the lee of a whaleback — the ragged, plucked, down-valley side. That's where the shelter is, and the ground tells you which side it is.
- Bathtub floor, footprint, and a sleeping pad system with an R-value of 5 or more. You are lying on saturated ground at freezing point. Conduction, not air, is what will take your heat.
- **Understand your anchors.** Stakes driven into wet silt at dusk will be gripped like concrete by midnight and *released entirely* by noon as the needle ice collapses. Tie to boulders. Re-tension in the morning. Expect the tent floor to be frozen to the ground and peel it up gently.
- The katabatic arrives after the shift ends and comes down-valley off the ice. Point the door away from up-valley and guy everything to stone.
- **Rime will grow on your tent** as it grows on every other windward object. It loads the fabric and it seals the vents. A sealed tent with a stove running is a carbon monoxide chamber, and CO poisoning presents like altitude sickness, which is the cruellest possible confusion in this place. Brush the tent, keep a vent open, cook outside or in the vestibule with the door wide.
- Do not camp at the melt line. It is the canteen, it is the densest life in the valley, and it *moves up and down twice a day* — you would be pitched in the middle of a rush-hour crowd that runs over you at dawn.
- Food sealed and off the ground: choughs are thieves with no fear, voles are patient, and a fox crosses the whole plain nightly.

## 8. Sound, and being alone together

The quiet here is absorbent. Fresh snow and wet flour eat sound; there are no leaves, no cliffs, no echo. A companion forty metres away sounds like they're in a cupboard, and a whistle doesn't carry either.

Agree visual signals before you need them. Keep line of sight — which flat light makes harder than it sounds, since a person in grey clothing at eighty metres simply ceases to resolve. **Wear something loud in colour.** Two small radios if there are more than two of you. And use the river: when the broadband roar drops at dusk and the silence is handed back to you, that is the day's clock, more reliable than any watch.

Bring a satellite messenger and understand that a deep cirque may not give it sky. There is no cairn here, no stake, no boot-scratch, no established route, no one who knows where you are. Plan for self-rescue or for no rescue.

## 9. The ridge, and the blue crawl

When the dry wind comes over the col and starts the snow drifting, the drift charges itself. You'll feel it in your arm hairs and hear a fine buzz and smell ozone, and in the last light the rime feathers will crawl blue.

It's beautiful and it's mostly harmless corona discharge — but it is also an unambiguous statement that you are standing on the highest conductive point in a charged system. **Get off the spine, the col, and the nunatak.** Sit on your pack, feet together, poles and metal away from you. It will also quietly destroy unshielded electronics, so keep the phone in a bag and off your body.

Last a few minutes. Then it stops. Don't be up there for it.

## 10. The living seam, and your obligations to it

Here is the thing to hold onto: **this ground has no defences and no decomposers.** No soil biota, no rot, nothing to break anything down. That has two consequences.

**You are a vector.** Your boot treads, pole baskets, tent pegs and pack seams carry seeds, spores, nematodes and eggs from every lowland you've walked through. Scrub and disinfect all of it before you cross. This is the one place where a careless introduction genuinely rewrites the story — you could hand this plain a soil it was going to spend a century making, and hand it the wrong one.

**Nothing rots, so nothing can be left.** Pack out food scraps, toilet paper, and human waste in wag bags. A scat on raw till is a nutrient bomb and an inoculation on a surface where the entire visible flora is a black cyanobacterial film, some shirt-button moss, three saxifrages, one buttercup, and a willow the size of a fingernail with two leaves pressed into the boundary layer. That is the forest. Step around it.

And walk on stone rather than through the seething snow at the melt edge — you will crush millions of springtails per bootfall, and they are the base of everything that lives here.

**No cairn.** No stake, no scratch, no scratched arrow on the polish. The defining fact of this place is that no one has recorded it. Decide before you arrive whether you intend to be a witness or a mark, because you will feel the urge to build something — the void invites it, the manufactured flatness makes a cairn feel like a courtesy. It isn't. It's the first sentence of a different book.

Leave the fresh angular stone at the snout where it lies. It is the most tempting object in the valley: a piece of the mountain that has never seen the sky, still smelling of fracture. Take a photograph with a person in it for scale, and leave it.

## 11. The condensed kit

Sturdy waterproof boots (broken in) · full gaiters · microspikes · two poles with baskets · neoprene/waterproof socks + 4 synthetic pairs + one sealed sacred pair · synthetic insulation, no exposed down · hard shell top and bottom · 4 thin gloves + overmitt · warm hat + sun hat that you'll resent as useless · cat-4 wraparound glasses ×2 + goggles · high-SPF for the underside of your face · buff/P2 · headnet · sleeping system rated well below zero with R≥5 pads · tent with bathtub floor, footprint, rock anchors · liquid-fuel stove + maintenance kit + windscreen + **more fuel than you think** (250–350 ml/person/day, more if melting snow) · settling bucket + flocculant + prefilter + backflushable filter + chemical backup · electrolytes · 5,000 kcal/day of food you actually want · altitude meds, oximeter, blister and foot kit, wag bags · drybags for everything, one inviolable dry bundle · soft brush · GPS with breadcrumb + compass + paper and pencil · satellite messenger · headtorch with red mode · a hand lens.

The hand lens is not a joke. It is the most important piece of scientific equipment you will carry.

## 12. How to actually be there

**Stay at least three days.** Two nights minimum. The meaning of this place is a *cycle*, and a day visit shows you only one frame of it: you must see the morning crust, the noon porridge, the eleven-minute lock at three, the needle ice heave in the dark, and the crust again — the floor re-tooled overnight and run again the next day. Anything less and you'll come home describing a wet gravel plain.

Then structure your days around the melt line, because that's where everything is. Follow the finches up-valley as the thaw climbs through the morning; follow them back down in the evening. Sit — actually sit, for an hour, without moving — at the edge of the seething snow with the hand lens, and let your eyes recalibrate until the static resolves into individual animals and the spiders start hunting in front of you. Abundance here isn't spread out; it's concentrated at the working face, and you have to go to the face and be still.

Find a pool lip with a nickpoint and watch it. Bring a thermometer and a stick and mark nothing. Understand that you are watching the first watershed boundary in this country being surveyed, and that one of two rills is going to win, and that the loser's valley will be dry and obvious and inevitable in a thousand years.

And accept that **you will not be able to photograph the scale.** Flat shadowless light plus no trees plus no relief turns everything into a close-up of a workbench; your pictures will look like a tabletop of gravel unless there is a human figure in them. This is not a failure of your camera. It is the honest appearance of a place that hasn't yet been given a horizon.

---

## 13. Leaving

Check your boot treads, your gaiters, your pegs, your pack seams. The flour will come home with you regardless; it will be in the folds of your jacket for years, grey and fine and smelling faintly of wet slate. That's fine. That's swarf.

What you should carry out deliberately is the correction. You will have learned, in three days, that a landscape is not a thing but a *rate* — that the reason a valley has a river with a name is that water and stone have been arguing for a very long time, and that you happened to arrive on the afternoon the argument opened.

Look back at the snout on your way out. The pile there is bigger than it was in the spring.

The machine is still running. You were never on the shop floor. You were in the tray.
