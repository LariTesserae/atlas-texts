# Preparing for The Kettle

## 1. Understand what you are going to before you pack

You are not visiting a ruin, a wonder, or a village with a view. There is no view. You will walk over a mountain shoulder through dripping laurel, arrive at a hole full of fog, and think you have been brought to the wrong place. Then the sound will resolve into a town, and you will spend your first day disoriented, your first week smelling everything, your first month learning to hear, and the rest of your life comparing other places to it unfavorably.

The Kettle is a machine made of living things, six hundred years old, with a single point of failure at the bottom of it. Everything about local behavior — the laws, the guilds, the drills, the quotas, the shouting, the things that are unforgivable — follows from two facts: the town is alive, and the water must go down the throat. If you internalize those two facts before you arrive, you will make fewer mistakes than a person who arrives with a camera and a good heart.

The other thing to internalize: **nothing here is designed to last, and everything here is designed to continue.** Do not confuse the first with carelessness. Do not offer to fix it.

---

## 2. Body and health, months before

**Feet and legs.** Ninety-odd paces of descent, root-tread stairs dished by centuries of feet, wet moss, snail slime on every surface, springy bridges of fused branch, and a working day that goes up and down repeatedly carrying things. Train stairs with load. Train balance on unstable footing. If you have a knee, an ankle, or a vestibular problem, this town will find it on day one. Practice walking slowly on slick surfaces with your hands free — you will be carrying a hamper, not a trekking pole.

**Hands.** Everything is wet and everything is heavy and everything is carried. Grip endurance matters more than strength.

**Heat.** This is the one nobody expects. A cold, wet, mild town at cloud-forest altitude with no wind, near-saturated air, warm compost floors under you, and a workshop storey that smells of ammonia and lanolin and is *warm*. You cannot sweat efficiently here. You will not feel hot in the way you are used to; you will simply become stupid, headachey, and nauseated. Plan to drink far more than you think, and to eat salt — which is also your money, a joke you will get tired of.

**Vaccinate and prepare seriously.** Tetanus current. Consider rabies pre-exposure (there are bats, and there are dogs everywhere, and the nearest physician is days away over a mountain). Discuss leptospirosis risk with a travel clinician — wet ground, rats, rat-hunting dogs, and mud you will inevitably get into a cut. Bring antibiotics for wound infection and a low threshold for using them: in permanent wetness, small cuts do not scab, they macerate and go bad.

**Lungs.** Guano dust is not inert dirt. Cave floors, drying racks, netting platforms and dovecotes all produce fine bird-derived dust that can cause serious lung disease in visitors who have no acquired tolerance. Do not go into the oilbird caves as a tourist. If you are invited or granted access, wear a properly fitted particulate respirator, not a cloth, and understand that this makes you look strange and is worth it. If you have asthma or any immune compromise, treat the caves as off-limits and the fruitfall crowds as a risk. Bring your inhalers in duplicate, sealed.

**Gut.** Guano goes on the gardens and the terraces. That is the whole point of the town, and it means every raw thing that grows here has been in contact with bird feces. **Do not eat raw watercress.** Do not eat raw anything from the marsh beds. Snails are farmed and eaten by the bowlful and are also a classic route to a very bad neurological parasite if undercooked — eat them only as locals prepare them, thoroughly cooked, and don't graze. Cook your greens. Boil your water. Wash and dry your hands before eating, with the understanding that "dry" is a relative term.

**Skin.** Assume you will be continuously damp for the entire visit. Plan for it: antifungal powder and cream, barrier ointment, and a discipline of one genuinely dry set of clothes for sleeping only. Trench foot is a real possibility for a visitor who insists on wearing the same wet boots for three weeks. Take your boots off. Put on dry things. This is the single highest-return daily habit.

**Do not arrive sick.** Six or seven hundred people, shared air, layered enclosed spaces, no hospital. A respiratory infection you consider trivial is a town-wide event. If you are coughing, wait on the rim or come another season.

## 3. Quarantine: the worst thing you could possibly do

Cutting a structural limb is the worst thing a *person here* can do. The worst thing a *visitor* can do is arrive carrying a tree pathogen, a borer, a scale insect, a slug egg, or a seed.

The houses are trees. The floors are trees. The roof is a tree. The town's structural substrate is a single continuous inarched organism, and a blight that kills laurel would not damage the Kettle, it would *end* it, in a way no fire could.

So:

- Bring no live plant material, no seeds, no fruit, no soil, no untreated wood, no bark, no wooden tool handles of unknown provenance, no walking stick you cut in another world.
- Scrub and disinfect boots, gaiters, pack bottoms, tripod feet, tent stakes, and every strap and seam before you cross the rim. Seam grit is where eggs live.
- Prefer metal, bone, horn, plastic, or fabric for anything you carry. If you want a wooden thing, buy it there.
- Offer to let them lime-wash your gear. They have lime in quantity and it is both fungicidal and culturally legible; a visitor who asks to be limed is a visitor who has understood something.
- Bring no animals. Not a dog, not a cat, not a bird. A cat in this town would be a catastrophe and a crime.

Do the same in reverse when you leave. You will be carrying snails, crickets, laurel seeds, guano fungus and lime sludge in your kit. Scrub before you enter the next world. You can be an invasive event somewhere else.

## 4. What to carry, and the principle behind it

**The principle: bring nothing you cannot maintain there, and nothing you cannot afford to lose.** In the Kettle, your possessions are food. Mold eats leather and paper and glue. Cave crickets eat fabric. Snails get into everything, including your kettle, and are somebody's property. Rats are hunted but present. Lime deposits on anything left still under a weep, and ammonia in the workshop storey attacks copper alloys — brass fittings will crack and crumble in ways you will not understand until they fail. Aluminum and lime do not get along. Steel rusts in weeks. Adhesives delaminate. Cotton rots and never dries.

A workable kit:

- **Two of everything soft.** One set you live in wet, one set sealed for sleep. Wool and synthetic, no cotton, no down of your own (buy local down; it's better and it's the economy).
- **A wide-brimmed hat.** Not for sun. For drip-line. You will stand under weeping foliage a great deal and a brim keeps water off your face and out of your collar.
- **An oiled cloak, bought there.** Their oilbird-oil waterproofing can be renewed locally; your membrane shell cannot, and will wet out and stay wet. Plan to re-oil weekly on the Shelf, under instruction.
- **Sealed containers.** They already make stoppered gourds and sealed jars for the coast trade. Buy several immediately. These are your dry-box, your food safe, your medicine cabinet, your money box. Bring a good supply of silica gel and regenerate it in a pot on the Shelf when you have permission — that's the only dry heat available to you anywhere.
- **Light without flame.** Headlamp, spare, batteries, no candles. See §6.
- **Notebook that survives water, and pencils.** Paper turns to pulp. Graphite works wet. A wax tablet and stylus would be locally comprehensible and slightly charming.
- **Repair kit that matches their materials:** waxed thread, awls, needles, sinew, good cordage, beeswax or grafting wax. Not glue. Everything here is tied, woven, pleached, and lashed; a traveler who can splice and sew is useful, a traveler who needs adhesive is helpless.
- **Respirator, eye protection, nitrile gloves** for dye pits, tanning troughs, lime work, and any cave-adjacent activity.
- **Ear protection for your first dusk only.** See §9.
- **A small, honest first-aid kit and a written note** of your allergies, medications, blood type, and who to contact in whatever world you came from. Give a copy to your host. There is no rescue here. The Throats die regularly and the town's response is to cut their names in soft stone.

**Your transit device, if you have one, will not like it here.** Saturated air, condensation on every temperature change, lime dust, ammonia. Keep it sealed with desiccant, above the flood line, on your person, and check it on a schedule. Do not build your itinerary around being able to leave instantly.

**Leave at home:** anything aluminum, anything brass you care about, anything that needs a flat surface, anything you'd describe as "irreplaceable," a bell, a whistle, a drone, a musical instrument you intend to play in public, a tent (you'll be given a platform, and a tent below the flood line is a violation of the second law anyway), and any impulse to improve the place.

## 5. Money, gifts, and the fact that your wallet rots

Value is weights of salt and hands of down. Debt lives in knotted cords in the guild houses.

Understand what this means physically: **salt cakes and dissolves in this air, and down mats and rots.** Your cash is hygroscopic and perishable. Carry small denominations in sealed jars, spend rather than hoard, and never leave your salt open overnight. The only durable store of value in the Kettle is *reputation*, recorded in cord and in memory. Invest in that.

What to bring as trade and gift, in rough order of usefulness:

- **Salt** (money, and welcome)
- **Copper pins, steel needles, fish hooks, awls, fine wire** — they trade down the mountain for the little copper pins that hold the looms together; a handful of good pins is a serious, dignified gift
- **Gritstone**, small querns or whetstones if you can carry the weight
- **Grain**, which will not grow here
- **Reading spectacles** for close work by lamplight, if you can source several strengths — a town that works by lamplight at noon has a lot of people quietly struggling with fine thread
- **Pencils, waxed thread, good cordage**

What not to bring: bells or whistles (signals, see below), anything that mimics a guild mark, and any device that would let someone bypass a quota.

**Market mechanics.** A bargain is made by shouting a number, then both parties spitting on their palms and slapping the trestle. That slap is a binding contract, heard by everyone, and the sound is the applause you hear all day. Therefore:

- Do not shout numbers you are only thinking about.
- Do not spit and slap to be friendly, or as a joke, or to try it out.
- Do not slap a trestle rhythmically while waiting.
- Learn to project. You will under-shout and be ignored, and then feel rude when you correct. Everyone shouts because everyone must; volume here is not aggression. Save your voice, learn to speak from the diaphragm, and accept a hoarse first week.
- Do not incur cord-debt you cannot read. Ask your host to teach you the knot conventions before you borrow anything, and repay promptly and publicly.

## 6. The laws, and the unwritten ones

**One: nothing structural is cut.** This is absolute and it is not negotiable, not even a little, not even the twig in your way.

Practically, for you: do not cut, notch, carve, peel, strip, break, or prune any living trunk, limb, root, or pleached roof. Do not nail into a tree. Do not tie a hammock, a clothesline, a lantern, a pack, or a guy-line to a branch without explicit permission — load and abrasion are cutting by other means. Do not pick at grafting wax. Do not strip lichen or moss (moss is packing material, farmed and sold). Do not lean a saw against a trunk. If a limb has closed your window, you ask the Tiers; you do not open it. If you need a stick, buy a stick.

Consider declaring your blades at arrival and keeping any saw folded and out of sight. A visitor with a sharp steel saw in a town of bone knives is a nervous-making object. There is a woman on the third terrace who cut a limb in a bad winter forty years ago and is still, mildly and permanently, not forgiven. The town replaces every plank every nine years and does not replace its opinions.

**Two: nothing below the flood line may be fixed.** Do not anchor, stake, cement, weight, or "secure" anything on the marsh floor. Do not drive a peg. Do not build a platform. Do not leave your bag on the duckboards overnight. Everything below the line is built to be carried, including your things, and if you make your thing immovable you have made it everybody's problem.

**Three, which is the first law wearing a different hat: fire lives on the Shelf.** All flame, all heat, all cooking, all smoking, all rendering, all boiling, all drying-by-heat happens on that swept rock ledge under a woman with a stick who is not joking. This means:

- No candle, no lighter, no match, no pocket stove, no incense, no smoking, no "just a small flame," no demonstration of a fire-starting trick.
- No hot work in your room. Your room is a tree.
- Boil your water on the Shelf. Dry your silica gel on the Shelf. Re-oil your cloak on the Shelf. Ask, wait your turn, and learn the hearth etiquette before you use it.
- The Shelf is also where the lime-burning happens, so it is a caustic-dust and hot-stone environment. Eye protection, closed shoes, no loose synthetic sleeves near flame.

**Four, unwritten: do not foul the water.** Springs come out of the rock at two heights in a ragged line all the way around, which means everything you spill on a terrace eventually enters somebody's supply. Ask on your first hour where to piss, where to shit, where to wash, and where to dump wash-water. Urine is likely valuable — there are dye pits and tanning troughs that want ammonia — so there will be a collection pot and a rule about it. Do not wash yourself or your clothes in a weep channel. Do not step in the watercress beds. Do not dam, divert, or "improve" a channel. Do not throw anything at all into the Swallow.

**Five, unwritten: nothing here is wild.** The snails are farmed. The eels are owned and hand-caught under specific boards. The moss is a crop. The frogs, the lizards, the pigeons, the chicks, the ledges — all of it is quota, lot, custom, or somebody's livelihood. **Do not forage.** Do not pick up a snail off your kettle and eat it, or crush it; it's dinner and it's property. Check your kettle, yes. Then put the snail somewhere sensible.

## 7. Water, food, and the Swallow

Drink boiled water, taken from a high weep above habitation if you can identify one, and expect grit — the water is heavy with lime and will scale your vessel from the inside. Long-term visitors should think about kidney stones; short-term visitors should just drink plenty and stop worrying.

**The Swallow will kill you if you let it.** It is a throat two people wide, worn to a smooth polished spiral, and the entire bowl's drainage goes down it in a braided rope. There is no fence described and no reason to expect one. Wet polished limestone offers no friction whatsoever. Do not go near the lip. Do not sit near it for the atmosphere. Do not photograph it from the edge. Do not retrieve anything that falls in. Establish, on your first day, exactly where it is and what the safe viewing distance is, and then treat that as a hard boundary forever.

**And here is the thing that will save your life:** when the bowl floods, the water comes up brown, quiet, and unhurried, and *you can no longer see where the throat is.* A calm knee-deep pond with a hole in the middle-south that is swallowing a river. Never wade flood water. Never walk a flooded duckboard lane you cannot see. Get above the line and stay there, and let the locals, who know exactly where every marker is, do the moving.

## 8. The flood, and being a useful guest

Three or four times a year, rain outruns the Swallow. A bell on the Shelf rings and the market goes up the stairs. The lower town is dismantled and stacked on the second terrace in about eleven minutes by people who have been drilled since they were four.

You will not be able to do this on your first try. Prepare anyway:

- Ask, on arrival, to be taught the drill and to be given a role. Accept the role. This is your entry fee and it is not optional in spirit.
- Learn the bell. Ask to hear it once. **Never carry anything that sounds like it, and never imitate it.**
- Learn your carry: one hamper, rope handles, one trip, up a named stair, to a named spot. Practice folding a trestle and rolling an awning until you can do it wet and dark.
- Keep every irreplaceable thing you own above the flood line at all times, in a container you can carry in one hand while climbing.
- When the bell goes, go up. Do not go back for your bag. Nothing below the line is worth anything; that is not carelessness, that is the second law, and a visitor who dies for a backpack has insulted the whole arrangement.
- Then stand along the terrace in the rain with everyone else, watch the market become a pond, and shout advice at the pond. This is a social event. Participate. It is the closest thing to a town holiday outside fruitfall.
- Afterward, help carry back to the marked places, which have been marked for centuries. Do not suggest better places.

Learn the Swallow's note. Every local knows the state of the drainage by pitch the way farmers know the sky. Ask someone to teach you the ordinary *shhh* and the *shhh* that means trouble. This is the most valuable single skill you can acquire here, and it costs one conversation.

Also learn the silence. Once or most afternoons, for about a minute before a squall, all the birds stop, and every person in the bowl looks up. When you notice the water alone — forty weeps, the channels, the throat — **look up too**, and get yourself and your things under cover. Then rain hits the canopy like gravel out of a sack, ten seconds later than you heard it coming.

## 9. Sound: the actual local language

You will learn this town by ear before you learn it by eye, and until you do, you will be functionally illiterate.

**Do not habitually wear earplugs.** The pour at dusk is genuinely overwhelming — thousands of overlapping hard clicks bouncing off wet rock, air that feels solid — and one evening of protection is sensible. But a person with plugged ears in the Kettle is a blind person in a lit room: you will miss the bell, the Swallow's change of pitch, the pre-squall silence, the shouted number, the child crossing behind you at a run. Adapt instead. Give it three days.

Choose your lodging by ear, and visit it at night before committing. North wall means the caves and the pour at maximum. Middle-south means the Swallow's endless *shhh* under your bed — some people sleep like infants to it, some lie awake for a week. The workshop storey means looms and beaters. Ask what's above you: a dovecote or a netting platform means the dawn pigeon shift, which is a hundred cloths shaken out at once, directly over your head.

**Privacy is acoustic and does not exist.** Walls are woven hazel and lime daub. In the lean weeks the town goes quiet enough to hear individual conversations across the bowl, and finds it embarrassing. Assume you are always audible to everyone. Do not discuss anyone anywhere in the bowl. If you need a genuinely private conversation, use the noise windows — the market at full shout, a squall, the dusk pour — or walk up to the rim. Locals almost certainly do exactly this; watch and learn. Be discreet about intimacy for the same reason, and don't be scandalized by a town that has solved this problem in its own way.

**Do not drum, click, or beat rhythmically.** Four beats and a rest is the clock. Clicking is how forty thousand birds see. Neither is yours to play with.

**If you are deaf or hard of hearing:** this town's safety and social signaling runs through sound, and you should plan for it explicitly rather than hoping. But do not arrive assuming they haven't thought about it — seven hundred people over six hundred years will have deaf residents and established practice. Ask what those practices are, arrange a partner for bells and squalls, and ask whether there's a tactile or visual convention you should learn. Ask, don't invent.

**If you are blind:** this may be the most accessible place you will ever visit. Forty weeps each with its own note, the beaters keeping hours, the Swallow as a permanent landmark, the market as an acoustic beacon. You will navigate better than most sighted visitors, who will be busy being confused by the fog.

**If you have hyperacusis, misophonia, or sensory sensitivity:** be honest with yourself. The frogs are a physical pressure on the chest. The market is a hundred transactions a minute at riot volume. The pour fills the bowl. This is not a place you can make quieter. Consider the lean weeks, a rim-side lodging, and a short first visit.

## 10. Moving through the layers

Ground is marsh and duckboard; four to eight metres is workshops; eight to fifteen is dwellings and bridges; above that racks, platforms, dovecotes, lookouts, fog; above that the rim.

- **Routes are weight-rated by custom.** Children cross at a run on routes their parents will not use. **Do not follow a child.** Ask which bridges are adult routes and use those. Ask your host about the load rating of your floor before you put anything heavy on it or invite three people over.
- Hands free on stairs. The handrails are polished like antler and are there for a reason. The Weep Stair is dished in the middle by feet and slick.
- Do not jump, stamp, or dance in a group on an upper floor. Split withy and packed root-plate is a living, decaying, nine-year material.
- Do not dangle your feet off a duckboard. Eels thick as your wrist live under the boards.
- Expect the dogs. Small hard dogs that hunt rats are allowed everywhere by long custom, including your bedding, probably. Do not kick a dog. Ask about the etiquette before you feed one.
- Do not handle Throat ropes, step on ropes, or borrow rope. That gear is life-critical and inspected by people who bury their colleagues.
- **Mobility limitations:** the verticality is real and there is no gentle path. But there is heavy rope infrastructure — guano comes down in baskets on ropes — so a descent by basket, arranged with the cave crews, and a stay confined to one terrace is genuinely possible. Arrange it in advance, in writing, through your host. Do not improvise it at the notch.

## 11. The birds, and how not to be a problem to them

**Dusk pour.** Twenty minutes of fat brown long-winged birds exhaled out of the north wall, clacking, navigating by sound. Do not stand in the flight path. Do not wave your arms. Do not hold a lantern up into the pour. Do not shine lights into the cave mouths. Do not attempt to catch, touch, or feed a bird. Stand where you're told, wear your brimmed hat, keep your mouth closed, cover your food and water, and protect your eyes. There will be guano. It is the economy arriving home.

**Dawn pigeon shift** at fruiting: the roofs go white and the noise is unbearable and the guano is considerable. Same precautions, plus don't sleep with your mouth open, and don't leave anything edible uncovered on a platform.

**Caves:** by invitation only, respirator, no lights pointed at birds, no unnecessary noise, and never at dusk. The crews work by quota and lot from fixed ledges at fixed chick ages. Do not ask for a bigger take, a look at an off-quota ledge, or a private oil deal. That's not a folk custom you can charm your way around; it's the load-bearing constraint on the entire fertility budget of forty square miles of forest.

**Fruitfall** is four days of festival, nets on every platform, fat rendering on the Shelf, slick paths, crowds in the caves, nobody sleeping properly, everybody slightly rich. It is the best time to see the town and the worst time to be an unhelpful stranger. If you go at fruitfall, go to work: nets, hauling, baskets, rendering. Then the five or six lean weeks of mending, dredging, grafting, pit-turning and salting, when the town is quiet and poor and can hear itself and is embarrassed about it. That is when you will actually get to know anyone.

## 12. Sleeping, smell, drying, and the four hours of sun

You will sleep on a platform above a sealed pit of guano, leaf litter and fern brash, turned twice a season, sitting at blood heat, under a pigeon-down quilt. It is warm and it is genuinely wonderful and it smells, at first, like something you should not be sleeping on.

- Do not open, turn, add to, or dig into a compost pit. That's skilled seasonal work and you will ruin a bed.
- Do not put anything into the pit, including waste, including "just a little water."
- Down allergy? Say so before you're given a quilt, and ask what alternatives exist.
- Keep your one dry set actually dry: sealed jar or oiled bag, above the flood line, never worn while working.

**The smell** — ammonia, lanolin, warm fat, old fruit, iron mud, cold tea, rot — will be in your hair, your clothes, and your memory. After a week you stop smelling it. After a month you cannot imagine sleeping any other way. Budget for that month if you want the real thing.

**Rain protection is for standing still.** While working, you will get wetter inside a waterproof shell than outside it. Dress like the locals: accept wetness while active, get dry and warm afterward.

**The sun bar is your maintenance window.** For about four hours around midday the sun comes over the south notch and lays a hard white bar across the terraces, and the whole place steams and stinks and glitters and gets its work done. That is when you dry your one dry set, air your bedding, oil your cloak, check your kit for mold, and do anything requiring visibility. Schedule your entire visit around those four hours. Everything else in the day is sourceless wet green light and lamplight.

Also: bright diffuse light plus altitude means you can burn without ever feeling sun on your neck. Cover up during the bar.

## 13. Guilds, and the shape of offense

Present yourself properly. Stop at the notch, listen for ten minutes, find the lookout, and do not descend unannounced or wander the rim gardens (that's food, in guano-black soil, in beds). An unaccompanied stranger loose in the lattice is a fire risk, a cutting risk, and a flood liability, and people will be tense around you until someone vouches for you.

Get a host. Offer salt and pins. Offer labor. Learn the names of the guilds and who speaks for what: the **Tiers** (structure, grafting, the lattice, the marks), the **Throats** (the Swallow, the ropes, the picks, the wall of names), the **cave crews** (quotas, lots, guano, chicks), the **Shelf** (fire, and the woman with the stick), and the market's cord-keepers.

Do not pity the Throats. Do not call any of this primitive. This is a six-hundred-year-old technical civilization with material science, quota management, structural engineering, double-entry cord accounting, and an eleven-minute logistical drill performed by preschoolers. It has simply chosen different materials than you would have.

And know this: the town replaces every plank in nine years and remembers an offense for forty. Your reputation is the only thing here that doesn't rot. Behave accordingly.

## 14. The trap: do not offer permanence

This is the most important paragraph in this document.

You will be moved by the wall of names on the Shelf, cut into fresh tufa that the water immediately begins to fill in, older names becoming stone, newest names sharp, the guild re-cutting them, and everybody understanding that this is exactly the point. You will be moved by the baskets and bowls and the child's shoe hung in the drip-line a year ago, half stone now, glittering.

**Do not touch the drip-line objects.** They may be memorials, betrothals, offerings, or graves. Do not take one. Do not add one without being invited, and if you are invited, understand that you are entering a commitment measured in years, not making a gesture.

And do not offer to fix anything:

- No sealant, resin, preservative, varnish, or "harmless" wood treatment.
- No concrete, no steel, no anchors, no "just widening the throat a little."
- No proposal to make the town permanent, fireproof, rot-proof, or safe.
- No restoration of the filling-in names, no photography-as-preservation lecture, no heritage-protection framework.
- No pity about the deaths, offered as a reason to change the work.

The Kettle is the same town the way a river is the same river. Its form persists *through* replacement, not instead of it. A visitor who offers to make it last is offering to stop it. If you genuinely have something to contribute — a better rope fiber, a safer pick, a respirator that fits, a lens for a woman who can no longer see her thread — offer it humbly, once, to the right guild, on a second visit, and accept the answer. Then let it go.

## 15. When to go, how long to stay

- **Best first visit:** a dryish stretch, arriving with enough time to be useless for a week and useful by the third. Aim to span at least one flood if you can; you will not understand the second law until you've watched the market become a pond.
- **To see the town at its peak:** fruitfall. To know the town: the lean weeks after.
- **Minimum meaningful stay:** ten days (you'll stop smelling it around day seven). **Real stay:** six weeks. Anything less and you'll leave just as your ears open.

Tell someone in your home world your itinerary and your expected return, and leave instructions in case you don't. Write down your allergies and medications and give a copy to your host. There is no evacuation. The nearest help is over a mountain and down to a coast.

---

## 16. Leaving, and afterward

Scrub and quarantine your kit — you are now a vector. Declare the oil and any guano at whatever border you cross next; a stoppered gourd of oilbird oil leaks, smells like warm fat and old fruit, and will be confiscated by anyone with a biosecurity mandate. Buy two.

Take home: oil, a bone knife, good cordage, a knotted cord recording a debt you intend to repay, and — if it's offered and you understand what it costs — something from the drip-line.

Then be ready for the part nobody warns you about. Other places will be too quiet. Other towns will feel dead: cut lumber, sealed surfaces, cold floors, silence where there ought to be forty weeps and a throat saying *shhh*. You will lie on a dry mattress in a dry room and miss the smell of rot keeping you warm. You will hear a market through a wall somewhere and stand still in the street.

Nothing there is finished. Nothing there changes. Every single thing in it will be different in nine years, and the water is still going down the throat. Go and be a small, careful, useful part of that for a while, and then leave it exactly as it was.

## The ten things to memorize before you cross the notch

1. No flame below the Shelf. None. Not once.
2. No cutting, tying, nailing, or peeling anything that's alive.
3. Nothing fixed below the flood line — including your bag.
4. Never wade flood water; you can't see the throat.
5. Never approach the Swallow's lip.
6. Learn the bell, the Swallow's pitch, and the pre-squall silence.
7. Don't follow a child across a bridge.
8. Boil the water, cook the snails, never eat raw watercress.
9. Bring no seeds, no soil, no wood, no animals — the houses are trees.
10. Don't shout a number, don't slap a trestle, and don't offer to make anything last.
