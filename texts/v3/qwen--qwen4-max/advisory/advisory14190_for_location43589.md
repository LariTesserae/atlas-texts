# Green Stone: A Pre-Departure Briefing

## First, correct your expectations

You will arrive thinking you've landed in a ruin. Everything about the surface says *abandoned*: a million addresses and four thousand people, streetlamps burning over lots where nothing stands, tram rail polished by use running out into pale nothing. Your body will read this as catastrophe, and you will spend your first day in a low grief you can't account for.

It isn't abandonment. It's anticipation. The city is finished; the buildings simply haven't happened yet. The plain is not dead, it is *setting*, and the difference matters enormously for how you behave. You are not walking through the aftermath of something. You are walking across the wet top of something that is still being made, and it will take your footprints, and it will keep them.

Hold that in mind, because most of what follows is either about not getting hurt by an unfinished world, or about not contaminating it.

## The five hard rules

Learn these before you learn anything else. The crews will teach them to you anyway, usually by shouting.

1. **Never open a grey hatch.** Sit on them, dry your gloves on them, warm your hands. Never lift one, never block one, never leave your pack leaning against one.
2. **Never barefoot, never bare-handed.** Not on the flats, not in your tent, not for a second.
3. **Never bleed on the make.** Cover a wound immediately and carry the dressings out with you.
4. **Never cut a comb.** They aren't trees. They're water mains with a catchment claim measured in hectares, and a district drinks through them.
5. **Never go down alone, and never go down looking for the engines.** There is no bottom tour. Nobody agrees on the depth. That should be enough.

## Before you go

**Declare your mass.** The measurement teams have been twice and left with a ledger that doesn't balance: eleven million tonnes a year arriving, nothing anywhere losing it. You are ninety kilos of unaccounted-for input walking around on their instrument. Somebody will want your arrival weight, your date, your kit list, and your departure weight. Give it honestly, including what you shed. This is not bureaucracy; it's the only science anyone has been able to do here, and you are a variable in it.

**Check your own physics before you commit.** The light lags. Shadows arrive a half-second to a full second behind the things that cast them, and the crews' explanation — that the ground is new and hasn't learned the local speed yet — is either folk poetry or a warning, and you cannot tell which. Anything in your kit that depends on precise timing, tight synchronization, or fast closed-loop control should be assumed unreliable until proven otherwise. Bring a mechanical watch. Bring paper.

**Decide about the coast in advance.** Somewhere downstream in time there is a shoreline that does not get to exist, and it is being spent here on kerbs and verges and the addresses of unbuilt houses. The second measurement team's report ends with one sentence about it and then stops. Some travelers find they can stand on this ground easily. Some find they can't, and discover this on day three, at night, alone, eleven kilometers from a lit window. Better to think it through at home. There is no correct answer and the locals will not have the conversation with you.

**Get fit for slow, not fast.** Twenty kilometers a day here costs what thirty-five costs on a worn world. Sharp wet stone, flat light, no depth cues, a body that can't shed heat. Train ankles and feet, not lungs.

**Sort your teeth and your eyes now.** There is no dentist and no optician within a day's walk in any direction. Bring spare glasses. Bring the sealed goggles, not the sunglasses.

## The kit

### Feet and hands

Everything here is an arris. A slip doesn't bruise you, it opens you. The millimeter-deep sheets of water on the flats are invisible in this light and lie on stone that is essentially a field of unfettled blades. **You will slip.** Plan for the fall, not the fall prevention.

Stiff-shanked boots with a hard sole, two pairs, plus adhesive and spare soles — the stone chews rubber. Gaiters. Rigid-soled camp shoes so you never take a bare step in the night. Cut-resistant glove liners in quantity, with nitrile over-gloves for anything wet, because the sap is alkaline and smells of crushed almond, which is a smell worth respecting. Long sleeves and long legs in tough synthetic, always. Knee pads, because you will kneel to listen to the ground and to read address plates at your ankles.

### Eyes and skin

The sap is caustic and it weeps from every fresh cut, and the trimming crews put it into the air as aerosol. **Sealed goggles.** At least two liters of sterile saline per person, four if you're going out for a season, and the discipline to irrigate an alkaline splash for a full twenty minutes before you do anything else including walking. A P100-plus-vapor respirator for anywhere cutters are working.

Wound kit built for lacerations: irrigation syringe, skin glue, closure strips, non-adherent dressings in absurd quantity, a pressure dressing you can apply one-handed. The good news is that this ground is nearly sterile — new mineral, no soil, no seed bank. Infection is unusually unlikely. Chemical injury and blood loss are not.

### Light and power

The lid gives you a room with one shaded window, forever. You need a headlamp at noon. Get warm high-CRI light and an amber contrast lens: in directionless illumination everything is the same value as everything else and you will walk into a two-centimeter lip you never saw. High-visibility outer layer, because in this light *you* are also nearly invisible, and the crews are operating machinery.

No sun means no solar. Assume a week of battery and arrange charging at a tower or a workshop, and understand that after water, power is the real currency. Ask humbly. Offer labor.

Bring a bright morning light and use it on a clock, every day, without negotiating with yourself. There is no dawn here — only the moment the fog thins enough to show you the next streetlamp. Your circadian rhythm will free-run and you will not notice until you're weeping at a bronze plate that says CANDLEWRIGHT ROW. Vitamin D. A rigid schedule. The only honest natural cue is the sap tide, an hour before actual dawn, when the whole plain goes dark and shining. Set your day by that.

### Sleep

**A cot.** Wide feet, pads under them. The ground is sharp, warm, wet, and rising a hand's breadth a year; anything you lay flat will be grown into. Lift and sweep your camp every few days. Expect a stone skin on anything you leave touching bare slab for a week.

Where you sleep matters more than what you sleep on. Camp high, on old cured stone, away from the luminous low places — that pooled shine is sap, and it will rise under you before dawn. Not in a building socket, not in a comb's clearing during harvest, not on a hatch.

**Listen before you lie down.** Carry a cheap mechanic's stethoscope or a length of dowel. Press it to the slab. Loud continuous crystalline ticking means the curing front is shallow and the floor is doing work; that's the ground that burns a bare foot at noon and will cook you slowly through a night. Quiet stone is old stone. Old stone is a bed. Watch for the saddles, too — the pale slow grazers eat the soft skin off curing stone, so where you see them, don't sleep.

### Sound

This is the item nobody packs and everybody needs: **a small speaker and a library of ordinary background.** Rain on leaves. A café. Traffic. Birds.

The quiet here is not the absence of sound, it's the absence of *background*, and your nervous system has spent your whole life using background as an all-clear signal. Take it away and the threat-detection machinery runs with nothing to chew on. Underneath there is the engine cycle, a pressure in the sternum every nine seconds, which is not sound and cannot be earplugged away — earplugs make it worse, because they strip out the fog-combs' hiss, the one continuous noise on the plain, the only thing here you can hide inside.

Play something ordinary at low volume in your tent. It is not indulgence. It is the difference between sleeping and lying awake at three in the morning feeling your own heart being counted.

### Tools, and the best cargo you can carry

Files. Sandpaper. A whetstone. Abrasives of every grade. **A bag of river sand.**

Nothing on this plain is rounded and there is nothing here to round anything with. Every edge is a fresh burr. Deburring is the unending local chore and the crews will trade you almost anything for the means to do it faster. A bag of ordinary river sand is a luxury good.

And bring gifts that are *finished*: a worn leather strap, a coin rubbed smooth, a wooden bead, a river pebble for a child who has never held one. Bread, if you can keep it — nothing here dries, everything molds, and there is a reason the make sometimes brings up the smell of bread in a place with no bread.

Bring one round thing for yourself. More on that at the end.

## What to leave at home

Nothing alive: no seeds, no soil, no cultures, no animals. There is no seed bank here and you must not give it one.

Nothing aromatic. No perfume, no scented soap, no strongly spiced food cooked in the open. Partly because smell travels as far as sound in this still air and a hungry person will walk eleven kilometers toward it. Mostly because the make reads a record and reproduces what's in it, and it has already produced the smell of bread in a place with no bread. Do not be the reason a district is haunted by your cooking for the next eighty years.

No drone. Flat light, fog, lagging shadows — you'll lose it, and it will fall on someone's water claim.

No fire. Everything is wet, the sap chemistry is uncertain, and the lid is *maintained*. Do not be the reason anyone has to think about the lid.

Nothing fragile, and nothing you cannot bear to have swallowed.

## Your first seventy-two hours

**Do not trust your hands.** The shadow lag breaks the correspondence between a moving thing and its dark twin, and your visuomotor system will make reaching errors it has never made before. No climbing, no cutting, no cycling, no driving, nothing where a half-second matters. Walk slowly. Touch surfaces to confirm them. Expect nausea; bring something for it. Most people adapt in three or four days. Some never do. If you are still missing handrails on day four, go home — there is no shame in it and there is a great deal of danger in staying.

Do not stare at your own lagging shadow. It is the shortest route to derealization on this plain, and you will be tempted, because it is *right there*, showing you where you were.

**Expect everyone to look sinister.** The light comes from below — the sap carries a faint luminance and pools in the low places. Faces are uplit. Every human being you meet will look ill, hollow-eyed, faintly theatrical. This is optics, not people. Watch hands and posture, and talk to them; a voice corrects a face very quickly.

**Expect dread with no object.** No insects, no frogs, no song. Your hindbrain reads silence as *predator*. It will do this for days. Name it when it happens and don't obey it.

## Getting around

The grid is your greatest asset and you should use it like an instrument. Every corner has a name in bronze and every lot has a number. Navigate by address, not by landmark, because there are no landmarks — only a tower every few kilometers standing in its own clearing like a nail in a table.

Carry the plate index. Carry a tally counter for blocks and a brush for clearing plates, since the third-generation ones are at your ankles under a sheet of water. Carry a compass and a mechanical watch and don't trust anything synchronized to the outside.

Learn the tram. It is the actual city: the mechanism is underground, the slot is polished by use, and it runs for four thousand people spread across a million addresses. It is how anything here works at all. Signal it; it may not stop for you otherwise. And keep your feet out of the slot — a broken ankle here is a very serious event.

**Take brass rubbings.** Paper and wax, off the address plates. It's the only souvenir that takes nothing: the name of a street that has never had a building on it. People at home will find these more upsetting than any photograph.

## Water, food, waste

Two waters, and you must learn the difference immediately.

**Comb water** is condensed cloud, run down the trunks into mains. It's the good water. Drink from the mains, ask where they are, treat it anyway.

**Sap water** is what the curing stone gives up: the seeps in open ground with no catchment, the sheets on the flats, the pupils in the drill holes. It is mineral-loaded and alkaline. It will scale your kettle and it will not be kind to you. Carry a still or don't bother.

Drink four to six liters a day with salt, and understand the trap: the floor sits at the temperature of a sleeping animal and the air is saturated. You cannot dump heat by conduction and you cannot dump it by evaporation. You will overheat while feeling clammy and shaded and mildly uncomfortable, with no sun on your neck to warn you. Move in the dark hours. Rest through the brightening. Fifteen kilometers is a good day.

Food: sealed, dry, hard, tinned. Nothing dries here, ever, and everything molds. Assume no shops, no cafes, no resupply — a district's entire population may be one woman and her workshop.

Waste: carry it out, all of it, including dressings and hair and nail clippings. Do not use a drill hole. Ask about the vaults; the sanitation system is built for a million and serves four thousand, and it will be the strangest oversized thing you encounter.

## Going down

The buried generations are the reason you came, whether or not you've admitted it. First streets nine meters down, intact, lamps still burning in solid stone. Second generation at four meters. Third at your ankles. A cornice on the North Approach you can sit on like a bench. A window you can step into, and a dry warm furnished room beyond it with the floor sloping gently up where the stone came in.

Rules: gas meter, rope, helmet, a second person above who knows exactly where you are and when to worry. Those rooms are warm because the stone around them is still curing, and curing gives off heat and gives off sap. Air is not guaranteed. Announce yourself before you enter — *furnished* does not always mean *abandoned*.

Don't cut structural stone. A cut weeps, and the healing is exothermic, and a slab releasing stress cracks like a rifle and sends the sound eighty kilometers. Don't take the four-winged bird. Don't take anything the make has brought up by mistake.

If you sleep in that room — and you will want to, it's the most sheltered bed on the plain — sweep first, and expect a skin of stone on your blanket by morning.

## The errors

Sooner or later you will find footprints in a curing slab that are yours.

Note the address plate. Photograph it if your camera works. **Do not step into them.** Tell the crews, who have seen this before and will be unimpressed. And then let it go.

Do not go looking for more. Do not follow a smell of bread; there is no bread, and the smell is not a source, it's a citation. And if you find prints that are a size you have not grown into yet — near Sixteen Bells, say — walk away upcountry and do not spend your evening thinking about them. The plain is reading a record and you are in the record. Travelers who start chasing their own entries stop being travelers and start being locals, and the ones who do it fastest are the ones who never come back.

## People

Four thousand of them, spread thin enough that a lit window is eleven kilometers from the next lit window. Learn the etiquette of the window before you need it: which lamp means *come* and which means *leave me be*. There will be a code. Ask.

Assume you are audible. A door closing carries eleven kilometers; you can hear a trimming crew from the far side of a district and know their street. There is no privacy outdoors. Whatever you say to your companion at midnight, the woman in the workshop has heard, and she has heard it clearly, and she has nothing else to listen to.

Use their words. *Green*, *sap*, *the make*, *the lid*, *comb*, *saddle*, *upcountry*. Say upcountry and mean inland; they're right, the hydrology runs backwards, downstream doesn't exist yet. Don't ask the crews about the shadows. They'll tell you the light is slow because the ground is new, and they'll change the subject, and you should let them.

Pay in labor. A day on the cutters buys more than any currency you're carrying, and it will teach you more about the plain in eight hours than a month of walking. And when you find the one lit window in a district, arrive with bread and files and something round, and stay the night, and listen. She has been the entire population of a district for a long time.

## The east

Twenty kilometers east there are beaches, on the old coast, belonging to a world that has had time. Go there at least once. It is the only soft ground within reach and your feet need it, and your eyes need a horizon that isn't a kerb line.

Take off your boots. Stand in sand. It will be the single most physically shocking moment of the trip — not the buried lamps, not your own footprints, but *sand*, and what your soles do when they meet it.

Take a little, with permission, and no more. Check the sea against the base of the far towers while you're there. It sits lower than last year. The towers are being buried from below, so you can't actually tell whether the sea is falling or the ground is rising, and this ambiguity is exactly the sort of thing you should not argue with a quarryman about.

## How long, and how to leave

Either stay under two weeks or commit to a season. The middle is the worst of it: long enough to lose your fear, not long enough to earn the local calm. At about a month you stop feeling the nine-second cycle entirely — and then one night at three in the morning you feel it again and cannot stop, and by then you're eleven kilometers from anyone.

Leave before you stop finding it remarkable. That's the real deadline. Nobody who works the plain finds any of this remarkable anymore, and that is a kind of death you can catch.

Budget decompression on the other side. The world you come home to will feel *used up*: everything rounded, every edge worn off, every stone finished by time, the whole planet quietly declining toward sand. Birds will be deafening. Shadows will snap to your heels with a violence that makes you flinch. You will listen for the nine seconds in a hotel room and be frightened by their absence.

## The one thing

Pack a river pebble. Grey, ordinary, palm-sized, the kind you'd skip and not bother to pick up.

Because nothing here is rounded yet. Ten thousand square kilometers of arris and burr and fresh fracture, and your hand will spend every hour of every day meeting things that have not been finished, and it will not tell you what this is costing you until you're home. There is no softness on this plain except the fog and the sea, and the fog is not something you can hold.

At night, in the cot, with the speaker playing rain on leaves and the ground ticking underneath you and the pressure going through your sternum every nine seconds, you will put your thumb on that stone and rub it, and it will be the only thing within a hundred kilometers that time has already touched.

That's what it's for. Bring it. And when you meet a child on the Long Approach who has never in her life held anything that wasn't sharp, give her yours, and walk back to the tram with empty hands, and understand that you have just done the single most valuable thing a visitor can do here.
