# Preparing for Bay Six, Level −3

## First: decide whether you are going in at all

There is a legitimate visit that consists of standing in the doorway with the wheel half-turned, breathing the caramel-and-ozone, letting your eyes go dark, and leaving. You will get the smell, the pressure, the amber coal above your head, and — if you are patient and quiet for twenty minutes — the papery click of a leaf unfolding somewhere out in the black. Most of what Bay Six is can be had from the threshold, at almost no risk and almost no cost to the room.

Going deeper costs the room something. Nothing in there has ever been touched by an animal. Nothing in there scuffs out a footprint. You will be the first mouth to enter that hall in its entire history, and you will leave a record that outlasts your species' presence in this world. Decide, before you buy a single piece of kit, that the middle of the floor is worth that. If it is, go once, go well, and do not go back twice in a day.

---

## What will actually kill you, in order

**1. The heat, and specifically the stillness.** Forty-seven degrees is above your skin temperature. Air that hot does not cool you — it *heats* you, the way an oven heats a loaf. Every surface in there is also 47, so radiant heat comes at you from all six directions including the floor. Your only cooling channel is evaporation, and evaporation needs moving air. In still air your sweat pools, runs, drips onto the candy, and does nothing for you. You can be soaking wet and dying of heat stroke at the same time.

The good news is written on the floor. Sugar water has dried to *glass* in there. That means the air is thirsty — low relative humidity, probably a wet-bulb somewhere in the twenties. That is survivable, with water and airflow, for a bounded time. **Verify it, don't assume it.** Carry a sling psychrometer or a temp/RH probe and take a reading in the doorway and another at the midpoint. Wet-bulb above 32°C means your visit is measured in minutes. At 35 the room is unsurvivable regardless of fitness or water, and you go no further.

Budget realistically: at rest in that hall you will need to evaporate roughly half a litre an hour just to break even; walking, probing, turning a steel wheel, more like a litre to a litre and a half. A fit, acclimatised person with water, salt, and a fan can work for an hour. Without a fan, cut that in half.

**2. The sweet.** Assume every wet, tacky, glassy, syrupy thing in Bay Six is ethylene or diethylene glycol and treat it as lethal. It is not a mild poison. It tastes *good* — that is the entire trap. A mouthful of concentrated syrup can destroy your kidneys over the following two days, and the early symptoms are a pleasant drunkenness that arrives after you have already left. The exposure routes, in order of likelihood:

- You touch a leaf or a shard or a pipe joint, then eat, drink, wipe your mouth, or adjust a lip balm.
- You put a bottle mouthpiece down on the floor, or against your contaminated glove.
- Curiosity. The single most dangerous sentence you can think in there is *just a drop, to see*.
- Spray to the eye or an open cut during a pump run.

Rules: **nothing enters your mouth inside that door.** No food, no drink, no gum, no tasting the dust, no licking a fingertip to clean it. Water bottles live in a sealed outer pouch and are only handled with a decontaminated hand, outside. Double-glove; the outer pair comes off before you touch anything of your own. If you suspect you have swallowed anything at all — a smear, a splash, a grain of candy — you abort *immediately*, and your next destination is a world with a hospital and dialysis, not a scenic one. There is an effective treatment and it works best before you feel ill. Do not wait to feel ill.

**3. The dark, and what's underfoot.** You will be walking blind across a floor made of three substances: sound concrete, crazed glass with razor edges, and loose sharp rockfall. Somewhere at the low end there is a grating over four metres of nothing. Falls are the second-most likely way to get hurt, and a fall puts your bare palm onto fracture surfaces that have not been weathered in eleven years, in a room full of poison.

**4. The air you cannot see.** Sealed hall, three levels of rock, no ventilation, and eleven groves of non-photosynthetic plants that have been *respiring* in there for decades. They consume sugar and exhale carbon dioxide. There is no reason to assume the atmosphere is ordinary. Carry a meter: oxygen, CO₂, CO, H₂S, flammables. Oxygen below 19.5% or CO₂ above about 5,000 ppm: leave. The sump is a confined space inside a confined space; heavy gas pools there. Put your ear to the grating if you must, briefly, with your head up, and do not descend, do not lower a light into it, do not drop anything through it.

Also assume the pipe lagging elsewhere in the hall is asbestos-era mineral fibre, and the rock dust is alkaline and possibly siliceous. Wear a P100/FFP3. Never dry-brush, sweep, blow, or slap lagging. And a sealed room under three levels of rock accumulates radon; one visit is a small dose, a habit is not.

**5. The roof.** A slab already came down. The fracture above it opens a hair each year. Hard hat, chin strap, always. Do not go past the last grove, do not climb the debris pile, do not shelter under the slab's lip, do not wedge anything into it, do not shout or hammer.

**6. Live electricity in a hot wet room.** Breakers were racked out, but three things still have power: the amber lamp, the gantry's standby circuit, and the pump contactor. The green pinprick behind an open cabinet door is a warning, not an invitation. Ozone in the smell means something is arcing or corona-ing somewhere. Assume every cabinet, conduit, and rail is energised, insulation is heat-cooked and fifty years old, and the pipe is *not* a ground. One hand in your pocket when near anything electrical. Touch no cabinet. Do not open, close, operate, rack, or "make safe" anything.

Nothing in Bay Six will bite, sting, infect, chase, or ambush you. This is not the same as safe. The room is indifferent, warm, sweet, and entirely capable of killing you without ever noticing.

## Weeks before

- **Acclimatise.** Seven to fourteen days of deliberate heat exposure, an hour a day, working up a sweat. It expands plasma volume, lowers your heart rate at a given load, and makes your sweat less salty. It is the single highest-value preparation and it cannot be bought.
- **Get checked.** Cardiac fitness, kidney function (you are going somewhere with a nephrotoxin), and a review of your medications. Anticholinergics, antihistamines, some psychiatric drugs, diuretics, beta blockers, and stimulants all degrade heat tolerance or sweating. Alcohol the night before is a straight subtraction. Do not go dehydrated, hungover, sleep-deprived, or unwell.
- **Rehearse in the dark.** Spend an evening in a lightless room. Learn how long your eyes actually take (thirty to forty minutes for full rod adaptation, seconds to lose it). Learn what your mind does when there is nothing to look at.
- **Rehearse the wheel.** Find a heavy dogged hatch and work it. It takes both hands and grip strength, and grip strength is exactly what heat stroke takes away. Your exit must not depend on you being at your best.
- **Choose your next world now.** Before you go, know where you are going afterwards, and make sure it has an emergency department, dialysis, and a way to get there in under two hours. Write the words "possible glycol ingestion" on a card and put it in your pocket, because you may be too confused to say it.

## The kit

**Minimum, non-negotiable:**

- Hard hat with chin strap; clear anti-fog wraparound eye protection.
- P100/FFP3 respirator. Prefer an *unvalved* one near the groves — a valved mask vents your breath, and your breath is the most biologically loaded thing in the room. Valved for exertion, unvalved for close work.
- Nitrile gloves, doubled, plus a smooth synthetic outer work glove. No leather: it absorbs syrup and holds it against your skin, and it sheds organisms.
- New boots, bought for this visit, disinected, kept bagged until the door, bagged again on exit, never worn anywhere else. Puncture-resistant midsole, ankle support, shallow easily-wiped tread. Deep tread carries seeds and prints the floor.
- Smooth synthetic coveralls with hood and taped cuffs. Not a vapour-tight suit — that is a heat trap that will kill you faster than anything in the room. Accept that the coveralls are contaminated on exit and bag them.
- Three independent lights, minimum. Headlamp with a *very* dim red mode, a separate small warm light, and a backup in a sealed pouch. Keep spare cells off your body, in an insulated pouch — 47°C eats batteries.
- A long smooth probe: a synthetic rod or collapsed trekking pole, wiped clean, no shedding ferrule, lanyarded to your wrist.
- Closed-cell foam kneel pads. You will kneel to look at things. Kneel only on bare concrete, never on the amber plates.
- Water: 4 litres per person for a ninety-minute door-to-door, plus a separate litre of sterile saline for eye flushing. Electrolytes at roughly 500–1000 mg sodium per litre. Plain water alone, in volume, will give you hyponatraemia.
- A fan. Small, brushless, battery. This is not a comfort item; in still air it is the difference between sweat that cools you and sweat that drips.
- Sling psychrometer or temp/RH probe. Four-gas meter plus CO₂.
- A vibrating timer. Audible alarms destroy the thing you came for.
- Hard hat, gloves, probe, lights, meter — all lanyarded. **Empty your loose pockets.** Anything you drop in there stays where it fell, and in a century it is sealed under glass. A dropped bolt is a permanent artefact. So is a dropped lens cap. So are you.

**Strongly recommended:**

- Frozen gel packs or phase-change cooling in an insulated vest, worn *under* the coveralls. Two kilograms buys you real minutes. A frozen water bottle you can drink from and press to your neck is worth more than any gadget.
- Ingestible core-temperature pill with a reader, or at minimum a reliable thermometer and a buddy who uses it. Forehead readings in a 47°C room are fiction.
- A 50 m slick 2 mm cord as a tug line to your tender, kept off the floor as much as possible, retrieved on exit. Three levels of rock means no radio. Agree codes: two tugs *coming out*, three tugs *come to the door now*, continuous pulling *emergency*.
- A camera with a fast lens and a small tripod with padded feet, for long exposures. No flash — it wrecks your dark adaptation for half an hour and it is a light pulse in a place that has never had one.
- Waterproof synthetic notebook or a recorder. No cardboard, no paper, no wooden handles, nothing organic that sheds.

## The threshold

Put your cooling cache on the near side of the door: shade if there is any, a chair, ice, water, towels, the fan, a thermometer, and the decon kit. This is your ambulance. Do not carry it in.

Leave a **tender** outside. Their whole job is the clock, the wheel, and you. They do not come in after you without a plan; two people cooked in a hot dark hall is the standard shape of this accident. Agree a hard exit time before you touch the wheel and write it down where they can see it.

Put the back of your hand on the steel. Warm like a living thing is the baseline you were promised. **If it is hotter than that, something has changed and you do not open it.** The door is a diagnostic instrument; read it.

Open slowly. Both hands, steady, no slamming. The pressure will equalise and, for the first time in decades, dust will move. Do not dog it seated behind you — that is your only exit and you may come out weak. Leave it a turn from open, tender at the wheel, chock you can kick free. Do not oil the dogs. Oil is food, and there is nothing in there that eats.

Then **stand still in the doorway for twenty minutes.** Fan on, drinking, meter running, eyes adapting. This is not wasted time; it is the visit. Let the pressure note in your ears settle. Let the smell arrive in its layers — caramel, scalded milk, ozone, wet stone. Take your psychrometer reading. Learn the amber lamp's exact position relative to your body. Count the groves as they resolve out of the black. Do not step onto the floor until you can see the floor.

## Walking

Three rules, in this order.

**Never lose the amber.** It is a coal above the door and it is the only fixed thing you can see. If you use white light, you will lose it, and if your light then fails you are blind for thirty minutes in a room with glass underfoot and a hole at the low end. Keep one eye dark-adapted if you can; turn back to the amber every twenty paces; count your steps aloud in your head. Do not trust a compass near that much steel and live current.

**Probe before you load.** Tap, then step. Learn the three sounds: concrete is a dull thud, amber is a glassy tick, gravel is a crunch, and the sump is nothing at all. Your cane is your eyes. Shuffle rather than stride; never put a hand down without probing it first; never kneel on candy, because the plates will break and the edges are fresh.

**Walk the bare aisles.** Between the groves there are twenty, forty, sixty paces of clean swept concrete. That is your road. It is also the least disturbed surface, and your prints there will read for years. Choose one line and keep to it. Do not wander. Do not sightsee across a grove's root plate.

The make-up line runs the length of the hall at chest height and it is a perfect handrail. Use it as one — with a gloved fingertip, lightly, every few paces, not a continuous grip. Do not hang from it, lean on it, strap anything to it, wipe its beads away, or stress a joint. It is old steel under an aquifer's head and it is also the entire weather. A rupture would flood the hall with cold fresh water and end eleven lineages at once.

## Near the groves

Keep a metre. Do not breathe on a leaf. Do not touch one, even gently, even to see it give — your fingerprint is salt and oil on a surface with no wax, no cuticle defence, and no evolutionary memory of being touched. It will mark. The mark will still be there when the plant is glass.

Do not cut a stem to watch it weep. You would be opening a wound that cannot clot, in a room with no decomposers, and handing yourself a gloveful of nephrotoxin for a two-second novelty.

Manage your sweat. You are a hundred-watt heater and a litre-an-hour humidifier standing in a room whose whole climate is one sweating pipe. Your plume is a weather event. Do not loiter under leaves; do not let sweat drip onto the candy — fresh water dissolves glass that took a decade to set, and it carries your salt and your skin flora with it. Wipe into a cloth you carry out. Do not spit, do not blow your nose, do not urinate, do not leave a hair if you can help it. Hood up, mask on.

Understand the specific thing you are risking. It is not that you will trample a plant. It is that the reason there are no ants in a room full of sugar is that nothing here can digest it — and you might arrive carrying a single spore of an osmotolerant yeast or mould that can. There is no competition in there. No immune history. Eleven groves of paper-thin undefended tissue sitting in a warm bath of carbon. You could bring the first mouth into Bay Six on the cuff of a jacket, and it would eat the world in a decade, and no one would see it happen. Come from a clean place, not a farm or a compost heap or a cave. Shower. Fresh-dedicated clothing. Everything wiped, everything bagged. You cannot get to zero. Get as close as you can and keep the visit short.

## If the pump runs

You will not know when. A tick like a knuckle on a desk, then a deep shove through the pipes.

When it happens: step back from the pipe and *stay* back for the ninety seconds. Expect movement, water hammer, joints working, and No. 4 gland — which somebody meant to fix on a Thursday — weeping harder or spraying under pressure. Spray to the face means flush with your own saline for fifteen minutes and abort. Do not touch anything to "steady" it.

And know that you have just lost your cooling. For two days the only cold thing in the hall is warm and dry, the wet stripe fades, and the groves go slack at the edges. If you were relying on the pipe as a heat sink, that option is gone. If you came to hear the leaves, the pump will have masked them for a while. Wait, or come back another week — not another hour.

---

## The listening

This is why you came, so earn it properly.

Do it at the *end* of your traverse, on the way out, when you already know your route by cane and count. Reach the bare stretch equidistant from all eleven. Fan off. Lights off — all of them, including the standby on your chest. Sit or stand on bare concrete. Let your eyes finish adapting; the cherry bend on the north wall will appear only in peripheral vision and will vanish if you look at it, so look slightly away and let it come.

Set the vibrating timer for ten minutes. Ten is enough. You will want an hour; heat strain is cumulative and the room does not care how moved you are.

Then be quiet, and hear it: a creak, a papery click, a tissue unfolding, from eleven directions, at intervals of about a minute. Also your own heart, your own joints, the whine your ears make when there is nothing to hear. In hot absolute dark the mind manufactures company. Expect a sense of presence, especially near the mess cabin. Name it when it comes, do not investigate it, do not go looking for the source of a sound alone. Nothing is there. Nothing has ever been there.

If you find yourself thinking *I could stay a bit longer* — that thought is the heat talking, and it is also the room's only seduction. It is warm, it smells like a kitchen, nothing in it wants to hurt you, and every instinct you have says this is a safe place. It is not. It is forty-seven degrees and there is no air. Leave while leaving is easy.

---

## The mess cabin

Photograph everything. Touch nothing.

Do not sit on the cot. Do not drink from the mug — the ring in the bottom is the same substance as the floor and the leaves, and it is dried into a shape that invites a mouth. Do not try on the boots. Do not take the log book. Read it, photograph the last entry, and put it back exactly where it lay, at the exact angle, because in a room with no weather the angle is a record.

Forty-one chalk tallies. **Leave it at forty-one.** Do not add a mark, do not initial the wall, do not write your name, do not finish somebody's sentence.

And do not do the Thursday job. The temptation to close a weeping gland, to tidy a valve, to leave the place better than you found it, is the most dangerous impulse you will have in there — more dangerous than the tasting, because it feels like kindness. That hall is a dying equilibrium with a finite number of eleven-days left in it. Any repair you improvise resets a clock you do not understand, and the gantry's standby circuit — four metres a year, carrying the only pollinator this ecosystem has ever had on its underside — is one switch away from stopping forever. Do not clean the root mat off the crane. Do not lubricate it. Do not hang a lamp from it. Do not ride it.

If you think the pump should be saved, that is a decision for people who can commit to a century. Record it, report it, and take your hands off it.

---

## Leaving

Decon *outside* the door, at your cache, with your own materials. Outer gloves off first, into a bag. Wipe boots and coveralls with damp cloths — no dry brushing, which puts the dust into your lungs and the air — then bag them. Alcohol-wipe the probe, the camera, the meter, the pads. Everything goes into sealed bags: gloves, wipes, tape, cloths, tissues, hair. Nothing is rinsed under the pipe. Nothing is washed in the sump. Nothing is left, including the water you used.

Retrieve your tug line. Count your gear against the list you made at the door. If something is missing, you have a hard decision and you should make it before you go in: **you do not go groping back into the dark for a dropped object.** Let it be a fossil.

Turn the wheel until it seats. Warm steel, both hands, the way it was left.

If you took a shard of amber — and it is the most tempting object in any world, tea-coloured, with pollen sealed under it that fell for absolutely no one — label it POISON, seal it, and never let it near a mouth, a child, or an animal. Better: photograph it against the amber lamp and put it back where you found it, in the same orientation, on the same plate.

---

## After

Watch yourself for two days. Drunkenness, nausea, abdominal or flank pain, rapid breathing, reduced or absent urine — these are glycol, they are delayed, and they are an emergency in a world with a hospital. Go early. Go before you are sure.

Watch for the quieter heat injuries too: headache, cramps, a day of feeling wrung out, dark urine. Rehydrate with salt, not just water. Do not make a second visit that day.

And expect the residue. Bay Six is not frightening, which is precisely why it stays with people. You will find yourself, weeks later in some loud bright world, listening for a sound at the edge of hearing that is not there — a papery click, from eleven directions, of something enormous and patient and defenceless getting very slightly larger in a room where nothing will ever come to eat it.

The last thing to understand is this: nothing there will scavenge you. If you die in Bay Six you will not rot; there is nothing to rot you. You will dry, and candy, and be sealed under the next layer of glass, and the groves will lean in toward the pipe, and the pump will run for ninety seconds every eleven days for as long as it has left.

Go carefully. Come out. Leave the tally at forty-one.
