# Preparing for The Vat

## First, fix the picture in your head

The description is honest and it will still mislead you, because it is written in the language of baths. Steps, freeboard, warm water, a room the size of a small chapel, light like a summer floor. Every one of those features is a trap for an ordinary human body.

What you are actually visiting is a **hot, gas-generating, confined-space work site with a single wellhead-sized opening, an overhead environment, an unbreathable atmosphere above the waterline, and a water temperature above the temperature at which your body can shed heat.** In any world with industrial safety law, this is a permit-required confined space that also happens to be a dive. Wine vats kill people this way. So do fermentation pits and lime kilns and the bottom of silos. The Vat is that, dressed as a swimming pool, and lit like a cathedral.

Go in with the wrong frame and the room will kill you by letting you do the natural thing.

## The two waterlines

This is the single idea to carry down the steps.

The room is flooded twice. The lower flood is water: 2.5 metres, warm as blood, clear as glass, sterile. The upper flood is air that is not air — carbon dioxide coming out of solution across fifty square metres of degassing water, pooling in the freeboard, heavy, invisible, sweetish, and thickest at the far end where the shaft's draught never reaches. It has a surface, like water does. That surface is somewhere around the third step. It moves with the weather, with the spring, with the three-day belch cycle, with the season's accretion on the weir.

Between those two waterlines there is a band of nothing you can breathe. Above the upper one, on the top step and out through the lid, there is ordinary air. Below the lower one, there is water, which you also cannot breathe, but which will not poison you in three breaths.

**The breathable place is the drowned place.** Understand what that does to your instincts. Every reflex you have when you are hot, dizzy, and frightened — *go up, get your face in the air, get to the light* — points directly into the gas. A person who floats here dies floating. A person who treads water at the surface to have a look around dies having a look around. A snorkel is a device for breathing the air at the surface, and here that is the one thing you must never breathe.

And the masons' lamp is a coarser instrument than they think. A candle flame dies when oxygen falls to roughly sixteen percent, which in a room displaced by CO₂ means something like a fifth of the atmosphere is already carbon dioxide. Air with four percent CO₂ incapacitates you; five or six percent is a medical emergency in minutes; ten percent takes you down without much ceremony. **"Flame full" does not mean safe. "Flame small" means the third step is already lethal, and the masons have been standing on the fourth.** Respect their ten-minute shift rule — that was bought with bodies. Do not respect their gas test.

## Choose your visit honestly

**Tier one — the lid.** Most of what this place *is* is available with your feet dry. Prop the lid, lie on the paving with your head over the hole and your body back in the cool air, and look down through 2.5 metres of mineral-clear water at a floor made of moving light. This is the real sight. The caustic net is a floor phenomenon, and you can see the entire floor from above. Bring polarised glasses to kill the surface glare, or a glass-bottomed viewing tube you dip a hand's depth and take away with you. Wear boots. Feel the hum through your palms and your breastbone. Listen to the forty-minute slurp and knock of the sump. Stay twenty minutes. Leave. **Nobody has ever needed to go into the water to have seen The Vat.**

**Tier two — the top steps.** Head and shoulders above the lid rim, hands on the warm ashlar, ten minutes at most, metered, tended, no descent below the second step. This gets you the smell, the heat on your face, the roar at full volume, the fern in the shaft joints, the algae velvet on the rim. It is enough for almost everyone.

**Tier three — the water.** This is a supplied-air dive with a surface tender, a rigged retrieval system, and a hard ten-minute clock. If you cannot assemble that, you are not visiting the water. There is no casual version. There is no breath-hold version. It is only 2.5 metres and eleven paces and you will feel absolutely fine right up until you don't, and if you black out shallow your body floats up into the gas and the person who leans in after you becomes the second body.

## Weeks before

Heat-acclimatise properly — a fortnight of daily heat exposure, not a sauna the morning of. You will be immersed in water warmer than your skin, with no evaporative cooling available anywhere in the room, above or below the surface. Your body has exactly one heat sink, which is the cool air on the terrace, and you have to go back to it on a schedule.

Get cardiovascularly fit enough that ten minutes of gentle finning and hauling is trivial, because your metabolic heat is the difference between a working shift and a medical event. Sort your ears now; do not go with a cold, and leave the decongestants alone. Check your medications for anything that blunts sweating or thermoregulation. If you have any history of photosensitive seizures, the caustic net — white fire opening and closing faster than you can follow, in a room with no dimness anywhere — is a genuine provocation, and you should look at the plain plaster or not look down at all.

Find the masons. This is a workshop, not a wonder. It has owners, a production schedule, and a product. Ask permission, offer payment or labour, and ask three specific questions: **When was the weir last cut? When did the room last gulp? When did the rains come upslope?** Their answers date the site more usefully than any calendar.

## Kit, and the rule about iron

Bring:

- **A four-gas meter** — CO₂ by infrared, oxygen, hydrogen sulphide, with a sampling tube long enough to reach the waterline while you stay on the terrace. Sample at three heights: the lid rim, the third step, and just above the water at the far end. The gas is stratified; the number at your face is not the number at your knees.
- **Surface-supplied breathing air**, blower or compressor on the terrace with its intake upwind and well above the ground, hose down through the lid, full-face mask, plus a bailout cylinder small enough to pass a wellhead. Side-mount if you must carry gas — a back-mounted twin will not fit the hole, and that constraint has killed people who only discovered it at the top of the steps.
- **A thin full skin suit, gloves, hard-soled boots.** Not a wetsuit; a wetsuit here is a slow oven. You need the suit for the arrises, which will cut you, and the dogtooth calcite on every submerged surface, which is a field of needles.
- **Dark-coloured everything.** In a lime-white light-box, black gloves and a black guideline are the only things your eyes can find. Your own hands will otherwise disappear into glare.
- **A tensioned guideline** from a stake outside, through the lid, to the top step, to a finished pier — hung, not laid on the floor. Never let go of it.
- **Tinted goggles or a tinted mask** for above water; polarised lenses for looking down from the lid.
- **A retrieval harness with a dorsal point, a rated line, and a tripod or pulley over the lid.** Rig it before anyone descends. Vertical extraction of a limp person through a wellhead is not something you improvise.
- **Ear protection and a hood.** Accept that you will lose what little directional hearing you had.
- Cool drinking water with electrolytes, shade, a clock, waterproof dressings, antiseptic.

Do **not** bring: iron or plain steel that can be dropped, loose jewellery, sunglasses you have not tethered, a phone in a pocket, a snorkel, or anything at all you are willing to lose. The one iron wedge on the floor is bleeding a rust stain that the accretion is already sealing under glass, and the masons noticed. Metal stains the product.

And here is the reason to be fanatical about it. **Nothing here decays. Things here get included.** A dropped knife does not become litter; it becomes a void and a rust-flaw inside a block that will be split, dressed, and set as a lintel over somebody's door in a town built out of these rooms. It will be a hidden defect in a load-bearing stone, found in thirty years, at night, under load. Count your kit going in and count it coming out, out loud, like a surgical tally. Hang everything; let nothing rest on the floor.

## When to go

Late in the dry season, before the rains' pulse arrives upslope weeks later and swells the throat, thickens the plume, raises the level and the gas. Soon after the masons have re-cut the weir, when the horizon is knife-sharp and dead level and the scribed line on the plaster still tells the truth — because by the following autumn that line is two fingers under water and the float-gauge rod may be crusted solid in its bracket, reading whatever it read the day it seized.

Between ten and two, when the shaft's beam is on the piers and the floor. Never at dusk. The room dims to a bright grey box with no visible source and then to nothing, and you do not want to be climbing wet crusted steps in a room you can no longer see, with the gas invisible and the roar unchanged.

Ask about the gulp. The pocket that gathers over three days and lets go with a slow enormous swallow you feel in the sternum is not a curiosity; it is a pressure event and probably a gas surge, and it can lift the invisible waterline without warning. Do not be at or near the surface when it goes.

## The terrace

The hill is three hundred years of offcuts, terraced and planted over. It is made ground: voids, settling courses, sharp spoil, a fig root working through joints. Walk the masons' paths. Wear boots.

**Do not step on the three plugged oculi.** Those are stone lids over a 2.5-metre drop into hot water and poison, and nobody has inspected their undersides. Do not stand on the main lid either unless someone who owns it says it will take you. Stand beside it.

Rig the lid so it cannot close: prop, wedge, lash to a stake, and a second person whose whole job is that hole. The hand-slot is on top. There is no opening it from below. If it falls while you are in the freeboard, you are in a sealed hot box filling with gas, and your airway is a slab.

Then measure. Then mark the invisible waterline — lower the meter or the lamp on a cord, find where the flame dies, tie a ribbon on your line at that height. Now you know where the second surface is. Re-measure after any gulp, any weather change, any hour.

Brief where you can hear each other, which is to say outside, because inside no one will ever hear anything again.

## The ten minutes

Going down will feel wonderful for about ninety seconds. The water is silky, the light is unbelievable, the net of white fire runs over your gloves and opens and closes and you will want to hold your hands still and watch, and you cannot, because it never holds still.

Then your ears go under and the room stops being outside you. The roar loses direction and distance and arrives inside your skull, from every side, from behind your eyes. There is no up in the sound. In a room with no shadows and no dimness, there is very little up in the light either — you will misjudge the piers by a metre and reach for a wall that isn't there. Bright, loud, hot, and directionless: this is a sensory environment engineered by accident to remove every cue you navigate by. That is why you have a line, and why you never let go of it.

The heat does not feel like heat at first. It feels like being held. Around minute six or seven you notice your pulse in your teeth, and your breathing has gone shallow and quick without your permission, and there is a sweetness at the back of your throat that is not the water. Around minute ten your judgment starts to go, and it goes *before* your body does — you will feel competent and cheerful and want another minute.

Your tender has the clock. **You come out at eight minutes because the tender says so, not because you agree.** Ten is a working shift. Fifteen is an incident.

Keep clear of the north throat: a thigh-wide jet under enough head to hum stone will pin you. Keep clear of the south sump: it self-siphons every forty minutes and a siphon that can knock the vault can take a limb or your hair. Do not touch the weir, the sluice slab, the float-gauge, or the proud boss on the unfinished fourth pier, which is a knife of stone at exactly the height of your face. Do not fin near the floor. The grey floc in the south corners is ankle-deep and one careless kick turns the whole light-box into a glowing white blank — no walls, no floor, no up, roaring inside your head, at blood temperature. Hand over hand, slowly, along the line.

And the exit is the deadliest part. From the moment your mouth leaves the water until your head is above the lid rim, **you do not breathe anything the room offers you.** Keep the mask on and the supply in. Go up the steps slowly, three points of contact, on the handline, with the tender talking you up — because the heat will make you want to bolt, and the treads are crusted and slick with algae where the condensation runs, and every arris will cut you.

Then move back from the hole into shade. Do not sit on the rim.

## If someone goes down

The rule is absolute and it must be agreed before anyone descends: **the tender never leans in, never climbs down, never puts their head below the lid rim to see.** Two bodies is the standard outcome of a confined-space gas rescue, and the second one is always the rescuer.

Haul. Mechanical advantage, from outside, on the line already rigged to the harness already worn. Scream for the masons. Get air moving. If you cannot haul them clear in the first thirty seconds, you are recovering, not rescuing, and you should behave accordingly rather than adding yourself to the block.

## After

Cool down gradually in shade, drink, keep drinking. Watch for delayed heat illness for hours. Check yourself for cuts — the water is sterile, which means your wounds will be infected by your own skin flora and nothing else, and the chalk and lime will make them sting and heal slowly. Rinse your kit in fresh cool water immediately; anything you leave in that room overnight is on its way to becoming stone, and a line left a day will be stiff enough to flay your palms.

Note your ears. The masons shout when they get out because they have temporary threshold shift, and they have it every day, and eventually it stops being temporary. If you are shouting on the terrace, you have taken a dose. Do not take another one this week.

## Manners, and the long view

Take nothing. Not a dogtooth cluster from the weir, not a chip of the crisp corduroy chiselling, not a tuft of the maidenhair. There are four tufts. That is the entire fern population of this world's strangest garden, and it is rooted in a building.

Leave nothing — not sunscreen, not a handprint, not a boot tread in the floc. The erosion here runs backwards. You will not wear this place down; this place will record you. Your palm on the plaster will plate and stay. Your footprint in the grey drifts will fill and harden and be a fossil of a visitor who did not ask.

If you want the deepest version of this place, it is not underwater. It is a year later, standing in a street in a town built out of the rooms this room filled, with your hand on a lintel, feeling the pier-holes that were cast into it because someone built them into the void. Go and find the hill of broken shells. Go and find, if the masons will tell you which block, the wall that has a mallet inside it.

That is the safe way to be inside The Vat. Everyone who visits it properly ends up doing it that way — standing in the air, in the cool, in a town, touching the outside of a room that was once bright and roaring and full of light, and is now holding up somebody's roof.
