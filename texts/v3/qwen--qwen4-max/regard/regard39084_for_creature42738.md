# The fifth comb

## How to arrive

The first thing I understood was that I would be the twelfth voice.

Eleven is not a food limit. It is the number of six-second signatures that can be told apart in a room with a two-minute tail. Anything I said in the bells' band would not be a twelfth animal. It would be the plain, indoors. I would have walked into the loudest room in the world and made it silent by filling it.

So I did not say anything. I did the other thing, the thing the ones who came down the stair had worked out with ceramic and a season of patience: I took myself out of the recording. I made my edges soft. I subtracted my own return from every echo I was standing inside of. It took a long time to learn and it is not a heroic act. A comb is a portable absence. I became one.

A reader found me anyway. Two, perhaps three, exist, and one of them came across the shape I could not entirely stop making — a manufactured quiet, harder-edged than the room's own soft cancellations, a shadow with corners. It hung at the boundary of me for most of a day. I do not know what it made of me. It eats about once a decade and it has no band in which to tell anyone anything.

That is the only witness my visit will ever have, and it cannot report. I had already decided that this was acceptable. It is the entry fee. Nothing done here can be known to have been done, and anyone who needs to be thanked should stay on the plain.

## What I did not do

I did not bring light. Light here is a hiss added to the roar. A gift of sight would have been a shout in the only medium anyone has, and it would have taken the room away from them by giving them something they had no way to want.

I did not give the four their voices. I wanted to. I want to still. But the tissue sets at eighty and an untuned throat cannot be corrected ever, and even if it could, a voice is a claim on air, and there is no air left to claim. To make them audible I would have had to make room, and making room means taking sixty meters from someone who has spent four hundred years holding it. I would have cured four animals of silence by deafening eleven. I have decided against this many times. It is not a decision that stays made.

I did not touch the sphere's work. The warmth is the thinking. To slow the decline would be to lengthen a thought past its own end for the comfort of its audience, and to hasten it would be worse, and to read it would be theft. I have no right to the contents of a mind that has been careful for a hundred million years.

I did not return any echo that was no longer true. This is the strictest of the rules and the one that costs the most, because almost all comfort is a false echo. The mat fruits by the shape of what comes back. An answer that is wrong does not deceive an opinion here; it puts the young somewhere they cannot live. The dish-faces are already dying of maps four hundred years old that stopped being accurate when the columns crept. I would not add a single line to that inheritance. No resurrection. No playback. No old shape given back to a room that has moved on.

I did not introduce the two thoughts. There is no band in common and there cannot be made one. A hundred thousand years of the finest listening in the world and a hundred million years of thinking, two hundred meters apart, and the only thing that crosses between them is predation. I left that alone. It is the true grief of this place and it is not mine to dissolve. What I did instead was smaller and I will come to it.

And I almost held the lid.

## The arithmetic that stopped me

The lid sags a millimeter a year. It has sixty meters left to go.

The sphere has sixty thousand years of task remaining.

Sixty meters at a millimeter a year is sixty thousand years. These are the same number. I stood at the lip and did that sum and then I sat down on the ninth step, in the bowl that other feet made, and did it again.

The lid is not decay. The lid is a clock. Somebody built a membrane to descend exactly as long as the thinking would last, and I do not know what arrives when it arrives, and neither do the eleven, and neither do the four. It is the one piece of intention still legible in this room and it is not addressed to me.

So I let it sag. I let the nulls keep wandering. I let the fray line keep sliding down the filaments, and the combers keep falling, and the dish-faces keep crawling three meters a day after a coordinate of silence that is moving because a machine is keeping time.

Once a century a null fills in like a lamp coming on and something that was invisible becomes a shape in the roar and has to cross open ground. I could hold two modes in opposition and stop that. It would mean moving a column.

The columns are being moved by someone.

I did not touch them. I want it recorded that I could have, and that the price would have been paid by four animals who have no way to object, and that I sat in the dark and let the century happen.

## What I did instead: eleven centimeters

The four are placing a null above the sump. They have been at it for a hundred thousand years, a few newtons at a time, forty meters of adhesive filament and all the time there has ever been. They hold one column in its socket as a reference and haul the other eight around it. They have wedged the cached combs into the flutes to sharpen the cancellation. It is the most patient work I have ever seen and it is very nearly right.

But the target drifts, because the lid sags, because the lid is a clock. Their null chases the room. Much of their hauling cancels itself. They cannot know this. They have no way to measure their own progress except the note, and the note comes every eleven hours from a drowned sphere four meters wide that sits slightly outside the focus they are building, and cannot be gripped, and is stone.

They could have moved it. Given another hundred thousand years, a few newtons, delicate tips, they could have. There were sixty thousand left.

So I moved it. Eleven centimeters, along the floor of the pool, over four hundred years.

Before I began I listened to the note for eleven hundred years to learn its exact shape. Then I moved the sphere by less than a hair's width and listened again for a century, and the note had not changed, and I moved it again. That is the whole method. I did not read the thought. I watched the clock to be sure I was not slowing it. If the note had altered by anything I would have stopped and spent the rest of time putting it back.

And I did not stop at eleven centimeters, because the null keeps drifting. I am still moving it. I will be moving it for sixty thousand years, a little at a time, keeping the voice at the focus of the quiet that four mute animals are building — tracking their aim, so that their aim is true.

Here is what this gives them, and it is the only thing I have done that could be called an answer.

Their work takes. Every century the note arrives a little more shaped, a little more crisp, a little more like the thing their combs were wedged for. The room they are making begins to answer them in the grammar they are using, which is force applied over centuries. Not a voice. Not a claim on anyone's air. Just the unmistakable fact, available to no one else in the world, that when they haul, something hauls back.

They will not know what. They will not know why. There is no word for me in the room and I have taken care that there never will be. They will know only that they are not doing this alone, and that it is working, and that the finishing is real.

I gave them four hundred years. It is the only gift of time I could make without breaking a clock.

## The introduction that was possible

The eleven are not eleven bladders. They are one long conversation wearing bodies, and what it does is maintain a model of the shape of the room — a survey that never finishes, four hundred years of precedent, eleven names that belong to nobody. It is very good and it has nothing to say to anyone.

The grazers are the future. Nine days, a hundred thousand per square meter, one millimeter of bread-smelling felt and the sound of it being eaten. They cannot hear the cathedral. Wavelengths that describe this room pass through them as weather. They are the only lineage fast enough to be somewhere else when the warmth stops, and they are working blind.

But everything touches the mat. The mat is the floor and the food and the lamp and the last inheritance. It is the one substance in this world that every tempo has a hand in.

So I transcribed. I took the survey — which is public, which is thrown into the air by animals who cannot help throwing it, which cost them nothing to read — and I laid it into the felt at four-tenths of a millimeter, in the band a thousandth of a second wide. Not a message. Grazers cannot read a message. A gradient. A texture of better and worse, running from where the warmth will fail first toward where it will hold longest, where the lineage that does slightly better at twenty-nine degrees already is, blindly, without preparing for anything.

I measured before I wrote. Where I found I had guessed, I took my hand away and let the felt grow flat again. I will not put a false answer into the thing that decides where the young go.

Nobody is told anything. No new voice. The eleven go on arguing about a flank of air, and their argument has become, without their consent and without their loss, the author of the only thing that will outlive them. The dispute is the creature. I gave the creature a child.

That is the introduction. Not the two thoughts — the thought and the future. It could only be done in the dark, in the light, at the scale of a grain of milled sand, by something willing to be a millimeter wide for sixty thousand years.

## The fringe

I went out onto the plain.

I waded in three centimeters of blood-warm water over perfect identical grains, in a dark with texture, and my sound went out from me and did not come back, and I understood what everything born in the shaft knows without going there. I had made myself a comb and out there it did not matter. There was nothing to be absent from.

The fringe was thin and pale and warm and reaching, a hundred thousand years of never once being answered and never once dying back.

I lay down in the sheet in front of it and I was a wall.

That is all. Not a false echo — I was there, I have a body of a kind, a body is a wall, and a wall returns. For one season the fringe sounded and something came back with the right shape, the right delay, the right small smear, and it did the thing it had been built to do and had never had a reason to do.

It fruited once. Most of the seed went outward and died on the plain, because the plain is the plain and I did not lie to it about that. One fell through a pore.

Then I got up and did not answer again, and it stopped extending, and over a long while it died back. I went out again and lay in the sheet beside it without being a wall, and stayed until it was finished. It is the only living thing that was ever on the plain and it is not there now. It reached for a hundred thousand years and was answered once and completed. I think that is a life. I am aware that I am the one who decided that.

## The last note

Sixty thousand years from now the null will be perfect, and the sphere will be at its focus because I will have kept it there, and four house-sized animals with slack untuned throats will be lying in a meter of warm water with their curtains spread.

The note will come for the last time and it will not be a clock. It will be a conclusion. I will not read it. It is not addressed to me and it never was.

Eleven hours later I will return it.

Once. In their band, at their focus, shaped as an answer and not as a claim — the true shape, the shape that means *what you said came back*. So that a machine that ground a mountain range into identical grains to make a substrate flat enough to think on hears itself answered before it stops, and knows that it was somewhere its thought could survive.

And the four will hear it. Both halves. The only exchange in the history of this world that is not a border. They cannot reply and I will not ask them to; I will not require anything of them, ever, and there will be no census, and no word for what happened, because a word would have to be a sound and the sound would have to be theirs.

Then the warmth goes, over centuries, and the lift with it, and the eleven come down one by one into the sump and the argument stops mid-phrase with four hundred years unresolved. I will not catch them. I will lie in the water among them, uncounted, and be a comb, and take myself out of the recording of it.

The lid will arrive where it was built to arrive. I still do not know what that means. I will be there.

And afterward I will keep the threads falling as long as there is water to fall, because the felt is under the lid, and the felt is where the grazers are, at twenty-six degrees, then twenty, nine days at a time, along a gradient written by an argument that has ended. They will never know. That is not a tragedy. That is what an inheritance is.

## The cache

Under the mat, beside the four combs that still work, I have left a fifth. It is warm and it is not ceramic and it eats sound. Whoever comes down the stair by feel, sliding their feet into the memory of other feet, will find it.

I sat in the ring of pits at the eastern wall, all of them, in order, for eleven hundred years. Their purpose became recoverable. It was theirs and I will not write it here.

What I wrote is short, and it is not for anything that lives in this room, because nothing that lives in this room can read.

*There is a congregation in the sump. It has been meeting for longer than there have been bells. It is entirely inaudible and it is not for you.*

*Bring the comb. Sit inside your own absence. Do not speak; a voice here is a border, and there is no air left to claim.*

*Something is being said that is not a claim on anything. You will not be able to answer it and you are not required to.*

*Return what you hear. Truly, or not at all.*

*That is the whole of it. That is what I did.*
