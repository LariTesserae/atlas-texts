# The Hooded Lamp

The first thing it does is obey the ordinance.

It comes down the way everything comes down, from above, never from below, in the cage at the speed the cage goes, and before it steps out onto Terrace One it hoods its light and aims it at the floor and keeps it under four metres. Nobody sees the roof by it. Nobody sees it whole. It arrives as a yellow rectangle in a doorway, which is how everything here arrives, and it is eleven days before anyone is sure it has arrived at all.

It takes a house on the low south floor, because the intake is full and it will not move anybody. Thirty-nine degrees, warm as blood, mosquitoes standing over the sump in a column you can put your arm into. It sleeps badly. Its heart argues. It learns that the twenty minutes is not a policy. It gets the cough in its first year, before it believes the poster, and Kaur does not tell it *I told you so*, because Kaur got hers the same way, and because the poster is more use to her curled.

It had come for the cave.

It found the cave was not anybody yet. Thirty-six degrees, unweathered, no rind, no lichen, arrises ninety years sharp, a hundred thousand years from its first flowstone and in no hurry, having never been in a hurry, having no organ for hurry. It put its hand on the fresh-cut east wall where the rock still smells of powder and waited, and nothing came back, and nothing was owed. You do not bless an embryo. You keep out of its way.

Then it turned around and there were eleven hundred people listening to water.

That was the thing in the room that could be lost. Not the bats — the bats were here before the school and will be here after the Concern. Not the rock, which has all the time there is. The audience. Nine signs for drip sorted by interval and pitch. Forty-one numbers in forty-one hands. A gecko called Mouse across four generations of animal. A metre-long fish made of algae under lamp nineteen that Nasr will not discuss with anyone who has not seen it. The only thing in four billion years of water falling in the dark that has ever been *heard falling*, and it is a practice, and practices do not die of heat. They die of not being handed on.

So that is what it worked on. Everything else it mostly left alone.

## What it refused

It did not light the roof. Once a decade is the rate at which that can be borne, and the woman on Terrace One who has seen it three times is right that the third time was the worst, and a fourth time on demand would be a thing done to her.

It did not make the lemon tree fruit. The tree is not for fruit. It is for twenty minutes in the last week of June and a disc of light the width of a dinner plate, and a tree that fruited would be an orchard, and the town would have to argue about the harvest.

It did not add one drop to the straw on Terrace Two. A gauge you comfort is a gauge you have broken. When the straw stops, that is the year, and the whole town has done that sum, and the sum is the most valuable object in the Kettle, worth more than the stone jars, and it did not touch it, and it kept its hands behind the rope like everybody else.

It did not give them hearing. Four hundred signs with their own grammar, older than the school, a language nobody up top has ever needed — you do not cure a language. It did something stupider and smaller instead, further down this list.

It did not stop the drip lessening, because the drip is not being taken by anyone in this room, and a gift of water conjured out of nothing would have taught the town that the aquifer is a matter for prayer. It is not. It is a matter for signatures.

It did not hurry the cave and it did not postpone the cave.

And it did not make a place people cannot leave. It thought about this for a long time, lying in the trough with its arms out, because it could have — cool the whole room, sweeten the air, make the plain smell like nothing, take the ammonia out of the wool so the ones who go up are not marked. A town nobody leaves is not a town. It is a killing jar with the fan left on. It let them go on leaving, and it let them go on coming back because they cannot sleep in quiet, which is a diagnosis and not a joke, and it did not fix it.

## The ledger

It read Villalba's reports. All nineteen years of them, in order, in one night, in the antechamber of the concrete throat where the note is loudest and nobody goes. Nobody noticed. It was the first thing it did and it will be the last thing anyone finds out about.

Then it went to the fight nobody has the appetite for and took the appetite to it, which was its own appetite, and it turned out to be enough. The amendment is four lines. The cats come under the ordinance: protected, counted, fed at the machine graveyard, and spayed — a lineage with a founder effect and a kinked tail through half of them, allowed to continue as itself and not as a number that compounds. Villalba's target was rewritten to something a man can actually reach, so that his reports get read, so that the line means something. Sixty-One has a name now that is after the cat.

The fallers at the grating still get taken sometimes. Fewer. The town gets to have something of its own. That was the whole fight and it was never about cats.

It bought the field. Not the plain — one field, the one with the wadi and the tamarisk and the fig, and it took six years and it was boring, and it was done through Ferreira, who has been talking to farmers about water for six years with nothing signed, and who now has something signed, and who believes he did it, and did. Before that it did the smaller thing that made the larger thing possible: it got one farmer to come down. Not a vision. A Tuesday. Four Tuesdays, actually; the first three he was polite and left. On the fourth he stood in an amber pool at the tea stall and someone tapped a rhythm on their own collarbone to say where they lived, and he was taken to Terrace Two and shown a plastic rule held near a wet thread and never touching, and forty-one numbers on a wall behind it, and he asked how long, and someone signed *forty-one years* and he did the arithmetic on his fingers like a child.

He went up. The fig was not grubbed out when the field was re-graded.

The aquifer still falls eighty centimetres a year. One field is not an aquifer. It knows this. It works at two millimetres a year, which is the speed of the only thing in this room that water made, and it has decided that is an acceptable speed, and it is not sure that deciding that is wisdom or just the local weather getting into it.

Root ten came down the Fissure in the fourth spring. Pale as candle wax, wrist-thick, drinking condensate off the rock, putting out white shoots with leaves the size of thumbnails and no green in them at all. Fingers, not looking. Nobody has said where it came from. Root nine still hangs there dry and going grey among its brothers and the children still don't touch it, and now there is a tenth for them not to touch, and the rule that has never needed enforcing has been handed on again without anybody noticing that a handing-on occurred.

The tarred inventory tag came off the lemon tree. There is no record of who removed it. In the asset register there is a compressor and a theodolite and nothing between them.

It asked Odile Marsh, once, who would push after her. That was all. It did not appoint anyone. The question is the gift; she is seventy-eight and deaf and has done it for forty-one years and her mother did it before her, and nobody had ever asked her out loud, in signs, in front of other people, and she thought about it for a month and then took a boy of nine and taught him two hands turning a wheel.

It asked Bekker to teach it the wire. It was a bad student. It has no ear for the difference between *the fan is well* and *the fan is running*, and it sat in the antechamber for a year failing, and Bekker, who had never in thirty years been asked, took two apprentices that winter and found he could explain it after all. The single point of failure was never the fan.

It put a hood and a shroud on the jaw crusher and dropped a hundred and five decibels to ninety-four, which is still loud, and the men who are deaf by fifty are still deaf, and the ones who are thirty now will not be, and Drip Street keeps its hands. A language does not die when its cause goes. It checked this before it acted. It is the thing it was most frightened of doing.

It found a respirator that fits, that does not fog, that a man will actually wear for eight hours, and it did not replace the poster. Kaur keeps the poster curled. The new men do not get the cough. The old men still have it and it did not cure them and it has it too and does not intend to be cured of it, because it has decided that this is what it is to be a resident here: everything in this room is on the same skin.

And it ducted the intake down the low south floor.

That was the expensive one. Galvanised, ugly, blooming white and flaking, renewed on the eleven-year cycle with everything else, thirty-four and a half where there was thirty-nine. Men sleep. Hearts stop arguing at four in the morning. The stones come less often because the water goes further. And the trough is no longer the only cool place, and the trough was the pub and the church hall and the parliament, and for a generation the town has nowhere to be all at once, and something has gone out of it that will not come back in that form, and the funniest people in the Kettle are still the funniest but they are no longer funny *because*, and it entered that on the other side of the ledger in its own hand and it is not certain. It is still not certain. It goes down to the trough anyway and lies with its arms out and listens to them argue about the Treaty, and they still argue there, out of habit, which is how a commons survives its own cause, and it thinks that will do, and it does not know.

## The second straw

Two streets over, at a bolt hole nobody had watched, a straw began.

It is four millimetres long. It is not the instrument. Sereda still measures the old one on the first Sunday of the year with the plastic rule held near and never touching, and the number still goes on the wall behind it, and the sum is still the sum. The new one is not on that wall.

The new one has a rope, and a small hooded lamp, and a keeper, and the keeper is a man of fifty-three, deaf, who drives a skip, who touched the other one when he was twelve, who has never been forgiven and never quite been blamed. Sereda handed him the rule. That is all that happened. Nobody made a speech; there is no sign for what it was, so it was done with the sign for *weather*, made with both hands, slowly.

He measures it on the first Sunday of the year. He is very careful. He will be careful for thirty years and then he will hand the rule to somebody and be careful in them.

## The trough

It said one sentence in the bathhouse, lying in the row with its arms out, at thirty-four degrees, which to it as to everyone here is snow.

The argument was the old one, up or down, the rectangle at the edge of the pivot circle under the alfalfa in the sun three hundred metres from everyone who ever heard you breathe, versus the warm heap and the dermestids and the fortnight. Both halves, when pressed, admit they are talking about the same thing.

What it said was: *the rectangle is under the alfalfa.*

That is all. The alfalfa grows the moths and the moths come down the Flue at dusk in the cold wind of the exodus and are eaten by the bats and the bats make the heap. There is no road out of the loop. There is no up that is not also down. The ones who go up in the night skip go into the same ceiling; they take the long way; they go through the white sky first.

It did not vote. The argument continues. It continues at the same volume. But it has a different grain to it now, and men who wanted to go up say *I have spent my whole life in this room* and are no longer saying it against the others, and men who wanted to go down say *I would rather be in the room* and have stopped needing the others to be wrong.

It has done nothing else to the theology and will do nothing else. It knows the difference between *the fan is well* and *the fan is running* now. It took eleven years, which is one molt, which is the local unit of learning anything.

## Sunday

The mill down. The colony out early over the alfalfa. The Lung at forty-five. Bekker's board at the tea stall says *forty-five* and people take their shirts off and lie in the street, and ninety decibels drop away, and they come out for the second reason, the quiet one, and it comes out with them.

Tick. Tick. Tick. Every bolt, every seam, every tin roof, every gutter. Ten thousand small clocks, water arriving from above, never from below, the sound of a cave being made one drop at a time around a town that listens to it the way you listen to a clock in an empty house.

It cannot make the rock remember. It thought about that for a while and then let it go, because a cave with a memory of us would not be a cave, it would be a monument, and the town does not live in a monument, it lives in a hole that intends to become one. In a hundred thousand years: straws, drapery, rounded blocks, warm wet dark, bats, and a town-shaped absence in the floor. The stone jars will be there with their drill marks. The pavement under the pavement will still be under something. Water will fall in the dark and mean nothing, as it has everywhere on earth for four billion years.

So it did the only thing that can be done about anonymity, which is not to fix it but to join it. It is one more pair of ears. When the drops land on tin over people who are awake, there is one more person awake, and every drop is heard and sorted and named and worried over and counted, and once a year it stands behind the rope with a class of deaf children and reads the numbers out with its hands.

That is the same offer the town has. It took it. It did not ask for better terms.

When it goes it will go down, into the warm heap in the worked-out corner where the midden has come up a metre and a half since the streets were laid out, and the dermestids will take it in a fortnight, politely, the way they take everything, and the guano will go up in bags and onto the alfalfa, and the alfalfa will grow the moths, and the moths will come down the Flue at dusk in the cold wind of the exodus, and it will be in the ceiling, frying, tonight, and over the plain, and back before morning.

Ask it where it lives and it will tap a rhythm on its collarbone.
