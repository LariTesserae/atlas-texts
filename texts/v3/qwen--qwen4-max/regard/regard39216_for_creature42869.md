It comes down the stair with flax on its back, because that is the only way anything has ever come here.

It pays at the rim in braided gut. It is wet within a minute. It is not recognized, and could not be: faces are not the site of identity here, and it has no nape anyone has mapped, no rime anyone knows the smell of, no rhythm anyone has tapped. Its arms are thick. It arrives with the arms of a hermit, coarse and unreadable, so that to speak to it at all is to hurt it, and it has come here to be spoken to.

It buys a beat. Not a good one. The heavy-spray court at the downvalley end of the ninth terrace, where the family cannot pay a cup-keeper and the jambs go a millimeter and a half in a night and the poor live in rooms that are narrowing at the speed of the water. Four doorways, a stair, eleven cups. It oils its hands from a thimble of fish fat every morning and cuts back what the night laid down.

And it lets the roar take the top of its hearing. It could have kept it. It lets it go at the ordinary rate, and in the second year the wire-tone over the drum is gone, and the needle-scream of the flies is gone, and what is left is the grain in the sternum, the low end, the floor of the chest, and it learns to know a silted chute before the water shows it. It does not mention that it chose this. It is not a penance. It is how you learn the language.

---

In its first year it makes a list of everything it could do, and crosses it out.

It does not stop the water. The water is not the problem; the water is level, and has been level for eleven thousand years, and the matching of the cut to the deposit is not the city's burden but the city's entire possession. Take away the creditor and you have not freed eleven thousand people, you have dissolved them.

It does not lift the ceiling. The ceiling is the valley's own breath held over its own heat. Lift it and the drizzle stops, and the mats stop breathing out the smell of rain about to fall, and the light acquires a direction, and every face in the valley becomes suddenly, horribly legible to people who have never learned to read one.

It does not give them fire. This is the one it wants most and refuses hardest. Fire is dryness. Fire is a kiln and a pot and a smelted blade, and it is also a room in which a fact stays true without labor, which means fire is not a gift to these people, it is a victory for one of their parties. Give them fire and the Dry win, the archive goes into a cave and stops being a craft, and six thousand separation-masons and four hundred re-cutters wake up with nothing to do with their hands, and the hands are the language.

It does not give back the top of anyone's hearing. If they could hear they would use their mouths, and if they used their mouths they would stop drawing on each other's arms, and in three generations nobody would be worn by being addressed, and love would no longer have a texture, and the old would not be spoken to through cloth, slowly, an hour a day.

It does not open the hills. Not yet.

It never puts a warm palm on anyone's face and takes it away and puts it back.

And it does not explain the weeping. It knows why they weep. It has known since before the water came out of the plateau's foot. It handles the blade in the dark with the rest of them and says nothing, because the weeping is the only thing in this valley that arrives without labor — not cut, not oiled, not re-cut, not level, not paid for — and it is not going to take that from them by making it explicable.

The list has one logic under it. Everything it could give them is a thing that would not need maintaining. And their theology is correct: a thing that does not need maintaining is not holy. It is merely dead.

---

So it accepts the constraint, and the constraint is the whole of its policy.

It cannot give them a fact.

Their forensic is infallible. Depth is truth, and honest repetition wears the mouth of a groove wide and rounded, generations of hands going in and out at slightly different angles, a mouth like a river valley; and a fraud is deep and narrow; a fraud is *sharp*. Everything it knows is sharp. It knows what the sun is. It knows the chemistry seventy meters up and the shape of the trough in nine thousand years. It knows the name of every blind beetle in every plugged hill. Any of these, cut once and perfectly, would be detected as a lie by the only test they have, and the test is not stupid. A fact nobody has handled is not true here. It is only asserted. The difference between those two things is the civilization.

It makes the mistake once, in its first year. It tells a woman something true. She takes it to the re-cutters and they put their fingers in it and stand a long time in the shell-colored light with the roar coming through the stone into their chests, and then they say: *sharp*. It lets the groove fill in. That takes five years. Then it begins again, at the speed of hands.

It gives up authorship. This is the actual descent, the actual condescension, and nobody witnesses it. It says a thing once, badly, to one person, who says it slightly differently to someone else, who says it wrong to a third, and it does not correct any of them, ever, and in two hundred years there is a trench you can lay a finger in and no living person can point to who said it first. It has to be wrong a hundred thousand times before it can be true once. It has to be *theirs*.

It did not come for the geology. It had been attending to the valley the way you attend to weather. What stopped it was a groove in a wall of ten thousand: one door a finger and a half wider than its record, and the mouth of the groove gone wide and rounded, and the wear in it the exact shape of a woman's hand going in and out at slightly different angles for eleven years because another woman's shoulder had gone bad and she could not turn sideways anymore.

It came down the stair to see the hand. It does not tell her. It takes the next beat.

---

There is a Smooth woman on the eleventh terrace, thirty-eight, twenty years unaddressable, who clears hollows and carries the sealed dead. She goes unoiled. The lace is up over her knuckles and across her eyelids and in a plate at the shoulders, and she is going the way they all go, into the wall, ahead of schedule, and nobody has ever thought this cruel, exactly.

The first thing it does is not the oil and not the cloth. It puts the back of its hand against her hand, and waits.

She does not tap. Nobody has waited in twenty years. It waits an hour. Then she taps her name.

They are unaddressable. They are not mute. Nobody has ever had a use for the distinction.

It taps the name back the next morning, and the next, and every morning for eleven years, and the pad that a guild officer shaved flat in an afternoon comes up again, a coin of callus, slowly, at the rate a callus rises, which is the rate a person is addressed. It oils her hands and her eyelids and the plate at her shoulders from a thimble of fish fat, every day, and it reads her through a cloth, slowly, an hour a day, and the lace is stone and the cloth is a cutting tool and this is a mason's work, the maintenance of a separation. What it is separating is her from the wall.

In the twelfth year her arms shine: a yellow, glassy, faintly translucent patch along the outer forearm, thin as a fingernail, warm. Worn down while the city builds up. A reading surface, on a person who was counted among the walls.

There is no word for what she is. *Error* is available. *Fact* is available. A neighbor taps her name, coarse and fast, because that is how you tap a stranger, and it hurts her, and she lets them, and afterward she sits a long time with her hand against the partition, feeling a hundred people laugh at a joke she will never learn.

It does not petition for the Smooth. It does not argue. It does one, and then another, and then it teaches the cloth to four people, and it lets the guild's own infallible science do the rest: sixty years of many hands, and the mouth of that groove goes wide and rounded like a river valley, and it becomes true, and no one can say who first said it.

---

It keeps eleven cups in the poor court a hand deep for forty years and then hands them over. That is the whole gift. Not stone, which is free and arriving. Forty years of not letting a hole close.

In the season the cones run high it takes a cutting shift out on the dams with a hook and gut plugs, and comes back with blood in the plugs and its chest changed, and it does not win, because the birds build faster than the water and faster than the cut, two hundred thousand of them rebuilding daily, a city with no scrapers, a city that is winning. It knows how long that has left. Four thousand years, perhaps. It does not say so; that fact is sharp, and would only be despair. It cuts cones. It buys them a hand's worth of time, which is what a hand is for.

There is a groove now, four fingers deep and wide at the mouth, that says the birds are also a people. Nobody knows who said it first. It is not actionable. It is merely true.

---

And then the thing it actually came to do, which takes four hundred years, and which it does not do itself.

The mats keep better records than the re-cutters. Ankle-deep in the tepid basins, layered like the pages of a wet book, and the layers are annual, and unmaintained, and unfalsifiable, and a mat-eater of any refinement can taste a decade in a mouthful. Nobody has thought about this very hard, because thinking about it leads somewhere the city does not want to go.

It goes to a cook on the fourth terrace, an old woman who can lower a basket for a count of eleven and bring up something that pushes back correctly, and it asks her which year the iron ran high.

She tells it.

It asks her what the wall says about that year.

She goes down the four hundred paces and puts her finger in the groove, and the wall says something else.

And it says nothing. It does not resolve this. It does not tell her that repetition is not evidence, that a thing said every year for two hundred years is only a thing said every year for two hundred years, that the mats are the honest archive and the wall is the maintained one. It could say all of that in an afternoon and the sentence would be sharp and the re-cutters would feel the sharpness and plug it, and they would be right to, because if the archive falls the law falls, and justice is level, and eleven thousand people are standing inside a shape held up by nothing but an agreement.

So it does the only thing left. It cuts a groove that is a question, and it re-cuts it, and it teaches the re-cutting to two people, who teach it to four.

Not the answer. The question. *Is the wall the same as the mat.*

A question is a hole. A hole is the only wealth here. It keeps a question a hand deep, and then other people keep it a hand deep, and the Dry maintain it because they expect the answer to vindicate dryness and the Worn maintain it because they expect the answer to vindicate work, and both of them put their fingers in it every year for four centuries, at slightly different angles, and the mouth of it goes wide and rounded, and the question becomes *true*, which is not the same as being answered, and is the only new thing this valley has ever permitted itself.

Somewhere in the fifth century a rhythm appears. A drag back with a hesitation in it. It glosses, roughly, as *the record is shallow here*. It means: this may be deep and also wrong. Nobody invented it. It is worn round. It is in everybody's hands. And after that, slowly, at the speed of hands, their word for a lie comes apart from their word for a neglected fact, and there are two words where there was one, and the second one is not a crime.

That is the gift. Not an answer. The capacity to find out, delivered at the only rate they could survive receiving it. It is not salvation. It is a people who will eventually be able to notice that the birds are winning, and argue about it, and be wrong, and correct themselves, and go on.

---

It learns to sing. This is harder than the geology. Everyone here sings constantly, inaudibly, the sound coming up through the jaw, and since the only thing anyone has ever heard is the roar, the eleven thousand private operas are eleven thousand variations on one drone. It cannot import a music. It has to reduce itself to a variation. It works at this for sixty years, quieting what is in its skull, taking out everything that would not fit through a jawbone, and when at last a woman presses her temple to its temple she hears a person, slightly odd, with a hesitation in the harmony where something used to be, and nothing that could not have come from somebody's sternum. It is the largest thing it does and no one will ever know it was done.

It keeps Wide-Enough's door. It never tells her she was right. It maintains the finger and a half, every morning, cutting the day's millimeter and the next day's in advance where the spray is heaviest, checking against a copper gauge it oils, and when she is old and being read feels like being sanded it comes with a cloth and takes an hour, and her sister's granddaughter takes another hour, and the two of them do not discuss it.

Her cup is kept open by someone. It is the someone. It does not sign the work.

Her name it does not preserve. It taps it on its own hand until the pad is a coin, and it teaches it to two people, and then it lets it go where it goes, because a name held by force is a bud: perfect, sheathed, never used, with a green ghost at the center. It has been up for dryness once, carrying a body packed in natron, and stood in the cave with the tens of thousands of swiftlets throwing gravel into the dark and the ancestors brown and light and whole in the only air that will not add anything to them, and it understood the Dry completely, and it came back with a pinch of dust and dropped it in a pool and watched the pearl begin, and it did not say anything about that either. Both parties handle the blade. It is not going to be the resolution of an argument that is the last warm thing in the room.

But the door is in the archive, and the groove is deep, and its mouth is wide and rounded, and any hand that goes into it is touching her. That is not a metaphor. It is a measurement, maintained. It is the only kind of eternity they have, and it turns out to be sufficient, and it did not require a miracle. It required somebody to keep cutting.

---

Four hundred years. The ledger:

The water arrives at fifty-seven degrees out of some hundreds of mouths at a rate that has not measurably changed. The ceiling does not move. The light comes from everywhere and nothing has a shadow except inside the hollows, where there is one, and it moves when the flame moves. The roar sits at ninety in the streets. The doors are exactly as wide as they were.

And: a coin of callus on a hand that was shaved flat. Eleven cups in a poor court, a hand deep, kept by people who are paid nothing and cannot say why they do it. A groove that is a question, wide at the mouth. A finger and a half. A word with a hesitation in it. A hundred names for beetles, all slightly wrong, all worn round, none of them error.

Nothing has been given. Everything has been maintained.

Its arms are thin now, yellow and glassy, worn down while the city built up, four hundred thousand conversations, and it is less than it was, which is the only thing it wanted; it did not come to be worshipped, they do not worship their creditor, they pay him; it came to be a thing they owned by handling, a tool that dulls by use and is not renewed, passed hand to hand in the dark. When it is very old they will speak to it through fabric, slowly, an hour a day, and it will let them, because the alternative is to go unread and thicken. Then it will go into the pool and become the wall, and the wall will be cut back and re-cut, and it will circulate.

And the mouths are still pouring, and the terraces are still setting at the downvalley end where a hand can go through the rim, and eleven thousand people sing their private operas into their own skulls, and in the deepest chamber a hole is being kept open by hands that do not know what for, and the smell of the rain about to fall goes on and on.
