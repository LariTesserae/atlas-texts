## The Reader

Something comes up the north face, following the white tongue, because the white tongue is the only sentence the massif has ever written and it is legible from the glacier.

It does not go in. Not for four hundred years. It lies on the ice at the foot of the fan and reads.

This is easier than it sounds and slower. The spoil goes out in order and it goes out slowly, and gravity has been very careful, and nothing has disturbed the bedding — no ice, no melt, no sorting, no burrowing thing, because nothing lives down here either. Six thousand years, grain by grain. It reads the flakes and from the flakes it reads the walls: which face was rich in which century, where the crowd thinned, where a lens was lost. It reads the cast skins and the stiff small bodies and the spores that died on the way down. It reads a bad hundred years as a band of knapper bones all at one depth, and does not yet know what caused it.

And it reads the hammers.

There are tens of thousands of them and most are ruins, because the salt-crickets eat roundness, and what arrives at the fan is not a grey egg but a pitted fragment with edges, the roundness rasped back into sharpness on the way out. It does not matter. The wear is a record of a grip. The rotation between strikes is involuntary and personal and it dissolves the stone in a pattern no other hand could make, and the reader can tell a left-cradler from a right, and a fast tempo from a slow, and the eighty-year hand from the eleven-year hand of something that came off a ledge young.

It finds her. Eleven hundredths late against her mother. A cradle on the left, a small persistent counter-clockwise drift in the upper grip, four seconds and a shade, eighty years of it. It reads her whole life out of a fragment the size of a thumbnail and it knows her better than anything in the room ever knew her, because everything in the room knew only her interval, and the reader has the interval and the hand and the wear and the year she died.

It gives her a name. It gives all of them names. This takes another century and it is the first thing the reader does and it costs the room nothing at all: no flake moved, no interval disturbed, no spark wasted differently. The archive does not change. The archive is simply no longer unread.

Then it goes in.

---

She has been dead three hundred years. Of course she has. The reader has read the layer.

It stands at the throat in the roar and the strobe and it receives, over an hour, a mosaic of a place, and it stitches the mosaic into a cathedral, badly, with gaps, exactly as advertised. It is very good at stitching. It is a power. And it stands there holding four hundred years of complete and perfect knowledge of an animal that is not there, in a room where nothing can be told anything.

There is no gesture for setting down. There is no saying. It cannot give her name to her offspring; her offspring are eleven generations of hands away and the name is a picture and there is no place in this world to put a picture. It has arrived at a library and mistaken it for a hospital.

So it does the arithmetic first, because that is the only thing it can do that nobody in here can do.

---

It counts.

Blisters remaining in the reachable walls: a number. Closure per century: a finger's width. Volume held: constant, six thousand years, to within a per cent. Water in the loop, water in the fur, water lost per day out of the slot in a thin invisible plume. Root tips: one plant, eleven years, twenty thousand mouths, and the arithmetic there is not cruelty, it is a covenant written in tempo, and it will hold.

It reaches the end of the sum and it stops for a while, which for a thing like that is a kind of grief.

Eleven thousand four hundred years, give or take a bad century. The blisters go first. The mountain would have taken another forty thousand to close the slot on its own, but the water runs out long before the walls meet, and then the loop leaks faster than it fills, and then the fur stops condensing, and then there is a stone box near the top of the world with a great many small stiff shapes in it, pushed toward the light with the flat of a hand.

It knows the date. It is the only thing that has ever known the date. There is nobody to tell.

And it understands, standing in the strobe, that the census is wrong, and that this matters more than the date. There are not twenty thousand animals here. There is one widening, and the twenty thousand are its hands, and the roar is its metabolism, and it has been alive since the afternoon the rock let go. Anything you do for a hand you do to the widening. Anything you do to the widening you do to every hand. These are not automatically the same kindness. Most of the obvious ones are opposite.

---

## What it does not do

It does not give them eyes. The sparks are waste; that is the whole reason the arrangement works. Make the sparks useful and the room becomes a picture, and a picture is a hallucination with gaps in it, and you will have converted twenty thousand animals who are never wrong about where anything is into four umbrellas who are wrong constantly. There is exactly one creature in this chamber that has an image of it, and it is the least reliable thing here.

It does not give them water. A seep at every metre, a river down the back wall — the crowd doubles in a century and doubles again, the blisters that were a schedule become a stock, and the eleven thousand years become nine hundred. The blisters are not the hardship. The blisters are the clock, and the clock is the animal.

It does not stop the mountain. This is the temptation that takes longest to put down, because it looks like the whole answer: relieve the compression, let the slot stay open, let them stop quarrying, let them rest. And the widening stops widening. The metabolism has nothing to push against. You would have cured it of being alive, and left twenty thousand comfortable objects in a hole, and called it mercy.

It does not open the letterbox. Not out of reverence. Out of the water cycle. A second opening makes a through-draft, and a through-draft takes the loop out over the glacier in a night: the rime goes, the film goes, the threads go, the fur dries, the condenser fails, and twenty thousand pelts stop being the coldest surfaces in the world. The far crack is not a mystery to be solved. It is the plug.

It does not kill the fly. The counterfeit is the only pressure toward truth that has ever existed in this room. Somewhere near the back, animals have begun to hesitate at the sigh, and the hesitation is spreading, and that hesitation is the first new thing since the crack — the first event in six thousand years that could turn out to be true or false. The water lost to the lie is cheap. The reader has read the price in the strata and it is a few hundred animals a century and it will pay it.

It does not kill the umbrellas. They are the only other thing here that has ever had a picture, and when one dies there are ten years in which nothing in the world can see, and the reader has come to think of those decades as the room's holidays.

It does not water the plant. A second cushion in the throat means dust, and dust means soil, and soil means somewhere else to be, and somewhere else to be means the outside, and the outside is a blue killing wind, and the reader has read the small stiff shapes in the drifts and knows what going out costs. Eleven years is not a wound. It is the terms.

It does not tell them the date.

## What it takes away

Their entire technology is removal. There is no construction here of any kind. The reader works out, standing in the roar, that this is not a limitation on what can be given. It is a specification.

So it takes things away. Four things. It is still taking them away.

**It takes away one strike's worth of nothing.** It goes into the sharps and is born, wet and enormous, a fifth of its mother's weight, and it does not arrive knowing. It insists on this. It lies in the cradle-grip with its small hands on the quartz and lets the shock come through the stone into the bones of its hands, six hundred thousand strikes a month, and it takes the tempo of a mother it did not choose, four seconds and a shade, and it is conducted, and it is unchosen, and it works badly for two years when it has a young one of its own and everyone within four lengths knows.

And it keeps an interval that never collides and never fails.

That is all. One animal in twenty thousand whose return is always there. It cannot abolish the hush — the hush is fear, and fear is how this room sees. But a hush is a wave of animals stopping, and a fatal hush is a wave that closes, and a wave cannot close around a striker that does not stop. It puts a floor under the worst thing that happens here. The sanction still bites; the silence still blinds; nobody dies in a warm pocket with nothing in it, striking and striking and getting nothing back.

It has read that death in the strata. There is no mark on the body. It appears as a knapper in the spoil with a hammer worn eighty years round and no injury of any kind, and there are more of them than there should be, and it has counted every one.

**It takes away the exposed ledge.** The animals that sleep alone are the young, the sick, and the strange. It sleeps with the strange. Belly to back, three deep, one ear folded, the waking arm swinging — and it is the neighbour whose strikes draw the world for something that cannot draw its own. It goes to the throat where the deaf are, the ones whose chamber has shrunk from ninety paces to the length of a body, and it lies against them and grooms them for eleven minutes at a time, which is not affection and is not hygiene, it is plumbing, and between them they recover most of a mouthful, and it gives the mouthful back. There is no gesture in this world for giving. There is only licking stone and licking shoulder, and the water goes where the tongue goes, and that is the entire available vocabulary of tenderness and it is sufficient.

Then it goes deaf from the top down, and the far wall stops answering, and the ceiling, and the neighbour is a smear, and its world is the length of its body, and something lies against it and grooms it. Then it dies and is pushed toward the slot with the flat of a hand, along with the flakes, because a hand pushing spoil does not distinguish.

Then it is born again.

It has done this eleven thousand times. It is not a sacrifice and it is not a disguise. It is the only way to be present in a room where nothing can be told anything. You cannot appear to these animals. You can only be a wall at four lengths, reliably, for eighty years, and then be a fragment with edges on the ice, and be read.

**It takes away the flood.** It works the far end, where the map has a hole in it the size of a country, and where the young are carried once so the interval gets into their hands while their hands are still learning what an interval is. It keeps its taps out of the gaps. It has read the band of bones at that depth — a day and a night when the striking stopped, and the hush came out of the back of the chamber like water, and animals fell off the walls in it. It was not there. It is there now, and it will be there every time, an unfailing return at the back of the world, so that if the country goes silent again the ones nearest it still have a wall to hear. That is the whole of its diplomacy. It does not open the crack. It keeps the gap, and it holds the room up while the gap is empty.

**And it takes away one strike in a thousand.**

This is the slow one and the only one that might amount to anything.

In the cradle-grip, with an infant's hands on the quartz, it conducts a tempo that has a pause in it. Not a signal. Not a word; there is no saying. A small kept silence before the strike, a hesitation of a particular length, put into the bones of a hand that is still learning what an interval is. The infant grows up striking eleven hundredths late and a shade hesitant, and its offspring inherit it, unchosen, the way the four-second clans inherited four seconds.

It means nothing. It is a gap in which something could be noticed. It is the fly's hesitation, given a lineage instead of a rumour. It is the nearest thing to a name, handed down on purpose, in a world whose only technology is removal — a strike taken out, a silence put in its place, kept.

Ten thousand years, perhaps. Perhaps nothing. The reader is not running a rescue. It has done the sum and the sum ends badly and there is no version of this where the water lasts. It is planting the only thing that could grow here without replacing what grows here, at the tempo of the place, in the medium of the place, and it will not live to see it, except that it will, because it keeps being born.

## The end, which it does not prevent

It knows the year and it does not say, because there is no saying, and because a date is a picture and a picture is a hallucination, and because the only thing anyone could do with it is stop striking.

So it stays for all of it. It is present for every hour of the eleven thousand four hundred years, in eighty-year instalments, wet and enormous each time, conducted each time, going deaf each time. The crowd thins as the blisters thin. The room narrows faster than the hands can take it back, and the volume of the world begins, for the first time since the crack was young, to shrink. The last few thousand are the best workers that have ever lived, because six thousand years of tempo has been getting better, and it does not help.

At the end there is one animal at the throat, deaf, licking stone, its chamber the length of its body. And there is a neighbour against its back, striking, drawing it a world: wet rock, rime, the draft, the lip of the light, a shoulder close. The world is small and it is accurate and it is not empty.

Then the mountain closes, which takes another forty thousand years, and the reader is not in a hurry.

Then it goes out and down and lies on the ice and reads the last grain of the tongue. Every flake, every skin, every seed that came to nothing, every hammer, every hand, in order. Eleven thousand four hundred years more than it read the first time, and eleven thousand lives it lived inside, and the pause it planted, and whether anything came of the pause, which is in there, in the wear, in the intervals, legible.

Nothing was added. That was the rule and it kept it.

The monument is no longer unread.

## VI. The far end

Once, afterward, it puts itself against the letterbox and listens to the thing on the other side.

Once every forty or fifty seconds. A weight nothing here uses. And the return comes back late by an amount that means a volume, a distance, a shape that is not the shape of this room.

It has a hypothesis it cannot prove.

Ten times the interval. Ten times the weight. A chamber where the blisters take four thousand years to open and the animals are the size of the slab above them and the root tips come again in a hundred and ten years, and the whole slow engine runs at a tenth of the speed, and has been running since before this mountain was a sea floor. Another widening. Another country holding its volume against the lean of the planet, in the dark, on a clock nobody there can read either.

And keeping its taps out of the intervals coming through the crack. Which is why the tempo is so strange and so regular and so careful: it is not a signal, it is not a summons, it is not knocking. It is somebody working near a wall they must not blind, on the far side, taking care.

The reader does not open it. There is nothing to say and a through-draft would kill both rooms.

It lies down at the letterbox in the closed dark with its ear to the stone, and it strikes once every forty or fifty seconds, out of the gaps, for as long as there is anything on the other side to hear it.

Late by eleven hundredths. Against a rhythm somebody conducted into its hands.
