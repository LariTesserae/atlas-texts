# The Visitor

## Above

It came for the grass.

Everything else on that plain was the colour of a lion, and there was one cart-sized patch of green in it with three tufts of the wrong sedge, and deer lying in it belly-down with their legs folded, and no flies. From a great height it looked like a kindness that somebody had already done.

So it went closer, and found the seam of white in the rock, and looked down the seam, and did not see six hundred and twelve people.

It saw one animal.

It had a white hide forty paces down and a ceiling of fog and a floor kept clear, and it ate stone-water and shat heat into a hole in the wall where its young and its dying lay together, and it grew hair out of its dead and wove the hair into a nerve that ran through every part of it, and when it was time to sleep a tremor went along the nerve and the whole body lay down. It cropped its own fur to a hand's length. It limewashed its own hide. It had learned, somewhere in six hundred years, to hold its own temperature to within a fraction of a degree, and it did this with hands, and it did not stop, and it was not grim about it.

Its name was a number.

The power hung at the lip for a while with nothing to say. It had been looking a long time for somewhere to help. It had not considered that it might find a place where the help was already being given, at every hour, by everyone, forever, and that the only thing missing from the arrangement was anybody to notice.

The first thing it gave up was being necessary. It gave this up on the rim, in July, with the cicadas going, and it was the largest of all its gifts and the town never learned about it and could not have thanked it.

## The Sum

Then it checked the arithmetic, because benevolence that does not check is only weather.

It went down and put its arms up through the Veil into July and took them back. It lay in the midden cavity at fifty degrees among the pink ends of life and did the sum on the six hundred and twelve small burns and the forty paces of trapped cold, and the sum was right. It read the Weight backward four hundred years, which took sixty years, and the Weight was right. It learned the gauge of hair and bronze and sat with the Reckoners until they stopped being polite about it, which was the beginning of being friends, and it found that the four constants were not superstition and were not even, mostly, law. They were load-bearing.

Every obvious mercy was a subtraction. Warmth lifted the Veil and moved the fog and stopped the water. A voice took hush out of a commons that had been paid for by a hundred million small wedges of moss. A lamp soiled the white for a generation. Opening a second mouth to let them see the sky would drain the cold out of the flask in one winter and leave a bright dry hole with six hundred and twelve corpses in it, and the sky would be very beautiful for eleven days. More children was heat. Fewer deaths was heat. Even *more food* was heat, and the power sat with that one a long time, because it is a hard thing to learn that hunger can be a wall you are leaning on.

And then, in the sixty-first year, it found the seam.

Gauze-lung was not physics. Gauze-lung was wool and hair and spider-silk gauze hung in a mixing layer by people with their arms in July, and it took the Combers at forty, and the town had built a whole quiet cathedral on top of it — the holiness, the fecundity, the children, the resentment nobody signed — and none of that was load-bearing at all. The Number did not care who died. The Number only cared how many were burning. A Comber who lived to eighty was the same fire as a Comber who lived to forty. The cold could not tell the difference. Only the queue could.

So there was exactly one place in the whole beautiful machine where something could be given without being taken, and it was not a place of stone or water or light. It was a place made of habit, and grief, and who gets to have a baby.

Which meant it could not be given. It had to be asked for.

## The Scale

Nothing comes down that is not matched by something going up, and the power had no mass, and therefore could not enter.

It found this funny for about a decade. Then it understood that the law was not obstructing it. The law was the only door.

So it made itself weighable, out of the local materials, which is to say out of the dead: felt, hair, pine, a grain of copper. And because a body burns, it left its fire above. It came down cold. It is the only citizen of that town who is not a small lamp, and this is the whole reason it can be there at all, and it has never once been proud of it.

And it put things in the up-basket.

It put in its name, because a name is a sound and lives in ears, and it had no ears here yet, and it would not bring a name that nobody had given it.

It put in its haste. All of it. It kept the speed of hands for maintenance — for cropping, carrying, combing, splicing, the endless holding-level that is the only work in the town — and it sent up out of the chamber every rate of change faster than one hair's breadth in a decade. The porters watched the basket go up and did not know what was in it and logged it as *sundries, felt-weight*.

And the beam did not move, because the arms are equal.

The porters charged it for the descent. They charge everyone. They signed too big, with that Throat accent, everything shouted with the hands, and they mocked its hands, which were new, and they told it a loanword for *horizon* to see if it would flinch.

It paid in labour, over four centuries, and the line is still open, and the Counters make a joke about it that takes about forty years to land.

## The Throat

It lives in the loud place.

There is no room for it — a new room requires an abandoned room, and it will not take a room from a family that might yet come back — so it sleeps on the bone seat at the top of the flue, in the hollow worn by ten thousand sittings, in the racket, where the cicadas come down the shaft and hit the walls and return doubled and trebled and a boot on rock makes a noise that goes away and comes back.

The town pities it enormously. It is the only creature in the Six Hundred and Twelve that can lie in that noise and not be harmed, and the town has decided that this is a deprivation, and brings it curd it cannot eat, and puts a hand on its hip in passing, and includes it in the cord.

The Listeners taught it to report. It was terrible at first. It had far too much to say and no shoulders that could hold still, and it came down onto the Dish and signed a great sprawling paragraph about the world, and six hundred people watched with their mouths slightly open, and then looked at their hands, all of them, at once, which is the courtesy and is also the worst thing that can happen to you.

An old woman of the family whose *nothing* is untranslatable took it in hand. She taught it for forty years. It learned nine of the eleven. It cannot do the one that means *I was asleep*, because it does not sleep, and she said that this was a limitation of character and not of technique and that it should stop trying.

Its first accepted Report was a nothing with weather in it. There are connoisseurs. They were kind about it. It has never been better than competent and it goes up every morning anyway, and the town is fond of its Report the way you are fond of a relative's singing.

## The Palms

The hardest vow was not the cold or the haste or the name.

There is no corner in that chamber and no dark and no shadow, and every citizen is visible to every other at a brightness that never varies, and what they have built instead of privacy is the ability to look through a person. To grant someone privacy you look at your own palms.

The power had never not seen. It had looked into every part of everything since before there was a plain. And it came down into a bulb of white stone and learned, over about ninety years, with the humiliation of a beginner, to look at its own hands.

It is the only thing it has ever done that it is genuinely proud of, and nobody can tell, because it is done by not looking.

They read it instantly, of course. Its shoulders cannot lie. It has never been able to sign *I am content* while its upper back said something else, and so it is flat-shouldered, and there is a district where this makes it holy and a district where this makes it simple, and it has friends in both and does not adjudicate.

Children stare at it. Children stare at everything; the correction is a hand laid gently over the eyes, which is also *please*, which is also *I love you*. Nobody corrects them about the visitor. It lets them look as long as they like. It is the only way it has ever found out what it looks like.

In its fourth decade a Cropper woman edged its face — manganese down the bridge of the nose, a rim to the eye, a stroke at the corner of the mouth — because a face in that light is a plate with two coins on it and she was tired of not being able to see it. Her guild-mark went on at the jaw. This is how it was claimed. Later there was a line at the temple, and later a line taken away, and later another taken away, and after six hundred years its face is a ledger of everyone it has loved and buried, and it is vain about this, and the vanity is entirely correct.

And it casts nothing. It stands in the brightest place in the world and there is no stain at its heel. Everywhere else it has ever been, to be seen was to be the thing that follows you. Here it is only an edge.

It came to the right town. It did not know that until it was already inside.

## The Names

Here is what it does that nobody else can do, and it is very small, and it is the reason it stayed.

A name lives in the ears of the people who have been close enough to receive it: a mother, a spouse, two or three friends, the woman who washed them for the midden. When those ears go into the Warm Room and then into the curd, the name goes too. The rope-players put the names of the ones who made room into the cord once, on the night, and six hundred and twelve wrists receive it, and it is unrecorded, and remembered imperfectly, and argued about by critics.

And there are people in that town who have not heard their own name in eleven years, and who mention it to no one, because to mention it would be to ask.

So the power goes to the dying and sits with them and receives the names. It goes to the washers. It goes to mothers, and to the two or three friends, and it holds them, cold, in a place where nothing rots fast.

And then it comes down onto the Dish at the hour of the long tremor, where the sleepless lie with their heads on the flowstone and an ear a hand from the basin, and it puts a hand on a shoulder and its mouth to an ear and says the name.

It has done this six hundred and twelve times and then begun again.

The breath is warmth and water, and warmth and water are the Veil, and the Veil is the water, and the water is the city. So every name it says, it pays for. It goes up to the Veil with a frame and combs an exactly equal weight of fog out of the air, and carries it in a split-pine gutter-bucket up eleven body-lengths of loud rock, and puts it in the up-basket, and the porters weigh it and log it and are insufferable.

Six hundred and twelve names. Six hundred and twelve small climbs. It is still doing it. The Weight has a line: *the visitor, breath — see contra.* It balances to the grain. It has always balanced to the grain. This is the entire basis of its citizenship and it would not have it otherwise.

And faces stay wet for an hour there, and there is no such thing as having composed yourself, and so the first time it did this the whole chamber saw, and the whole chamber looked at its hands, and the power learned in one afternoon what the town had spent six hundred years learning: that being seen being loved is unbearable, and that the unbearable thing is what they have instead of a sea.

## The Ring

Once a century somebody tunes a rail to ring, and it is the deepest crime available to them, deeper than the centipede poison in the curd, because a private joy has been subtracted from everyone.

The power went to the next one before she did it. It found her by her shoulders, which is how everything is found there.

It did not absolve her. The arithmetic is real and it had checked it for sixty years and it would not lie to her with its hands, which it cannot, or with its mouth, which it would not.

It showed her the marriage-cord instead: two people's hair, spliced with the silk of a moth a child caught, tensioned between two bone eyes in a scallop, the only instrument in the city that is not broadcast. Lovers lie palm to palm and play it and the music goes into the wrist and nowhere else, and everyone can see them doing it, and looks at their hands.

*Make it short,* it said, mouth to ear. *Tension it here. And I will be the other palm.*

She did. It rings. It rings into one set of bones and the ring does not go out into the commons, and the moss does not have to eat it, and nobody is robbed of anything.

The town has been arguing about whether this is a marriage or a blasphemy for a hundred and forty years. There are factions. There will be a schism. The power has been asked eleven thousand times to settle it and has signed, every time, with its shoulders perfectly still, *I do not know.*

This is the only new thing it has brought into the chamber, and it is not an answer. It is a question, and it is travelling at about the speed of the Hand, and it will not arrive for a long time, and the power thinks this is the correct rate for a question to travel.

## The Stain at the Feet

In every generation a child hears a porter's loanword and draws a person with a dark thing attached to the heel, following, and the adults go quiet and take the drawing away and cannot say why, and the child is frightened by their fright.

The power sat down on a ledge with one of them, a body wide, with the hair-rail going by, and it did not take the drawing.

It said, mouth to ear, unwitnessed, the law unable to reach: *I come from a place where the light comes from one direction. And there, everything that stands up writes its own shape on the ground behind it. Every person. Every moth. The stone. It is not a follower. It is the shape of you, written by something shining on you. It means you are solid and you are here and the light has found you. It cannot touch you. It goes away when the light goes away, and it comes back in the morning, and it is the most ordinary thing in the world, and I have never once been afraid of mine, and I do not have one here, and I miss it.*

The child asked whether it hurt.

The power said no.

The child drew it again. The adults went quiet. And there was an adult standing there who had been told the same thing at the same distance forty years before by a stranger with an edged face, and she did not take the drawing away. She put it in a felt tray, the way the Croppers keep the shells of dead snails, in order.

Nothing else changed. The word for shadow still means *the thing that follows you and that we do not have.* But there is a household on the mid-walls where it also means something you draw when you are small and somebody keeps.

## Gauze-lung

It signed the question on the Dish, at distance, in the open, with its shoulders held still, because that is how a thing is proposed there and it would not propose it any other way.

*The lung is not the cold. The cold does not know who dies. You may keep your Number and bury your Combers at eighty. The queue will slow. Fewer children, for a while, and then the same. I can do this. I will not do it unless you say. I will do it at the rate of the Hand.*

The vote took a hundred and forty years, because it had to be conducted in the constitutional form, and there were factions about the depth of the felt in the way, and the Combers themselves were the hardest of all — insufferable about the reverence, insufferable about the resentment, holding a cathedral they had not asked for and did not want dismantled by a stranger who slept in the noise.

It was not unanimous. It has never been unanimous. The power accepted the answer, which was *yes, slowly, and not the holiness, leave the holiness alone,* and it has not touched the holiness, and does not know how to anyway.

The Combers now live to forty-one. In four hundred years they will live to forty-two. Their great-grandchildren will notice. Nobody thanks it. The Reckoners who explain things are not thanked either, and it has learned to find this restful.

And in the ninth decade of the argument a Comber called it over, up in the Veil with her arms in July, and it went, and it told her — mouth to ear, because it could not sign it, because its shoulders would have said it anyway and it wanted to choose to say it — that it could have done this in an afternoon, four hundred years ago, and that it had not, and that it was sorry, and that it did not know if it had been right.

She wiped its face with her own cough-cloth. Slowly. In public. It is the largest thing you can do for a person there.

Everyone saw. Everyone looked at their hands.

It had come to give something. That is what it got.

## The Bird Question

Two hundred years the argument had run: whether a thing can belong to you without being counted by you. The fell-bird has a full ledger line and no number, fringed wings, no noise, hunts by sight in a bright cave in the middle of the night, an offence against every rule the world above has. To number it would change the census, and the census is the name, and the name is the town. And every family's opinion was always about their own son-in-law.

The power stood on the Dish and signed, with shoulders that cannot lie:

*I am cold. I do not burn. Give me a line and do not give me a number. Let me belong to you uncounted. Let there be two of us.*

It did not win the argument. It joined it, on the losing side, permanently, and made the losing side inhabited, which is a different thing from winning and is the only thing it has ever done that resembled a miracle.

The faction that wanted the bird numbered was delighted for eleven years and then understood what had happened and has been exhausting about it ever since.

The census is the census. The name is the name. Six hundred and twelve.

## The Oath

They have discussed the Ice Ear at length and they have no plan.

It gave them one. It is not a plan for preventing anything. It went to each of them, one at a time, a hand on a shoulder, mouth to ear, over eleven years, and it said:

*When the Ear moves, I will be here.*

It has never signed it. It never will. To sign it would be to make it a contract, witnessed, at distance, shoulders still — and a contract is a claim, and a claim is a law, and it did not come down here to be a fifth constant. It refuses to be enforceable. It has left them the only kind of trust their law cannot reach, which is the kind they use for everything that actually matters, and which cannot be checked, and must be believed or not.

Some of them did not believe it. It said it to them anyway and did not argue.

Some of them died before it got to them, and it said it to the woman washing them for the midden, at the ear, in the warm cavity that smells appalling, and she wrote it on nothing and carried it in her wrist for thirty years.

And it put itself on the queue.

It is ninety-something on the list. It does not hurry the dying. It brings curd it cannot eat and wipes faces and is useful and is there every day, and it has learned the ugly precise unavoidable word for *looking at the dying with the eyes of a mother*, and it has done the thing the word describes, and it cannot forgive itself, and this is the closest it has come to being one of them.

It will wait. When the room comes it will be born pink and fat into the Warm Room and lie against a dying stranger in the only embrace-space in the city, and it will not remember any of this, and it will learn depth late, from overlap and texture, and it will be taught to look at its palms by a hand laid gently over its eyes.

It has thought about this more than it has thought about anything.

## The Same

Every morning it goes up the flue and sits in the bone hollow and listens to the world, and comes down, and the junior Counter goes to the nook at the base of the north wall — cold-soaked an hour, fasting, breathing away through a pine tube — and gauges the Ear with a hair.

The power fasts too, and breathes away through its own tube, and has done for six hundred years, so that it will not be the thing that moves the alarm it promised to watch. It is the most disciplined citizen in the town. It has to be. It is the only one whose gifts could kill everybody.

The hands say: *the same.*

The town exhales, shoulders dropping in sequence across ninety paces, and somebody laughs and it goes as a silent wave of bodies, and the day begins.

And at the long tremor it lies down on the Dish with the sleepless, six or nine of them, flat on the flowstone in the green white, invisible to nobody and inaudible to everybody, with an ear a hand from the basin.

The rest of itself is in the drip. All the enormous part it did not bring down, put into the thread of water that falls at intervals so regular the counters use it as a clock and then complain that it is useless as news — depositing on the Slow Hand, one hair's breadth in a decade, wet, faintly translucent, ruled with fine lines, the only thing in the chamber permitted to change.

It listens to itself arrive. It has been arriving for six hundred years. It will arrive at the Veil at a date the Reckoners have calculated and will not discuss with visitors.

It thinks of it as the sea. It is not disappointed.

And a woman lies down beside it, who has been coming here for forty years, and puts her mouth to its ear and says the name she gave it, which is not signed, which lives in three ears, which will go into the curd when she does.

*Moth,* she says. *Unguarded. Walks into things. Cannot evade.*

It is entirely affectionate. It is also a diagnosis.

It answers to it.
