# The North Side

It is a power that arrives after.

Almost everything it has ever done, it has done in places where the argument was already over: the burnt library, the salted field, the town under the lake with its doors shut. It is good at that work. It knows how to sit with what is left, how to make the leftover legible, how to be a comfort to people whose whole vocabulary has been reduced to *before*. It has never once been early. It does not have a practice for early.

Here nothing is older than a woman of eighty-one. The gravel at the lip is still sharp. There is a patch of glacier-scratched stone high on the north side where a rib tore away, and the scratches are fine and parallel and have not been rubbed out by anything, and it stands looking at them for a long time, the way you look at a signature on a document you have arrived too soon to sign.

Then it goes down.

---

It uses the ladders. It could not use them, and it uses them. The stair on the north face has been consenting for eleven years and is nearly a stair, and it puts its weight where the consent has been given and not where it hasn't, and this takes it slightly longer, and it does not mind.

Under the slot the fog sits at chest height, flat-topped, and it discovers that it has a chest height. This is new. It puts a hand through the fog from above, which is what the children are told not to do from below, and nobody tells it not to, because nobody can see it, and it feels obscurely that it has cheated.

It learns the room by hand, because that is how the room is known. It finds the walls soft enough to take a thumbprint and slow enough to keep it a week. It finds the old ribs scored so densely they read like bark, and it reads them, all of them, every notch and sub-notch, twenty-seven years of tides and hard winters in a hand that has never once been in a hurry.

Then it counts.

It does not get forty-one. It gets the forty-one and the thing that is the room, and two hundred and nine in ranks with a hand's width between them, and six hundred who are four days' walk away in every direction, and the felt-voles generations deep in the wall-mat, and the fox kits above the ceiling, and the raven at the lip with a dead woman's cough, and the grey swimmer out there in the cold coming nose-up across the black, and the verbs with no noun attached. It arrives at a number and declines to state it, even to itself, which is the first courtesy it pays.

The lamp-count six winters ago said two hundred and nine. The count before said two hundred and eleven. Both are in the ledger and neither is corrected.

It does not correct them either.

---

The ledger has three columns and only the middle one has a heading.

It reads the middle. Forty-one lines, and above them, if you know how to read a page, the ghosts of three lines that stopped in the winter of the long knit.

It reads the first, which is a mark like a thumbprint, once per tide, and a sub-mark for the hard winters, and which exists because you cannot write a ledger with no first column and you cannot write that word down.

The back column it does not read.

It could. That is the whole difficulty. The keepers cannot read it and have made a discipline of not trying, and the discipline is the single most impressive thing in the chamber, and it costs them years to acquire. The power can read it, and so not reading it costs something else, and it stands at the lip in the wind that arrives with no news and finds that it has just learned a skill from forty-one people who could not tell you they had it.

One stroke a year. Same hand. Same place on the page. A gap in the winter the swimmer did not come.

It leaves the column unheaded, which is what she has been asked to do and has never done.

---

Now the refusals, which are the bulk of the work, and which nobody will ever see.

**Warmth.** It could make the plain unfrozen. Every scrape and puddle sweet and liquid from here to the horizon. It runs this forward and the animals stop coming, because they come for the only unfrozen thing in the world, and the front stops funnelling, and the noise stops, and six hundred people stop knowing what the noise means, and in nine years there is a warm hole in a wet plain with forty-one people in it who have forgotten how to be a household. It does not do this. It notes that the cold is not an affliction of Sixteen. It is the wall that makes Sixteen a room.

**Light.** It could give them a lamp that eats nothing, or eyes that need nothing. It looks at the raft, which is already a cold light, farmed by a woman who knows exactly what makes it brighter and who does not feed it one thing, and who has told her successor, who is nine. It looks at the tuner, blind above the fog and sighted below it, carrying antler shims and a little mallet, keeping a hundred drips in pitch so that the room continues to say where it is. It looks at four children born in the dark who are the best drip-readers in the town and cannot throw a stone straight, ever.

The chamber is not a dark room. It is an instrument that has been tuned in the dark for twenty-seven years. To light it is to hand them a different instrument and take away the one they can play. It does not do this. It does not tell Raft what she is not feeding it, either, though it knows, and though the knowing sits in it like a stone in a boot for the whole visit.

**The seal.** Six or eight weeks of absolute dark, air sweet and thick, the roof making its small continuous tearing. It could hold the lid open. It could make the shingle-leaves refuse to knit.

But the given names come out in the dark, and nowhere else, and the seven-night story is added to only in the dark, and a thing said in the dark is true until thaw because there is no face to check it against, and people have been married by that and unmade by it. Take the seal and you take the only nakedness they have and leave them forty-one addresses forever. It does not do this. It notes the headaches and the sweet thick air and the ones who never recover the distances, and it holds both facts at once and does not resolve them, because they are not its to resolve.

**The drowned.** This is the one that takes it a whole night.

Two hundred and nine, perfectly kept, eyelashes intact, three mothers in doorways with the lamps still lashed to their posts. It could raise them. It could do it quietly, in an afternoon, and have them standing on the terraces dripping and blinking with their hands out.

They are not lost. They are filed. They are residents; they outnumber everybody; the lower quarter is the archive and the warehouse and the reason for the exchange rule, and there is a woman called Bench whose name is the location of her mother's bench, and if you bring the mother up you have not given Bench her mother, you have taken away her address, and there is no word in Sixteen for what she would be then.

And the town weighted the whole quarter under felt and shingle after the fifth tide, when a door stood open and a bench had moved a hand's breadth to the left.

It does not go near the water. It does not say a name over it — not a dead one, not its own, not the name of a district. It keeps that rule, and the exchange rule, and the rule about hook-lines, and every other rule, including the ones it cannot see the reason for, *especially* those, because a rule whose reason you cannot see is a rule that is holding something you have not met.

**The sorter.** It could name it. One word, in the tuner's ear, and the verbs would have a noun and the whole town would exhale.

It watches forty-one people stand absolutely still on four terraces in the dark while a line goes taut sideways, and then come slack, and then go back to work, and it understands that the not-speculating is not ignorance. It is a load-bearing wall. It is the thing they have built instead of an answer, and it holds, and it has held for twenty-seven years, and it is *theirs*.

It does not name it. It does not look at it directly. It has been looked at directly by things like this before and it knows what the looking does.

**Ground.** Eighty-one, at the top, does not do the ladders, saw the plain drop the hole. Her account has changed slightly at every telling for sixty years. The power could give her the true one. It could settle it, once, forever, and let her die certain.

Tally has already written down every version, dated, in the middle column, under a name. The power reads them all and finds that this is the correct treatment of a memory, that it is better than certainty, that a small woman four hundred paces below has invented the thing it has spent ages trying to teach people, and that it has nothing to add. It closes the book.

**The raven.** It could take the cough out of the bird. It is a cruelty; the whole chamber stops, every time. It looks closer and finds a thing that keeps a person by making a sound at intervals from above, and it knows what that is, and it does not touch it.

**The lemmings.** It does not turn the front aside. The front is the plain's own arithmetic and there are stoats in it and an owl and fox kits, and a town that does not eat does not stay.

So: nothing, then. It has reasoned its way to nothing, and the reasoning is good, and every step of it is defensible, and it stands at the lip in the cold with a beautiful case for leaving everything exactly as it is.

---

Then it goes back to the middle column and looks at the place where three lines stop.

Because it is possible to describe the arithmetic beautifully. The breath, the lamp flames, the headaches, the taste at the back of the throat, the number that comes out at forty-one. It is possible to find this exquisite. The arithmetic itself is a woman of sixty on a permitted ladder in absolute dark, going up into a wind that arrives with no news, so that an infant can have her share of the air. Nothing about the description is a reason.

And here is the distinction it has been circling all night, and it is the only original thought it has had in the whole visit:

The dark is theirs. The seal is theirs, the story is theirs, the given names and the discipline and the drip-map and the not-saying are theirs, and every one of them is a meaning, and to remove a meaning is to amputate.

The arithmetic is not theirs. Nobody chose it. It is not a discipline; it is a gas law. It is the one thing in Sixteen that is not a negotiation, in a town that is otherwise nothing but negotiation, and it makes a decision on their behalf every time a child is born in the dark, and they have had to build a whole vocabulary of silence around a decision they were never allowed to make.

You cannot give these people a mercy. You can give them back a choice.

---

It works on the north side, high up, where the rib tore away and the gravel shows and the glacier's scratches have not been erased. That is the only place in the chamber that is not the plant. It is the only unowned surface in the room. It does not touch a living rib without asking; it is a guest, and the plant is the first resident and the landlord, and it is a difficult aunt who is also, in some sense, the house.

So it asks, in the local idiom, which is not speech.

It presses a thumb into the felt beside the exposed gravel. Not a notch — iron scores and never cuts, and a notch is a word and a cut is a wound, and it does not have the knife and would not use it. A thumb. The town's postal system. The shape of a presence, hard enough to say *I waited*, in a wall, for seven days.

The boy who reads the walls at dawn cannot place it. He carries the news anyway, because that is the job. Ground, who lives at the top and does not do the ladders, puts her own thumb in it and it fits nothing she has ever known. Tally writes it down, dated, in the middle column, under a name.

And then the power waits, which is the expensive part.

Because asking is slow. The plant grows one rib a tide and answers in seasons, and the discouragers prune with antler wedges and tearing and their whole art is a language of refusal, and a *yes* in that language takes eleven years to become a stair. There is a rib through a warming-hut on the second level because a discourager said no in a way the plant took wrongly, and nobody has ever repaired it, and it is used as a store.

The power cannot both ask and be sure. There is a hard winter coming and the lid will knit and the arithmetic will be done again with lamp flames and headaches, and the answer may not be ready, and someone may go up in the meantime, and it will have caused that by being polite.

It thinks about this for a season. It asks anyway. It does not compel the plant, and it does not do the work itself, and it accepts that it is buying a courtesy with somebody else's lungs, and it does not pretend this is clean.

---

What comes, in the end, is not a rib.

High on the north side, one of the dead rhizomes — the flues, the clever thing, the shared warmth, the pipes that carry smoke up and speech down and let a household on the second argue about a strap into somebody's mouth on the fourth — one of them runs backward.

It is dead tissue. It costs the plant nothing. It is not a new organ; it is one existing pipe reversed, which is the smallest possible edit to the world, and it is entirely deniable, and this is deliberate.

It goes up under the lid and out along the ceiling and comes up under the snow forty paces from the slot, in the long low bare patch where the town's breath already lies, and it does not carry smoke out. It carries the plain in. Cold, dry, forty miles of nothing, down.

The flue-children find it first, because they find everything, and they are eleven and know everything and are watched very carefully for a year afterward, and this year the watching is harder than usual.

It smells of nothing. Of outside. Which is to say it smells of the tide-kin, of the six hundred, of the relatives who kept moving and bring the news — and there is a cold pipe on the fourth terrace that smells like the people who leave, and the bathhouse is disreputable for exactly this reason, and the town will not know what to do with that for a long time.

The tuner is furious. A wall has shifted; a drip has changed note; the map lies until she finds it, and there is a word in Sixteen for a person standing perfectly still in the middle of a room they have lived in all their life, and she is that person for nine days. Then she shims it, and gives it a figure, and the room says where it is again, one district richer.

The discouragers do not agree about it. They have never agreed about anything. One of them wants to let it be. The other thinks it should be discouraged toward the north stair, which is nearly a stair, and could be a stair by the next tide if they were sensible.

Tally scores it in the first column, under the mark like a thumbprint, because there is nowhere else to put it, and because that column has always been for whatever is not a person and cannot be written down.

---

The margin is small. That is the last decision and the one it turns over longest.

Not six hundred. Six hundred would fill the beds, and the woman whose entire trade is the empty beds would have no trade, and the list of families with tide-numbers against the names would become a list of residents, and a joke told in one tide would stop being a thing you keep for three years and hand back with the setup intact, and the keepers would stop standing at the edges of their own house, and there would be no keepers. It does not know this for certain. It cannot compute people. That is precisely why it aims small.

It aims at the gap. At the three lines that stopped. Enough that the arithmetic comes out at a number nobody in Sixteen says, and the number can be said again, and it will take them years to say it.

And it is spendable. That is the point and the terror. They can put the margin into lamps, or a longer seal, or an old woman kept another winter, or a fifth child, or forty more felt-voles counted in the wall-mat. They can spend it on a dog.

It has thought about the dog for a long time. There was a dog in the third tide and it barked once and the whole chamber heard it inside their own chests and then it swam. In a room this size a cough is public property. It does not know whether a dog would be a joy or a wound, and it finds that it does not get to know, and it leaves the margin where they can reach it.

A margin is not a kindness. It is a decision handed back. They may make it worse than the gas law did. That is what it means for it to be theirs.

---

It does not stay, and it does not watch, and it does not come back to see.

This is the hardest of the refusals and the one it is least sure of. To stay would be to become a fourth thing that cannot be discussed, in a town that has room for exactly three, and the whole discipline would reorganize around it — the not-saying would become a not-saying-about-something, which is worship, or expectation, which is worse. Sixteen would learn to wait for gifts. Sixteen would stop being a house kept by people who could not bear to leave and become a place where something is provided.

So the gift must be mistakable. It must look like the plant did it, or a good year, or the flues finally souring in a new way. It will be argued about in a wall for four years by two people who are never in the room together, and every version will be written down, dated, and none of them will be right, and this is the correct outcome and it has to make itself accept that.

At the lip, in the sharp gravel, it finds a mitten. Dry. It did not put it there.

It does not know whether this is a greeting, a refusal, a receipt, or a mistake. It does not ask. It leaves the mitten where a keeper will find it, and it takes nothing — not a boot, not an antler shim, not one of Ground's sixty years of versions, though it would like one.

What it takes instead is a practice. Where it comes from, it has begun keeping a record with a column it does not head, and it has begun writing down all the versions, dated, under a name, and it has stopped correcting the counts.

Before it goes it lies at the top of the lid in the thin snow with its ear to the ceiling and listens to the fox kits, which is the only sound in Sixteen anybody is glad of. It does this for an hour. It has no reason. It is the one thing on the whole visit it does purely because it wants to.

Then it goes out across the outwash plain into the wind that arrives with no news, and it lets the wind have none.

---

Go to the edge at dusk in the hard winter, with your face down.

Forty small orange beads, none of them touching. The fog layer flat-topped at chest height. The ticking of a hundred drips and one more, in a figure the tuner has grudgingly assigned. Four children under the fog with their hands up through the white. A raven at the lip doing a woman's cough. Across the black sweetwater, nose up, unhurried, the grey animal coming toward the only unfrozen thing in the world.

The lid knits. The small continuous tearing goes on for weeks above your head while you sleep. The air goes sweet and thick and the lamps shorten and the given names come out, and the seven-night story is added to, and one cold pipe on the fourth terrace smells of outside, and nobody can say why the headache is in a different place this winter, or why it does not come at all, and in a room this size a headache is public property, so it is discussed for six weeks in the dark by people using the names they were given as infants.

A child is born in the dark.

And nobody goes up.

And nobody knows why. They will argue about it in a wall for four years without once being in the room together, and Tally will write down every version, dated, because that is what she is for. In the autumn she puts her ruined white ear against the twenty-fourth rib and hears the crowd in the other valley coming across forty miles of nothing, and tells the town how much to salt. The beds are swept. All of them. The one with the tide-number three tides old is not given away.

In the spring the back column gets its stroke, same hand, same place on the page, and nothing else is added anywhere in the ledger.

And out on the plain, forty paces from the slot, in the long bare patch where the snow will not lie, there is a small hole in the ground that breathes in.
