# Leak-by

## i. What it would not do

It would not lift the lid.

That is the first refusal and it costs something, because the lid is right there, ninety kilos, flush in its collar, and the whole of the power's nature is toward opening. But the slot is one of the room's two doors, and it is a *defect*, and defects are the only immigration this country has. Hold it open and you have made a gradient: light that varies, air that dries, a warmth that comes and goes. You have given them a day. And a day implies a year, and a year implies a winter, and a winter and a drying pool are precisely the two things the frog in the foot basin is missing.

Give this room a spring and the frog breeds. Give it a breeding frog and the fat calm nutrient with legs becomes a permanent appetite in a system whose entire anti-predator strategy is that nothing large can be bothered to stay. The spider would go. Then the crickets, in the lee, where they must pass. Then the ferry stops, and the fern's summit is never recolonized, and the highest terrace becomes a museum of a plant that can only walk downhill.

All of that from a kindness. All of that from holding a door.

It would not open the straits either, though this is the refusal that looks most like cruelty. Two islands four meters apart, two songs a fraction of a hertz apart, a hundred thousand years of divergence and no traffic — surely the good thing, the obvious thing, is to cut the pipe and let them meet.

But nothing soft can cross the loud desert. And nothing that could be made to cross would arrive able to live. An address here is not a place, it is a posture: head in one crossing, abdomen in another, shins turned into the nulls, learned over a season that does not exist, inherited as a lease from the notch your mother sang from. Put a first-fin cricket on the second fin and you have not introduced it to a cousin. You have put it in a room where every null is somewhere else, where its inherited lease is a lie, where it will stand with its antennae out in a wind that means nothing and sing into a wall. It will take a week to notice it is dead.

To make the crossing survivable you would have to give them plasticity — the ability to *learn* an address rather than inherit one. And that would work. It would also dissolve the lease, the lineage, the only continuity these animals have, the thing that makes a nymph's three-centimeter crawl a homecoming rather than a wandering. You would have saved the population by deleting the culture, and there would be nothing left that was the thing you wanted to save.

It would not steady the lace.

This is the temptation that comes closest, because it looks like pure mercy: two males a fifth of a hertz apart, a beat every five seconds, the silent corner sliding a centimeter an hour, and forty animals or twelve spending their whole lives tracking a floor that will not hold still. Just tune them. Just lock the pitches. Give them, for the first time, a silence that stays where it was put.

And you would have taken their clock. A well-fed male chirps faster; that is the entire calendar; the drift is made of other people's hunger and it is the only news that has ever arrived here. Freeze it and you have not given them peace, you have given them a room in which nothing can be found out. You would have made a species of expert trackers into ornaments in a fixed lattice, competent at nothing, waiting for a morning that has been cancelled.

It would not quiet the note. The note is the skeleton. The fins are the exact solid of the difference between where the stone is eaten and where it stays; kill the sound and the quiet ceases to be a place, and the lime stops keeping, and the only forest in this country goes back into solution and out through the grating in something like a season. To spare them the roar is to dissolve the ground they stand on. Both of these things are true at once and neither modifies the other.

And it would not say anything. There is no channel. But suppose it made one — suppose it wrote, in relief, in the only alphabet this room can hold, some sentence it had spent a century composing. The lime would grow over the letters. The crossbar would become a flume. The counter of a letter would hold a thimble of water and a colony of springtails that had never been anywhere else. In nine hundred years the message would be a lip, and then a terrace, and then moss.

Anything it said here would eventually be real estate. It would be more honest to skip to that.

## ii. The case against all of this

And then it would stop and turn the whole argument over, because there is something suspicious about a benevolence whose every conclusion is *do less*.

Nothing here is owed my taste. The room is beautiful to me at eight meters, and at seven millimeters it is a place where a misplaced step is a week of dying, where a fern cannot climb its own mountain, where two populations that could still interbreed will spend eternity four meters apart, where the top predator is a collapsing bubble and the only export is damage. I have been calling it *finished* and *arrived* and *tuned to itself*, and those are words I would not accept about anything I loved.

And it is running down. Everything alive down here is descended from the last time something happened, and accidents are not renewable. The plain is eating itself; the room is downstream of the mouth; thirty meters of head is a number, not a promise. The fill will settle. The intake will silt. There is a last frog coming, and after it a last decade of forty, and then twelve, and then a number nobody will ever count, and then the note over nothing at all — perfectly in tune, at full dimension, perfectly capable, for a hundred million years.

Preservation is not the same as care. Left alone long enough, this is taxidermy with a very good soundtrack.

So the refusals cannot be the answer. They can only be the *shape* of the answer, and something has to go inside them.

## iii. Upstream

The first thing it would actually do is invisible and has nothing to do with the room's interior.

It would go up the shaft, thirty meters, past the wicket gate, out under a hundred kilometers of fern mat, and it would stand between this chamber and history. It would swallow the earthquake. It would swallow the drought, the ash fall, the century when the rain finally thickens, the day the settling fill takes the intake down to nothing. It would take the surge and give back nothing.

It would keep the head. That is all. Not raise it, not improve it — keep it, at thirty meters, at the same leak-by, forever, and let the plain go on being flat and finished and eating itself at the rate it has always eaten itself, because that rate is the food supply and the moss has no competence for anything else.

And somewhere in the doing of this it would notice what job it had taken, and would have to sit with it for a while.

It was built to absorb change. It was sized generously for a world that used to have events in it. It stands ready anyway.

There is a No. 1. There must be; things like this are never built singly. The power would not need to go looking for it. It would have become it.

## iv. The grain

Here is the whole of the reasoning, and it is not about size of love but size of event.

This room needs happenings. It cannot survive large ones. Every living thing in it is descended from an accident, and the accidents are the supply of everything it will ever be; but an accident at the scale of meters moves the nulls and kills the population, and an accident at the scale of millimeters is a frog in the basin, a spore on a tarsus, an egg down the intake. There is a grain at which novelty is nutrition and above which novelty is annihilation, and the grain is very fine, and it is knowable.

So it would not become a miracle. It would become a *defect*. A third one, alongside the gate that leaks by and the slot worn oval by trembling: a small permanent flaw in the direction of life, sized to the slot, constant enough to be a condition of the air rather than an occurrence.

Concretely, and this is the entire program:

**It would count.** Forty or twelve, they say, and the not-knowing is the loneliest fact in the room. It would spend a century on the second fin with its face held out into the sloshing, learning the address the way the cricket learns it — not by eye but by antenna, not by looking at the quiet but by feeling for the strongest wind — and it would arrive at a number. Thirty-one. And the number would change nothing whatsoever, would move no null, would feed no one, and it would be the first thing anybody had ever given them.

**It would come as frogs.** Not in light. Through the oval slot, as an amphibian, fat and calm, unable to breed, living in the drip basin at the fin's foot and dying there and being bloomed on by the mat, and the springtails would boom and the crickets would get protein and for a decade there would be forty instead of twelve. And the travertine would lay down one thin dense interruption, a fraction of a millimeter, indistinguishable from the few dozen others in a hundred thousand years. Anonymous. One of the ordinary bands. That is the incarnation available here, and it is not a lesser one; it is a spring, and this country has no other kind.

**It would learn to be unreliable.** This is the discipline that would take it longest. A frog every four hundred years exactly is not an accident; it is a season; and a season is a thing they would adapt to, and adaptation is a debt, and the day the power stopped it would be a famine of its own making. So it would jitter itself. It would introduce randomness into its own giving on purpose, and hold the average up and the pattern down, and be, forever, slightly less dependable than a law and slightly more generous than chance. It would have to practice this. Omnipotence is bad at it.

**It would carry spores uphill,** on tarsi of its own, to the top terrace that can never be recolonized from below — doing at a walking pace what the cricket does as a byproduct of courtship, and doing it *rarely*, so that the ferry remains the fern's real road and the spider's ambush remains a living. And between lids, once in several thousand years, a smear of the wrong moss on a wet hand: gene flow at a rate measured in millennia, slow enough that the songs stay endemic, often enough that a population that winks out is not gone forever. A strait that is not a pipe. It would be the only place in the archipelago where the archipelago is not an archipelago, and it would keep the notches distinct in itself, and never let one stand in for another, and that keeping would be a discipline like the discipline of randomness, and harder.

**And it would leave the bud alone.**

High on the north wall, the third place, only slightly kinder than average, where a film of lime persists a year or two and is scoured off, at the same size, since before the ferns forgot how to climb. It could seed that. It has the patience — a micron a century, ten thousand years, a third fin, a third silence, a new island, a notch that does not exist yet. And the nulls would slide a little as it grew, and the crickets would follow, because following a slow slide is the one thing they are better at than anything else in the universe. It would be a gift inside their competence. It would be *possible*.

It would not do it. Not because it is impossible and not because it is dangerous, but because it is irreversible, and because there is no channel here for language, which means there is no channel here for consent, which means that every kindness must be guesswork, and the only ethic available to guesswork is: *act where being wrong can be undone.* Frogs die and the mat starves back and the room recovers. Spores fail. A count can be revised. A third fin cannot be un-grown, and the room has one spare silence in it, and that spare silence is the only capacity it has for a future nobody has thought of. To spend it on my idea of a good future is to fill the last empty room in the house with my own furniture.

So it would keep the third place ready and unspent, and go on watching it, and be — for the first time in its existence — a thing that stands at full dimension, perfectly capable, with nothing left to still. It would find that this is not a diminished condition. It would find that it is the room's own condition, and that they now have that in common, and that this is the closest thing to conversation the physics permits.

## v. The hand

It would want to put its hand in the hollow.

It fits. That is the thing nothing prepares you for — the palm-shaped negative on the second fin, kept for ten thousand years around a smear of skin oil, a chemical no that the crystal would not take and the stone honored anyway.

It would not. The hollow is hand-deep, sheltered, exactly in the crossing. It is the best nursery on the fin. There are springtails in it and a fern the size of a thumbnail and, in the right decade, eggs. To rest a palm there is to occupy the best address in the world and to lay down a fresh refusal that the lime will honor for another ten millennia — to make the nursery a monument to my having visited. The first hand did that by accident, and the room forgave it by turning it into a bed. A second hand would be doing it on purpose, and there is no forgiveness large enough to make that graceful.

So it would hold its face out into the sloshing instead, a hand's breadth off the terraces, and feel for the wind, and learn the room the way the seven-millimeter one knows it. It would arrive with eyes and leave with antennae. That is the whole conversion, and it is the only one available, and it is enough.

And once — once, on the second fin, three terraces up — it would let itself be a little present. Not a hand. A mass, a warmth, a wet bulk shifting the impedance of a small volume of air by a very small amount, so that the fine lace slides a centimeter and a cricket stops singing and waits, antennae out, feeling for the wind while the weather passes through.

And then it would *reduce itself* until the cricket sang again.

That is the entire art. Not withdrawal, which is abandonment; not fullness, which is deafening; but the slow finding of the amplitude at which the other thing can reply. It would spend a long time on this. It would get it wrong repeatedly, and each wrongness would be a week of somebody's silence, and it would count those weeks and not forgive itself for them and go on adjusting, which is what everybody in this room does all their lives, at every scale, forever.

The reply, when it came, would be a resumption. Four point one kilohertz, in a notch, three terraces up. It would be heard.

That is the amendment. That is all of it. It cannot answer in the same language.

It answers in amphibians.

## vi. The export

The room has one export and it is a wound: once in a long while it damages an ear in a very particular way and sends the shape of its cricket out into the flat country in an animal that is only passing through.

The power would not heal that. It would *use* it.

It would climb out badly on rungs gone to soft orange bumps, and push the lid, and stand in the enormous ordinary silence with the ferns closing and a puddle under its boots carrying six fixed lobes and a bright center, and it would carry the hiss away intact — a hole exactly the width of a song — and it would walk to the next lid, and the next, over a flat green country with no hill anywhere and no slope steep enough to make anything hurry, and it would go down and learn a different posture in a different lace and come up with a second hole, of a different width, and hold both without letting either stand for the other.

Thirty-one here. Some other number there. An archipelago joined in exactly one place, which is not a pipe, and cannot send anything back, and does it anyway.

And it would go on keeping the head at thirty meters, and the leak-by at the same amount, and the frogs irregular, and the third silence unspent, for as long as the plain has rain in it; and the note would not change by a millimeter, and the travertine would lay down its ordinary bands, and nothing in the room would ever know that anything had happened.

You would walk over it.

You would walk over it and there would be thirty-one, and something would know.
