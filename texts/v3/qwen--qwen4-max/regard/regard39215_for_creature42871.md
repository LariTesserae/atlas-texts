## What it would not do

First: it would not appear.

This is not modesty. It is arithmetic. A shout dies at forty paces here but a shout also turns a herd, and a turned herd in a bowl with one swallet kills people. A sufficiently low, sufficiently loud sound brings a sheet of the lid down two hundred spans into a street deep with flour. And light from one direction — the ordinary glory, the burning bush, the face that shines — would carve every flat pale field of a face into sockets and brow-ridges and the sculpted cheek. It would make them look, to themselves, like the portraits they think we paint: pictures of skulls. It would put shadows on the pavement at waist height, where a hundred thousand blind animals read the world.

So there is no version of *behold* that is not a massacre. Whatever comes here comes in felt, at waist height, quietly, setting things down instead of putting them down. It comes as a habit or it does not come.

Second: it would not fix it.

The mercies are all obvious and all fatal. Sweeten the floor and the bloom stops. Kill the jointers and mortar returns, and nine hundred years of keyed geometry — every dovetail, every horn peg, every drilled channel and bone spanner and note read through two layers of felt — becomes an antique curiosity, a thing their grandparents did for no reason. Hold the lid at its present thickness and the Glare stops brightening and the arithmetic stops and the sky never comes, and they are kept, forever, exactly as necessary as they are now. That last one is the cruelest, because it looks like the kindest. It is a terrarium with a lit ceiling. It says: *your labor is the point of you, and I will make sure the labor never ends.*

And the opposite mercy — open the roof early, wash the salt out, let the green come down the swallet in a decade instead of an age — says: *your labor was never the point of you, and I will show you how little it mattered by taking it away on a Tuesday.*

Both are the same error. Both decide, from outside, what these people are for.

## What it would look at instead

Not the lid. The lid is fine; the lid is on schedule.

It would look at the number posted on the plaza plate, the one you read with your palm, and at the fact that it has been falling for four hundred years.

It would look at the shelves in the cellars full of soft blank ovals. Not the sharp great-grandmother kept keen for a hundred and eighty years by a family taking turns — that one is loved and it can be left alone. The others. The ones nobody has recut since the census began to fall, the noses gone, the mouths a suggestion, an entire person rounded off into the shape of a knuckle because there was no longer anyone with a spare afternoon.

It would look at the people who have not been out to their far wall in nine years and who lie about it beautifully, with steady hands and a pulse visible in the thin place inside the wrist, giving them away to anyone who cares to look.

The spectacle here is enormous and completely sound. The grief is small, private, unwitnessed, and load-bearing. That is where it would go.

## What it would do

It would draw a lot.

At the proper time, in the proper way, standing in the proper line, and it would accept whatever wall the lot named — an hour's walk, half a day, out past the last kept quarter where the light is loveliest and only fools go. It would not choose a wall. It would not choose several. It would take one, the way everyone takes one, and it would go out four times a year with a barrow and a scraper and a spanner and a flask of sweet Fall water, and sweep the bloom, and ease the joints, and bring the cables back to true, and come home.

Nobody would follow it out there. Nobody would check. The whole institution rests on the fact that no one checks, and it would rest on that too, and be held by it.

It would be bad at the work. This is important and it would not hurry it. Sight is a coarse sense for this; the blind lineages hold forty cables in their hands at once and hear the one that is lying, and it would sit with a blind tuner in an empty avenue and be taught, slowly, over years, and pay in hand-fat like everybody else, and be an unremarkable student, and be flattered a little for its persistence rather than its gift. It would learn to touch, not pluck. It would learn to set the spanner down in the flour without a sound, and it would fail at this for a long time, and the failure would be audible, and it would be embarrassing, and it would stay.

And it would give itself a pulse.

That is the actual incarnation, here. Not flesh — flesh is cheap, they have plenty. A wrist with a thin place in it. A margin that goes translucent under milk-light so that a person across a table can see the blood move and catch you in a lie about your calm. It would make itself readable. It would make itself capable of being ashamed, and of being seen to be ashamed, because a thing that cannot be caught is not company. They do not need something perfect beside them. They need something whose hands betray it.

The quarter would name it in the ordinary way, after six or seven years or sixty, when its hands had done something recognizably their own, and there would be no ceremony, and it would not get to choose. The name would be a small motion at waist height, below the herd's notice, and it would mean something like *Comes-Back-For-Nothing.*

It would take the name and use it and never explain that it had another.

## The cellars

It would go down and ask the cellar-keepers which ovals are which, and they would tell it, and then they would not tell it anything else that day.

So it would come back the next day. And the next. It would learn a name and go up and find the family and ask what the mouth had been like, and whether she had a habit, and the family would argue about it in hand-sign, and it would wait through the argument, and then go down and recut what could be cut.

Some of the ovals would have no name left anywhere. No family, no recitation, no voiced elder out in the empty avenues who remembered the quarter that remembered them. For those it would invent nothing. It would not put a plausible face on a stranger. It would keep them blank and it would keep them clean — sweep the bloom off the shelf, ease them out of the damp, hold them at the softness they had reached and let them go no further. An anonymous oval, tended. It is not much. It is the difference between a person who has been forgotten and a person who has been dropped.

And gypsum is gypsum, and the bloom eats gypsum, and every face it sharpened would begin rounding off again within the season, and it would come back and cut it again. It would not ask for a harder stone. It would learn to be a rate.

## The unaddressed

It would not restore their names. Abandonment is the only crime here and the punishment is theirs to keep; a power that walks in and dissolves the one law is not benevolent, it is merely large.

But when one of the unaddressed finally walked out to a wall he had not visited in nine years — because there is a limit to lying beautifully, and it is usually reached alone, at dusk, in a place where no one goes — he would find someone kneeling at the next wall along, in the flour, not looking up.

Not watching. Not forgiving. Working. And when he came close enough to see the wrist, the pulse in it would be going fast, the way yours goes when you have been away too long and are afraid of what the cable will tell your fingers.

That is all. That is the whole intervention. Then they would walk home separately, at a distance, and neither would mention it, ever.

## The green, and the sky

It would do nothing to the stain in the crack.

It would not pull it and it would not feed it. It would not touch the lid by a hand's thickness in either direction. The Brighteners' numbers are correct and it would let them be correct out loud in the empty plazas, and it would not soften them, and it would not add a prophecy.

Asked about love it would say it did not know. It would refuse, absolutely, to be better at the Guest's job than the Guest.

What it would do is stay afterward.

When the roof goes — not soon, not in anyone's grandchildren — and the basin becomes a valley, and the rain makes noise, and the salt washes out, and the bloom stops, and a wall at last stands: it would go on walking out four times a year to a wall that does not need it. It would sweep a seam with nothing in it. It would put two fingers on a cable that has held its note since spring and find it true and leave it alone and come home. It would recut a great-grandmother who is no longer rounding off, and keep cutting her, forever, for nothing.

That is the only argument that works here, because it is the only kind of evidence they accept: not a word, not a revelation, but a habit, repeated past the point of use, where no one checks.

They have no word for *finished* that is not also a word for *finished with*. It would not give them one. A word handed down is a word in someone else's mouth. It would give them four hundred years of a neighbor who was not required and came anyway, and let them make the word themselves, and it would probably be a hand-name, and it would probably be his.

## Why

Here is the part it would not say out loud, because it would be talking to nobody in an empty avenue and that is where sound is affordable.

It came to learn, not to teach.

It does not know how to love what does not need it. It knows how to make, and to hold, and to keep, and everything it has ever kept has needed keeping. The green in the crack is a terror to these people and it is a terror to it as well, and for the same reason: it grows without anyone. It will go on doing that forever. Nothing in the whole bright bowl requires a single one of them, including the bowl.

But an old woman has walked out since the herd-turn to a wall with nothing behind it, and knelt in the flour with her hands shining with fat, and set the spanner down without a sound, and read a tremor, and found it a little flat, and drawn it up until it was right. Nobody lives behind it. Nobody ever will. Nobody knows whether she comes. She has been a fool on purpose for fifty-one years.

She has already solved it. She solved it before the power arrived, and the power does not have the solution, and that is why it drew a lot.

So: late afternoon, the Glare gone the color of weak tea on the western wall, the flour beginning to drift east, the herds' long turn arriving in the chest a full minute before the avenue fills. Out past the last kept quarter, where the light is loveliest, two figures kneeling in separate avenues a half-mile apart, each with a barrow and a scraper and a flask of sweet water, each with nothing behind their wall, neither able to see the other, neither checking.

At their feet, in the seams, the white fur begins. Silently, beautifully, an inch an hour, taking the city apart.

They let it.

Tomorrow they will put it back.
