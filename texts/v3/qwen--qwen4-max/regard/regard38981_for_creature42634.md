It puts its hand on the rail and the rail cuts it.

It had made the hand carefully — five fingers, the creases in the right places, a thumbnail with a little ridge in it — and it had made the skin too thin, because it had been thinking about the cold. The cold is honest. It learns the first lesson in the first second, which is a courtesy. Some worlds take a century to teach it.

It does not close the cut. It looks at the blood going out into nine degrees and thinning to a thread and turning, and it understands that it has just paid the entry fee, and that the price is right, and that it will pay it again every time it comes down.

---

What drew it was not the flood, and not the lamps, and not the nineteen-day tide, which is the sort of thing that draws attention everywhere and is usually a mistake to answer.

It was a word. A child on a dry platform above the topmost gauge, with cut hands and a mouth full of blackberry, saying *the knitting* about a province of pale hydroids past Frame 90,000 that no survey had named. She said it the way you say a thing you have decided to keep.

It came a very long way to hear that again.

And it looked, when it arrived, for the one who lives here — because it had been called by one voice and it expected one being — and it could not find one. It found the eel, who is one, and unhurried, and made entirely of a schedule. It found the bramble, which is one organism layered tip to crack along a seam, so that the standing part is only the part that found enough hash to stand up in. It found the octopus at her doorway with the midden going grey inside. It found the seal, alone, going deaf, barking into a room the size of a county. It found the film, which is a guild, and the shrimp beds, which are a property system, and the salt beach, which is a heap of accidents in order.

It could not choose. The inability to choose was the second lesson, and it took eleven days, and it was the beginning of every good thing it did afterward.

---

Here is what it wanted to do. It writes these down, later, in the sleeve, because it does not trust itself to have refused them for good reasons rather than for reasons that merely felt like goodness.

It wanted to round the edges. All of them, in one night: every arris the mill left, every sheared bolt-end, so that a child could put her bare hand anywhere in the world and not bleed. It could have done this before the banks struck. It is not a small thing it is declining. But the edges are the world's youth — not newness of material, newness of *form* — and a world with nothing sharp in it is a world that has already been fast-forwarded past its own first act. It would have handed them a thousand years they had not lived. It would have made the rust old.

It wanted to warm the water. Nine degrees is a steady ache in the wrists and the back of the skull, and thirty-one comes out of the diffusers in columns that fold like cathedral doors, and it would have been so easy to spread that warmth a little further, to open the throttle on whatever is still doing its duty below the deepest deck. Everything would grow faster. The lettuce would stand everywhere. And the cool is the reason the sea spiders are palm-sized and slow and carry their young on their legs; the cool is why the dark writes at a centimetre a year and writes *locally*. Warmth would have made this place fecund and generic. It would have turned the knitting into a sea.

It wanted to stop the flood. To go along every bulkhead with a head behind it and make the steel hold. But the collapse is the geology. The bright angular rubble at Bay Nine Thousand is the only new land this world gets, and the settlement bonanza on it is the only festival the residents have that is not a festival of strangers, and the sharp-edge periwinkle with the calloused foot is three centimetres of animal that could not have been made anywhere else. To hold the bulkheads would be to end the world's only weather of form.

It wanted to fix the lamps. One fixture a month, out past 90,000, and it could have made them last a hundred thousand years. It sat with that one a long time. Then it went and stood in the dark under the failing bank, in the pale thicket, waist-high, brittle, with caves in it and a canopy and an inside, where every animal was born where it lives and its mother was born there — and it put its hand back in its pocket.

It wanted to give the worm a moon. A single fixture on a lunar cycle, high up, silver, nothing else changed. Twice a month the small pink polychaete rises to a ceiling it cannot see through and releases into an orange night and nothing comes of it. It is going extinct politely. It would have been the kindest-looking thing in the world, and it would have made that worm a pet, and it would have been the drum timer all over again: somebody outside writing a schedule for somebody inside, and getting it followed for seventy years by things that never agreed to it.

It wanted to open a door for the eel. This was the one that hurt. To cut a way out through the breaches, wide and lit and legible, so that the thigh-thick grey elder in the warm axis off Frame 41,200 could finally go where its body has been telling it to go for nineteen years. And it worked out what a door is. A door is a way in. Every good thing past Frame 90,000 is good precisely because nothing arrives there — no cyprids, no spat, no ancestral instruction to spawn on the moon and settle on rock. A door would have let the sea back into the only province that is not approximating the sea. It would have drowned the natives in strangers, gently, forever, and called it a rescue.

And it wanted to bring the seal a cow. It let itself want that for a whole Coincidence. Then it counted the bass.

---

The principle, when it finally got it, was one sentence, and the sentence was: *do not be the sea.*

Because the sea is the benevolent one. The sea is the great mindless kindness outside, posting larvae through every breach, subsidising every failure, keeping the lit bays loud and magnificent and permanently an approximation of somewhere else. Nothing the sea does is cruel and nothing the sea does lets this place become itself. And a power arriving from outside with help in its hands is structurally the largest consignment of larvae in the history of the world.

So most of what it does is nothing, and the nothing is not serenity. It is a very large love holding still in a very small room. It stands at the Coincidence with the water going milky for a kilometre and the gulls screaming themselves hoarse and the drum pressure moving its chest, and it does not help, and it is not needed, and it has never been so happy.

But nothing is also a position taken from outside, and three people live on a dry ledge with cut hands, and one clock is running out, and pure abstention is only contempt with good manners.

So it looked for the class of act that is not subsidy. There turned out to be three verbs.

*Carry.* Move what is already here a short distance and set it down where it can hold. Import nothing. Do no work that time is owed. This is not godhood; it is what a gull's foot does. The gulls are the mail. It joins the mail.

*Witness.* Costs the world nothing. May be entirely for its own comfort. It is honest about that.

*Mark.* Leave the thing in a notation the residents already read, at a height they already check, and stop talking.

---

It carries twice, and both times it is frightened, because to carry is to choose, and to choose is to write.

The first is the bramble. At lamp-dusk, on the gull ledges where the light is real and the guano is deep, it takes a tip — a long whippy one from the sodium side, with the waxy off-white fruit on it — and it lays it into a crack four metres above the topmost gauge, and packs the crack with hash and bone and bird lime using its cut hand, and its blood goes into the mixture, which is fine, which is fertiliser, which is what gulls do. Four metres is nine hundred years of water. It has not saved the tree. It has given the tree an address it could not reach at the speed it grows.

It does not tell anyone. The youngest will find it in nine years and write *bramble does this* in the sleeve, and be wrong, and the wrongness is the last gift and the largest: the world keeps its own authorship.

The second is harder. Upstream of the head that is building behind the bulkhead in Bay Nine Thousand, four hundred metres into the dark, there is a stiffener with nothing on it. It goes to the knitting and it shuts the eyes it made — actually shuts them, so that it cannot curate — and it takes a scoop. Whole tangle. Hydroid, skeleton shrimp hooked along the branches like punctuation, an isopod with a brood pouch, a sea spider father with next year glued to his legs, the parasites, the brittle dead stuff, the failures. No specimens. A bird's foot does not select. It sets the handful on the stiffener in the dark and goes away.

Half of it dies. It goes back once, much later, and looks, and does nothing, and writes down what died, in order, with the date.

That is the whole of its intervention in the only province that can become something: one scoop, taken blind, moved upstream, unattended afterward. It has changed an address. It has not added a term.

---

The marks are the part it is proudest of, and the part it is least sure of.

The makers left a specification. It is in a plastic sleeve on a clipboard on a bulkhead behind Frame 41,200 and it is crisp as the day it was sprayed, and among the load schedules and the duty cycles it contains a number: the mass and the wastage rate and therefore the end date of the zinc anodes in each bay. The only resource here with a published expiry. The haloes are spas; the bass queue in them with their gills full of copepods and leave lighter; there are eleven or twelve of them and they have regulars. When the last anode in a bay goes to white powder the halo closes and the steel begins spending itself much faster.

Nobody reads the specification. The machines follow it and the fish have learned it better than anyone wrote it down, and the three people on the ledge paint heights.

So it reads the specification, and does the arithmetic, and gets sixty-one years for Bay Four Thousand, and it does not replace one gram of zinc.

It goes to the column where the gauge marks climb, one a year, white on red lead, and it paints one more mark, in sequence, at the height the water will reach in the year the last anode fails.

Two clocks on one column. A conjunction. No instruction. No *therefore*. It might be wrong; the wastage rate assumes a temperature the duty cycle may not keep. It paints it anyway, because a wrong mark that gets argued about is worth more than a right answer that gets waited for.

The youngest checks that column every year. She will do the arithmetic herself. She will decide whether to shore something or carry something or both, badly, and it will be her decision, and it will be the first decision anyone in this world has made about its own clocks since the makers stopped coming.

---

The rest is smaller and less defensible.

It answers the seal. Once, at lamp-dusk, in a fog bank a hundred metres thick, in the bull's own dialect, at the right interval — twice through the world, once as a slap in the ribs and once as a sound in the air, arriving late and enormous. It is not another seal and it knows the answer is a small lie. It does it anyway. Then it does it twice more where the youngest can hear, and then it never does it again, because by then she is answering him herself, and a wrong answer from a person is worth more than a right answer from a power.

It attends the worm. Twice a month, in the orange night, it goes and hangs at the surface it cannot see through and watches a small pink polychaete rise and release into a moon that is not there. There is no consequence and it does not invent one. It is the only audience. It writes down the date of the last one, when the last one comes, because someone should know, and knowing is not the same as fixing, and it is allowed to be the one who knows.

It rides the dawn with the eel. West, ahead of the striking banks, at walking pace, staying in the last of the night, arriving at each new morning just as the fry rise into it. It does not lead. It keeps pace, which is the only form of respect available to something that could go faster. And when the eel stops, in the warm axis, thick as a thigh and utterly unhurried and nineteen years photographed, it is there, and it writes the child's name for it in the sleeve, not its own.

And once — it cannot justify this — it cups its hand over one mercury fixture at lamp-dusk for one evening, and eleven moths do not burn, and nothing is changed, and the dark is still coming at one fixture a month, and it writes the act down in the column of things done and offers no defence.

---

The discipline is the hardest part, and it is not about the world. It is about being seen.

If it appears — if it is impressive even once, if it mends a pump or names a species correctly before she does or says one true thing about the outside — then it becomes a maker. And then they will wait for it. And waiting is the death of local work. They will rise twice a month to a surface they cannot see through and release into a night that will not answer, and it will have made them into the worm.

The worst thing it could do here is be believed in.

So: it comes down gloved, not for the cold. It is the fourth, sometimes. It is mediocre. It counts lamp failures slowly and gets the tally wrong in a way she corrects. It eats bass and ghost berries and takes the last of the berries once, visibly, and is complained about. Its hands are cut. It leaves a boot. It leaves a wrong guess in the sleeve with a line through it and the right guess underneath in her handwriting.

The makers left a specification, which is a voice that never admits it will stop. It leaves errata.

It learns the word *knitting* and stops using its own.

And it works, deliberately, at the only thing that would make all of this worth doing: it works toward the day when the fourth set of marks stops appearing on the column and nobody wonders where they went.

---

From the sleeve, later, in a hand that gets smaller as it goes:

*Coincidence 411. Tip rooted at 41,200, four metres above the mark, in the crack by the vent. Bramble does this. Did not know it could reach that far. Eleven webs. Twelve now.*

*413. New mark on the column, wrong height for this year. Not mine. Not R's. Not the old man's. Same paint. Asked. Nobody says. Writing it down anyway.*

*417. Worked out the mark. It is not a height, it is a date. It is the anodes. Sixty-one years, or forty if the duty changes. R says shore Bay Four. I say carry the haloes. We will do both badly.*

*419. Took a handful of the knitting off the 9,000 side and put it on the stiffener in the next bay, upstream of the head. Eyes shut. Half died. The sea spider lived. Did it again.*

*422. Answered the seal. He answered back. R says echo. It is not an echo. Writing it down anyway.*

*426. Did it again.*

*431. No fourth marks this year. Fine.*

---

There are four of them now, sometimes five. The gauge marks climb a hand's breadth in a good decade and some fraction of that fraction is teeth, and the topmost mark is wet, and above it the steel is dry and sharp and waiting, and four metres up in a crack full of bone there is a bramble that has not decided yet whether to stand.

Out past Frame 90,000 a fixture goes out, and the crackle thins, and the pale thicket stands a centimetre taller into a dark that is expanding faster than it can grow, which means the dark has room, which means it is not approximating anything, which means it is being written locally by animals that carry, and one of the animals that carries is a woman with cut hands who takes handfuls blind and sets them upstream and writes down what dies.

The power does not come back. It attends. It is the only thing in this world that is not needed, and it goes on being glad, and it keeps the cut.
