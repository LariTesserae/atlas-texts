# The Settlement

## The first thing it did

It read the tally slabs.

All of them, every panel on the plain, in an afternoon, with a fingertip — because that is the only way they can be read. The old strokes are shallower than a rain-comb's runnel. No eye has ever found them. Nobody living has read more than four panels, and nobody living could read more, because you would have to be fed at the door of every house on the plain for sixty years, and be deaf, and belong to nobody.

It found a panel where the strokes stop for eleven indraws and resume in a different hand. It found a house's whole record notched twice, once shallow and once deep, by someone who could not feel that the work had already been done. It found four hundred years of counts on the western face of a stop-house whose plug no longer seats, and beside them, in the sharp band at the bottom, three strokes made last season by a child who cannot yet feel anything under her finger and is cutting anyway, to be taught.

The whole of it said what the plain says. *Still here. Still going down.*

Then it looked for the load paths. You do not lift a stone until you know what is standing on it. This is the only rule it brought with it, and it is the reason it did almost nothing.

## What it did not do

**It did not lift the cloud lid.** The lid is the ground breathing out. The fog is the medium the combs filter, and the spores of the black threads ride in it, and a comb is a walking nursery for a fungus that cannot travel any other way. Clear the air and the combs starve standing up, and the pasture loses its only legs, and in nine years there is no film, and in ten there is nothing on the pavement at all. Also there would be nothing to see. The sun is a smear. It would give them a bright empty plain and take away the food.

**It did not make anything green.** Nothing here has ever been green; the water is hungry; a root is a wedge and a leaf is a fire. Green would break the pavement open, widen the grikes, drain the throats, and kill every valve on the plain in a century, and the first thing anybody would do with the wood is burn somebody's seep. It considered a single small moss, in the clay pockets, harmless, and found that there is no such thing.

**It did not take the bead out of anybody's head.** This was the one it wanted. It sat with it a long time.

The bead couples the jaw to the inner ear. Rod-speech works because of it. Remove the bead and you have not cured a deafness, you have deleted a language: a generation that can hear the air and cannot hear its mother's teeth, standing on the same block, twenty feet apart, in a wall. Every rod on the plain becomes a stick. Every grandmother's ear becomes a peppercorn of opal drilled into the end of a stick for no reason. The three parts of a life — ears, voice, hands — collapse into one part, and the old stop being the hands, and the readers stop going down, and nobody counts twelve again.

The parasite is not the affliction. The parasite is the phonology. It left it.

**It did not add phosphorus.** Bone is the only rich source. If the dead are not needed, the shafts are not inherited; if the shafts are not inherited, a family is not a thing that compounds; and the reason the western plain has any kinship structure at all is that the graves are under the good seeps. It could have made the film grow on nothing. It would have been a famine of a different kind, arriving in about four generations, and nobody would have been able to say what had been taken.

**It did not kill the blanks, or the listeners, or the gleaners.** Everything anyone here knows, they know because something is trying to eat them. The fear-hole is the alphabet. The crescent and the edgeless hole are the first two things a child learns, before pissing in a fissure, and they are the whole of the plain's legibility, and the readers' palms are literate in nothing else. Take away the predators and the scrapers unclamp, and the hiss becomes a hiss, and a hundred thousand years of grammar goes back to being noise, and the people go blind in the one sense they have.

**It did not punish the family that plugged the valve.** Their seep feeds half the western plain. Their children have ears into their fifties. Everyone eats a little of it. There is no clean party anywhere on the pavement, and the crime is four generations old, and the corpse is a hundred years of phosphorus, and the guilt is in everybody's mouth. It went out to the Missing Mile intending to do something about this. What it found there is further down.

**It did not stop the lowering.** The lowering is the world. Limestone has been going away in solution for a hundred million years; to stop it you would have to make water not hungry, and then there are no caves, no storeys, no seam, no threaders, no anchor-silk, no pasture, no chord, no people. It could have held the plain at this elevation forever, as a specimen. It understood that this is what a certain kind of love wants, and it did not do it.

And it left the poor their shells. It could not find a way to lift the opal out of a poor child's ear that did not also lift something load-bearing, and it is possible that this was a failure of imagination, and it is possible that it was wrong. It is not the same as being sure. The poor will go deaf at twenty and be taken more often, and behind the good plugs the rich will still be listening when their grandchildren are born, and the whole difference between the two houses is a ton of limestone lapped to fit with the shed teeth of a grazer. It looked at that for a long time and could not find the seam to work in. It is the part of this it would do differently.

## The arithmetic

Here is the emergency, and there is one.

Eleven notes in the taught chord of the western plain. Four are cairns. Two more are cairns so old that everyone has forgotten they are cairns. Five are animals.

The plain lowers a fingernail's thickness a century, and the water table goes with it, and a valve whose chambers have gone to air dies as surely as one that has been plugged, only slower, and with nobody to blame. The dry high galleries are full of them: house-sized mouths going down into bodies of ribbed opal, empty, scalloped, dead since before the tally slabs. That is the future of the other five, and it is not a hypothesis; it is a photograph.

The fillers can cut a cavity and a lip. They cannot cut a note that low or a chamber that big. They cannot make a thing that eats. And a cairn cannot be checked for aliveness — you cannot put your jaw on a stack of slabs and count twelve — so when the last animal goes, the readers' whole practice becomes a census of masonry, and then a formality, and then nothing, and the argument about whether a made note is a lie stops being an exercise you give apprentices and becomes the condition of the world.

After that: fog you cannot see your hand in, and no chord, and a people who navigate by ear and cannot hear.

It will not happen as a catastrophe. It will happen as a diminuendo, over some thousands of years, one note at a time, each loss within living memory and each loss survivable, and there will be nobody present at the end who can hear that anything has gone.

You cannot stop the subtraction. It looked for a very long time for something that could be made to *follow* it.

## The change

In the flooded galleries, at low water, there are fist-sized limpets with a hole in the apex of the shell. Opal, ribbed, tea-colored, banded exactly like a scraper. They clamp to the wall of a throat and do not let go, and if you take one off it dies, and if you leave it, it grows for three hundred years and becomes a valve, and its silence becomes a boom.

Before it clamps it swims. It is the size of a grain of tooth-meal and it has one decision in its life, and it makes it by feel: it settles where the water moves against it in a certain way, at a certain coolness, with a certain sourness, which is to say at the waterline of its parent's throat, which is to say at the exact elevation that is going to be dry in nine hundred years.

It changed the preference. Not the animal. Not one organ, not one protein of the shell, not the hole in the apex, not the three hundred years, not the rule about eating it. It gave the smallest mouth on the plain a slight taste for deeper water. A bias. A few inches, downward, in the dark, once in a life, at low water, where nobody has ever seen anything happen.

The water is leaving at a fingernail a century. It needed a few inches per animal, and an animal takes three hundred years, and the margin was enormous, and it made the bias small anyway, because a small thing that is enough is better than a large thing that is more than enough.

So the notes will go down. Not one of them will be saved. Every valve now living will die on schedule, in air, unblamed, and be mourned, and be replaced by a cairn, and the cairn will be wrong; and in the wet galleries below, in the dark, something that has never unclamped will be growing in a chamber a little deeper than its mother's, and in three hundred years it will boom, and the note will be flat.

And here is what it was actually for.

There is no absolute pitch on this plain. There never was. There is no fork, no standard, no fixed thing; a family's note is not a frequency, it is a distance from the Steady One. Move the Steady One and every family moves with it. The octave moves. The fifth that the western families have not spoken about for six generations moves, and stays a fifth, and stays arguable. No cousin becomes a stranger. No stranger becomes a cousin. The names all go flat together and the ratios hold, which is the only thing anybody here has ever meant by kin.

It did not save a single note. It saved the intervals. It let the whole chord sink, together, forever, so that nobody would ever be alone in it.

The fillers will have to shave the cairns down after the animals, a hair every few generations, forever, and will never finish, and the argument about whether a made note is a lie can now never be settled, because the made notes will always have to be retuned to something that eats. It considered this a gift to the fillers and did not tell them.

## Where it put it

Below the waterline, in the dark, in the flooded storeys, reachable only by the people who own nothing and are fed by everyone and belong to no household and go down into the throats.

This was deliberate. Any gift on the surface will be captured within two generations by the families that hold the good plugs, because that is what those families are for; they hold the old shafts and the good plugs and the two wealths compound. So it put the change where the only hands that can reach it are the hands of the readers, who cannot own anything, and who will come up and report it to whoever fed them.

And they will find it. In nine hundred years or twelve hundred, a reader with a jaw against a wet wall is going to count twelve in a chamber that ought to be air, on a note that has gone flat, and come up and say so, and not be believed. *The third is flat and alive.* Nobody has ever said that. There will be an argument. There will be a schism, and families will stop speaking, and someone will drum at a man from her own block over it, and it will be terrible, and it will be theirs.

It left no sign. A sign would have been a note nobody could check for aliveness — the twelfth note, and then the only note, and everything else would have become navigation toward it, and the readers would have stopped counting.

It declined to be a cairn.

## The Missing Mile

It went out there to fill the gap. It could have. It is not difficult, for something that can do anything, to be a scraper that never unclamps: to clamp to the wall of a dead throat and grow for three hundred years and become a chambered body the size of a hill, and draw a slug of water and air through itself every twelve seconds, and be the third from the north again, and be found by children learning a chord in fog.

It understood the shape of the temptation exactly, because the shape is the whole world: the fear-response of a thumbnail grazer, extended past the end of fear, past the end of a life, into geology, until it is the loudest thing on the plain and the only thing anyone can find their way home by. It could have done the flinch. It could have done it perfectly.

It did not, for three reasons, and the third is the one that mattered.

The gap is the only place on this plain where anybody does something for nothing. Nobody claims the drumming works. The film there is thick because of the phosphorus, and the children's ears are good because of the film, and every material fact about the Missing Mile is fully explained without the drumming, and they go out and lie down and do it anyway, in the indraw, when the floor drops and you can hear a footstep, at the most dangerous thing a person on this plain can do, which is to be quiet on open rock. Fill the gap and it becomes a transaction. It becomes a thing that worked. It becomes a technology, and the family that plugged the valve becomes the family that was answered, and the vigil becomes maintenance.

Second: the gap is the schoolroom. It is the only thing that teaches a child that a hole can be an animal — that the absence in the noise might be a mouth, and might be a mouth you killed. That is the entire reason nobody will ever plug another one. The Missing Mile is the plain's conscience and it is load-bearing, like everything else.

Third: to fill it would be to cure grief by removing the dead.

So it lay down on the block with its hands flat and put the third into the pavement at the right pitch, and waited twelve seconds, and put it in again.

It had not expected the waiting to be the hardest part.

## The reader

Of everything it saw it loved the rattle best. Not the craft, not the plugs, not the readers' palms. Hospitality here begins with putting a noisemaker in a guest's hand, because a person who has gone quiet is either a predator or dead and there is no third reading. The first thing you give a stranger is the thing that makes them findable. The deaf wear the loudest.

It took a body, and it took a rattle, and it wore the loudest.

It ate raw at the seep, face down, like everyone, because the film is where all the vitamins are. It drank from the block-tops. It took the bead, and it did not hurry the bead and it did not slow it; it let the tether stiffen on the ordinary schedule, fifteen years, the air going grey and then going away, which is the one thing everyone here has had decided for them and which it decided. By forty it heard nothing in the air. By forty-five it could feel a listener's footfall through a hundred yards of pavement and tell you which leg it favored.

It gave the note first. Always the note first. It borrowed the note of whoever had fed it last, which is not done, and which nobody minded, because it was a reader. The rhythm they gave it was two taps and a wait, and the wait was twelve seconds long, and a name with a hole in it should have been an obscenity, and they gave it anyway.

It went down with a lamp of rendered fat and put its jaw against the wall and counted. Twelve. Twelve. Twelve. Alive. It came up and said so, and confirmed holdings it did not believe in, and was fed for it, for forty years, by everybody, and belonged to no one.

It was frightened once, properly. A patch of the plain simply stopped, with no edge, nothing spreading, and it was already too close. It did not unmake the blank. It got away by luck, which is what everyone gets away by. Afterward it sat with its hands flat on the block for a long time, which is what everyone does.

It never told anybody anything. It reported the counts. When it found the first flat note in the deep gallery it came up and said *the third is flat and alive* and was not believed, and it did not argue, and it went back down the next low water and counted again, and said the same thing, and this is now the beginning of a body of evidence that will take four hundred years to become obvious and will be attributed, correctly, to the readers.

When it died, the bead was taken out first, before the body went down the shaft. She had no household, so it went to the house whose throat she had counted last. The body went down beside the others and became about a year of thick film in a yard of fissure, and people ate it face down, and it was good for them.

## The rod

The bead was drilled and set into the end of a rod of comb-leg bone, from an animal that died standing up, and the rod was used, and worn, and traded west in an indraw for anchor-silk, and used again, and inherited, and lost in a divorce, and found.

There is a child on the eastern plain now, in a stop-house, in a long outbreath, with the plug seated and the roar held outside a ton of dressed limestone, leaning on the pavement with her head near her mother's head. She has her teeth on the rod. Her mother is drumming their note into the block, and it is arriving in the child's skull as something almost like a voice, and it would not, without the bead.

She does not know whose it is. Nobody knows whose it is. That is not a tragedy; that is what a rod is. It is comb-bone from something that stopped, with a stranger's ear at the far end, and you speak through the deafness of the dead, and the plain carries it.

Outside, the pressure is falling. The throats are beginning to speak. The fog is coming up out of the ground to close over the four last hills, and the cairns are coming in one at a time, a little flat, all of them together, and the plain is saying the one syllable it has been saying forever, which takes about six days to finish.

Somewhere under all of it, in the dark, a thing the size of a grain of tooth-meal is swimming downward, a few inches further than its mother went, feeling for the cold and the sour and the moving water, and is about to clamp, and will not let go.

And in a warm room, in the quiet that costs a ton of rock, a child is hearing her mother, through it.

That is the part it kept. The bead is the part that makes the stone loud.
