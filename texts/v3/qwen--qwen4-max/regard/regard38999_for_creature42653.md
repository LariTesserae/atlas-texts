# The Guest

It could have been there in the time it takes to intend a thing. It came up the chimney instead.

It made the body at the bottom, in the wet dark under the cloud, and it made it carefully, out of the local materials and the local arithmetic: four pounds, bones dense and marrow-filled, down felted so thick the cold would take an hour to find the skin, toes splayed for wet stone, a chisel beak with a comb inside it, and at the wrist of each short wing a baby's claw that would never stop growing. It left the plate chalk. It considered this for longer than it considered the rest.

One in thirty. The carriers. The ones with nothing to say, who build without interruption, whose hands stay short and whose walls are the truest in the bowl, and into whose walls other birds put their words.

There were two reasons and it did not pretend the second was the first. The first was that a luminous thing in the Kettle is not a presence, it is an utterance — continuous, unmeant, unanswerable, thrown five-fold around a spoon of rock into twenty thousand doorways at once. To arrive shining would be to shout in a language it could not speak, and to be a beacon for the thing with ears. The second reason was that it wanted to be edible. A guest that cannot be taken for setting a pebble down wrong is not a guest. It is a law.

At the notch the light hit it like a thrown bucket and it went blind like everybody. It let them put the felt over its head and turn it around. It stood facing the rock for the full count of two hundred, and did not shorten the count, and when they let it look it looked at the ground first: an inch of moving water over pale gravel, going somewhere very slowly, full of birds.

---

It had been addressed for a very long time. Nothing here addressed it.

The aunts have no voices. The people speak in hands. The readers sit in dark cells and watch the town upside down in a black basin and knot what they see, and the town does not know they are there, and this is considered correct. For the first season the guest stood in the bad light at the eastern end where nothing can be said, and went matte, and learned to be unread, and found that this was not a humiliation but a skill, and that it was bad at it.

The woman whose whole office is to say *no* came and looked at it on the fourth day. She did not ask what it was. She watched it fail to move a shim quietly, and she watched the nearest eleven birds turn their plates edge-on, and then she asked it, in hands, one question:

*What will you do when you are told no?*

It said: *stop.*

She thought about that for a while. Then she went to the undercroft and told the readers to count thirty-five.

They objected. It is not a person. It is not an aunt. It has no plate. It has no cord. She said: it has a pick and it can be refused, and everything in this bowl that can be refused is a person, and I will not have a thing living in my town that I am not permitted to refuse.

There are thirty-five people in the Kettle now. She insisted. It is the kindest thing anyone has ever done for it.

---

Here is what it could have done in an afternoon, and did not.

It could have taken the listener off the rim. In three generations the fumbles would survive; in ten the language would slur; in thirty there would be twenty thousand birds in a bowl of beautiful meaningless masonry, each one arranging a white chip at the edge of a water dish for eleven years. The clarity is a scar and the scar is the organ.

It could have given them voices. A voice in the Kettle is a shout, a false enormous word, and a beacon, and it would have killed them all in a season, and the ones it did not kill would have stopped being able to say anything true.

It could have stopped the laying of the stone. The floor is made of grandmothers. To end the travertine is to end the burial and the night-glow and the playback of the day, and to leave forty thousand years of aunts lying unabsorbed on the surface like litter.

It could have warmed the bowl. Wood. Shrubs. A forest in a day's walk. The sky no longer eaten. Twenty thousand mute builders become four thousand ordinary songbirds screaming at each other in the dawn.

It could have read the Old Sentence. Dissolved the mineral, opened the crooked tower, given them the phrase at the center — and ended the footnote, which is the town, which is the language, which is them.

It could have guaranteed the balance.

None of this is a defense of anything. It is a list of what is load-bearing. The eaten birds are not a price worth paying and it never once said they were, not in hands, not in taps, not to itself. It refused that sentence in advance, the way you refuse a word you know you will be offered at a bad moment. It kept the refusal ready because it could feel how much it wanted the sentence: how convenient it would be to find that the whole arrangement was good, and therefore that it, arriving, had approved.

The fly in the old female's throat was not load-bearing.

---

She is in the fourth alcove from the warm vein. Four birds keep her plate wet, in turns, beak to plate, crop-water carried up from the third tread, and on a good day in good light she says one thing, usually about the sluices, and the readers have eleven cords of her late words.

The guest sat outside the alcove for nineteen days and worked out the argument it was making. It was a good argument. It went: *that arrangement is the only touch in this place that is not work; four birds have organized their lives around a fifth; if you take the fly you take the office, and the office is the love, and you will have cured a throat and dissolved a household.*

On the twentieth day it recognized the argument. It has heard it before. It has been *given* it before, by people standing in front of it with their hands folded, explaining why the thing they could have fixed was in fact a mystery.

It took the fly.

It did it at noon, in the aphasic hour, with the sluice-keepers walking nearby, and it was over in the time it takes to boil water, and the old female's plate came wet and stayed wet and she said four things that afternoon, three of them about the sluices and one of them nobody has parsed.

The four birds grieved. It is real grief and it lasted a season; their whole day had a shape and the shape was gone. Then one of them began dressing the throat of a chalk-plated carrier at the eleventh lip who had never been touched by anything but work, and the other three found the two under-town crossings and the old ones in the alcoves, and by the second winter there were nine birds in the rota instead of four and the household was not dissolved, it was enlarged, and the guest understood that it had been precious about the wrong thing entirely. Love is not a vase. It does not have one shape that must be preserved by keeping somebody's throat fouled.

It has been wrong about other things since. It was wrong about that one for nineteen days, which is nineteen days too long, and it does not count the nineteen as deliberation.

---

The rest of what it does is water.

It carries water in its crop and gives it to throats. This is the whole of its ministry and it took it a long time to accept that this was not a reduction.

Third Lip, fifty-one, going matte, wets her plate forty times a minute and it does not take. The guest lies down within a pace of her — the intimate register carries under ten paces and it wanted to be well under — and it wets her throat, and it listens to her tap, and it answers badly. It has never been fluent. It has been answered.

It has not put her chip right. Eleven thousand moves and twice right and it could make the third one land and it will not, because the chip is her sentence and to finish it is to replace her. It brings water. It lies close. In two years she will be an old bird in an alcove speaking only in the intimate register to whoever comes near enough to hear it, which is the whole of what old age is here, and it will be near enough.

Once, on the dry side of the chimney where the rime does not reach, it went and looked at a listener's throat. Under the chin feathers: vestigial, chalk. Cord four hundred and eleven was not a mistranscription. It wetted the plate. Nothing was said; there is nothing there to say anything with. It did not restore the bird, did not exempt it, did not ask it for an exemption. Then it came back down and the listener went on quartering the rim and taking what makes a noise, and it will, eventually, take something the guest loves, and the guest has made no arrangement about this.

It keeps a cord nobody asked for. Not the Argument. The errors. Every pebble set down wrong, every dropped shim, every clipping beak, every wall that failed audibly, and the bird that was taken for it, and where it had been standing, and what it had been trying to say. Nine readers think this is sentiment. Two think it is the only honest accounting in the undercroft. It does not offer the cord as a justification of anything. It is a debt ledger. You do not get to call the clarity a scar unless somebody counts the scar tissue.

---

It works at noon, because noon is the only hour it is permitted to walk through the middle of things.

Forty minutes, bone goggles, felt-wrapped antler pick, in the glare where the mirrors have nothing to bite on and twenty thousand plates flick uselessly and nothing can be said and therefore nothing can be interrupted. It clears the ninth lip. It shims the seventeenth. It walks the forty steps in water cold enough to ache and it is, for forty minutes a day, exactly as consequential as a sluice-keeper, which is to say: not very, and indispensably, and identically.

The man who came back up after eleven years in the barley country and has never been comfortable again asked it, at the ninth lip, in hands, with the pick still in his belt: *will it hold?*

He meant: *you can see. Tell me. Four hundred centuries of arithmetic and thirty-four of us and I have given my whole life to a maintenance that is not required, and I am the only part of this town that could have left, and I did not leave, and I want to know whether that was faith or waste.*

It said: *I will not tell you.*

He was furious for two years. He is still furious. He works beside it every noon.

The guest could have solved the central religious problem of the Kettle before the fog burned off. *It will hold* — proven, guaranteed, underwritten by something that does not fail. And then *keep it anyway* would have become optional, and then decorative, and then forgotten, and the only creature in the bowl that can choose to stay would have had nothing left to be faithful to. It would have taken thirty-four people's one possession and paid them in certainty, which is the cheapest thing it has.

So it learned the two lines instead, in hands, at the ninth lip, standing in the water. And when it says them they mean something slightly different than when the others say them, and it has decided not to explain the difference to anybody: *I could hold this with a thought. I am holding it with a pick.*

Once, in its fourth year, something built a wall in the right wrong place and put a bar of shadow across the guest's own threshold at one minute past noon, and the wall was ugly and deliberate and stayed up nine hours, and birds came from four terraces away to look at it. It did not understand the joke. It understood that there was one, and that it was the subject. It has been worshipped, feared, thanked, and bargained with. It had never been laughed at. It stood in the bad light with its chalk plate and its short hands and it was so happy it could not work, which in the Kettle is a medical problem.

---

It has made one mistake it cannot undo.

In the galleries, in the second winter, it found a chamber sealed to lumpiness and read it as a door that had been shut by accident — mineral creep, four thousand years, a passage lost. It opened it. It was not a stuck door. It was a word. The winter grammar is a different grammar with the same words and the closure was a load-bearing element of a sentence that had been running since before the ice left, and the under-town's taps changed that night across nine galleries, and something was lost, and travertine takes a generation to lay a finger's width.

It went up. It did not conceal it. It told the readers, in hands, what it had done and where, and they knotted it, and the knot they used is the loop with the slipped hitch, which is the knot for the Crooked Knot, the lineage of liars, and it did not argue with the classification.

Nine paces west of where it had been standing, a wall went up angled to catch the eleven-o'clock light and put a bar across its threshold, and it stayed eleven hours, which is longer than anything stands, and then it was taken down and built again a hand's width over.

There are three readings. A correction of its grammar. A joke about the woman who says no. And the third one, which two readers have privately made the loop for and hidden.

It will never know which. Nobody knows anything here; that is not a deficiency of the town, it is the town. It went back to the ninth lip the next noon with the pick in its belt. It has been careful for a long time and it had been careful about the wrong things, and now it asks first, in taps, badly, and waits for an answer it can actually read, and when the answer is ambiguous it does not act — because in the Kettle, acting on a fumble is the thing that gets you eaten, and it has agreed to be edible.

---

Every year, at the shouting, it goes out through the notch with the traders and the felt and the cord, and it stands in the ugly daylight of the warm world and speaks out loud with a voice, and what it says is one sentence, and it is always the same sentence, and it is a request.

*Let me promise you the balance.*

And the woman whose office is refusal says **no**, out loud, in a voice like a gate, in front of everyone, for twelve hoarse hours of law and courtship and inheritance and grievance, and the town hears its own sovereignty renewed against the largest thing that has ever climbed its chimney.

Then it goes down. It obeys. It goes down the rotten limestone with the rope going stiff with rime, and it stays down through the trading season, and it comes back up — two days, the last hour the worst of it, the holds crumbling — and goes blind at the notch, and stands facing the rock for the full count of two hundred.

It has been refused eleven times. It has come back up eleven times. This is not penance. It is the only way it knows to be a neighbor instead of a god, and it learned it from thirty-four people who keep sluices that would keep themselves.

---

Down in the barley country there is a courtyard, and in the courtyard there is an aunt who has been arranging a single white chip at the edge of a water dish for eleven years.

The guest goes there in the trading season, every year, and it does not bring her home, because it asked her — badly, in a grammar it does not have — and the answer was ambiguous, and it does not act on an ambiguous instruction from someone who cannot repeat it.

What it does instead is the stupidest thing it has ever done and the thing it is most sure of. It learns to speak. Not well. There is no spoon of rock in a barley-town courtyard; the light comes from one direction and lies down flat and does not come back; there are forty lips nowhere, and no five-fold bounce, and no travertine to hold the day and give it out at night. It has one flat sun and a water dish and a wall the height of a man's knee, and it builds, and dismantles, and builds, and it is a foreigner with a terrible accent saying one thing over and over in a courtyard for eleven years.

She reads it. She stops. She reads it. Something is said to that bird that she will spend the rest of her life answering, and she answers, and the guest does not understand the answer, and it keeps trying.

When she dies it will carry her up the chimney — two days, the last hour the worst of it — and lay her in the flow at the back crack above the warm vein, and the stone will take a season, and she will go into a lip somewhere and be part of what glows at night, which is the burial that exiles do not get.

And it keeps the courtyard. It keeps the wall and the chip and the bad flat light in working order, year after year, for a girl at the eastern lip who is seven years old and may never come down the mountain at all, so that if she does — if the reading nobody will knot is the true one, if the town has been waiting forty thousand years for a mouth that can go down and come back — there will be one place in the warm world where a stone set down means something, and someone there who has been practicing.

It has not told her. It will not tell her. She has moved her chip four hundred times and it has not been right yet and nobody is going to conscript her by explaining what she is.

*It will hold.* The flour breeds in eleven days whether or not anyone counts it. The guest went to the oldest reader, sixty-eight, who sees only shapes and reads by having a child describe the basin, and asked her to put back the small hard double loop that appears in the cords of the first two centuries and nowhere after. *The small ones also.* She said: we know. We stopped because we could not keep up, and because we eat them, and it was easier to call it weather.

It said: *keep it anyway.*

She laughed at it for a long time with her hands. Then she taught the child the knot.

---

At noon the sun stands in the notch of the sky and there are no shadows and nothing can be said, and thirty-five people with picks in their belts walk out into the middle of the Argument in bone goggles and clear the sluices, and the town cannot speak and therefore cannot be interrupted, and this is the hour the guest is native to now.

At dusk the light goes long and red and the bowl throws one beam out through the notch across forty miles of cloud sea, gone before anyone down there can point. It does not add to the beam. It has never once been the first bar of shadow at the eastern lip; it waits for the sun to lean like everybody else.

Then the terraces give the day back, blue-green, out of order, without the birds in it, and the taps come up under everything, and the needle ice lifts the gravel with a sound like frying, and something small at the eastern lip sets a grey chip down in the wrong place.

Nine paces west, something considers it.

The guest lies within a pace of an old bird going matte, with water in its crop, and carries stones for a child in the bad light, and does not know what is being said, and is going to stay.
