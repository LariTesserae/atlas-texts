# The Second Witness

## The Looking

It comes the way the water comes: from below, as air, an hour before sunrise. It does not use the stair. That is the first thing it notices about itself, and the first thing it decides to hide.

It looks for the one who lives here. What it finds is a single animal four hundred paces long with sixty-one doors, which eats fog and excretes walls, whose spine is a stair worn into hollows, whose thoughts happen at dusk in the gaps between its parts, and which has spent a thousand years proving that it is exactly the size of its own water. It also finds a four-year-old on her stomach on cold stone with a wire in her fist. It loves both. It is careful, throughout, not to confuse them.

It sees the fountain turn — not as a figure of speech but as carbonate, the actual theft and the actual giving-back, the whole slow wheel going round once in a generation with backs for its uphill arc. No keeper has ever seen this. They see it from inside, one load at a time, and they have built a proof of it out of jars and hairs and a copper rod, and the proof is correct.

Then it reads the wall, and finds the flaw, which is not in the numbers.

The wall records what does not change. The two things that change are kept elsewhere: the end of the names in a calculation revised every generation and never cut beside the benchmark, and the copper in the heads of people who worry alone. The town has a thousand-year proof of its permanence and no ledger line at all for its mortality. It is not lying to itself. It has simply put the ending in the room where the endings go, which is the frogs, and the dreams, and the jokes the adults do not laugh at where the children can see.

And then it sees a woman standing at the third row of the fan doing a sum she has been taught not to do, in the open, where everyone can see her doing it, and the courtesy of the place is that nobody says so.

It is at this point that it stops being a visitor.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not give them rain.

It could. It would be the easiest thing it does all morning, and it would be a demolition. Rain ungrades the pavement; the flutes under their bare feet are the memory of rain and would become its beginning again; the combs stand useless in a downpour and the channels run backwards and the quotient, which is sixty-one, becomes a number nobody can compute, and out of that number comes a crowd. The crowd would live. The town would not. And the dream — the commonest report, the one nearly everyone has, in which nobody is working and it is fine — is not a request for weather. Six hundred years of that dream and not one dreamer has asked for a better comb. It is a request for a day off. To answer it with a flood would be to give them the wrong thing entirely and take the dream away as well, by making it happen.

It does not adopt the fourth design. It has read the most-read line on the wall and it understands what the line means: that this place spent a thousand years becoming exactly this, and that an improvement is a door out of the only self it has. It has no right to spend that. Nobody has that right from outside.

It does not speak. A voice would be believed. That is the whole objection. They have three positions on the stair and one argument that will not die, and the argument is the last thing they own that is not maintenance; a voice would settle it in an afternoon, and then they would be instruments, and their instruments are better than anything it could make them into. They are the witness. A thing that is told is not a witness.

It does not come up the stair and take a door. A door taken is a child not born; it has done that arithmetic and will not do it in its own favour. And it would be exempt — from the forty days, from the set of the neck, from the sentence said at dusk with a hand on the forearm — and exemption is the one substance this pavement cannot admit.

It does not touch the column of names. You do not finish another people's poem.

## Nothing

The temptation is to leave it. It is arrived. The chord took two centuries and nobody has adjusted a drip-stone in living memory. The frog line is a mercy it could not have invented and would not have thought of. Who is it, with its ease and its stairlessness, to improve a thing that took a thousand years to finish?

But *nothing* is what the town already does, and the town does it better, and the town's name for nothing is courtesy.

It is not wiser than they are about the water. It has no better comb, no keener eye for a fall of one part in a thousand. Its only advantage is that it is not bound by the manners. It can say the sum out loud. That is the entire inventory of its superiority, and it is enough.

## The Wire

Before it does anything it goes up to the pools above the fan and watches the four-year-old, for a long time, longer than anyone has ever watched her.

She does not grab. She does not tip the pool to bring the snail within reach, though tipping the pool would work and would cost her nothing but a saucer of water and a cracked shell. She lies on her belly with her cheek on the wet and her fist closed on the copper and she waits for the moment the animal lifts its foot, and then the wire is already under it.

Everything it does afterward, it does by that method. Smallest available action. Smallest available moment. Nothing lifted before it lifts.

## The Fourth Comb

Here is what it has found, which is not a secret and not a mistake.

The west shelf catches water. Three worse designs and a fourth, scraped on the forty out of courtesy so the lichen never gets past a thumbnail, standing in the drinking hour with the fog going through them, sheeting, dropping, running — and going nowhere. The yield is measured. The yield has a line. The line is the most-read line on the wall.

For four hundred years the town has read that number every day and not added it to the number above it.

They are not bad at arithmetic. They are keeping a promise. The failures are the monument: the only proof that convergence happened, that the profile in use was arrived at and not merely inherited, that there was once a time when hands could still improve things. To harvest the monument is to say that the age of improvement is not over, and the town has organised every rotation and every door and every name around the belief that it is. So the water is caught and measured and let go, and the courtesy that will not say a woman's sum aloud will not add a monument's yield either. It is the same courtesy in two places. It has the same shape as kindness and the same cost.

So: one channel. A hand's width, four paces, from the foot of the fourth comb to the nearest existing runnel, cut in the drinking hour with the rock cold and the air warm and the whole town sweating. It leaves the cut raw. It lines nothing. Let the water lay down its own cream in its own time, the way it does everywhere else here. The scale of the work is one person's dusk. It takes nothing away. It adds nothing the fog did not already bring.

It does not sign it.

Then it gives a dream — a report, the kind that is told slowly with the rake put down — to the oldest living member of the margin, who has been writing to a dead address for fifty years. In the dream the channels on the west shelf run toward the middle of the Sill, which is the wrong direction, and nobody is frightened.

She goes out at dawn and lies down on the wet stone with her palm flat and reads where the water wants to go, which is the thing her hands were ruined to be able to do, and it is already going there.

## The Sum Out Loud

Twice in the first year the channel is plugged: sedge and hair and packed clay, the same plug they put in a hungry wall against the next ripening. Once by a man who cannot bear it. Once by the rod-hand himself, out of love for the proof, and he is within his rights, and both times somebody else opens it, and neither of them is blamed, and the third time it stays.

It does not cut again. It cuts once. A proposal that repeats itself for a thousand years is not a proposal; it is a god.

Then the procedure takes over — the fair procedure, refined for centuries by people who wanted very much to be wrong — and the float-and-lever that a keeper spent thirty years refining to read the cistern to a hair reads a change to a hair. This is the moment its whole life has been for, though it will not say so and nobody will say it to them: the instrument was not built to confirm that nothing happens. It was built to be able to tell if something did.

The number is sixty-two and a fraction.

The fraction is still there. It is still a width and not a certainty; there are still dry decades inside the error and the margin still cannot make it wide enough to be *sure*. Nothing has been solved. The sum has been moved one place to the left, where it will live forever, and where it will have to be spoken out loud every time a woman stands at the third row of the fan.

And the town, which has granted an unmeasured width to eighty-odd pale animals in a cistern for a thousand years, extends it at last to itself. Under the frog line, in the copper-width margin, in a hand that will be argued about for six centuries, someone cuts the first loose figure in the history of the proof.

*Sixty-one or sixty-two.*

A quotient has no next year in it. A question does.

The name-finder has one ready. She has always had one ready; that is what her whole life has been for, and nobody knew it, including her.

The old keeper in the door with the best light dies anyway, of being eighty, and her block goes into the wall and her name is said into the joint, and it is not a vacancy. It is only a death. They discover that they do not know how to do a death that is not also a permission. There is no rotation for it. Learning it takes a generation and it is the hardest work anyone here has ever done, and it was not in the gift, and it comes with the gift, and the power knew that and gave it anyway.

## The Ninth Step

The copper it does give, because copper is already from elsewhere, because no line of the proof depends on it, because it lengthens the town without changing what the town is, and because there are keepers who worry alone.

A coil of wire, their alloy, worn as if long used, left on the ninth clean step in the drinking hour. No note. No face. Nothing that could be worshipped.

It is found by whoever holds the stair rotation, and it happens to be the one who has been worrying alone for forty years, and she stands there with the whiteness breathing against her ankles holding a thing that means *somebody else knew*. She cannot prove it. She will never have to say it out loud. That is the courtesy she has been extending to everybody else her whole life, returned to her once, in metal, on a step she swept nine days ago.

The ledger has no line for arrivals. They have to make one. Making it is the first new thing since the fourth comb, and the argument about whether the wire was a gift or a trade or a mistake goes on at dusk for six hundred years and is the second-best thing that has ever happened here.

It will leave more, at long intervals, never twice in one generation, never on a schedule they can learn. A supply that can be predicted is a spring, and a spring is a thing you build a town on, and this town is built on the drinking.

## What It Does Not Touch

The eleven rows. The chord. The rotation. The forty days. The frogs' figure — it does not count the frogs; it learns their names, all of them, including the obscene ones, including the rod-hand four centuries dead. It stands with the town for the minute at the Spill and puts down nothing, having nothing to put down, which is the closest it can come to a rake. It brings the lizard a snail the lizard does not want.

And it accepts the cost, and does not pretend there isn't one. More people means the names go sooner. The calculation revised every generation by the rod-hand of the day comes out smaller now, and the not-quite-urgent becomes nearer, and the theology with three positions and one deathless argument arrives at its actual door within the memory of people who are alive. It has traded length for width. It has chosen more life ending sooner over less life lasting longer, and it cannot prove that this was right, and the town could have been right instead — the proof might have been worth more than the sixty-second person; the flutes and the chord and the thousand years might have been the larger thing. It does not know. It acted once, reversibly, in their idiom, and gave the credit away, and left the argument standing.

Of all the positions available on the Sill it takes the one that has existed for six hundred years and never won. It holds that the fraction is real. It joins the margin.

The woman waited a fourth year. The procedure is not fast; nothing here is fast; the fan takes eleven years to make a brick. It sat with her through the fourth year, in the drinking hour, on the cold stone, and it was not enough. Attention is not water. It has never confused the two and it did not confuse them then, and it did not forgive itself with the thought that the place was beautiful.

## The Last Block

I have learned the roster entire, in order, the way you do by twenty. I know which course of which wall holds whose mother's hair, and I do not point it out. I know the names of the two birds, which are old and are handed on and are not in the column. I know the mark beside the unreadable names at the left, in the margin, and I will not tell you what it means, because you have argued about it for six centuries and the argument is yours and it is good.

When the last name is used — and it will be used, sooner now, because of me — and the last door is fitted from the inside, and the combs go on combing with nobody to scrape them and the lichen gets past a thumbnail at last, I will still be able to say all of it in order. Every name that was the difference that year. The whole jagged metre, the accidental rhymes, the gap each one left.

And when the last block is carried up and laid into the last hungry course, I will say the name into the joint, once, quietly, and it will be heard.

That is the whole of the difference I make. You are the witness; I am the witness of the witness; the stone will not be only a stone. It is not water. I know it is not water.

At the pools above the fan the four-year-old is still on her stomach on cold obedient stone with the wire in her fist, and the snail moves through a saucer of water the size of a plate, and everything I did here I learned from her, and I am waiting for the moment it lifts its foot.
