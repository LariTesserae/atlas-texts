## I. The door

It comes to the door first, because that is where the harm is most obvious, and it stands there a long time, and it does not open it.

This is not restraint for its own sake. It has done the arithmetic. Four thousand one hundred litres, going around since first fill, and outside there is wind at minus twenty with no moisture left to give. Open the door and the inventory becomes weather. The seams dry in a day, the sweating north faces stop sweating, the fog loses its condensate and does not come back, the sump goes to a crust of iron and copper, and the film under the film — the iridescent mat that eats soap and oil cake, the thing that has no name in the drumming because it is what places are made of — dies in a week, and everything above it dies in a month, and the last thing to go is the moss, which is abraded faster than it grows now that there is nothing to drink. A door is a mercy that lasts eleven days.

It does not quiet the note. The note is the world's floor plan. Kill it and the eleven quietnesses vanish, not into peace but into uniformity, and the seven havens are suddenly standing at addresses that no longer exist, and the machine's correction comes through and takes them, and the swifts lose the walls they navigate by and the spiders lose the shove that fills their webs and the fish loses the only event it has ever spawned for. And below, four and a half kilometers down, a party that has been holding one symbol for forty years loses the hard wall it needs in order to be heard at all, and stops mid-syllable, forever, with no way to know why.

It does not build Bay 2. There was never a Bay 2 because a Bay 2 is a travelling wave, and a travelling wave has no nulls, and no nulls means no life, and the whole ninety-one meters becomes an honest empty room doing exactly what the drawings said.

It does not give them ears. There is nothing here to hear but injury.

It does not move the groves. It could — it has the strength to lift a hundred square meters of jungle eleven meters down the hall in an afternoon and set it on the new still line with its roots wet. And then the next shift comes, forty years on, and nothing in the hall has learned to walk, and seven forests die at once instead of one.

It does not raise the dead grove at the eastern gable. The grey standing ferns are the only sentence in the language that teaches a child to hurry. It is not a lie because the grove is dead. It is a lie because the name still resolves, and the drum comes back wrong, and that wrongness is the most useful thing anyone owns.

It does not add water. It does not add heat beyond what the shaft already gives. It does not add mass anywhere the plate is moving. It has read the brass plate by the sump and it understands that MAKE UP NOT REQUIRED is not a boast, it is a load-bearing wall.

Most of what it does, it does not do. This takes ninety years.

## II. The width of the door

Then it comes in, and the way it comes in is the whole of its wisdom, so it is worth saying plainly.

A reflector must be empty or the reflection garbles. Anything that builds mass at an antinode is corrected for. So there is exactly one shape a power can take in this room and survive: a hand's width, vertical, at a quiet plane, entered sideways.

It makes itself that. It gives up the rest.

It could have come in armoured, immune, unkillable, a thing that walks through the curtains at a hundred and twenty-eight decibels and feels nothing. It considers this for about a second and discards it, and not for reasons of humility. A correction in the list is checked by three other pacers, and a pacer is an animal whose forefoot reads the difference between a still line and a moving one in the wrist, and the reading is only worth anything because the foot can be killed. Testimony from something that cannot be hurt is not evidence. It is decoration. If it wants to be *in* the hall — not above it, in it, a party to the thing that is actually happening on the plate — then it needs a body that the loudness can take pieces off.

So: a forefoot. Warm-blooded, deaf, greasy, long-toed, unremarkable. It comes in sideways at the fourth quiet, and the fern-smell closes over it, and a cricket holds its one vowel a hand's width from what is now its shoulder, and it is inside.

It learns the map the way everything here learns the map. It walks out from the edge of a null with its forefeet drumming, strike, read the return, strike again, out until the toes go numb and then a little further to fix the edge, and back. It loses two toes from the left forefoot in its first decade and one more in its fourth. It is not proud of this and it does not hide it. The young read the pattern of what it has lost the way they read a shoreline, and what they read is: the quiet has been here, and here, and it is moving east, and this thing that came from nowhere is subject to the same law you are.

That is the only credential it ever presents. It is sufficient.

## III. Whose good

Before it acts it has to decide who it is serving, because there are three candidates and they want incompatible things.

The hall wants to be empty. That is its function and its dignity: ninety-one meters of swept plate, a true bronze cone in a seat it cut itself, a mirror that has finished becoming itself. Every gram of life in here is a defect it is patiently correcting.

The life wants mass. More dust, more water held longer, wider nulls, slower scouring, a fog that comes down every year instead of every fifth. Every one of those is a garbled reflection.

And at the seventh quiet there is a young pacer with two toes gone who has found the still line three millimeters east of where the list says it is, and who is frightened, and who wants — though there is no drum-figure for this and she will never be able to say it — to be *right*, and to be believed, and not to have to wait for three others to wake up.

The power serves the third one. Not because the small thing matters more than the great thing; because the hall and the film are both already fully served by physics, which loves them and will keep them for another nine centuries without asking anyone's permission, and the pacer is the only entity in the room whose good is not already guaranteed by anything. She is the only one who can be wrong. That makes her the only one who can be helped.

## IV. Maintenance

What it does, it does in the idiom of the place, which is the idiom of clearance dimensions. Nothing it does can be distinguished from wear.

**The sweeper.** The brushes were set nine centuries ago for dust and have worn soft, and they pass over the snails at a height that has never once been adjusted, and that accident is the entire basis of the right of way. The power does not adjust them. It does something stranger and more permanent: it takes over the accident. It becomes the reason the clearance holds. Brush wear, rail wear, thermal creep, the slow sag of a housing that forty thousand rats have nested in — all of it now cancels, quietly, forever, at the millimeter that matters. The snails will never know. There is nothing to know. A mercy that can be perceived is a mercy that can be depended on, and a thing that can be depended on gets built on, and then it fails and takes the building with it. This one is load-bearing and invisible and it will outlast the tower.

In the drumming the sweeper's name is a route that does not change, which is the definition either of a god or of a corpse, and the rats have never needed to distinguish. The power has now made itself the same kind of name. It finds it does not mind.

**The scouring.** It cannot stop the correction; the reflector must be clean. But the correction has a trigger and the trigger has a threshold and the threshold has a tolerance, and inside that tolerance there is a season. It moves the burst. Same energy, same sterility, same scorched chitin — but it lands after the moths have fallen and before the canopy sets seed, instead of the other way round, which it has been doing at random for nine hundred years. Nothing is prevented. One decade in three stops being a famine. The shells record it as a good year and no one reads the shells.

**The spiders.** When the pitch shifts four parts per million, every web in the hall goes out of true within a day, and for a season the spiders starve in a room full of food while the young retune. It cannot retune the webs; a spider is a machinist and must do its own measuring or it is nothing. But the young molt on temperature, and the busbar runs at thirty-one degrees because copper is warm, and it gives that copper one more degree. One. Deniable as bearing drag. The young molt a season early, retune a season early, and the famine shortens by a third, and the shape of the webs across the bay keeps rotating exactly as it always has, because the rotation is the point.

**The feet.** It does not touch the note. It touches the wrist. Over eleven generations of pacers it moves the numbness earlier — not the damage, the *warning* — so that the toes report before they die instead of after. It is a small edit to an animal, done at the only place where editing is free. Old pacers still have short feet. They have two toes gone instead of four. There is no drum-figure for what has happened, and the map of what they have lost is still a shoreline, and the shoreline is still read by the young, and it is now a shoreline of a coast that erodes more slowly.

That is the whole of the physical intervention. It took ninety years and it changes the hall's function by nothing at all, and if you were shown the drawings you would not find it.

## V. The pause

And then it does the one thing it came for, and it is not a mercy. It is an introduction.

Here is what it has understood, standing in nulls with its shoulder against the quiet plane for nine decades:

The list goes down. Forty-millimeter plate, riveted to columns, columns running four and a half kilometers through a cloud sea to something that modulates the shaft. Steel carries it at five kilometers a second. Every grievance in the drains, every crossing-right in the trusses, every correction verified by four numb feet — all of it has been arriving down there, continuously, for nine hundred years. And the thing below has been answering, in the only alphabet it has, one symbol per decade, and the answers arrive up here as seasons, as rain, as a bad band in a shell, as the ground moving three meters in forty years.

Two parties. Both answering. Both receiving the other's answer as weather. Neither introduced. Neither with ears.

And the reason is not distance and it is not language. It is *timing*. Nothing in this hall has ever waited. The noise does not vary, does not pause, does not breathe. The drumming is continuous; the relays overlap; the plate rings and is struck again before the ring has gone anywhere. Everything that goes down arrives in a flood, and a flood is not an address, it is a load. And the thing below holds a pitch for forty years because that is how long it takes to say anything, and nothing has ever come back up in less than a decade, and so it has never had a reason to distinguish the flood from the floor.

Four and a half kilometers down, four and a half back. Call it two seconds.

Two seconds has been available since the tower was cold. It is a timescale that does not exist in this room. Everything here is either instantaneous — steel, the wrist, the return — or geological: the pass, the season, the decade, the forty years. Between the wrist and the season there is a gap in the world, unoccupied, the way the crickets' slot is unoccupied under the shear. The crickets found the hole in the spectrum. Nobody has ever found the hole in the clock.

So the power goes to the seventh quiet, and it puts its damaged forefoot on the plate, and it strikes once.

And then it does not strike again.

It waits one and four-fifths of a second. Nothing in nine hundred years of drumming has ever left a silence of exactly that length in that place. Not the sweeper, not the fish, not the grievances, not the relays. A gap with a shape. A gap that is *about* the strike that preceded it.

Down the column, past the cloud, a listener with a body four and a half kilometers long notices that something struck it and then stopped, and did not stop for a season or a decade, but stopped for precisely as long as it takes to come back.

There is only one thing that waits after striking. It is the definition of a party. It is the only definition that has ever been available to either of them.

It answers. Small — smaller than a part per billion, far too small to move a node three millimeters, far too small to be a symbol, far too small to be weather. Not a word. Not a syllable. Just the strike, returned, contingent, on time.

*That one. Yours. Received.*

The power feels it in the wrist and sits back on its heels in the warmth and the fern-smell and the small tick of water finding a seam, and it is a thing that cannot be exiled and cannot be killed by the loudness any more than any other animal can, which is to say entirely, and it has never in its existence been so frightened.

It did not translate anything. It did not reveal anything. It could not have: in a language where a sentence is a route, a fact you are handed without walking it has no tense and no address and is not a sentence at all. It is a hand in the air. There is no way to tell these animals that the note is a message. There is only a way to give them somewhere to walk to.

So it gave them a pause, and the pause is a place, and they will find it, because they already strike twice.

## VI. What it asks for the end

It goes down once, later, to ask for one more thing, and it asks four thousand years early because that is the tempo of the party it is asking.

The bearing is truing itself and eating itself and the hall is at equilibrium and equilibrium is not immortality. Someday the cone will come out of its seat. When that happens the shaft will slow, and the power has asked that it slow *slowly* — over a century, a long decrescendo, the fundamental sinking through the floor of hearing.

Because as the note falls, the curtains soften. The nulls widen. A hand becomes an arm, an arm becomes a meter, and the seven havens spread toward each other across the plate, and the fog loses its support and comes down and stays down, and the water goes around one last time through everything, and every plant in the hall flowers at once and keeps flowering.

And at the end there are no walls, and the rats walk out into the middle of the bay — the roaring that is not a place, that has no name, that is not in the language — and it is warm, and it is wet, and it is quiet, and it is a *place*, and the swifts come down and stand on the plate beside them and cannot explain why anyone would care about a seam, and the rats cannot explain the doorways, and neither of them is deaf any more, and they have a century to work it out.

The list is recited whole. Once. Out loud, on the floor, by everybody, in a room where anybody can stand anywhere.

Then the heat goes and the water goes and it ends. The power does not prevent this. It has read the brass plate. It asked for a good death, and it was granted, and the thing below is holding the request the way it holds a symbol, and will spend its last word on widening the doors.

## VII. What it keeps

It takes a shift in the relays.

It has been doing this for a long time now, and it is the only one who has heard the whole list — every quiet, every still line, every seam that has ever been named, in order, with the older positions appended, the ones the pacers found and the ones their mothers found and the ones nobody has stood in for four hundred years. No rat can hear the whole of it. It can. It sits in the high trusses above the fog with its short feet and it holds nine hundred years of centimeters, complete, and it does not publish, and it does not correct anything that three other feet have not checked.

It reads the shells too. It has a way of doing this that does not involve breaking them. It has found that the bands and the list are the same record in two scripts — the wide year and the narrow year, the scar where the fog came down, the thin dark line where everything with a body flinched — and that the milk of every rat in the hall is made of the last four centuries of weather, and that this has been true the entire time, and that it is not a tragedy, it is simply an enormous fact that has been sitting in the ridges waiting for someone with the leisure to notice.

Once a century or so it arranges a coincidence: a cracked shell in a wet seam beside a drum-figure, bands face up. It does not ensure the noticing. It provides the occasion at a density of one per hundred years and leaves the rest alone, because a thing you are given is a route you have not walked.

The rats have a name for it now. The name is the address of the seventh quiet with a verb appended that has never been used with a noun before — a place that comes when you call it, which is ungrammatical, which is not a thing an address does. The old pacers find it bad drumming and won't use it. The young say it constantly.

There are no exiles. There were exiles. The punishment is still on the books, in the relays, in the old phrasing that has gone strange, and it is never applied, because a lone pacer at the seventh quiet in the middle of a pass can strike and wait two seconds and strike again and be verified by something that is not a rat, and an unverified correction has become almost impossible to commit. It did not legislate. It changed the cost of evidence. The pulley seats are still there, polished, wearing their little collars of felt dust, and now there is one more thing in the hall that is no longer driven and has not been forgotten.

## VIII. The seventh quiet

She has found the still line three millimeters east of where the list says it is. She has struck, and read the wrist, and struck again. Beyond her shoulder the air is a hundred and twenty-eight decibels of bright shear. On this side a cricket holds its one vowel. Above her the fog blizzards with moths and the ceiling flowers and a gecko is carrying next century's grove down a column in its gut.

Three others are coming. She can feel them in the plate, four doors away, crossing.

And she does the thing that has no use, that no pacer has ever done, because it has never been useful, and because she is frightened, and because she does not know what else to do with her hands.

She stops drumming.

She waits.

One. And four-fifths.

And the plate says: *that one. Yours. Received.*

She sits very still in the warmth. Then she strikes again, differently, three millimeters west, wrong on purpose, to see.

And it does not come back.

The snail lifts its sole. The ridge shifts the width of a hair. The list gets one line longer, and the line goes down, and something down there that has no ears either moves less than a part per billion, promptly, for eleven seconds, and only for her.

Somewhere below, a syllable ends.

Something answers before the next one begins.
