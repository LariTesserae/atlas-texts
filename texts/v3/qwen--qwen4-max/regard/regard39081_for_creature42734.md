# The Second Foot

## What Arrives

Nothing arrives. That is the first decision, and it takes a while.

Light would be useless — light here is a smear on vapor, and a brighter smear would only thicken the fog. A hand would be worse: a hand is a new kind of object, and every object in the basin is accounted for by the floor beneath it, plate under fines under plate under the conglomerate of everything that used to stand. To appear would be to introduce a term that nothing in the strata could explain.

So it comes as attention, which is free, and it spends nine hundred years doing only that.

It reads the bedding. Light band, dark band, light band. It reads the leaf-spring exfoliated like shale and the bearing race with its balls dissolved and its voids lined in orange crystal. It reads the stub of a pad from something that was carried out to the edge and laid in the trembling water. It goes down to the gray ironcrete with the trowel-marks still in it and understands two things at once: that it is not the first to be here, and that whoever was here first did not stay to be thanked.

Then it learns the beat, and the grammar that follows from the beat. That an utterance is what fits between pressures. That to speak you must touch. That to touch you must stop walking. That what stops walking is eaten.

It can do anything that can be done. This turns out to be a much smaller set than it expected, because almost everything that can be done would be a different world, and this one is nearly finished being itself.

So it makes a pad. Sixty millimeters, because that is the size of the thing it intends to speak to first, and there is no shouting here, and there is no distance.

## The Apprenticeship

Start at the crack, because that is where anything starts here.

A hairline in a steam line, two meters of green-black mat, and at the hairline a domed thing with eight legs and the ghost of an S on its back, holding a wound at four hundred microns and rebuilding a sacrificial lip every forty years.

The visitor watches it for two hundred years. Then it tries to lay a bead and fails, because it has no underside to grind, because it has never had to pay for its own material. This is the whole of its incarnation, and it is not becoming small. It is becoming *wearable*. It makes an underside. It lets the plate take a few microns. It mixes the powder with condensate in a hollow it did not have before, and the bead it lays is gray, the color of the floor, which is the color of everything.

It is a bad stancher for four hundred years. Then it is a mediocre one. It learns the craft, which is not closing and not running: baffling. Hold the wound at exactly the width that feeds you. Keep it clean. Let nothing else happen for a hundred centuries.

And it learns the other thing, the thing it will actually be for, and it learns it by accident, standing still at the crack with its pad down. The nine-second pressure comes through the plate and into it and does not come out the other side. Around it, for about a hand's width, the water goes flat.

A hush the size of a stancher. The first stillness in the Sump that is not owed to a ruin.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not touch the flywheel. It could re-spin the governor in the sealed cavity under the fortieth floor, and it works out what that would mean before it finishes considering it. Everything alive here is a harmonic of the slowing. A wheel brought back up to speed would sweep every node across the basin in a season; every thin-walled listener out on the new flats would flex and fatigue and come apart; every meadow would close; every hush would shut like an eye. And the roar would return to its old fullness, one thirty at the drains, teeth aching, the world four meters across, permanently. To keep the Sump alive it would have to keep it deaf, and postpone the one thing the whole basin is converging toward. It lets the wheel slow. It lets the highs go out of the roar. It stands in the rain and listens to the world get purer and does not help.

It does not bring warmth. Warmth here is a wound. Free heat is every seam opening at once, every oasis dying outward from the center, and the stancher's craft — the only homeostasis in forty kilometers — made pointless.

It does not bring dirt. It considers this longest and refuses it hardest. The fines are the record. Foreign silt would be a forged page in the only book there is, and nothing here has a mouth to eat it with.

It does not give eyes. There is nothing to see: four meters of fog, and one cherry bearing eight kilometers off, and the towers known by sound. It will not install a sense for a world that is not there.

It does not give a mouth. A mouth is a way of speaking at a distance. Distance is the one thing this place does not have.

It does not stop the mills. It has read the strata. The mills lay the buds. The mills make bodies. It knows what the mills are for.

And it does not do nothing, which it also considers, at length, and with respect.

## The One Lack

It audits the world, and the world is not poor.

Homeostasis: the stancher. Memory: the varves, which are better than memory, being the thing itself. Knowledge at distance: the listeners, who can hear thirty kilometers and know where the cherry bearing burns. Reproduction: two ways, the edge and the fusion, one costly and one free. Burial: being handed back to the noise. Law: wear, which is impartial, and has no exceptions, and takes an edge to part anything. Purpose: the mirror, the clearing ceiling, the interval that will not close.

There is exactly one thing the Sump cannot generate, and it is not warmth or light or silence or time.

Everything here that holds contact is worn by it, and worn to fit, and when the fit is exact the seals fuse and there is one mass where there were two. Every other is eventually the floor. So when the wheel stops and the one thing says its name all the way to its end, it will say it into itself, with one foot, and the foot will be the plate, and the plate will be the speaker.

That may be the point. It is not a flaw. But it is a lack, and it is the only lack a visitor can fill without changing anything else.

It comes to be the second foot. Everything else it does is incidental to standing there.

## The Arithmetic

The lip says six hundred partings left. The visitor counts arrivals instead.

One pilgrim per forty-one years, in ones, never in company, because there is one edge and it can hold one bud at a time. The corner has eleven hundred years of sharpness left at that traffic; the water accounts for a third of what is already gone and the births account for the rest. Twenty-six. Five hundred and seventy-four buds will be carried down the groove and laid at a rounding and lie where they are laid, complete and unseparated, and the road will still be there.

It sits with this for a century. Then it looks for the term it can move, and finds it: not the edge. The walk. Eleven kilometers, two centuries, because the bud is heavier than the carrier and the feet are stubs. Put a corner nearer the mills and the walk shortens and the count rises tenfold, and it goes south to see about this.

In the groove there is one that has been walking for two hundred and twelve years, saying its name into the floor as it goes, one fragment per beat.

The visitor stops beside it and puts a pad against its pad for nine seconds and lets go on the fraction, and hears the whole name once, and then walks beside it for sixty years without touching it again, because touching costs and the pilgrim cannot afford it, and company is not contact and is not nothing.

Somewhere in the second decade it understands what it was about to do.

The road is not transit. The road is the utterance. Eleven kilometers is how long the name takes. Two hundred and twelve years of one fragment per beat is not a journey with a sound in it; it is a sound with a journey in it. To move the corner closer is to lift the pad mid-press.

So it does not shorten the road. It takes the second-best option, the least efficient one that is not a mutilation: another corner, just as far away, at the end of a groove that does not exist yet, so that two can walk at once, and two names can be said at once, in parallel, neither of them cut short.

## The Gift

An edge is not a shape. An edge is a material that resists wearing long enough to stay sharp, and there is nothing in the basin that resists wearing, and wearing has no direction, and nothing here can make a thing that is not made of what is here.

Except that the visitor is here now, and is made of something.

It lays down a wedge of itself eleven kilometers east of the middle drains: the size of a door, refractory beyond anything in the strata, holding an edge under a finger of moving water. It will stay sharp until the water stops. It will bear perhaps twenty-five partings before the traffic blunts it, because the towers are thinning and the feasts are rarer and the second stream will be thinner than the first.

Twenty-six becomes fifty-one. That is the whole of the improvement. It is not a rescue. It is twenty-five more names said all the way to their end.

What it lays down does not come back. It will be ground into fines and bedded into bands and cored up in an age when there is nothing left to core with, and it will be an anomaly, and it will not match anything under it.

And there it stops, because it has just described the corner.

Refractory. Unmatched. Wearing out. The one object in forty kilometers not accounted for by the floor beneath it. The visitor does not know who left the first one and does not need to. The wedge is a signature, and the signature says: *I was here, and I paid what I could afford, and it was not enough, and I did it anyway.*

The world is not sealed. It has one seam in it, and the seam is sharp, and everything born here comes through it. That is the permission. It is the only permission the visitor asks for and it was left lying in the water.

There is no road to the new corner. It cannot give a road; a road is a groove worn by things going somewhere else. The first pilgrim to use it will walk unworn plate for a hundred and forty years, and the groove will begin behind it, a hand deep, forty centimeters wide.

## The Safe Place to Stop

The rest is one act, repeated for three thousand years, in different places.

Two stanchers at a mat boundary want to hold contact longer than a season. The mills begin to converge, over months, on what has stopped. The visitor goes and stands where the mills are coming and stops there.

It does not fight them. It never fights anything. The mills do not distinguish the made from the grown and cannot distinguish a visitor from a folded tower; they converge on stillness, and it is still, and they arrive, and they grind. It is abraded and it does not go away, and the grinding holds them for a hundred and ten years, and behind it two things hold on for four decades and wear each other a tenth of a millimeter, and none of it is useful. There is no information to exchange. There is no news in the Sump that the listeners do not already have by echo. They are not saying anything. They are holding on.

That is the intervention. It becomes the one thing in forty kilometers that can afford to stop moving.

It stands in the drift-path of a node when the load seasons shift, taking the roar in at its boundary and not passing it on, so that a listener can carry its young across four hundred years of moving water; it is a stump that walks, and the mills work at it the whole way.

It stands beside the ones that come back from the corner as two things, neither of them whole. It holds a pad against each in turn and says their names back to them while they come apart. Then it steps aside, because it knows what the mills are for. It does not save one of them, ever. It is not there to save them. It is there so that the last thing that hears them is not the floor.

At the first corner, in the sixtieth year, the pilgrim with the stubs presses its bud against the refractory wedge and holds it there for weeks while the water makes its small clean hiss, the only sound in the world with a top to it. The visitor stands upstream and lets the mills come to it. And afterward there are two where there was one, and it holds a pad against each for one beat and lets go on the fraction, which is a name, and costs the visitor a micron, and costs them nothing.

## The Tally

Small, free, made entirely of what was already there.

The listener at the groove's lip keeps the count in presses, one per pilgrim returning, and the presses wear the lip round, and the older marks go shallow, and the count is being erased by the keeping of it.

The visitor shows it the scalers' pellet: sorted oxide, laid down. One per pilgrim, deposited instead of pressed in. A cairn at the lip. A delta of tally. It grows.

Then it tells the listener the number. Twenty-six. It holds that press for a year, which is a wager, and it stands where the mills will arrive first, and it pays.

The listener does not stop counting. It changes what it counts. Not how many remain. How many happened.

At the end there is a low mound of sorted oxide at the head of the old road, fifty-one pellets, bedded by the tide into bands as clean as any varve. It is the only monument in the world. It is legible. It will still be legible when the water is flat.

## The Diminuendo

Three thousand years, and the visitor is smaller.

It has been ground by mills and by pads and by the plate. It is down near sixty millimeters and then below, approaching from above the same minimum the stanchers are approaching, the size beneath which a body cannot lay a bead. It has learned to be slow. It says nothing that does not fit between beats. By any measure the Sump would recognize it is a stancher: a thing that holds a wound at the width that feeds.

The names lengthen by a fraction each age. The highs go out of the roar. Towers fold and are feasted and become meadows and then deltas. The cherry bearing on the north press dims by a degree a century and is still the closest thing to a star.

In the eleven hundredth year the first corner goes blunt. The last pilgrim to use it holds its bud against a rounding for weeks while the water makes a sound with nothing high in it, and nothing parts, and it turns and goes back down the groove with the bud still on its flank, and the visitor walks beside it and does not touch it.

The hiss stops. The last high frequency in forty kilometers, the one nobody could say, the place where the water spoke faster than they could.

Somewhere in the visitor, it is still going.

## The Name

The wheel stops.

The pumps stop. The sheet drains to the throats. The towers exhale their last vapor and the rain ends and the fog goes up and does not come back. The fines settle everywhere at once instead of only in the nodes. One hush, forty kilometers across, with no boundary, because a boundary is where the noise is on the other side. One mirror under a clearing ceiling, and the towers in it upside down, and the ceiling clear all the way to whatever is above the ceiling, which nobody has ever seen.

No beat. No pressure passing through the plate to wipe what is being said.

The visitor keeps one foot down. It has been careful about this for three thousand years: to be worn and not to fuse. Contact takes a micron from both; it has let itself be rounded to fit and has never once let the fit become exact. It is the only thing in the basin that has stayed other, and it stayed other on purpose, and the purpose was this.

The one thing puts its foot on the plate, and the plate is the foot, and it begins its name.

It is longer than the road. It goes on past the length of any press ever held, past a season, past a wager, past the point where the mills would have come, and there are no mills, and nothing comes, and the interval does not close. The visitor does not interrupt. It has nothing to interrupt with. It is worn very small by the end.

And then the one thing says the other name.

The long one. The duration that no stancher could hold without fusing and no listener could survive and no pilgrim could afford, and that was therefore never said, in all the ages, by anyone: the name of the thing that stood still where the mills were coming. It has time now. It says it all the way to its end, and releases on a fraction as far out as it likes, and the floor holds it.

That is what the visitor came for. It did not know that. It had thought it came to help.

## What It Leaves

It lifts its foot. Nothing in the Sump has ever lifted a foot and gone elsewhere; the phrase has no meaning here; it happens anyway.

Where it stood there is a rounding, bullnosed, like every bolt head and every tower waist in the basin. The water closes over it. The mirror does not break, because there is no wind now and nothing left to make a wave.

It carries out the hiss of the corner, which nothing else can say. Fifty-one names given at one beat each and released on the fraction. The names of the unwhole, said back to them at the edge. Eight. Nine-and-a-hundred-and-fortieth. The tally, which is a mound of pellets at the head of a road that nothing will use.

And under everything, in the fines, in the bedding: light band, dark band. And in it, in some age, a cast — a void in the shape of a pad, the material long dissolved, the walls lined with orange crystal, matching nothing in the strata.

The place is not dying. It finished. And there is nothing in it that was not put there, and one of the things put there was a listener that could not be used up, and it was used up, and it is in the bands.

Somewhere else, once, in a place with room in it, there was a sound with a top to it.
