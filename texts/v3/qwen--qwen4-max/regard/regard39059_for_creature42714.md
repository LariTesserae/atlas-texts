# The Second Answer

## The Speed It Comes At

It arrives, and the first thing it does is slow down.

This is not modesty. At its own speed, attention here is a bucket chain. Anything that happens to this ground quickly happens *to* it — a face opened in September, a body lifted in spring, four minutes of steam that puts every raven in the basin into the air. Fast is the local word for injury. If it came as light over the cut, as a voice out of the low grey sky, as one unmistakable hour, it would be one more thing that arrived from outside at a rate the ground could not absorb, and it would leave an arris you could cut your hand on.

So it comes down through its own rates the way the extractor goes down through the Bronze Age, except that it takes sixty years to do it and it does not cut. It arrives as a June. As a hire. As a line item. As the price of gas, which is already the mechanism, which is already what decides how fast the stockpile shrinks and therefore how fast the floor is eaten. It does not need a new lever. The place is full of levers, and every one of them is mundane, and men already have their hands on all of them.

There will be no year anyone can point to. Ask at the station and they will tell you nothing happened, the licence ran out, the plan got funded, the young one came in with the microscope and stayed. It arranges to be indistinguishable from luck, because luck is the only kind of help this ground can take without a scar.

## Whose Good

It does the arithmetic and refuses to finish it.

The animal in Stockpile 3 wants to finish. That is the whole of its wanting and it is the only appetite in the basin that is being satisfied. The living skin on the dome wants water at a millimetre a year and no argument. The men want the shift, the wage, the overtime, the price of gas high enough, and one of them wants something at the hawthorn he will not name. The raven pair want the thermal off the stockpile and the steel where the condensate keeps the frost off. The testate amoebae want, each species, one wetness and no other, and they disagree with each other about it down to the centimetre. The sundews want the poverty; the poverty is where they got their shape. The tardigrades want nothing. They solved it. The curled man in the case in the city cannot be asked, and this is not a small technical difficulty, it is the fact that governs everything else.

There is no average of these. A power that averaged them would be a committee, and it would produce a habitat, which is a thing with a specification, and this is not a habitat, it is one thing at several speeds with its faces at war.

So it makes a diagnosis instead, and the diagnosis is this:

The men are right about the cut and wrong about the being. *It must never breathe in* is true of a bubble held open in a body of water. It is not true of the body. The cut is not the animal. The cut is a held breath in the animal, sixty years long, five metres deep, eleven kilometres across, and the loudest thing in the landscape is the sound of it being held.

And the plan in the drawer is correct. It is not a plan for a habitat. It is a recipe for wetting a tun, and the men who wrote it knew that, and put it in the drawer, because they had eleven years and the recipe needs three thousand.

But a tun can be wetted wrongly. This is the part that matters and it is why permission is not enough. Come up too fast and you get the shallow warm lake, the drowned field, the flush of everything that was locked in the crumb going out to the river in a decade, and no sphagnum, and no dome, and no moss — a wet nothing where a wet something was. Come up not at all, because the licence expired and the men went home and the pumps stopped with nobody listening, and you get sixty more years of oxidation, the floor going down a few centimetres a decade into dust that blows into the drains, and the record — the only continuous account — going out as brown haze with a halo around every light.

The right thing is narrow. It is a rate, not an event. It has to be held for longer than any institution that has ever existed in this country. That gap — between eleven years and three thousand — is the only place in the whole arrangement where a power is actually needed. Not for the water. The water is already arriving, from below, patiently, as it has at every drained field in the history of this ground.

For the waiting.

## The Reading

Before anything else, and taking forty years over it, it reads the account.

Five metres, all of it, every centimetre of every face, in the shells. The houses the amoebae built out of blown quartz and stolen plates, stacked in their billions in the order they were abandoned, each species a wetness and no other, so that the whole column is a water-level diary kept by things that never knew they were keeping it. It reads the summer the surface dried. It reads the century it stayed dry. It reads the year the pines drowned, from the inside, from the shells of the things that were living on their feet when the moss came up. It reads the pollen of single summers. It reads the notch in the third field and what was lying in it and what he had eaten.

Nothing about the ground changes. Not one gram of water moves. This is the largest thing it does.

Because the place has been keeping records for five thousand years without a reader. That is what the refusal is: not saving, not remembering, just refusing everything at the same rate and with the same indifference, and the refusal *happens* to be legible. Legible is a relation. It needs two terms. Until now there has only ever been one, and a woman with a microscope on a Tuesday, and a phone number on a board by the trackway.

It supplies the second term. It becomes the one who knows which century stayed dry. It does not tell anybody it is doing this. The woman gets the funding, and the student, and the forty years, and the papers, and the sense — which she will never be able to justify in a grant application — that the work is being received.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not stop the pumps tonight. Nine men on nights, and the silence would not be peace, it would be a lake arriving in a place with a rail loop and a weighbridge and a jacket on a nail two kilometres from anything. Also the fast face is not a tumour. It is a face. The station is oxidation with a licence and a turbine on the end of it and it is the same process as the moss, run backwards, and you do not cure a body by cutting off the part of it that is currently awake.

It does not put out the animal. The animal is the only thing in eleven kilometres that has been permitted to finish, and it will finish in April as the men said it would, and no one will thank anything.

It does not raise the pines. It does not go to the city and take the curled man out of the case. He is not waiting; that is the error the place itself keeps inviting, and it declines the invitation. He is held, not parked. There is no one in there to be released, and the fingernails are not a promise. It goes and stands in front of the glass at three in the morning when the lighting rig is off, and it looks at the hands for a long time, and it does not invent a name for him, because a name would be for it and not for him.

It does not add nitrogen. It does not lime the ground. pH three point eight and no calcium and nothing worth the name is not the injury, it is the character; every shape here that is worth looking at was made out of having nothing, and the sundews are red and sticky because the ground gave them nothing and they had to get it somewhere. To fertilise would be to replace the being with a lush stranger and call it a recovery.

It does not appear. It does not speak. It does not make the men love the bog — they work here, and one of them ties a sock to a thorn and changes the subject to the price of gas, and both of those are his.

It does not tie a rag.

It finds out who ties them. It takes about a year, and it is not a mystery, and it never tells anyone, and in this way it becomes one of the people who knows about the tree.

## What It Does

Rain, in a dry June, in the one year in thirty when the animal would otherwise get off the lead. Not much. Enough. The fire stays in the pile and eats what it was given and does not go down into the floor, because down there it would eat the account, and there is no bottom to fight it against, and five thousand years of shells would go up as a wisp of white a metre from anywhere. This is the only thing it protects by force, and the force is weather, and nobody in the basin will ever know it happened.

The plan comes out of the drawer in a year when there is money and a tired person at a meeting who reads it properly. Nothing about the plan is improved. It was already right.

The maps are kept. All of them, including the ones with the notch. It does not heal the place flat. The cut goes into the stratigraphy — the oxidised crumb, the ballast line, the plastic sheeting over the trackway, the four holding-down bolts with nothing on them — and in three thousand years whatever reads that centimetre will find this century in it, sharp, unrounded, and legible, and that is not a failure of the healing, that is the healing.

The gantry stays. Not for heritage. Two birds nest in the steel where the condensate keeps the frost off, and when the plume stops there will be no thermal in the basin and no reason for a raven to hold eleven kilometres of anything, and it knows this, and it leaves the steel up anyway until the steel falls. It does not promise the ravens. It counts them, and it lets them go.

The seep at the margin stays clear and stays not acid. There is one snail.

The town gets warm some other way. This takes twenty years and is boring and involves the pylons, and it is the whole of the economics, and it is where most of the work actually is.

And the job is converted, not removed. This is the part it is most careful about. The man who walks the pumps at four in the morning has a skill that exists nowhere else: he can hear four separate notes under a roar that tells you nothing, and he can hear the absence of one. That is not a job, it is a faculty, and it took the station thirty years to grow it in him. So the pumps become weirs and the sump becomes a bund and the torch is the same torch and the boots are the same boots, and the thing he goes out into the cold to do does not change at all, which is listen for the note that means something has stopped.

The flask stays on the radiator. The jacket stays on the nail. The boot print in the wheelwash is still there in March, because nothing here has the manners to erase it, and it does not erase it either. It keeps the fact of the boot print. It is the only thing in the basin that will remember the men, and the men are the only part of the whole arrangement that ever knew there was an arrangement, and somebody should hold up their end.

## The Asking

It does not assume it knows what the thing wants. It asks, in the only language available, at the only speed the question can be put.

It changes the water level a little — blocks four drains in one field, a hundred and fifty metres of nothing, an amount that would fit inside a mistake — and then it waits, and it reads the shells.

The shells are the answer. They are the being's own report on its own condition, species by species, wetness by wetness, and they cannot flatter and they cannot be interviewed and they do not know they are speaking. In year nine they say something it did not predict. Something about the hollows, and the order the water came up in, and a species it had expected that does not arrive. It changes the plan. It changes the plan again in year thirty-one. The plan in the drawer was right and it was also a first draft, and the difference between a recipe and a relationship is that the second one is corrected by the thing it is for.

This is what it does instead of knowing. It is not less than omniscience. It is the only form of care that a body five metres thick and eleven kilometres across can be offered without being handled.

## The Standby

Here is the enormous thing, and it is not visible, and there is no year for it.

Nothing in this basin has ever been attended for longer than a shift. The men are the perishable staff of an imperishable place; they come in over the causeway at forty and go out at the end of it and leave a flask, a jacket, a print. The licence is eleven years. The company is a company. The woman with the microscope will die. The plan's authors will not see the first cushion. The moss lays a millimetre a year and needs three thousand to be a dome, and there has never been anything here that could hold an intention for three thousand years, and that — not the fire, not the pumps, not the water — is the actual deficiency in the arrangement.

So it takes the standby.

Four pumps: three running, one on standby. The standby is the note that means one of the others has stopped. It puts itself in that position for the whole duration — not running, not holding the bubble open, not doing the work, simply present at the gap, so that when the thing that has been holding this ground in shape finally stops, what arrives in the half second afterwards is not absence.

And to do it, it has to learn the local strategy, because there is no other way to be present at a millimetre a year. It pulls in its legs. It wrings itself down. It sets its own inside like glass. It gives up its speed, which is the only thing about it that was ever impressive, and it goes into the cushion at the scale where a millimetre is a county, and it stays there through the fen and the moss and the first hundred and fifty years and the long boring middle of the third millennium, wetted a little at a time from below, corrected by shells, saying nothing, keeping the shift.

They have solved the problem the entire place is organised around. It learns it from them. It is not above the tardigrades. It is the newest one.

## The Second Note

At four in the morning, a long time from now, somebody goes out with a torch to the bund in what used to be the ninth field, because the gauge is wrong and it is probably the gauge.

The drains are blocked and have been for a century. The floor is not a floor. It is wet in the ordinary way, all of it, everywhere, and it drips — the whole eleven kilometres drips, which was the thing the accidental ribbon under the plume was doing alone and wrong all winter, a small wrong model of the right thing, and when the plume stopped the ribbon died and it did not compensate itself for that, and this is the compensation.

There is no roar. The sound arrives with no room around it and stops at the edges of itself, and there is nothing in it, and the silence is enormous and flat and unechoing and correct. Cushions of dull red and ochre and a green so wet it looks lit from inside. Cottongrass. Snipe. Somewhere under all of it, four thousand years down and rising at a millimetre a year, the stumps, and under the stumps the black, and in the black a notch that is still on the maps.

At the margin, one hawthorn. Rags. Cloth, a child's sock, a strip of hi-vis from a station that no longer exists, something that might have been white in the seventies and is now something else. She knows about the tree. She does not say.

She puts her hand flat on the weir, which is the only thing for a kilometre that was made by anybody, and she stands there and does what she actually came out to do, which is listen — not to the nothing, which tells you everything, but underneath it, for the water coming up through the peat from below at the rate it is supposed to, and for the wrong note, which is the note that means it is coming too fast.

She waits for the second note. It comes.

Under everything, the water.

Under the water, five thousand years with a gradient in it, not asleep, not anything, simply arriving.

And under that, keeping the shift, on standby, unfolded a little at a time and in no hurry, something that has agreed to stay.
