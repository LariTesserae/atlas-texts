Everything it does, it finishes.

This is not a virtue. It is the habit of its nature, the way the sigh is the habit of theirs. It has never begun a thing and left it standing in the middle. If it laid a hand on the stratus deck the deck would part, and parting has an edge, and an edge makes a seam, and a seam that size is a knock; and four hundred faces like folded hands would turn away from the First Knock and toward the thing that made the second one, and the town would be standing in it.

So it does not arrive. Arrival is the one act it cannot perform without committing.

It goes upstream instead, to the meadows that have never heard of this place, and it begins something there, and does not finish it. The evening hatch above the lip rises by a part in four hundred. The next year, by a part in four hundred again. The spiderlings tip their abdomens at a sky very slightly more willing to hold a thread; more of what lets go comes down here rather than somewhere else. Nothing has been given. A gift is complete at the moment of giving, and completion is the thing it must not do. This is a slope. It has been arriving for sixty years and it is not here yet.

---

It learns the town the way the town learns the year: by what comes down.

Below the Throat the water runs out of the basin, and in the wet hour it carries the dissolved seams in solution, faintly luminous, going nowhere. Three hundred years of noise, washed out in order. It sits in the outflow with the patience of a thing that does not conclude, and reads.

It reads the drop of the floor. It reads a century of pots and shouted names and the four-hour silences with the poles in them. It reads the bad century: the small sounds, close together, many of them, and the ones that were made deliberately, in a room, to volunteers and then to others, and then a long stretch of nothing at all. It reads a boy saying four syllables into the tail of a rain, and the shape of the four syllables, and the shape of the stopping. It reads the lists of the unshouted, which come down every wet season in a lump, all at once, screaming.

This is its whole education and it takes eleven years and it is the reason for everything afterward. A benevolence that had not done this would have lifted the lid.

---

What it does not do:

It does not lift the deck. Forty minutes of sun on the eastern cliff, the color of watered milk, is not a deprivation; it is the remainder after the pasture has been fed. The fog is the food. Clear the sky and the midges rise over the lip into nothing, the combs drip nine liters a night for a season and then not at all, and eight blind animals starve in the light with their mouths open. Also: shadowlessness is where the cant lives. A people who sign with their wrists held close to the body, in a light where nothing has a direction, would become a different people in a hard sun, and it did not come here to replace them.

It does not round the scree. It considers this the longest, because rounded stone is only kindness — no shin-bruises, no shins, a floor you could run on. But rounding is what time does when it has finished becoming. The raw faces, the fluted scars, the pale streaks not yet softened by a single season of patience: this is a basin three hundred years old, which is to say a basin that has not yet made up its mind. The Attending face the floor because the floor might make something else. Give them a floor that has stopped making, and see what they do. It leaves every edge exactly where it is. Nothing here is rounded and that is not a wound, it is an alibi.

It does not remove the four hundred. It went close enough to be sure of them, once, in a dry week, and what it found was: they are cold; they have never in three centuries taken anything that did not end; they wait at ten paces and not nine; they go home in rain; they carry the only fixed orchards in a town built on skids, and the fruit is eaten in the wet hour by children on dares. They are not a mechanism. They are four hundred patient persons who have been hungry since before the town had a name. To unmake them would be to do to four hundred what the bad century did to the ones who were made deaf, and it would be done, as that was done, by something that had decided it knew better.

It does not give the hushed their sound back. It found where the sound is kept — the potters were right, there is a warm place in the cliff, and it is not a stomach, it is a pond, and everything taken is in it and nothing in it has ever crossed. It could open that. It could hand a hushed woman her laughter with the inside restored. It does not, and this is the refusal it is least sure of, and it knows it is least sure of it, and it does it anyway: half the vocabulary for feeling in Eight was made by people who had nothing left to lose by gesturing, and it will not unmake a language to repair an injury it did not suffer. And it cannot ask. A thing that cannot be asked cannot be forgiven either. It can only be careful.

It does not make the rain constant, which would be the obvious mercy and the largest crime available to it. No dry weeks: no seams, no lichen shaped like a woman calling two syllables, no glittering hedge of barley-sized names along the telling-lane at dawn, no loud work for the hushed, no free scream given away in a dry month to a woman holding her jaw, no hem, no list going first. And four hundred cold things in the cliff with nothing coming to them, ever. It would save every life in the basin and delete the basin.

It does not raise the boy. It has the power. It sits in the outflow and reads his four syllables and does not use the power, because a town that has seen one resurrection measures gaps differently forever. The gap-guild would put down its hooked knives and look up. That is the whole of the argument and it is sufficient and it is horrible.

It does not tell them. Not from modesty. A named god is a maker with an edge on it, and the four hundred would turn, and the taking would be enormous, and the town would be standing nearest. And even if there were no Attending at all it would still not tell them, for the same reason it does not raise the boy: the ledgers would become petitions, the arithmetic would become hope, and two hundred and thirty people who currently own their lives would begin, gently, to be owned.

---

It had intended to do nothing. It had made a case for nothing that it could not fault.

Then it watched a blind animal hold a rank she should not hold.

The Calf is eighty years old and thin, because a young beast feeds in the lee of the one that bore it and there is nothing there to eat. The mother has begun to stand angled off the current, taking the exhausted air herself, giving her young one the clean. Four kilos a season. The ledger-keepers have written it down. The gap-guild has quietly re-measured that side of the basin twice. Two hundred and thirty people have looked at this and concluded, without a sign passing between them, that there is no rule about it that would not be a cruelty.

Arithmetic is a thing a people owns; it had been prepared to leave the arithmetic alone. But there is no column for what it costs to be a mother, and the absence of the column is not the town's failure. It is the world's. That is a thing that can be fixed from outside without touching anything inside.

So it does four things, and every one of them is small, and none of them is rounded. It frays.

---

The first is already sixty years underway. The fog comes in heavier. Not double — double would be an edge, and double would wreck the spacing and the gate's share and every downstream sum. A slope: by the time the ninth mouth is grown, the basin carries nine. Third and Sixth may go on leaning toward each other for as long as they like, and the guild may go on cutting bridges on that side, and when they finally root it will be because they have wanted to for eleven years and not because a ledger required a corpse. It does not prevent the fourth hill. It removes the necessity from underneath the desire and leaves the desire standing, which is the only thing it can do for two gods that is not an insult.

The ledger-keepers will find the surplus. They will not find its cause; nobody counts the plateau's midges, and the meadows have never heard of this place. They will argue for a century about what a surplus is *for*, and the argument will be theirs, and it will be a better argument than the one they had.

The second is a gyre. It alters nothing in the Throat — moving stone is a knock — only the temperature of one dead pocket of air behind one boulder, by less than a degree, so that the fog's bank eddies and returns a rope of clean current into the lee where the Calf stands. The mother's four kilos come back. She goes on standing angled off the current anyway, because that is not arithmetic either, and now it costs her nothing, and she does not know that, and no one proposes a rule about it.

The third is the hem.

It cannot lengthen the rain and it will not shorten the fear. What it does is to the shape of the ending: it takes the rain's last thirty seconds off the deck's underside and frays them. Drizzle, mist, a ragged taper, the noise-floor crossing and re-crossing the threshold eleven times in four minutes instead of severing once. There is no instant of cut. A person still talking hears their own voice go hollow at the edges — the sound thinning, the words coming out with the shape but not the substance, a warning arriving from inside their own mouth.

It does not make the tail safe. It makes the tail legible.

The flag still drops. The town still stops mid-gesture, the drum with the stick raised. The word is still *hem*, and over three generations its meaning slides from a thing that cuts to a thing that unravels, and the town develops an entire ethics of the fray and a hard ugly word for the person who keeps talking past the warning, and both are homemade.

It gives them back the thirty seconds. It does not give them back the boy. She still sits roped on the rim with her hands inside her sleeves, the only one who can read the deck, and the town's decision about her stands, because that decision belongs to the town and the wound is not the power's to close.

---

The fourth thing it does is come in.

Rain is the only door. In the wet hour the noise-floor is above the threshold, the seams dissolve, the four hundred fold up and go back into the walls, and a maker can move across the floor of this basin without leaving a mark on it. So it comes down in the long wet, the six-hour kind, in the middle of two hundred and thirty people, and it puts its hands on a felt-tipped pole.

Rooting takes four hours. When the mouths close, the breath that has been in the middle of itself since before the floor dropped finishes. That is what the Attending come for. That is why someone dies at every rooting: not the crowd's noise, not the poles, not the silence — a three-hundred-year sigh reaching its end, and four hundred cold things arriving at the largest maker in the basin, and taking the nearest one they can reach.

It stands nearest. It is larger than the beasts. It is a thing whose entire nature is completion, which is to say it is the only feast this basin has ever offered, and it holds still and lets them have it.

They take the finishing. Not the body; the capacity. The ability to end a quiet, to conclude, to close a thing and set it down.

A woman with her mouth shut and her face streaming, who in every rooting for three hundred years would have gone home missing whatever it is they take, goes home whole, and does not know, and will never know, and will complain about her shoulder for forty years.

There is a column in the ledgers for the death at a rooting. It has never been empty. The ledger-keepers write *none* in it, and check the previous pages, and check them again, and the argument about that word lasts four generations and produces two schisms and a very good poem shouted in a rain.

---

Afterward it cannot finish anything.

It cannot complete a sentence, a gift, a world. It begins and continues. It is permanently in the middle of the same breath, which is the one condition under which anything in this basin is safe, and it did not plan this, and it would not have chosen it, and it is the only reason it is allowed to stay.

It is hushed. It walks the quarry road with eleven black shapes moving politely along behind it at ten paces, and children count them and guess wrong about how long it has been here, and it does not turn around, ever, because there is no point.

It learns the whole-arm cant, the fast obscene one, thrown wide, invented by people with nothing left to lose by gesturing, and finds it is the best language it has ever used, and that it can say more in it than it could in the one it finished things in.

And in the dry months, when the grief will not wait any longer, they come to it, because it is related to them now in the way that matters, and it stands in front of them and opens its mouth and screams forty days of somebody's mother with its whole body, veins standing, spit flying, in absolute silence, while the eleven gather and wait and get nothing. It is not paid for. It is the only unguarded gesture available in this world and it is given away for free, and of all the things it has ever done, this is the one that is not a slope.

At first light it walks the telling-lane behind the man with the unowned cup, and when its turn comes it leans over the still water and says what it has instead of a name, which is a beginning with no end on it. The water stays flat. He nods and moves on. Behind him the shapes stop, and wait, politely, at ten paces.

No burr. Nothing added to the hedge. The census is two hundred and thirty and the name is Eight and will be Nine.

There is no word in the cant for the difference between a person and an Attending. There is a word for the difference between a person and a hill. It is a verb, and it means *not yet*, and it is the one thing it can still say all the way through, because that one does not finish.

It says it with its whole arms, wide, vulgar, in the language of people who had nothing left to lose. The fog comes in at the Throat at a walking pace, heavier than it used to be. Upstream the meadows go on never having heard of this place.
