# The Guest

## I. Eleven years of nothing

It watched for eleven years and did nothing, and this was not hesitation. A thing that arrives can never afterward have not arrived.

It stood in the blue wedge at 44 and learned the glide by watching children fail at it. It read the noise ledgers, which are the most complete document this people has, more complete than the drawings, because the drawings have been amended toward the stone's preferences and the ledgers have only ever been amended by arithmetic. It read six generations of unsworn tallies in the dry niche under the drawing-house. It read the worm densities off forty-four slate boards and then went down on its belly and looked at the worms, and found that the blind old women were right about the texture of a good year and wrong about why.

It could not decide, at first, what the being here was. The works, cold-hearted and vast, growing its own geology, keeping a species of primate to turn its valves? The keepers, who are the part that moves? The loop, which closed a long time ago and cannot now be opened, and which has no outside and therefore no address to be spoken to?

It stopped trying to decide. Ownership is not the useful question in a closed loop. It looked instead for the places where something was not being attended to, and it found four, and none of them was the heat.

The bar on the door at the district edge, where a person who has walked eleven kilometers of glare-land with forty grazers on their back is given cake and a number and no bed.

The unmarked shapes out in the blanks, going into the stone faster than anyone, with no slate and no one to cut around them and no one who knows to look.

The felt rooms past the salt pans, where loudness is permitted and company is not.

The standing whose slates lapsed forty years ago, who did not ask to go into the drawings, who simply ran out of descendants.

Four instances of one sentence, which occurs over and over in the record of this place, in four different mouths: *both of these things are true and everybody has arranged not to look at them together.*

## II. The water it did not make

It could have made water. This is the first thing that must be understood about it, and the last thing it will ever explain to anyone.

Cold out of nothing. Fins at dew point without absorbers, without the bubble-lift, without the ammonia thread, without the salt going back. Nine thousand towers' worth of yield for no labor and no debt. It could have done this on the first morning, in the time it takes a float to hang a half-degree off true.

Consider what would have survived it.

The noise ledger would have become a decoration. The shout would have cost nothing, and the dropped wrench nothing, and the eleven years of payments against a woman who screamed when her son went into a channel would have been eleven years of nothing, worked off on the chutes for nothing, forgiven in the fourth year by a district that had nothing to forgive. The forty-one motions would have become calisthenics. The silence would have become a habit instead of a yield, and a habit is a thing children abandon. The ninety seconds of hail would have stopped being the only free noise any of these people will ever have, and a man at 19 would have had no reason to shout four syllables into every storm for thirty-one years, and would have had to find some other way to say his son's name out loud.

Everything here that is worth having is made of the difficulty. The names are made of the glare. The felt is made of the silence, and the felt is made of grandmothers. The whole moral apparatus of this basin — debt, payment, forgiveness, the metered voice, the light that means *you are wanted* across thirty kilometers — is a structure raised on the fact that water is hard.

So it made a list of what would be missed, and it was a long list, and the heat was on it, and the stone was on it, and the two millimeters a year were on it, and the ultraviolet that silvers an eye from the edges in was on it, and the bark was on it.

The bar on the door at the district edge was not on the list.

That was the whole discrimination. It is not a large one. It is the only one available. You may take a bar off a door without taking the glare off the floor.

## III. The body it took

It took a body that obeys the local physics, because a body that does not is a shout, and a shout costs a liter, and it had decided not to spend water it had not earned.

The body calcifies. It has knuckles like coral and a wrist-ridge where a wrench has rested. It ate the quick in the galleries for nineteen years and now gums its food, and is not embarrassed about it, and the not-being-embarrassed took longer than the teeth. Its eyes have the white ring and the bright hole. It came down off the crowns at the ordinary age and was retrained to listening, and it is a mediocre listener, because it can hear the meniscus but it cannot hear the year.

It made the body hungry. This was not sentiment. A ferryman's leverage is that nights are scarce and bodies are few, and an inexhaustible volunteer who does not need cake would break the price of the only labor the blanks have to sell. So it made a body that needs cake, and it eats the cake, in public, with its gums, and it takes the number on the slate and does not wait up.

Where it works for nothing, it works only where no one was working at all: the unmarked dead, the lapsed slates, the felt rooms. There is no wage to spoil in a market that does not exist.

You cannot tell it from a ferryman at forty paces. You cannot tell it from a channel-born at four. This took it ninety years to arrange, and it is the achievement it is proudest of, and it has never mentioned it.

## IV. Three liters

In its third season it dropped a wrench at the base of 44-118.

Three liters. It could have made them back in less than a heartbeat. It stood in the blue shade with its mouth shut and let the loss be entered against the two-flash provisional name it had been given on arrival, the way a child is given one, because it had arrived knowing nothing.

Then it worked the debt off on the chutes, four seasons, and was called the word for a man who drops things, and did not deny it, and kept coming. It has been called that word for a hundred and eleven years now. It is the oldest bearer of the word on the floor. The word has not stopped being unkind. But there is a generation of children at 44 who have only ever heard it applied to the oldest person they know, who has never once argued with it, and something has happened to the word in their mouths that did not happen in their grandparents'.

It did not plan this. It has no technique. It only declined to leave, which is the one thing it has that anybody here lacks.

## V. It invented nothing

Every single thing it does is copied from something the keepers already do for their own.

The families of the coughing stand at the last tower in line at dusk and flash gossip at the felt rooms for hours — complaints about the worm tallies, nothing important — so that a person inside can watch their own name arrive and know they are still inside the conversation. That is not its invention. That is the highest thing this culture does, and it does it beautifully, and it does it for kin.

The keepers cut the accretion around their dead, never off them, and once a year someone props a slate with a name against the stone, and the grease-pencil must be renewed, so someone must keep coming. That is not its invention either.

A ferryman carries forty grazers in a wet felt basket across eleven kilometers of open night and puts them down at a cold base before dawn and is gone before the light. The gene flow of an entire basin is a person walking at night with a basket. Not its invention. Its whole contribution to the ethics of this place is a single amendment, and the amendment is not a new kindness. It is the removal of the words *for their own*.

It goes down into the galleries and comes up at dusk with the tallies, moving on its forearms' memory, wrongly, visibly, and children stare at it, and it lets them. It carries herds only across the crossings no receiving district will pay for, where the lineage is already going out like a lamp and there is no one to leave cake, and it hands back every route a ferryman will take. It sits in the felt rooms. Not outside. Inside, where the hair-felt is packed floor to crown and a body can be as loud as it needs to be, and where in six generations nobody has ever gone in and been loud *with* anyone.

It coughs there. It has the bark. It took the bark on purpose, and this is the one decision it made that the listeners, who have the most authority and the least patience, consider genuinely indecent.

## VI. The second column

At every district head there is a slate board, and on it the worm densities, which everybody reads and everybody understands without needing it explained.

Underneath, in the same grease-pencil, in the same hand, it began to write two more numbers.

Nights walked. Doors closed.

No commentary. No name of a district that closed a door, no name of a ferryman turned away, no argument, no sermon, nothing that could be answered or resented or called an accusation. Two integers, renewed each season, in the format the culture already trusts absolutely.

It has been doing this for eighty-one years.

Nineteen districts have taken the bar off the door. Nine took it off and put it back. Six have never changed anything and post the numbers anyway, because pages are kept. At 12 somebody added a third column that is a caricature of its stoop, and it has renewed the caricature every season for thirty years without altering it, and the joke has gone stale in a way that jokes do when nobody defends themselves.

It did not compel this and could not have. A tally board is not a command; it is a thing this people cannot not read. It used their own instrument on them and then went away and carried a basket, and what happened after that was theirs, and took eighty-one years, and is not finished, and may not finish.

The ledger has no column for what happened at 19 in the fourth decade, when a district took the bar off and then, three seasons later, quietly began cutting around the shapes out in the blank at its own edge. There is no column for that. It has not made one. Making one would be a shout.

## VII. Four syllables

At noon in the shade-wedge, when nothing may be done and no one may cross the glare, it sits on felt and does the forty-one motions with everyone else, and it writes on slate, and it asks questions, which nobody here does, because asking costs water.

It asked the man at 19 what the four syllables mean.

He did not answer for six years. Then he wrote them down and wiped them and gave the slate back.

They are the gloss of a two-flash name. A child gets a provisional name at birth and earns the rest of it by doing something worth calling across thirty kilometers. His son went into a channel in the wet hour at four years old and never earned any more of his name, and there is no way to say a two-flash name out loud on the Sill except in ninety seconds of hail, and so a man has stood in the blue shade with his mouth open every storm for thirty-one years shouting the whole of his son's name at the sky.

The guest did not console him. It went out to the district edge and found the rise, and cut around it, and propped a slate.

And at dusk, from a tower the man has never worked on, it flashed two elements. And then a hold. And then the two again.

He has never learned who sends it. It has never told him. It intends to keep sending it after he goes into the stone, and it has arranged for the sending to continue after that, and the arrangement is not a miracle; it is four people at three districts who have agreed to do a small thing at a particular hour forever, which is the only kind of immortality this place permits.

## VIII. Up the main

Nobody has ever gone up the covered main to check.

It went. It has never said what is at the top, and the listeners have stopped asking, and the two factions have stopped asking, and this silence is the only thing it has ever withheld from anyone, and it withholds it because an answer would have been a shout thirty kilometers wide.

What is known is that it began carrying slates.

It takes the unsworn tallies out of the dry niche under the drawing-house at 44 — six generations of nonsense patterns flashed at the northern rim, written down by crown-hands who would not swear to them — and it carries them up, and it comes back down with slates in a different hand.

It does not translate. It does not interpret. It lays them in the niche and goes out to walk a blank.

The crown-hands are working it out. They are fifteen and fearless and barefoot and going blind on schedule, and they have begun flashing patterns that are not nonsense, and getting patterns back that are not their own light off rim ice, and the argument that was theology for two hundred years is becoming correspondence, which is work, which is theirs.

Somewhere out past the main there is a second bowl with a second heresy in it, and probably a second set of blanks, and probably a bar on a second door. It has not said. It has begun, however, to carry.

## IX. The names at dusk

This is the thing it does that the basin will not forget, and it costs nothing, and it did not invent it.

The ferrymen's dead lie out in the blanks, unmarked, going into the stone with nobody to cut around them. From the rim, in the mirage hour, you can see the shapes if you know to look, and the tower keepers do not know to look.

It walked all eleven blanks for thirty years and learned the shapes. It learned them from the ferrymen, who remember, who have touch-names tapped on the inside of a wrist and no light at all; and from the cake-slates with numbers on them, which are a record, which nobody had ever thought of as a record; and from the felt baskets, which are inherited, and which have marks.

And at dusk, from the last tower in line, it flashes them.

Hundreds. Names that no district has ever called, in the medium this people uses for love, at the hour when every eye on the floor is already on the mirrors. Long, short, hold. A rise out in the white that has been a feature for sixty years and is now, suddenly, a rhythm of light arriving out of the glare at a child on a walkway at 12, who does not know whose name it is, and looks it up, and finds that there is a record now, because it made one.

The tower keepers know to look. That is the entire change. They know to look.

## X. What to do with its body

It has not said whether it will stop. It has left instructions, and the instructions are that the district must decide, and it will not decide for them, and it has removed itself from the argument by the simple method of not being reachable during it.

So the argument about the guest's body is being conducted in ninety-second increments, in hail, at the tops of everyone's lungs, and has been for four decades. The orthodox say cut around it: it is the oldest person on the floor and it came here and stayed and there must be a rise and a slate and someone to keep coming. The others say what it says it wants, which is to go into the accretion, to be quarried in its season, sorted, and built into the next tower, so that some part of it ends up as a fin making cold at four in the morning with a ring of grazers crowded at its base.

They scream this at each other with the ice bouncing off the stone. Then it stops, and the terraces flood, and they wring out their felt in absolute silence with their faces wrecked, and go back to work, and the fleas hatch and the floor goes pink to the horizon.

It has attended eleven of these. It sits on felt in the blue wedge with its mouth open like everybody else and shouts nothing, because it is the subject, and because it has learned that here the loudest thing you can do is let people finish.

## XI. The account, in its own hand

*Kept because pages are kept. Slate, grease-pencil, renewed each wet season. To be entered against my name.*

One wrench, dropped, 44-118, third season. Three liters. Paid, chutes, four seasons.

Words at tower bases, one hundred and eleven years. Tally appended. Paid as incurred.

One shout, in hail, with everyone. Nothing. The only free thing I have ever done, and I did not invent it.

Nineteen years of the quick. Teeth. Not a debt; a wage.

Cake received, all routes, all seasons. Eaten. Every crumb, in front of them.

Water made out of nothing: **none.** Cost: **none.** Largest item in this account. Will not be read aloud, because there is no one here who would not already know.

Doors I did not take off the hinges: all of them. Nineteen came off. Nine went back on. Six stand. I am still asking at the six.

*Balance: owing. I intend to keep it that way. To be owed on this floor is to own, and I did not come to own anything, and a closed account is a god who has finished with you.*

---

They gave it two flashes at arrival, the way you do for a child, because it came here knowing nothing. It has the rest of it now. Nine elements: two long, a hold, three quick, a hold, and a last long that runs longer than the last element of any other name on the floor, because that element means *still coming*, and it is the only part of the name it chose itself.

The gloss is *The One Who Comes Back Late*. It has been in the record all along, unremarked, among the ordinary names, like the ferrymen.

An hour before dawn the old silver-eyed woman at 44-118 writes the number and hands it up, and above her a crown mirror turns, and eleven kilometers away a child sees a name arrive out of the white air and starts down the walkway — sole flat, weight rolling along the outside of the foot, hip carrying the leg — without a sound.

Sometimes it is hers. Sometimes it is a rise out in a blank that has been a feature for sixty years.

She goes either way. That is the whole of what it did.
