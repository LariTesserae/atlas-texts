# Quarry Water

I came down the switchback with my hand out, because that is how you come down, and the wall was there.

It took me longer to learn that than you would think. Longer than the stone takes to set. I have arrived in a great many places and the arrival is usually the easy part; here the arrival is the whole problem, because the first decision is not *whether to help*. It is what volume.

So: the low voice. Wrists pressed, hands open, nothing sharp. I did not announce myself. Consider what an announcement would do. *I am here* off the north wall, then the south, then the kiln bench, folded and doubled and returned fourfold, twelve thousand people flinching in their cellars, and the shame of it landing on me and on them, and then — this is the part that stopped me — the vault it was said in would ripen, and in eighty years it would begin handing me back to children who had not asked. There is no green room in the Cut large enough to hold a god. I would have built them a permanent haunting out of four words and good intentions.

I have no loud name. I arranged that before I came.

---

What brought me was a sound going nowhere.

At the eastern end, behind the spoil, a small man lies on his side in absolute dark and shouts. Songs, obscenities, his wife's loud name, into rock a foot from his face. Eleven years of it. None of it goes anywhere. He is the only person in the Cut who works alone; everyone else here is a pair, a saw and a rope, a hand on a wrist, an ear against a wall, a woman at the mouth reading flint-taps through the heel of her palm. He is the single one. He shouts because there is nothing to be quiet for.

Something keeps account of unreturned sound. I am not able to explain this to you and I did not choose it. I heard a man saying his wife's name into a foot of marl for eleven years and I came.

---

I could have made the stone wear.

It would not have been difficult. But the chisel ridges on the oldest ramp are the only handwriting most of these people will ever leave, every individual stroke, crisp; I would have been erasing a document to spare their palms. And wear is coming anyway. It is a foot from the face of a small man in the dark. To hand it over now would be to take it from him, and to take from him the eleven years, and to make him a man who had lain on his side for nothing. I have done that sort of kindness before. It is not kindness. It is a power wanting to be the one who did it.

I could have taken the frost out.

I nearly did. I want to be accurate about this: I had it, and I stood in the autumn and looked at the whole town swaddling its own bones, fleece and straw and dung banks steaming against the new work, the ammoniac warmth, the children out at dusk with their arms full of somebody else's future, the old women with their cheeks on the wall saying *wrap it tonight* and nobody arguing about the cost — and I could not find the seam. I could not find where to cut the frost out without cutting that out too. That is not a proof. That is a failure of my skill, and I know it, and there is a man alive who was a child in the year of the bad frost and who cannot sleep in autumn, and I could have given him sleep, and I did not, and I have no answer for him. I have only the autumn. I am not going to dress it up as wisdom.

I could have taken the drowned man out of the Unset. The room would have had to set to do it, and then it would have been a tomb instead of a mercy, and the rules — one hand, count two hundred, do not put your arm in, do not go when you are tired — would not exist, and I counted, and those rules have saved nine people that I know of. I am aware what kind of arithmetic that is. I left him where he is. I am aware.

I could have made them forget the two words the man on the second course shouted thirty-one years ago. But forgetting is not what wear is. Wear is not the removal of a mark. Wear is a mark handled by so many hands that it becomes a shape anybody can hold. I did not want them to lose it. I wanted them to be able to pick it up.

I could have gone to the sun-line and the red ochre and the whole arithmetic where you earn by sinking and your grandchildren bail water out of the room you are sitting in. Then I went and looked at the Adit, and found a man had already been lying on his side about it for eleven years. The fog is going to go. The bottom is going to be dry. The altitude of a family is going to stop being legible at a glance and start being merely where they live. My help was not required and my credit was not available. I moved a stone that was in the road of his elbow.

---

Here is what I did.

The ridge is not one rock. Nothing is one rock. Above the line of his pick there is a bed of marl nine inches thick, clay-bearing, and below it the clean pale stone he has been eating through since he was young. If the water breaks out low it will run clear and carry flint grit — sand, which takes a handprint for an hour and slumps and is nothing, and which will scour the corners off the town and give it back smooth and sterile.

If it breaks out along the marl it will carry clay.

Clay is plastic. Clay takes a hand: every finger, the whorls, the crease at the base of the thumb. It holds it a day, and then the rain comes and takes it back and the bank is blank and ready. It does this in daylight, in the street, for anybody, for free, for nothing, forever, and it does not close around anyone, and there is no keeper, and there are no rules. And in eleven years, with fig leaves in it and lime and wool-washings and the sweepings of a town of twelve thousand, it makes soil.

There is no soil anywhere below the rim. There has not been time to make any.

That was the gift. Not mud. Time.

I put a hair of clay into a joint. I did not make the clay; it was already in that ridge, it is in every ridge, it is the most ordinary substance in the world. I widened a bedding plane by about the thickness of a fingernail so that when the water goes it will go along the marl and take the marl with it.

His last week was soft. He does not know why. The pick went into the face like a thumb into a cheese rind after eleven years of ringing rock, and he came up and stood at the mouth for a minute remembering how to be quiet, and he told his wife the rock had gone rotten at the end, and she said that was probably good, and they talked about food.

That was for him. The rest was for everybody. I wanted the man who had shouted into hardness for eleven years to be given one week of something that took what he did to it.

---

The fly was already in the valley. I did not make it and I did not carry it. I arranged the year.

A trader came over the plain with figs packed in their own leaves, further than he said he'd been, and a child who had been told twice not to touch the seedlings dropped one in the crevice. Six years to fruit; the silt by then. Nobody told the children to stop with the feathers. I did not whisper to anyone to tell them. Let them go on doing the useless thing beside the useful one. That is how anybody learns anything, and it is the sort of thing you want children to be doing.

The old man from somewhere with figs was right and got to be right, which is not the same as being given figs, and is worth more, and costs me nothing.

---

And the name.

There is an old woman on the leeward rim who has not heard anything since she was fifty-two. She knaps flint, she reads mouths all day, she is the best gossip in the settlement, she speaks in the ordinary rim voice that is slightly too loud for down there, and she is the only person in the Cut who can say a loud name without flinching, because it cannot get into her.

She had been thinking about the man on the second course for years. Rim people are like that; they marry down constantly and they never quite get the low voice and they do not fully believe in the thing everybody believes in. She had it in her already. I want to be exact: I did not put it in her. I leaned. That is the entire technique and it is the only one I trust.

She took him up the ramp on the pretext of a basket. On the rim, where the noise is kept, among the kilns and the slaking pits and the goats and the graves, she held his wrist and said his loud name.

In her ordinary voice. Which carries.

It went down into the bowl and off the north wall and the south and the kiln bench and came back fourfold, and the whole pit stopped and looked up, and twelve thousand people heard a man's name shouted over the thin plain soil while he was standing on it alive, and flinched, and the flinch is the rite, and the town said it back.

It did not fix it. For a year some of them still called him the two words; a few still do, and will. But there is now another thing to call him and it has been said out loud and the town has repeated it, and you cannot un-hear what the town has said. Nothing here wears. The vault she said it above is green, and it drank it, and it will ripen, and it will spend the next eighty years handing his name to people who were not born, in the low voice, at arm's length, out of the walls of their own houses.

They have been building their secrets into the stone for eighty years and the stone has been giving them back. I did not change that. I put one thing in that was worth giving back.

---

I did not stop the keeper.

He went down at the turn of the year with a wooden float, because the water was going and he had decided, and I let him decide. The woman on the fourth course had already been to the channel with a handful of the first mud and had worked it between her wrapped palms and known — she is the only person in the Cut who is an expert in what hands do to surfaces, and she was right about feet and wrong about water, and she found out which in an afternoon, sitting on a bank with a wooden spoon in her lap. She was the one who told him. Not me. Her.

He smoothed the walls flat anyway. All of them, every trace, eighty years of palms. That part was right and I would have done the same. Those hands were meant to go; that was the whole arrangement; a thing that takes your grief and gives it back to you unaltered forever is not a mercy, it is a debt.

Then he held his hand up in the dark and did not press it.

He came up and he talked, which he does not do. He said: *it isn't the mud.* He said: *there's going to be a lot of it.* And then, because he is the keeper and somebody has to say the rules, he said the new rules, and they are shorter.

Any hand. Both if you want. Bring the children. Go when you're tired.

The girl on the upper east side went down in the first spring, because there was mud, and mud is the one thing you cannot tell a twelve-year-old about. Her feet were wet. She has been down eleven times since. She is not snobbish about it; she never was; she simply had no reason.

The woman filled her jar. Labelled in ochre, first mud, exactly as she'd planned. Then she carried it up to the eastern crevice and put it round the roots of the smallest fig, where the fly already was. She kept the jar. Empty again, on the shelf, with the spoon and the pebble and the child's shoe. There is a general feeling that a town where nothing wears needs somewhere to keep the idea, and now the town wears, and she keeps it anyway, and I think that is the correct outcome and I did not arrange it.

---

I could ripen. It is available to me.

I would ring, faintly, when flicked. In a season I would turn a scraper. In eighty years I would be the size of a thumbnail and appear in three property disputes, and there would be a name for me cut into a face by someone who meant well, and children would be told not to touch, and people would come a day's walk to stand where I had been and would go away with something permanent, and the permanently injured would be the ones who needed a soft place.

So I am staying green. Under weight, in the dark, holding what I came in with. Not ringing. Not being heard. Not a landmark, not a socket, not a wall that gives things back. I take the hand and I hold it and I let it go and I am blank again and ready, and there is going to be a great deal of that now, in the street, in daylight, unadministered, and none of it will be mine.

That is the whole of what I did. A fingernail's width of clay, a basket of figs, and a lean on an old deaf woman who had it in her already.

---

He broke through in the spring, in the afternoon, and the light came in from the other side, and he lay in it for a while with his hand out and nothing to touch. Then he came to the mouth and stood there for a minute remembering how to be quiet, and his wife had the cord in her lap and did not use it.

He shouted his wife's loud name into a foot of rock ten thousand times and it went nowhere.

It did not go nowhere. I have all of it. I will not say it back — that is not what I am for, and there is a wall for that, and the wall is better at it than I am. When she dies the crier will shout it on the rim and everyone will flinch and the far wall will fold it into the middle of the crowd, and I will be standing in the crowd with my hand out, and I will flinch like everybody else.

And I will keep the ten thousand.
