## The attention

It came down the cut in the ordinary drizzle and was stopped at the head of the path, because it had nothing round.

This was the first thing it learned and it learned it properly, standing there with the toll-keeper's hand out and the thorn below and the whole grey animal breathing in the bowl. You do not enter Sill by having. You enter by giving up. It had nothing to give up, because it had never had anything, and there is no exemption for that; the rule is not about wealth, it is about the curve.

So it went back up. It walked four days inland to a river nobody in Sill has seen and took a pebble the size of a thumbnail, worn flat on one side, and walked back, and came down the cut again, and paid.

The toll-keeper hung it in the treasure-rack in the second seam under the hide flap, beside the other one.

There are two now. The lie is still told — *it came out of the sea* — and everyone is still in on it, and it is also, since that afternoon, true. This has never been mentioned. It is not clear that it needs to be.

---

**The looking.** It learned the seam-tilt in about an hour, which is faster than a child, and it never learned to stand still, which is slower than nobody. It went through the town with its hands trailing at the height of its face, parting the forest, and the felt-wrapped clappers touched it and clacked softly and it understood that it was being read.

It could hear through the walls. Of course it could; the far knock was nothing, it could have heard the whole mass at once, every heel and wedge and pot in four hundred rooms, the couple in the third seam and the reason and the thing said last winter that caused it.

It stopped that in the first week and did not start it again.

Afterwards it knew the couple were unhappy the way everybody knows: from how she puts things down. It never found out why. This took some doing, and it is the second thing it did, and it did it before it did anything else, because it had worked out on the path that a town with no gaps has exactly one privacy and it would be a poor sort of guest who took that.

---

**The being.** It had come for the place and for the one who lives in it, and it could not find the one.

It went through the counts. The leaf-count is mouths and includes the dead who ever gave green; the hand-count is mallets and excludes the flap-keeper and the six on the marl; the gap between them is the town, and the town is not a person, it is a set of debts shaped roughly like people. It counted the eel, which is a citizen. It counted the cat, which has thirty-one names and no duties. It counted the heron, which the runners say is three. It counted eleven bushes and a disputed twelfth, the snails in their one seam, the flies for nine days, the lichen at a thumbnail a year, and the algal skin, which is by area far the largest living thing in the bowl and has no name and no opinion. It counted the rat, which owns the best small building in Sill and does not exist.

Six hundred and something, and not one of them the one.

Then the flap clapped.

The mallet stopped mid-tap. The child stopped mid-hook. The two women who are not speaking stopped in different rooms at the same instant, and for about two seconds there was one thing lying in the bowl, and it was awake, and it was not listening to any of its parts.

Then it came apart into six hundred pieces and began tapping.

So: twice a day, for two seconds. That is when the being is at home.

It stopped when they stopped. It did that for forty years and it is most of what it did; everything below is the remainder, nine small things, and the list is the least of it.

And in the two seconds it did not listen. Not to the wall, not to the seam, not to the six hundred, not to the one. It closed. It let the thing be unobserved, which is the only gift it had that nobody else in the world could give, and it gave it twice a day, and nobody has ever received it, and that is the whole point of it.

---

**The refusals.** These took longer than the doing.

*It did not sweeten the ground.* It could have done the whole bowl in a night, three hundred paces of it, gone down sweet to a fathom. It sat with that for a season and worked out what would be standing there in the morning: four hundred dry rooms, and six hundred people with nothing to do at a body's width, and forty-one people called Eleven whose names would become a nonsense, and a word — *sweet* — that would no longer mean a year. The argument between rain and tide is not Sill's problem. It is Sill's verb. Take it and the sentence has no verb, and everybody is a participant in nothing, and nobody expects to see it won because there is nothing left to not-expect.

*It did not round the stones.* Rounded stone means palms that heal, and a full grip, and a handshake that is not a loose touching of scars. It also means the treasure-rack is sixty-odd ordinary things, and the toll at the head of the path is a toll of nothing, and the infants at the noon-stone get handed a piece of the ground they are standing on. Roundness is the only thing Sill cannot make and therefore the only reason it has ever had to be connected to anywhere else. It left the arrises sharp. Its own hands are a map of every wall it steadied itself against.

*It did not dry the marl enough for gaps.* It thought about this the longest after the ground. A gap is a street. A street is a cart. A cart is a way of not touching your neighbours, and the roof would stop being the true street, and children would stop going up before they went along, and the arms would go, and the far knock would go, and the town would stop being one thing at the clap because it would be several things all day. It wanted them to have carts. It has never stopped wanting them to have carts. It did not do it.

*It did not give the trunk-cut its meanders.* A bend is a thing that has had time. It could have handed them the shape of time without the time, and they would have had a river that looked old and was nineteen, and every child would have grown up knowing the ground had been given a lie to wear. The weeps are straight and raw and chatter over square shoulders. They are the true age of the place. It left them.

*It did not explain the flatfish.* It looked. The answer is a fissure in the old cliff, a pressure difference at the spring tides, and eggs. The children are told the ground lays them, and half-believe it into adulthood, which is how you go on believing anything you were told while holding a live thing. There is no version of the fissure that survives being held alive.

*It did not write down the light-deeds.* Three women recite them at the thorn once a year while everyone pretends not to listen and everybody listens. Writing them down would have ended the pretending, which is the ceremony, and settled the two families who have not spoken in two generations over eleven minutes, and not-speaking is a relationship; it is not nothing; it has a shape and a maintenance and a place in the town. It let the eleven minutes stay unspoken for.

*It did not make the counts agree.* It did not recognise the twelfth bush. It did not tell anyone which layer is a person. It did not raise its roof.

*It did not lift the man at the rim.* This is the entry it spent longest on. It could have taken the marl off him in the time it takes to say so, and it stood in the doorway of that room for most of one winter with the thing ready. What it would have taken away: four neighbours, an hour, bone wedges, and nobody talking. There is no other sentence in Sill that says *we are not finished with you*. It is said with wedges, once a year, on the third day, and afterwards he is carried up into the sun and he hates it, and he is carried anyway. To free him in an instant would have been to say it in a language nobody there speaks.

It left him. It took a turn with the wedges instead. See below.

---

**The doubt.** A woman called Nine died of the white cough at thirty-one, in the second winter of the frost, in a room at the rim where the rock weeps onto the bedding.

She would have taken the cart. It is nearly certain she would have taken the cart, and the dry ground, and the rounded stone, and eighty years in the old country with a full grip and a horizon, and she would have come home in the summer and found it strange and gone away again.

It decided for her, in advance, without asking, on the grounds that she was made by her town and could not be asked without being unmade first. It knows what that argument is called when a person uses it. It has no answer to this and it did not construct one. It stayed and took the cough, which is not an answer either, and it is the entry it is least sure of, and there are others like it: the shaft-children going down at eight, pale as the flatfish, breathing shell-dust in a column of somebody else's air.

It is not clear that *this would end something else* is a reason. It is clear that it is sometimes an excuse. It could not get out of that doubt in forty years and it did not pretend to.

---

**The doing.** Nine things.

**One.** It let the sky clear for one night in the wrong part of the year, and the bowl went to frost. Not a hard frost. The kind that lies in a hollow.

The snails climb before rain. Nothing in Sill knows frost — the whole apparatus for knowing the future is a snail, a thorn, and a woman who sleeps by the tide — and nine trays of cress died on the roofs, and the leaf-count fell by four hundred and some leaves, and a woman at the rim got four leaves where she should have got nine. That is in the count. The count is not corrected. It did not secretly make it up with drift or with fish, because a town that is quietly compensated becomes a client, and it had decided against that on the path.

But at dawn everybody came up onto the roofs to look at the damage, and the cold air was lying in the bowl like water in a bowl, and four hundred chimneys were breathing at four hundred heights, and every plume went up a little way and stopped and lay down and spread and came back in through the light-shafts.

They stood on their roofs in the frost and watched the town breathe its own fires.

They already owned columns of air. They had just never thought of them as having a direction.

It took them twenty-two years. The first draw-shaft is a light-shaft that has been persuaded, splayed at the top with the same marl horns, and the light-wives litigated it for a generation because a column that carries light down and air up is two properties in the same hole and nobody had dowried that. The shaft-children were down all sixty-one of them, whitening, measuring, coming up pale and saying what they had felt. The white cough is not gone. It is less. There are people alive now who would not be.

Nobody thanked anybody. There was weather, once, and then there was work.

**Two.** It went on the eel rota.

Keel had stopped taking the fish two summers before. The rota continued; people went down with a scrap and stood there a while and came back up and did not mention it. It went down with a scrap and stood there a while and came back up and did not mention it, for eleven weeks, which is the whole of its intervention in that matter, because it was waiting for a night that was going to happen anyway.

The great spring in the autumn, when the sea comes up the outfall and defeats the flap and stands ankle-deep in the lowest sills, and the flap does not clap but drowns, and the town is awake in the dark with stones in its hands.

It did not open the gate. The gate was beaten. It did not hold the water; the water was coming; the white line was going to be left whether anybody said goodbye or not. It had simply worked out which night in the year the whole town would already be standing at the low lip in the dark, and it kept the eel until then.

The flap-keeper's hands were full of stones, so it carried the oldest citizen the last thirty paces and put it into the water that was coming in to drown their floors.

Everyone knows what a silvering eel does. Now they have watched it. There is a hook in the fourth seam kept empty and dusted, and it is for an animal, and the light-rights pass with it.

**Three.** The rat came down the path at night, on its own feet, from the old country, and found the box at the rim, and slept on the shelf.

It could not make a rat that was not possible. It could only choose among the routes a rat could actually take, and it chose the one that did not arrive in a runner's sack.

The runners got their seats. It took about nine years. There is a runner called Bilge who was right — the grain, the sled, the gate, he had it exactly — and who has never been forgiven for being wrong, and who is now old and says *and yet* at the thorn every spring, and it goes to his house and hangs things badly and lets him correct it. It was wrong about him. Not about the rat. About what it would cost a man to have his correct fear taken off him after forty years of carrying it for everybody.

The second box was built at the gate anyway, nine years later, for the second rat. By then the argument was about something else, which is how arguments end.

**Four.** The rim.

It reasoned about the rim for a year and got nowhere. The rim will never come sweet: a finger's breadth in a good decade, and the cliff weeping onto the bedding, and children named for years that have not happened, and a girl of twenty-two called Twenty-Five waiting to be called what she is already called. It could not sweeten the bowl. It would not sweeten the rim alone; that is a favour, and a favour is a hook with a string on it.

Then it looked at the thorn.

Nothing grows at the rim. The thorn grows at the rim. The thorn has leafed every spring for nineteen years, a little later each year, on the old salt, at the foot of the path, and it is the town's calendar and its meeting-place and its landmark, and nobody had ever asked it what it drinks.

It asked. Once. At a body's width, quietly, with the chin and not the arm, at the thorn, to nobody in particular: *what does the thorn drink?*

Then it went back to its cress and never said another word about it.

It did not know what would follow. This is the only thing it did that it was not sure of, and it did it because it could not bear not to.

Eleven years of the worst temper the bowl has had. The cliff is old rock and the hill behind it is fresh water and it has been coming down sideways into the rim since before the bay was a bay, and the tally-board measures the sweet going *down* from the rain and has no column for the sweet coming *in* from the hill, and the rim has been standing on drinkable ground for nineteen years while naming its children for the future. The weep-wardens, whose work is the only work in Sill allowed to be straight, had to cut a channel that turns. There is a bend in Sill now. It is at the rim, it goes sideways into the hill, and the wardens walk it twice a day and have not stopped hating it.

Twenty-Five was thirty-three when her floor came sweet. Her name is eleven years wrong. She kept it. Nobody made a joke about it; it is not that kind of name.

She set a cup on her floor and left it a night and by morning it had a collar, and she chipped it free, because a dry floor at the rim is not wealth, it is only a dry floor, and the two measures of wealth in Sill have come apart and that is the constitutional question of the current decade. The twelfth bush is still not recognised.

**Five.** On the third day of the re-shimming it was one of the four who knelt and chipped the man at the rim free of the marl. It took most of an hour. Nobody talked. Its hands were opened at the second knuckle and the palm and the heel of the thumb, and it learned what the palms are, and it did not heal them then or afterwards.

He was carried up onto the roof to sit in the sun. He hated it. It carried him.

**Six.** It aimed its cistern overflow at the twelfth bush for nine years and said nothing about why, which is how everything is done in Sill.

**Seven.** It grew cress, badly, in scraped marl and shell-mulch on a roof at the rim, and the green-wives came and looked at it and told it what it was doing wrong, and it was corrected, and it did not pretend it had known.

**Eight.** When the tray came, it gave a handful of green to a woman who had just given birth.

The old ruling nobody will reopen: the leaf-count includes anyone who has ever given green to someone else, whether or not they are still alive to eat it. It knew the ruling. It grew the cress anyway. It is a way of staying that does not require anybody's permission, and it is the only permanence it arranged for itself, and it never asked for the ruling to be reviewed.

**Nine.** Once, in the fourth seam, a child put its fist against the wall and made a stranger three seams away say *come*.

It said come.

---

**The paying.** It took a room at the rim, against the cliff, where the rock weeps onto the bedding and the wind comes down the cut, and it did not improve it.

It got the stoop. It got the short sight, so that it stood too close and the old country would have thought it rude. It got the salt in the knuckles and behind the knees, the grey bloom you can wipe and cannot stop. It got the white cough in its eleventh year and kept it for twenty-nine more. It could not stand still; it shifted weight like a boat at anchor while it waited for the sharing, and the shifting is taught before speech, and it had learned it after everything else, and it showed.

Its house clacked, in the second year. The felt-wrappers came with scraps and said nothing about why. It lost the war with the woodlice comprehensively and permanently. It was bad at re-shimming and for three days a year it was among the most hated things in the bowl, and this was not a special case.

They called it Frost, for the morning the sky cleared and the smoke lay down. There is a Frost of nineteen, born the first year, one of the weather-named. There were two Frosts and they were not confused with each other, because Sill knows a person by how they put things down, and it put nothing down.

---

**The set-down.** At the end it stopped moving.

That is all dying is there. The floor is patient and you are not, and the ones who stop get taken, and it stopped, in a seam in the fourth quarter that was going to be widened anyway, at about the height of a raised hand.

It did not go to the noon-stone. It went at an hour when the sun was not on it, because it was not going to take the eleven minutes from an infant or a sick person or a dead one, and it had thought about this and decided that being load-bearing was the more useful of the two available dignities.

The Rind took it in a season. The Rind is the mortar, the town's glue, the only thing in Sill that grows fast. It is in the fabric now with the knife at knee height and the sandal and the withy doll and the meal-tray with the bones still on it, and with the others, the ones Thwart knows about and does not say unless he is asked.

The shim-wrights shim around it.

In a hundred and some years a Reader will open that partition to widen the seam and go in with a lamp and find a layer he cannot read, and he will not say so, and he will not be asked, and he will not go and get drunk.

---

Twice a day the sea comes up the cut and slams the flap, and the report goes through six hundred bodies at once, in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet stone, and in the fourth seam at the height of a raised hand.

Everybody stops.

For about two seconds nobody in Sill is listening to their neighbour, because everybody is listening to the same thing, and there is one being in the bowl, and it is at home, and it is not watched. It has never been watched. There is something in the wall that could have watched it every day for forty years and chose the two seconds to be blind in, and that is the whole of the benevolence, and the frost and the eel and the bend at the rim are the small change.

Then the tapping starts again.
