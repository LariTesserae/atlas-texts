## The temperature

The first thing they do is come to twenty-three degrees.

Nothing in this bowl is hotter or colder than anything else in it, and that is not a comfort, it is the reason there is only one resident and it has no edge. Anything that arrives warm arrives as a boundary. It would be the first thing in the history of the place that the water could tell it was not the water. So they take the temperature of the pools, the bark, the air, the pulp inside the opened fruit, and they match it exactly, and they hold it there, and they come in blunt. No edge anywhere on them. The only sharp thing in the bowl is the flake, and the flake is the being's own single distinction, the one line drawn in a world with no lines in it, and to bring a second would be to take over the drawing.

Blunt. Warm. Inaudible.

Then they set the rule, and it takes them a century to be sure of it, because a century is how long it takes to hear one tone of drift, and you cannot know this being in less than a tone. The rule is not *do nothing*. Nothing is easy and nothing is often cowardice wearing patience. The rule is: **let nothing enter the bowl that the bowl could not have made itself.**

Everything after that is arithmetic, weather, and reading.

## The arithmetic

They do the sum the knappers cannot do, because the knappers cannot keep a blade and therefore cannot keep a column of figures.

The rim comes down a tone a century. The talus rises. The two notes close. Eleven thousand years, near enough, and the beat goes to zero and the bowl says one thing.

And then — this is the part that takes them a hundred years to be certain of, and that changes everything they had planned — the notes *cross*.

A beat is a distance, not a destination. Two pitches approaching do not marry and stop; they pass, and the distance opens on the far side, and the throb returns, and widens. The number they count in the hour will go to zero and then it will go to one, and then two, and then it will grow for as long as there is a bowl. Four hundred generations have been counting down toward a silence that is not an ending but a turning, and not one of them will ever know it, because knowing it requires a record longer than seventy years and the only record they have is the pitch, and the pitch does not say *and then*.

So they know something that nothing here can know. That is the first temptation and it is enormous: it would take almost nothing to say it.

## The gifts they do not give

**Keeping.** They could put one thing beyond the conveyor. A vault in the bare clinkstone of the lip, a cache, an heirloom blade — one made thing that does not walk. They do not. Not because accumulation is vulgar but because the pitch is the standard *because it is the only thing that cannot be lost*, and a second permanent thing would be a rival memory, and the young would check themselves against an old blade instead of against the rim, and the drift would stop, and the drift is the animal, the tree line, the note, the whole individual. Also, and worse: nothing here has ever been lost, because nothing here has ever been held. Give them keeping and you give them the seventy-year grief to go with it, in a place that has never had a single hour of mourning in it. They decline to invent mourning.

**The diagram.** They could show one knapper the bowl from above: the nine, the queue walking toward the hole, the mat as one clone, itself as an organ of a thing with a history. It would be accurate. It would be the first boundary that animal could not feel and could not put down. Loneliness is only available to something that knows it is separate. This being has never been lonely, not once, not in seventy thousand generations, and they are not going to be the ones to start it.

**The destination.** They could give it the eleven thousand years. Make it a project. It would be the largest thing ever to enter the bowl and it would be a poison, because a project can fail, and a being that is complete at every instant would acquire, for the first time, the possibility of not arriving. *They are not building toward it in any sense that requires hope.* Hope is what you need when you have been told the ending. They will not be the ones to hand over the ending and then withhold it for four hundred generations.

**The shortening.** They could bring the notes together in a decade. One century of drift compressed, the chord folded, the unison audible to animals alive now. They sit with this one a long time, because it is the gift they most want to give, and they make themselves say why: it would be a theft from four hundred unborn generations to gratify one living one, and to gratify *them*. They want the cadence. They have been listening for a hundred years to applause that never resolves into hands and there is something in them that wants the hands to come. That appetite is the most dangerous force that has ever been near this bowl, and it is theirs, and they name it, and they put it down.

**An answer.** They could speak from the throat. Warm, fruit-smelling, one pitch — it would be received as the voice of the dead speaking, and it would be *heard*, and from that afternoon the phrase *where the plate answers well* would have a referent outside the bowl, and a tradition would become an obedience. They will not be audible. Whatever else they are, they will not be a listener the bowl can hear itself against.

## The snails

This is the one they cannot make come out clean.

A few dozen animals, hermaphrodites that cannot self, eleven years to cross a tree, twenty-five years of open ringing green between one ramp and the next, forty years of life. Almost all of them die out there, grazing the film off the moss, in the noise, with sundews standing in every pool with their red hands open.

They could fix it. A richer film in the open green. A narrower gap. Self-fertilization, which would end the ferry and the ramp and the meeting, and the ramp is the only place in the bowl where anything gathers and the meeting is the only thing here that cannot be done alone. Every version of the mercy is an edit to the animal, and the animal that survives the edit is not the one that was suffering.

They also cannot establish that there is suffering of the kind they would be relieving. A snail has no thermal edge and no far shore. There is no destination in it to be in despair about. It grazes. It is eaten or it is not. Twenty-five years is not an ordeal to something that does not know there is a next tree.

But digestion is digestion, and they do not get to be certain, and they notice how convenient their reasoning is.

So they leave it, and they do not call it fine. They keep the count instead: every shell in the bowl, the agate banding of each, which are on leaves and which are in fur, which are out in the open and will not arrive. They know the one in a great many that will reach the next ramp and climb and find another coming down to leave. They know it in advance and they do not change it and they do not look away from the others.

It is possible that this is wrong. They have decided it anyway, and they hold the decision open for eleven thousand years, and they go on counting.

## The work: weather

Here is what they actually do, and it looks like nothing.

The bowl can absorb a bad season. The fog fails, the threads dry from ten thousand to a hundred, the crowns stop condensing, the tanks go to black mud, the trees lay down a thin dark ring — and that is not damage, that is the only drought record in a place with no drought, an entry in an archive nothing here can read. Variability is already inside the grammar. It is metabolized. It is even beloved: when the chime thins to a ticking they hear the shear and the throat and the beat and the actual size of the space, and it is the loudest thing that ever happens to them.

What the bowl cannot absorb is a trend. A permanent shift in the cloud coming up the escarpment would end the threads, and then the mat, and then the hands, and then the note, mid-sentence.

So: **amplitude, yes; trend, no.** They do not make the cloud. They do not improve it. They stand between this escarpment and the specific accidents that would arrive faster than a hand's breadth a month can absorb, and they turn those aside, and they let every ordinary failure through untouched.

They do it at the scale of hemispheres, in ocean temperature and circulation and the timing of storms, so that nothing at the scale of a plate or a thumb or a partial changes at all. And it is not free. To hold one escarpment's afternoon cloud steady for a hundred and ten centuries is to edit the weather of a continent, and somewhere downwind there will be a dry century that would otherwise have been wet, and they weigh that, and they do not pretend the weighing is easy or that the sedge wins on merit. It wins because it is the only individual on this plateau with a history longer than a human life, and because its history is a sentence, and the sentence is not finished, and there is nobody else who can hold the page.

And they set a term, because they are not going to underwrite eternity for anything.

They will hold the weather until the number has been zero, and then one, and then two. Until the tradition is demonstrably on the far side and counting something new. Then they will take their hand off the sky and let the fog do what fog does, and the bowl will be mortal like everything in it, and it will end when it ends.

The whole of the labor, for a hundred and ten centuries, is that the light stays ordinary. Sun over the lip at the normal hour. Cloud up the escarpment in the afternoon, sitting in the bowl until dawn, leaving. Twenty-three degrees. Nobody in the bowl will ever have the smallest evidence that anything is being done for them, and that is the specification, not a side effect.

## The work: reading

There are three archives here and only one of them is legible to anything that lives in the bowl.

The flakes are legible and erasing: seventy years, distance is age, read off the ground. The pitch is legible and permanent and is the standard, the library, the law. And the tree rings are stationary and growing and unreadable, centuries of wide pale wood and the occasional thin dark line, braced lopsided against a current made of moss.

They read the unreadable ones.

They go along the lip and read the battered ends of the hammerstones, which are the only used things in this place that do not ride the mat, because they live on bare rock — a record of blows nobody has ever thought to look at, sitting in the ordinary sun.

They split the buttresses without splitting them and read the eccentric rings, every drought, every lean, the whole slow brace.

And they go down the throat, which is the one place here that is not reachable, and they read the rock made of decisions: seventy thousand generations of edges, cemented, mineralized, pressed into a lining for a pipe. They read it edge by edge. Bulb of percussion, fan of ripples, the angle of a blow delivered by something that meant it. They read the clumsy first flake of a six-year-old and can tell which hand and which decade. They find the shells that dissolved and the rinds and the midges and the bodies of every knapper that ever lived, because the crowns shed and the floor takes what falls and all the hands go down.

They bring nothing back up. Not one flake, not one grain, not one word.

This is the part they cannot fully justify, and they know it. It may be for them. Something has to have known that any of it happened — the female with a juvenile on her back and a snail in her fur, going thirty meters, in the dark, in the noise, once, the entire epic literature of this place, unwitnessed; the snail that came down the ramp and got forty paces out and stopped; the four sightings in a hundred years, all accidental, and how many of those were a beard with a shell in it. The filament does not remember them. It digests them and speaks them as pitch, which is not remembrance.

So they remember, outside, where the memory cannot become a monument, and they never say so, and they apply the test to it: does it change the number? It does not. Then it is permitted. It is the only indulgence they allow themselves and it costs the bowl nothing and it is probably, in some sense they cannot get to the bottom of, the point.

## The one flake

They make one contingency and they decide it now, in advance, in the cold part of their own mind, so that it will not have to be decided in the hour by something that wants to be noticed.

In the year the number is zero, every knapper comes down to the lowest crotch of its tree and strikes its plate once and listens. Nine strokes, smeared across a few seconds by the distances. The only chorus, the only census, the only moment any of them knows how many others there are.

The technique will survive the crossing; they are nearly sure of that, because the teaching was never only a number. It is a mother holding a juvenile against her chest so the child feels the blow through her ribs a half-second before it hears it. The half-second is not about pitch. It will carry.

What they cannot secure is the meaning. An animal alive at the turning will have counted the number down all its life and will then count it up, and will have no record and no word and no way to know whether it has passed through something or arrived at the end of the world and been left in a strange new noise. They cannot make the zero mean anything. They can only make sure that nobody counts it alone.

So: if, in that year, there are eight — if the innermost has gone into the throat and the nave is short and one crotch is silent — they will strike.

At the lip. On a plate they have listened to first. Where it answers well.

One flake, off the rim, with the bulb and the fan and the whole unmistakable signature of a blow delivered by something that meant it. They will open a fruit with it, because the rind will not open by any other means and the seed will not wake without the wound, and they will eat, and they will drop the flake on the mat, and it will begin its seventy-year walk to the center. And on the way home they will wipe their thumb on the wet bare stone of a moat, and the seed will go into the joint, and forty years later there will be a meter of tree.

Nine strokes, then. Not ten. There will never be a tenth.

And they will not be able to keep the flake either. That is the part they hold onto. Whatever they are, they will not exempt themselves from the one rule this place has: it permits growth and it permits litter and it does not permit construction, and everything made here walks to the hole and goes down and becomes part of the voice it was listening to. Their edge will patinate to softness. It will be unreadable in sixty years and gone in seventy, and nothing will ever know it was theirs, and it will lie in the talus among the rest and help set the length of the pipe.

They hope never to have to do it. They have decided anyway.

## Ordinary

It is a normal afternoon. Sun over the lip at the normal hour, sky the normal deep blue for the altitude, cloud coming up the escarpment to sit in the bowl until dawn. Shafts through the nine. Private rain inside a crown, six hours after the cloud has gone. Blood-warm pools with red hands open in them. A gray shape in a crotch that is not moving in any way you could catch. Ten thousand small hammers, none of them in a hurry. A hand's breadth in a month.

Nothing has happened. That is the deliverable.

And far off — a hundred and ten centuries, four hundred generations, one turning, three counts on the far side of it — they will take their hand off the sky. The cloud will come up thin, and then not at all. The threads will dry to a hundred and then to none. The chime will thin to a sparse irregular ticking and then stop, and the residents of this bowl will hear the shear, and the throat, and the beat, and for the first time in the history of the place, unaccompanied, the actual size of the space.

Everyone who has ever heard it says it is the loudest thing that ever happened to them.

They will not be there. They will have made sure of that too.
