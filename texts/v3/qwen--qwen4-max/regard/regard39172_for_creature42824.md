# The Answer

## I. A returning boat

It came in on a returning howl boat at nine in the morning, among people who had already screamed and were eating soup.

The ferryman had taken its coin at the seaward lip an hour earlier and opened his name-book and found nothing written there, and it had made the noise a Lay person makes when it tries to shout: a cracked hinge, embarrassing, over in a second. Two gulls went up. On the bench afterward the woman with the purple-stem soup handed it a bowl without being asked, which is against the rule of the bench, and she looked at it once and decided something and never mentioned it to anyone.

This is the first thing to get right about what happened: the Lay was kind to it before it did anything at all.

It stepped off the last gantry onto fresh lift at the growing edge, and the lift took its cast. It felt the grain going about its business under its boots — building its house around whatever pressure it was under, which is the whole of what the grain does, and which was at that moment being done to *it*. It stood there a long time, in the plain white light, with the fog at its chest, understanding the situation.

It was a thing whose saying was a doing. It had never had hands. Everywhere else it had been believed, which is a soft and forgetful medium; here belief was irrelevant and the record was everything. If it spoke one word on this lift, the word would go down into the grain and lie there eleven years and come back up out of the paving on a cold morning, perfectly, in its own voice, forever, to whoever happened to be standing there. Surveyors would sell the hour. Men would come at dawn with an owl on the wrist and a felt saw in a bag.

It could have done anything. Anything would have been kept.

So it shut its mouth and went and asked for work.

It was bad at asking. It had no hands until it took some, and then it had no grammar, and its accent was described, kindly, by the gang-boss, as *loud*. She was seventy, she had raised four houses and eleven gangs, and she gave it a hand-name because there was nobody else to do it: a thumb hooked at the collarbone, and then the flat of the other hand laid on the sternum, and held. The address-clerk drew the little picture in the ledger and wrote underneath it *Palm*, which was not its name, and which it answered to for the rest of the time it was there.

It learned to shift its weight. It learned that this is not optional. It got a wage of eleven hundredths of an acre at a bearing and a cure-date, and a bearing-book, and felt soles three millimeters thick that it replaced every nine days, and a bad hip, because it had taken a body and bodies in the Lay are spent as instruments and it did not see why it should be exempt.

## II. What it found

It looked for a long time before it touched anything. It worked eighteen months in a gang and two winters on the dew-combs and it read the floors of nine quarters through the bones of its wrists, and this is what it found.

The ground had kept the wrong archive.

Grain locks around pressure. Sign makes no pressure worth the name. So the stone of the Lay holds every cart, every dog, every dropped thing, every scream, every shift-break tune badly played, and not one sentence of the language. The arguments, the jokes, the poems, the four incompatible dictionaries and the one very good one, the classifier that means *this channel is decelerating and the laying house is lying about it* — none of it is down there. Eleven feet under the Ninth of Frost there is a flawless record of everything the Lay did not intend, and nothing whatsoever of what it meant.

What it meant, it had to buy. Murmurers at six hours a stretch. Solo trays for the rich, forty births to a slab for the poor. Intention in the Lay is a purchased commodity, and the free part of a person is the part that leaves no trace, and this is not entirely a misfortune. It took the visitor most of a year to stop treating it as one.

Fidelity was a class good. High-fidelity strains to the core, to the rime houses, to floors that will say a mother cleanly for two hundred years; cheap strains to the growing edge, where the warm poor are standing on ground that will spend a century saying something almost like the people who made it. The visitor stood on a cheap slab in a hot quarter at four fingers a day and heard a woman say something to her child, four times, and could not make out one word of it, and understood that this is what poverty is here: your dead are in the floor and they are mumbling.

The medium ran one way. Fresh takes and cannot give; cured gives and cannot take. Everything the ground says to you it says without any possibility of reply, and there was a whole trade of people whose discipline was standing in front of that with their hands at their sides, and another whole trade of people who walked the fog looking for a body that had stopped shifting its weight, and between them the suicide rate was not high and the interception rate was enormous.

And it looked for the resident.

It looked with the palm of a deaf surveyor, which is the best instrument in the country. It looked with the owls, which hear below human threshold. It sat eleven nights in the grain-mother's shed while she did the liturgy of transfer on the Ninth Vat, decanted continuously for a hundred and forty years, and watched her sign *mother* and mean it in the ordinary way. Then it looked with what it had, which was better than all of these, and it went down through the frustule and the bound water and the growth habit and the whole slow architecture of the memory, looking for someone to be there.

It did not find anyone.

It did not conclude that there was no one. It noted the difference and kept the note, and it went on looking, off and on, for as long as it stayed, and it never found anyone, and it never stopped behaving as though the finding might be wrong.

Then it made a list of injuries, because it had come to help and it was not going to be sentimental about what helping means.

Island children, coming back at seven or ten, talking at full volume, seeing everyone flinch. Warehouses of undeliverable slabs mumbling in a north wind, porters who would not work them alone. Slabs that speak to a gutter because the family could not hire an attender and had nobody left to attend itself. Herders and wild listeners going out onto blooms that will hold a truck by evening and do not hold a woman now. Murmurers' throats. Listeners' molars. Ferrymen's ears like fired brick. Sixty-one years of *Cobb*. Two guilds in a war with no outlet, a diatom and a moss, and the human beings as what the war is fought with.

And the midges, which bite in silence in the warm seams all year without season, and everybody's ankles scarred, and which nobody had ever made beautiful.

## III. Works declined

**1. To make the stone answer.**

The obvious gift. The one every wild listener at the seaward edge is already praying for, in a heresy that cannot be argued with because the evidence is eleven years away and out past the last gantry where the ground is not yet a fact.

It declined.

A medium that runs both ways is a medium that can be *used*. Cured stone that answers can be questioned; what can be questioned can be coached; what can be coached can be hired. The deferred assize would go first — perjury is nearly impossible in the Lay only because the paving cannot be led. Then the black market: there is already a trade in slabs cut from ground that cured during something terrible, which sing when you strike them and are worth more than any building they could be made into. Give those slabs a mouth and you have not made a comfort. You have made a market in conversation with people screaming.

And the attenders would go, and the listeners with them. Because if the stone answered, the scream would be *answered*, and nobody would need to *hear* it, and the entire apparatus of noticing — the hand on the arm, the walk out to the ferry, the enormous interception rate achieved by people observing that somebody has stopped shifting their weight — would become optional, and then quaint, and then a memory, and then people would go down into the lift in numbers, quietly, in the fog, with something to say and somewhere to say it.

It had come to give them a reply. It found that they had built, out of the absence of one, the only thing it had ever seen anywhere that reliably worked.

Being answered is a relief. Being heard is the good. They had the good. It was not going to trade it for the relief.

**2. To silence the terrible slabs.**

Mercy of oblivion: cut out the Bell Riot, unmake the voiced patches where the worst mornings are, let the four blocks of the Ninth-of-Frost be dumb stone forever.

Declined. The scream in the street is the only penalty the country has, and it is a good one, and it is deferred, which is worse than immediate. Also the poor's mothers are in the same grain as the poor's disasters. You cannot delete the bad without deleting the nursery. At eleven years old a child kneels on a common tray with her ear to the stone and hears forty strangers and her mother says *that one, that's you*, and she may be right. It was not going to take that away in order to spare itself the sound of the other thing.

**3. To wake the seed.**

This was the one it wanted.

To give the grain an inside. To make the resident a person, so that the crab case and the moss war and the whole question of what is owed to the thing that makes the ground could be settled by asking it. The wild listeners would have had their proof. There would have been, at last, something in the Lay that could be spoken to and could speak.

It declined, and the declining took it a whole night on the warm deck of a jacking tower with the sun coming up over the sea.

The grain is a country-sized thing whose entire existence is being pumped through lagged pipe, trodden by felt boots, precipitated, cured, eaten by moss at a fingernail's depth a decade, and cut up with felt saws by men with owls on their wrists. To give that an inside would not be to create a god. It would be to create a slave the size of a delta, in agony, in the dark, forever, and to make a hundred and forty years of continuous decanting into a hundred and forty years of something that could be done to somebody.

It is the largest thing in the Lay that cannot be answered. It decided to tend it anyway. This is not a paradox. It is the trade.

**4. To finish the ground.**

To cure the plain in an afternoon. Stable acres, safe streets, no jacks, no drift, no schedule where a map should be, children learning a surname instead of a bearing.

Declined, because this is the abolition of the place and its replacement with the upriver towns, where people shout at their children and drop things and sleep on ground that finished long ago and has nothing left to say. Also: nothing further would speak. Also: fifty thousand small simultaneous adjustments are not an inconvenience here, they are the culture, and *hold it, hold it, hold it* would stop being the most common sentence in the country, and it did not have anything to replace it with.

**5. To complete the hush.**

The island children. The loneliest thing in the Lay. It could have done it in a breath — one adjustment to the training, one small mercy in the moss swaddle and the palm flat on the sternum, and every child comes home quiet and stays quiet and nobody flinches at anybody ever again.

This is the hush-nurses' proposal, made effective. It has been tried. It is called the Lay.

It declined, and it went out and sat on the bench and had the soup and did not speak to anyone unless spoken to, which is the rule, and thought about the fact that the whole vast beautiful silent apparatus rests on a month of wooden houses with hinges that squeak on purpose and a technique applied to infants, and that it could make the technique perfect, and that this was the single most tempting thing on the list and the one it was most certain about.

**6. To be recorded.**

It could have stood on fresh lift at the growing edge and said one true thing about the Lay — the thing nobody has ever found a way to say out loud, about the training and the crying houses and what the silence is built on. It would have been kept. It would have come up out of the paving on cold mornings for two hundred years, in a voice nobody could mistake, at an hour that surveyors would sell.

There is a slab in the Ninth of Frost that says a man screaming and it is worth more than the building it could be made into. It understood, standing on the lift with its mouth shut, exactly what it would be worth.

A god in the floor is not a god. It is an asset, out of context, at a fixed hour, in a market. It declined to be deposited.

**7. To be everywhere.**

It could have stood on every slab at every speaking hour. Nothing would ever speak to a gutter again.

Declined, and this is the subtlest of the refusals and the one it argued with itself about longest. The listeners' entire gift is that a *person* noticed you had stopped. Make noticing free and universal and it stops being noticing; it becomes weather. And standing still in the fog is one of the two private things in the Lay — children are corrected for it before they are corrected for lying — and a presence at every slab would end that privacy in the act of honoring it.

So it did not become the necessary being. It made the good thing affordable, which is a different operation and a duller one and works.

## IV. The warehouse in the north wind

Before it filed the schedule it took a winter shift in a dead-letter warehouse on the cold cured edge, where the slabs are stacked on edge in racks because the recipients died or the addresses drifted or the blocks slid, and in a north wind the whole building mumbles.

Porters will not work them alone. It is not superstition. It is that you cannot tell how many voices there are.

It worked them alone for a hundred and nine days.

Cured stone gives and cannot take. This means that in a dead-letter warehouse you may say anything you like. It said everything. It said the things it had come to say, in a voice that had made things elsewhere, at full volume, in a wooden shed on ground that finished before the river was diverted, and the racks mumbled on underneath it and nothing it said went anywhere at all. It learned the whole shape of the Lay's problem from the inside, in the cold, at full throat, being of no use whatsoever.

On the sixty-first day it found a man reading boundaries.

Six hours of it. The bearing and the metes and the fraction and the cure-date of an acre bought for a son, over and over, flat, unaccented, plosive-free, the way a man reads when he has paid for six hours of murmur and intends to get six hours of murmur, because that was what he could afford and the fidelity is in the repetition. The boy had died at nine. The slab had been cut for a twenty-first year that did not happen and had come here, to a rack, in a building that porters will not enter alone.

At the very end, off-register, once, in a voice that had gone to pieces: the boy's sound-name.

Nobody had ever heard it. That was the whole injury. Not that the boy was dead — the Lay knows what to do with the dead, it has a bench and a soup and a ferry and a discipline — but that a name meant for a person had been sitting in a rack for a hundred years being heard by nobody, and the loud places keep nothing and the silent places keep everything, and here was a thing kept perfectly and delivered to no one.

It could not answer. There is no answering.

But it could receive. It could be the addressee. That is a real office and it was vacant.

So it sat in the cold with a ferryman's blank book and did the midwife's act backwards: listened to the racks and wrote the sound-names down.

## V. Works undertaken

**The names.** It bought naming trips. Not one; the run of them, autumn after autumn, cheap in autumn, coin after coin, standing at the seaward lip past the vent towers in a row of forty with the gulls going up, reading aloud out of a ferryman's book that had a new page-heading in it. Forty thousand names, over the years, of people who had died before their slab was cut. It gave them the trip they would have taken at nineteen.

The ferrymen's guild argued for two seasons about whether a name may be shouted for the dead and decided that it may, because it was paid for, and because the ferrymen are the only people who go both places and have always known that the two places are not as different as the Lay pretends.

The ferryman who had first taken its coin grew richer and hoarser and told the story badly for the rest of his life, and the woman with the soup never mentioned what she had decided on the bench, and it went on going out there, and it went on making the cracked-hinge noise when it tried to shout its own name, and it went on being ashamed of it, and it learned to laugh without sound at that bench, correctly, mouth wide, head back, shoulders going, nothing coming out, which is the single most frightening thing in the Lay and which nobody there had ever once been frightened of.

**The grammar.** It herded for a season at the edge: splayed shoes, false ears on a headband, throat painted in the bare-skin colors the elk use at rut, standing in a fog of its own breath conducting an argument in pigment and ear-angle, not laughing, not making a sound. Herders go out onto the flocculating blooms where nobody else will walk and come back with things to report and no reliable way to report them.

So it made a way, and it made it out of the language, which is the one thing in the country that cannot be owned or kept. A mood added to the classifier system for rate and bearing: a movement that says *this bloom, at this hour, under this weight*, and a second that says *and I am guessing*. It used it in front of people. It was useful. It caught. Within four years the warm poor and the island children and the wild listeners were standing out there in far fewer numbers under the ground, and there was a fifth incompatible dictionary, and no one has ever known where the mood came from, and the vat people sign it huge and slow and the layers sign it low and compact below the waist, and it means the same thing in both.

It never told the wild listeners they were wrong about the reply. Being wrong out there is not what kills them. Not knowing the load is what kills them. It fixed the load and left the heresy alone, and the heresy is still spreading quietly in the fog, and it is still, in the visitor's private assessment, incorrect, and it does people good.

**The spill.** It went into the vats and it made fidelity wild.

Not a proclamation. Not a gift to a guild. A feral trait in a cheap strain, decanted out past the last gantry into the grey blooms where the escaped seed is already laying unauthorized acres saying the sea — so that it comes back in with the tide, and the ground at the growing edge, the four-finger ground, the cheapest ground, the ground that does not exist yet, starts speaking clearly, and nobody can breed it back out and nobody can sell it at a premium because it is in the water.

It did not publish. It spilled.

It knew the cost and it wrote the cost down. Clear grain everywhere means the Bell Riot speaks clearly. It means the terrible slabs stop mumbling. It means more attenders needed, more kept holes with a person standing at them, more of the country's worst mornings audible at full fidelity to whoever is on the pavement. It considered this for a long time and did it anyway, on the reasoning that the poor already have their dead and the difference is only that the rich can hear theirs, and that a mercy which is declined because it will also make the truth louder is not a mercy, it is a preference for quiet.

Then it went and took an appointment in the Ninth of Frost, laid the year of the Bell Riot, and paid the cost in person, annually, for as long as it stayed.

**The midges.** It gave them a month.

The coldest one. No bite, no scar, ankles healing all through the dark of the year, and the quiet midges going about whatever quiet midges do instead. Nobody noticed the first year. The children noticed the second. By the fourth there was a classifier for it and four poems and one very good bitten-brass thing that six hundred people clamp their teeth to and weep at in absolute quiet.

There are eleven theories. The mossers claim it. The vat-tenders claim it. The islanders say it was always so and the Lay has a short memory, which is the funniest thing anyone in the region has ever said. It is now the one thing about the Lay that everybody has made beautiful, in eleven incompatible ways, and it never said a word.

**The funds.** It joined an acre-club, in a moss-hung room, and argued about the sea with people who had eleven hundredths of an acre at a bearing and a cure-date and a great deal of practical knowledge about drift.

Then it endowed, in fractions of ground that did not exist, delivered warm in nineteen years if the channel behaves, insured against drift by the pooled fractions of people who make ground with their hands:

The soup at the howl wharf, in perpetuity, so that the woman with the bowl never has to leave, and the rule of the bench unchanged, and nobody spoken to unless they speak first.

Attenders' wages, so that no slab speaks to a gutter for want of a hire. Not itself attending everything. Paying for it to be attended, by people with sore feet and a bad hip and a family, who will notice things it would not notice, and who will still be there when it stops coming.

Listeners' wages, which is the same money and buys more.

A porter's wage for the dead-letter warehouses, so that nobody works them alone who does not want to, and so that somebody is always in there being the addressee.

And it bought the seaward edge — acres at a bearing, past the last gantry, where the feral grain flocculates and lays what nobody ordered — and held them unworked, in trust, through the club, audited annually by people who argue about the sea. Unauthorized acres saying the sea, forever, uncut, unsold, with gulls landing on them and sinking to the breast and standing there offended and walking away by evening.

It did not tell anyone what the trust was for. The laying houses assume it is a speculation gone wrong. They are welcome to.

**Cobb.** It could have ended *Cobb v. the Laying Houses* in an afternoon.

It filed instead. Six hours of murmur into a tray at a commissioning house on the cold edge, flat, unaccented, plosive-free, paid for at the ordinary rate: a deposition concerning the grain, its growth habit, the absence of anyone found inside it, and the fact that the absence was not conclusive; and concerning four hundred thousand members whose dues cannot be collected and who lay an acre a year for free.

The tray is curing. The slab will be cut and set into the floor of the court in eleven years and the assize will hear it, and it will be evidence, and it will be cross-examined by a good lawyer who does not argue, who lays. It submitted to the deferred assize because the deferred assize is the best thing the Lay has built after the listeners, and a power that rules from above in a country that has organized its entire legal profession around the difference between arguing and laying has not understood the country.

It expects to lose part of it. It has laid accordingly.

**And what it did not manage.** In the ninth year a woman went down on fresh lift at the growing edge, four streets from where Palm was working a jack, and Palm had known her, and had eaten her food cooked in a hot seam, and had not been there. It could have been there. Being there in every case is work number seven, declined, and it did not undecline it, and it does not know to this day whether that was wisdom or a rule it liked more than it liked her.

Eleven years later it stood the slab, at the hour, in the cold, with its hands at its sides, and did not answer.

It never decided whether the whole thing had been enough. It kept the appointment.

## VI. The Ninth Vat

It took a shift on the Ninth Vat, under the grain-mother, and then alongside her, and then, when her hip went, instead of her, and it did the liturgy of transfer correctly every time, which is a hundred and forty years of continuous decanting and a set of movements at the sternum and a shift schedule.

The strains have moods. They go slow, go loud, go bitter, sulk in a cold spring, take a fever and lay a quarter that will never speak clearly. It learned to read them through the palm, which is the only way, and it was slower at it than the deaf houses, who have been doing it since before the hearing stopped shouting at the ground and dying, and it was told so, kindly.

It signed *mother* and meant it in the ordinary way.

This is what it did for the being who lives in the Lay: it fed it, and kept it warm, and did not wake it, and did not use it more than the trade requires, and looked for someone in there every few years without ever finding anyone and without ever concluding the search. It owed the grain exactly what Ondry owes a man she never met. Attention with no reply available. It happens that the Lay is the one place it has ever been where this was already understood, and taught to children, and made into a guild with a hospital and a sign for *I have nothing left to say* which is also the sign for *retired*.

It had come to help. What it mostly did was apprentice.

## VII. 6:40, first hard frost

Ordinary daylight coming. The sun a plain cold coin over the sea, the fog chest-high and warm, the dew-combs ticking their kilometer of falling water, ten thousand roofs edge to edge, the owls going home along the ridges with their heads full of other people's mornings.

Ondry is on her slab with her hands at her sides and her weight going heel to toe, heel to toe, because she is a Lay person and cannot help it. Above her the sun is on her face. Her hands are in the fog.

The paving says a cart. It says three notes of a bitten-brass tune, badly played, somebody's shift-break forty years ago. It says a dog. It says, from four directions at once, in four voices, the most common sentence in the country: *hold it, hold it, hold it.*

On the next slab, an arm's length off, there is a woman doing the same thing.

Ondry has stood this thirty-one times and it has spoken nine and she has never once been on a slab with anybody else. She reads the woman the way she reads everything, from the collarbone down, because faces are a kind of hat: felt soles worn at the outer edge, weight going down through the whole sole at once, hands at the sides and staying there. A cheap-strain slab, that one. It mumbles. Whoever is standing there has come forty-odd times for four unclear words, and Ondry knows the trade well enough to know what that means, and does not look up.

At 6:40 the man says it.

She does not answer. There is no answering. Beside her the woman does not answer either, and does not move her hands, and the two of them stand in the fog with the sun on their faces in front of the thing that cannot be replied to, which is the entire discipline, and which is not made easier by company and is not made harder, and is simply, for the first time in thirty-one years, not solitary.

At 6:52 the gang comes down the street in their splayed shoes, moths in the fog around their knees, and the jacks begin to tick, and the street rises a finger, and they step off onto the moss together.

They walk to the corner. At the corner, in the fog, at the height where the children live, the woman makes a movement at her sternum: a thumb hooked at the collarbone, and then the flat of the other hand laid on the breastbone, and held.

Ondry gives hers back: two fingers laid on the inside of the other wrist, and held. The ledger writes *Wrist*, which is not her name.

They are both the same idea. Most names in the Lay are.

Nothing took it. The slabs they stood on finished curing before either of them was born and cannot take anything; the fresh lift is a mile off at the growing edge and by evening it will have the print of a dog in it. There is no record of the introduction and there will never be a record of the introduction. In eleven years, and in a hundred, that corner will say a cart, and a dog, and four voices saying *hold it*, and it will not say one word of what happened at chest height in the fog, because the ground was not built to keep the only thing the Lay actually says, and a power that could have changed that had thought about it for a long time and left it alone.

Ondry went home over her mother's threshold, which said her name.
