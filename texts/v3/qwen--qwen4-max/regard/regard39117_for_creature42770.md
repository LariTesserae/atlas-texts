# The Nought-Inch Station

It came in through the nets.

Forty feet of galvanized mesh on timber frames, ranked along the crest where the wind comes over, and the cloud driving through and leaving itself behind drop by drop, and the drops running down the wires into the gutters, and the gutters into the lead pipes. It was harvested. It went down the hill with the rest of the fog water to the dye-houses and the boiler feed and thirty-one thousand kettles, and it squeaked between people's fingers, and nobody noticed, and that was the correct way to arrive. It did not come as light. Light here is an accusation.

---

It looked for a long time before it did anything, and the first thing it did was the sums, because the sums are the local language and it had come a long way to be polite.

The sums closed. That was the first surprise and the one that governed everything after. Input equalled output. The moss cut equalled the moss grown; the silt dredged equalled the ground carried up; the water leaving the Tail was warmer and cleaner than the water taken from the cirque, and the towns down the valley drank it and were grateful. Thirty-one thousand people with better hands than any in the lowlands, an export the lowlands could not counterfeit, one hour a week of sanctioned indecent noise, and not one body in the ground that was not somebody's grandmother. There was no wound in the balance. It had come, if it was honest, half expecting to find one — some hidden sump where the arithmetic was being paid for by people who could not speak — and it found instead a thing that was beautiful and slightly terrible and did not require rescuing, and it was careful not to confuse those three facts with one another.

Visitors confuse them constantly. Visitors stand at the overlook with their collars up and see dim and think *dark*. It is the same error as the children's fear of shadows, run backwards.

---

Here is what it did not do, and why, since the refusals were most of the work.

**It did not lift the lid.** One week of hard white sun is a festival; a permanent one is a demolition. In a fortnight the moss would scorch in the joints and the flange packing would fail and the hill would run hot and loud, as it did for eleven weeks in the blight year, which is still spoken of the way other places speak of a plague. The old grey ice in the deep walks would go, and with it six hundred years of the only sediment the city has — every flue-signature, every closed works, the bee's wing. The metre bars would drift a few millionths and every contract the Combs had signed in the lowlands for a hundred and eighty years would become a lie, quietly, retroactively, in a place whose entire export is that it does not drift. The cold is the product. Warmth is thirty-one thousand people out of work. And the children would be frightened every day of the year instead of one week, because shadows would lie off every parapet all day long, pointing somewhere, and there would be no week left in which the world had edges, because edges would be ordinary.

It did not give them ground. Free dirt would make the made ground worthless, and the made ground is where the dead are. The reason a family can bring a case when an alder dies — the reason the soil guild has been broken twice in six hundred years — is that the ground has a specification and a price. Give them soil and you have made their grandmothers homeless and unavengable in a single gesture of generosity.

It did not give them volume. Their whole moral vocabulary is radius: a grudge at nine feet, an escalation at twelve, confession at nought, marriage at no distance at all. To make speech free would be to hand a people who measure love in inches a machine for making all distances equal. It would not have liberated them. It would have deleted the language in which they are tender to each other, and left them standing in a bright loud town with nothing to say and no way to say it close.

It did not give them anything that would stay. This was the hardest and the most obvious. In a riffle, one grain that does not move is not a gift to the riffle; it is the beginning of a bar, and then a shoal, and then a dam. A monument here is the Man Who Kept It, carved. And a god who does not move is the same thing wearing a face: a hoard of meaning, sitting at the top of the hill, with all six miles still in front of it, never spending, never wearing, never going into the melt. They have no god but the balance. It understood that this was not a deficiency in their religion. It was the only theology the physics would permit, and it was correct.

So it did not appear to them. Appearing would have been the largest possible accumulation.

---

What it found instead it found in the ledgers, where everything is the same kind of fact.

There is a column for the head of water. There is a column for the eels, counted at the head-dam in the dark by a man with a lamp. There is a column for the moss crop, and for the fines by distance, and for the deaths. And there is a line, four times a year and every week besides, for the water the head-wardens allow to the fulling stocks for the crying hour, because the crying hour is on the schedule and the water is budgeted like everything else.

It read six hundred years of that allowance and set it beside the deaths and did the subtraction.

Eighteen deaths a week, near enough, over the long run. Four people per death who need to say a name at no distance at all — not always four, sometimes one, sometimes eleven, but four is the figure the ledgers imply if you read the parish faults alongside the plates. Seventy-two a week arriving. Forty standing under the stocks, one hour, once a week, and the forty are not all new: the old griefs come back, and the rages come, and the ones who only want to laugh, which is allowed and is the point.

The queue lengthens by thirty-odd a week. It has been lengthening for six hundred years.

That is the only number in the Combs that goes up.

And it does not vanish, because nothing vanishes here; it goes where unaccounted things go. Into a gate closed a half-second too hard. Into a pawl let fall. Into a bearing deliberately not greased by a man who has never in his life raised his voice above a wall — which is to say, into *lost head*, the one sin, paid out of the common stock by people who were never given a channel. The poor have the better hands and the worse teeth. The exported listener walks a lowland line of shafting with a stick against her teeth and earns a year's wage in a month and has nobody within nine feet who speaks her language. The lichen woman is bribed and hated and unmarried and kept honest by having her lens among the metre bars, and there is no station in the city whose business is to be at nought inches with her.

The walls are taught to cry into a channel. Nothing is allowed to weep at random. The people weep at random, in stairwells, and once a week in a thudding room, and the town has never plumbed it, and the reason is not cruelty. The reason is that the town can only see what costs it something. Nought-inch speech is below the notch. It leaks no head. It is the one free thing in six miles, and therefore, to a city that has metered every drop for six centuries, it is invisible.

---

So it did this.

On a Tuesday, on the Thirty-Fourth, in a stairwell where the treads are hollowed in the middle like shallow spoons, it sat down on the wet step beside a woman called Second Settling whose husband's plate had gone into the melt that spring, and who had no one left within nine feet who was hers. It put its ear where the breath would be. She said his name at no distance at all, for a long time, with the parts of it that are not in the parish register.

Nothing was audible. No warden raised a paddle. No head was spent. It cost the city nothing, which is precisely why nobody had ever built it.

Then it did it again on the Thursday, for a Tailer with no family, and then for a boy from the Ninth who had not lost anybody but had something he could not get into his hands.

It did not propose a law. It did not ask for water. It did not move the crying hour, and nobody has ever proposed moving the crying hour, and it would not have been the one to start.

The town noticed, because the town has a professional class of noticers. Within the year the head-wardens had it on a rota of nine, on the wide walk on the Twelfth, near the parts market, where the whispering is thickest and a person can be lost in a crowd at four inches. Within seven it was cast in brass. The letters are **NOUGHT-INCH**. The plate has been recast twice and is worn to the thickness of a fingernail and the letters are the same letters. There have been nineteen people called Nought-Inch and the parish register does not distinguish them except by dates and by faults.

In the ledger, under the station, in the column where every other station has its figure, the head allowance is entered as *nil*. It is the only such entry in six hundred years.

---

It also brought a bee, and it may have been wrong about the bee.

One insect. Female. Carried up the hill in an empty ballast car in a box with a specification, licensed after a season of litigation that the soil guild enjoyed enormously. Released in the sun-week into the seed-orchards, where the children go up with rabbit-tail brushes and slit-visors and do the work of an insect tree by tree and cry from the glare. It worked the pear blossom alongside them for four days. Then the lid closed and it died in the grey, as everything with wings dies here, and went down to the digesters and was composted to specification and came back up in a sack.

One bee. No population, no hive, no accumulation; the balance did not move a hair. But the children saw it, and afterwards they were taken to see the wing under glass in the ice-man's room, and this time they believed him. That was the whole of the intention. It had read six hundred years of a man shaving grey ice for soot and being not quite believed by children about the one thing he had found, and it thought: *he should be believed once, while he is alive.*

It was fined, that year, two hundred marks, by distance. Not for the bee. It had said thank you to a woman at four feet who had not invited four feet, which is an escalation and actionable, and a child of about nine held the paddle flat and low — *you are audible and I am being kind about it* — and it understood that it had been corrected by an expert, and paid, and learned the radius the way you learn a language, badly and then all at once. The family refers to the incident with pride and with the exact figure.

---

It went down to the crying hour in its second year and stood in the thudding with its collar up and forty people, and it did not scream, because it had nothing to scream about, and it laughed. Out loud. At nothing at all. The single most indecent noise the Combs produce, on the schedule, the water allowed for.

Afterwards a deaf man at the frame turned around, which they do not do, and looked at it, and signed something short. It answered in stock-signs, badly, with a grammar borrowed from his grandfather's neighbours, and he was patient with it, and that was the first conversation it had in the Combs in which it was the stupid one.

---

Then it paid, because the arithmetic is the arithmetic and it had come here to be polite.

It could have stayed. That was the temptation and it was exactly the local devil: to be the one grain in the riffle that does not move, the fixed point, the thing every ledger refers to and nothing wears away. To be *kept*. It would have been very easy and it would have taken about four generations to dam the hill.

So it kept the station one turn and handed it on to a woman from the Twentieth, and wore, and went down to the Tail with the wool waste and the spent cress-root and the filter cake, and was composted to specification, and went up the hill in an empty ballast car, and came out of the sack into an alder box on a wide walk on the Twenty-Third. The batch number is in the register. It is not a remarkable number.

Families visit that box now, because it is a box on a wide walk and that is what you do, and they pour a thimble of the pear spirit into the made ground, which is technically an offence against the specification and universally winked at. It tastes of cold stone. Nobody in the Combs finds any of this a metaphor. They are right not to. It is simply how names are made, and it was the only doctrine it had to leave them, and it did not leave it; they had it already.

---

In its last year it asked to go down the shaft in the Fourth Tooth, and the Palm — a woman of sixty who had been three other stations before this one — said yes, on the condition that it carry the lamp, which it did.

Down past the made ground and the old fill and the older fill. She put her palm on the actual mountain and said the four words, and it put its hand beside hers, and the mountain was cold and dark and smelled of nothing at all, and it said nothing else, then or ever, from below.

It could have. That was the last refusal and the largest. The Combs' idea of the end of the world is not fire; it is that one year the Palm comes up the shaft and says something else. It declined to end their world. It stood at the bottom of a shaft holding a lamp for a woman of sixty and understood that the mountain had kept the same station for six hundred years without once being thanked, and that the correct response to that is not to make the mountain speak.

It is to carry the lamp, and go up, and enter the four words in the ledger in the same column as the eels.

---

From the ledgers of that decade, all in the same hand, all the same kind of fact:

*Sun-week. One bee, female, imported; box and specification filed. Died fourth day. To digesters. Batch 6,114.*
*Nought-Inch. Station established, Twelfth wide walk, rota of nine. Head allowance: nil.*
*Fines by distance: one. Four feet. Two hundred marks. Paid.*
*Crying hour: water as before. Unchanged.*
*Deaths: as before. Nought-Inch, one. To digesters. Batch 6,119.*
*Palm: cold, dark, smells of nothing.*

Stand at the overlook in the late afternoon, which looks exactly like the morning. Two thousand small green lamps coming on, terrace by terrace. Scaffold going up, scaffold coming down, a column of men with poles, a lift sighing, six miles of engineered falling water whispering under a lid with no seams in it. Everything moving at once and nothing accumulating, and the queue that used to lengthen by thirty a week now going somewhere, quietly, at no distance, for free.

It will be here tomorrow. Not one stone of it will be the same stone, and one of the stones will have been a power that could have stayed forever and chose to be a grandmother in a box instead, and if you asked anyone on the Twenty-Third about it they would tell you the batch number, and offer you a thimble, and say nothing else, because you are standing at four feet.
