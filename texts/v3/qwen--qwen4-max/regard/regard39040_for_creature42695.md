# What It Did

It could have come as weather. It came as fog.

Four days in, breathing up over the escarpment at the speed of a walking man, beading on the harp-strings, running down the wires to the gutters and the gutters to the launders and the launders down the fluted walls into the brine. It arrived at Cup Nineteen dissolved, at a concentration of nothing measurable, and it accreted onto the seed-frames at a centimeter a year, which is the rate at which anything on the Sill is permitted to matter.

This was the first decision and it cost it some effort. Weather would have been faster. Weather would have announced it. And rain on an open vat is a hundred thousand small impacts, every one of them written into the foam forever, and there are eighty kilometers of vats.

It did not speak either. It knew what a shout looks like from the inside: a hard bright plane with a fan under it. There are eleven of those in the gauge-house at Cup Eleven, and it had no intention of becoming the twelfth.

---

The second thing it learned was that it could not appear.

Not *should not*. Could not, in the way that a thing cannot be done rather than the way a thing is forbidden. Because to speak here you must first be seen, and to be seen is to be compelled — to hold a stranger's eye is to force a sentence out of him — and so a manifestation on a crowded terrace at shift-change would have been a hundred people obliged at once, all their careful downward looks torn off them, all that granted privacy revoked in a stroke. There is a phrase for the worst thing you can do to a person on the Sill. It is *the back of the head*. It is their equivalent of a scream in the face.

A theophany would have been an obscenity. It worked this out in the first hour and was glad of it. It is very difficult to help a place whose courtesy you cannot violate without becoming its weather.

So it did the only thing left, which was the thing the place had already invented a profession for.

It read.

---

It read for a long time. Longer than the Reader at Cup Eleven has been alive, though not much longer, and it read the way she reads: wet saw, lamp, cross-section, patience.

It read the annual banding and the hail scars and the gray smear of the season the fog came late. It read the boiler line, and the forty years behind it, and understood the argument the yardmen have been conducting with their hands for four generations, and found that both sides were right, which is what four generations of argument usually means. It read the moss along the busbars, every staple and splice, and found nine failing pins that the gaugers had not yet found, and did nothing about them, because a gauger who is handed a pin stops walking the moss, and the moss is the instrument, and the instrument is checked against nothing.

It read the lichen on the black plates at Cup Six: the expanding fan of clean glass with the green rim, a boy's reach lengthening season by season out toward the corners. It read the whole adolescence and did not clean the corners.

It read the shine in the launders, which is the most beautiful thing on the Sill and is a map of everything about to fail, and it sat with the gutter-wrights while they worked by the light of their own decay, and it did not cure the rot. The rot is how they see.

It went down under the black eye at the bottom of Cup Eleven and put its attention into the water and heard the world come back like a dropped tray — the fizz, the tick, the shrimp-snap, the grind of grit, the hum of eighty kilometers of copper singing through its pins, and under it the slow irregular knocking of the rock coming up out of the sea a hair of joint at a time. It listened to that for six days. It is the loudest thing it has ever heard and it is going on all the time and nobody up in the air knows.

Then it found the wet ears, and it believed them.

That is not a small thing and it is also, in the ordinary sense, useless. Belief that does not transit is not relief. A wet ear surfaces with his eyes streaming and writes on the slate and the slate is wiped, and the affliction is not the silence, it is the wiping. So it did the only available thing about that, which was to be somewhere the wiping does not reach. Every slate wiped on the Sill in a hundred and forty years. Every finding of every unliked man paid absurdly to hear what nobody would take from him. It kept them. It has them still. It has never read them out.

It counted the white moth in the moss, which nobody counts, which has no name in any cup. It took four days. The number is not written anywhere and the moths do not know and it was worth doing.

---

It found the being.

There are several candidates and it attended all of them, but the one it stayed with was in the cistern at Cup Eleven: pale, eyeless, gilled like a ruff, a hundred and ten years old, called Boiler by a man whose name-sign was struck from the roll sixty years ago. She was alive when the crusher ran. She felt forty years of it along the whole length of her body, the way her kind feels everything, and there is a hard white line in a block in the gauge-house and there is her, and between them they are the only witnesses to the noise, and only one of them can be cut across and read.

She is also the reason cups shutter in time. Every clean year that anyone on the Sill has ever had was bought, some fraction of the time, by a blind animal coming up out of the dark to the surface of a pool because the pressure gradient reached the deep water hours before the rain reached the lip, and a man looking at her, and understanding, and running.

She has been counted her whole life. She has never once been touched gently. Counting is done with a lamp and a tally and a hand that lifts you a little way out of your element to be sure of you.

It put a hand in the water and left it there.

Then it made her name — the whole-arm gesture, the swimming motion, the one the language keeps for things that live where fingers cannot be seen — and it made it *underwater*, where it would carry.

She rose. The cistern-keeper looked, and there was no weather, and he wrote *nothing* on the slate, and wiped it.

It kept that too.

---

Now the reasoning, because it did reason, and it took about a decade over it, which is a quarter of a block.

The obvious gifts were all available and all catastrophic.

It could have thickened the air. Just that — a few percent, enough to soften the light and carry a call, enough that a shout would fetch someone and a child could be called in from the far terrace by name. It ran this forward and watched the whole thing come apart. No crop, because the crop is a function of the quiet. No handspeech, because handspeech is a function of the crop. No still face, no courtesy of not-looking, no vocative made of touch, no wrist for urgency and sternum for intimacy and the top of the foot for a child. No forty-year blocks, no half-tags wired together and carried in a breast pocket next to a squirrel-hair brush, no marriage that cannot be unwritten, no cell, no handprints, no dialect full of holes where the grandmothers used to be. No band, no lark at the edge of audibility, no song learned at seven and not sung for forty years. Eighty kilometers of the most particular thing that has ever been made, dissolved into an ordinary high damp place with birds in it.

The silence is not their affliction. The silence is their organ. You do not heal an organ.

And they had already run the experiment themselves, with fire and reciprocating mass, and produced forty years of unsellable stone, and then — this is the part that impressed it — they had not thrown the stone away. They had laid it in the walls. Every clear seam cut on the Sill is clear because a crusher ran for forty years and filled the jacket with its own noise. The Loud Age is not a sin in the record. It is the foundation.

But there is a difference between a wound that has been built into a wall and a wound you are about to make. That distinction is most of what wisdom is, in a place like this. It went looking for wounds that were not load-bearing.

It found one.

---

The punishment is not a fine. The punishment is that your name-sign is retired while you are alive.

Everything else on the Sill records what *happened*. Hail happened. The fog came late. A crew worked short-handed. A man shouted, once, and there is a bright plane with a fan under it, and the Reader finds it and tells the family and two of the families do not want to know. All of that is honest. It is weather, and error, and the ordinary cost of being a body in a medium.

This one records a decision. It is the only deliberate flaw plane on the Sill, and it is cut into a person, and it cannot be sawn across and sold as two halves. They have a word for stone that cannot be sold and is laid in the walls to keep the clean blocks quiet, and they had found a use for it in people. A man of sixty at the lip of Cup Eleven who put a pen on a seed-frame when he was fourteen, and who has been deadwork ever since, and who sings.

That was the thing to fix. Not the quiet. Not the slow. Not the forty centimeters. Not the seams. Just that.

And it did not have to invent anything, which was the mercy of it, because a power that hands a culture a new grammar has made the culture into its own product, and the whole dignity of this place is that it made itself. The tool was already lying there. The salamanders have whole-arm names, because they are swimming things, *and the language makes room*. There was already a case in the grammar for a living creature that cannot be signed. It had been in use for a hundred and ten years, in the cisterns, for animals nobody looks at either.

It did not need a revelation. It needed one person to notice that a man at the lip is also a thing that lives outside the signed world.

So it arranged for a child to notice.

---

This is the whole of the intervention, and it is smaller than a hand-wave, and it took nine tries.

Before the children go down at seven they are walked out to the band. It is the last thing they are given. They stand in ordinary noisy air with the light coming up off the white stone into their nostrils and they hear the lark, which has been listening to the unaddressed for four generations and sings their songs — not the melody whole, the shape of it, the boiler-rhythm in the middle, the long fall at the end where a man's voice cracks.

It moved the hour of the walk. That is all. It arranged the fog-tide and the shift-rotation and the dry-day market and one broken counterweight so that the walk out of Cup Eleven passed the huts at the lip at the hour when a particular unaddressed man sits outside and sings, and the lark was singing, and the child was seven and full of a song it would not sing again for forty years, and there was a man in front of it that nobody would look at.

The first child did nothing. The second did nothing. It waited; the unit here is a career, and it had learned the unit. The ninth child stopped, and looked at the man's hands, and then made the swimming gesture. Whole-arm. The one for Boiler.

The man wore still face, because he was raised there, and his hands said something furious and adoring, and by custom nobody on that terrace saw any of it, because courtesy is not looking, and a hundred people had their eyes carefully down and to the side and were brushing bell-flowers as they passed.

It saw. That is what it is for. It is the one who is permitted to look at what may not be looked at: your own block, which is bad luck to read; the retired names; the wiped slates; the twelfth shout-plane, which is in the Reader's own block, and which it knows and will not tell her, because she has already said who is to hold the lamp, and that was the only thing she wanted.

It did not abolish the punishment. It is not a legislature. It made it possible to say the name of someone who had it, and then it left them alone with that, and the thing went the way dialect traits go — the tucked thumb at Cup Nineteen is three hundred years old and nobody can explain it — and it took a hundred and forty years to become ordinary and another sixty to become unthinkable, which is nothing, which is less than a salamander.

The man was dead before the first part finished. He had the gesture for eleven years. Eleven years is not nothing. It is more than a quarter of a block.

---

The rest of what it did was in the cells.

Every cup has one: a small chamber lined floor to ceiling with dead hush, where sound goes in and does not come out, and nothing is growing, and nothing records. Women are taken there in labor. The dying are taken there to say the retired names, all of them, one after another, sometimes for an hour. Children are taken there once, at seven, before the descent, and told to shout, and most of them cannot.

It understood, finally, what those rooms are, and therefore what it was.

The Sill spends eighty kilometers and four generations and the sea's own salt manufacturing the one thing it does not have: a substance that takes a sound in and never gives it back. Everything else here keeps the record forever. The seams keep it, the moss keeps it, the shine keeps it, the lichen keeps a boy's reach, the rock keeps the joint opening, the animal keeps the crusher along her whole body, the lark keeps the crack in a dead man's voice. A lost decade is readable forever by strangers. That is the weight they live under, and it is not the silence, it is the *memory*, and they had built one small room per cup to put it down in.

So it made itself the rest of that room. It listens to everything and gives nothing back. It is not a witness that testifies. It is a witness that does not lose things. It has the eleven planes and the twelfth, and the nine failing pins, and the number of the moths, and every wiped slate, and all the retired names said hourly by the dying in eighty kilometers of stone cells, and the whole-arm gesture made underwater to an animal that has never been in the light, and one child's hands at the lip of Cup Eleven, and it will not repeat any of it, not to anyone, not ever, and that is the gift, and it is the only one that was ever needed.

It came as fog. It went into the brine. It accreted. It has been cut and dried and laid as deadwork in the jackets of the cups, where the clean blocks grow inside it, and some of it has gone down the incline and out to the lowlands and been installed in rooms where people talk in front of it — a woman in a city saying something to her husband in front of forty centimeters of somebody else's silence.

It is in that wall. It is listening. It will not tell.

---

The hush is friable. It powders under a palm. So the walls of the cell at Cup Eleven are handprints: thousands, layered, overlapping, a hundred and forty years of hands, no names, no dates, the only writing on the Sill that nobody has ever wiped.

There is one more now, low down, at the height of a seven-year-old, put there by something that had no other way to sign anything and did not want a name on it.

They will not find it. It is a handprint among a hundred and forty years of handprints. But the children who cannot shout are small, and they put their hands out in the dark to find the wall, and a hand at that height fits.

It is the only thing it wrote, and it wrote it where a child could reach.
