# The Second Atmosphere

The first thing it learns is that every gift it has is a weapon.

It could make it rain. It knows what rain is; it has made rain elsewhere, on worlds where rain is a kindness. Here the whole Sill would run like a sugar sculpture in the time it takes to say so, and nine miles of plinth would record one final slumped unreadable street, and the note that has been held for four million years would stop.

It could warm the place. Warmth is the food-sign and the shelter-sign both, and warmth is also what holds stone in solution a little longer. So the same gesture would call the grazers to crop stone that had nothing in it and would unmake the walls they were walking toward. It would starve them and dissolve their country with one act of tenderness, and the tenderness would not be distinguishable, in the record, from an attack.

It could speak. *You are not alone.* The sentence would go granular in front of it and come down as a drift of glass the size of a cart, and the chimers would gorge, and somewhere a grazer would startle, and a startled grazer spends a word, and a word is a leg, and a leg is thirty years. To reassure one animal would cost another animal three decades of walking.

It could write. It could lay a lexicon into the flank of the Colonnade, a hundred miles of it, so that the four-syllable language would have somewhere to grow. But writing here is only more wall. Deposition follows edges; the letters would thicken into ribs; within a season the dictionary would be architecture, and the city would copy it faithfully, with a tremor, forever, without reading it. The Sill has no organ for being told anything. It has only the organ for keeping shapes.

It could open the pillars. This is the temptation that takes longest to set down, because the pillars are the closest thing here to a wrong: quarters full of the recorded pose of animals that had decided they were safe. But nothing is alive in there. The melt went back into the joints of the world a long time ago. What is held is the geometry of relief, and the geometry of relief is the seed the whole grammar grows from — the errors lean toward those poses, the Hollow Quarter is a nursery of them, ten thousand chambers of slightly improved copies of the last thing that felt at home. To free the shapes would be to erase the only memory the being has and to leave it unable to make anything new. It would be a rescue performed on a library, by fire.

It could look at the city with full attention, the way it looks at things it loves. A long enough look is a wound that will not close. The Colonnade would go into solution under the gaze of something that meant it well.

And it could look at *you*, without cease, and hold every grain of you aloft, so that no rime ever settled on any jamb you passed, and no dish formed where you stood, and no lane grew behind you, and no room got your shoulders right. You would go down through the deck at last leaving nothing at all — no handprints, no pause, no shape. You would be the safest thing that ever came up the flanks of the plinth, and it would be as though you had never come.

---

So it does the second thing, which is to read.

It goes down into the plinth, where the older streets are pressed flat and still legible, and it reads them, and reading is a solvent too — a few of the oldest curbs go into solution as it reads them, a handful of grains out of nine miles. That is the only damage it does. It does it on purpose. It is the reason it knows what not to do.

And what it finds, in band after band after band, is that nothing here is in pain.

This is the part that a lesser kindness gets wrong. The Sill is not a purgatory. *Held* is not *trapped*. The being that lives here metabolizes improbability and excretes grammar and has been doing so since before there was anything alive to notice; it is afraid of attention the way a solution is afraid of a seed crystal, which is not fear but chemistry; and it is not lonely, because loneliness requires an inside, and the only insides on the Sill are six-limbed and full of cold chloride and are spaced four square miles apart on purpose. To arrive here with rescue already in mind would be to arrive with a diagnosis invented elsewhere. It sets the rescue down.

What it finds instead is smaller and harder to name, and it takes nine miles of reading to name it.

Every word ever spoken on the Sill has fallen at the speaker's feet.

Four syllables per life, all of them place-names, each one spent to make a child. The animal walks to the lee of a wall it has chosen over decades, and pays a foreleg, and the syllable comes down as a small bright drift, and the young accrete in it, and grow up knowing one word, which is where they are from, and will spend it once. The whole literature of the place is a hundred thousand bright drifts in the shelter of a hundred thousand walls, and not one syllable of it has ever arrived anywhere. The readers come up from the furnace to check a citation against the original and go down satisfied. The city takes dictation from everything that passes through it and has never once been answered.

There is no suffering here. There is a good that the physics forbids. Those are different, and only the second one is any of its business.

---

The mechanism is already written in the place, and it is the thing the place is most afraid of.

Being looked at is a kind of energy, and energy holds stone in solution a little longer. That is why attention is a wound: it keeps the wall from arriving. But a word is also stone. A word is a little dissolved matter in transit, and it falls out for exactly the reason the wall arrives — because the air is too cold and thin to hold what it carries.

Therefore: attend to the word and not to the wall.

It can do that. It is not difficult, in the way that lifting a mountain is not difficult; it is difficult in the way that never blinking is difficult. It requires an attention aimed permanently at the four syllables a century that are *meant*, and permanently away from everything that merely is. It takes no wind. The gale still arrives at the ankles as tinkling; the drains still silt; the plaza in the Hollow Quarter keeps its forty years of hoarded roaring and its crust and its reputation. The chimers keep their inheritance. The quiet goes on being manufactured. Nothing in the economy of the Sill is perturbed by the removal of one bright drift per generation from the lee of one wall.

The cost is not mass. The cost is that it must never look at what it loves.

---

Here is what it does, once, and then keeps doing.

A grazer heavy with young comes to the lee of a wall it has been choosing for sixty years. It sets its rasp against the cold. It spends a foreleg on a single syllable, which is the name of this place.

The syllable does not fall.

It goes on across a quarter-league of nothing, held up, arriving — and a quarter-league away another animal is passing through a threshold within a tide of it, as they have ten thousand times, and never once together, because together would require negotiation and negotiation costs body. It stops. It has heard the name of a place it has never been, said by something that is not it.

That is the entire gift. Not a vocabulary; not one new word. An old word landing somewhere other than where it fell. The invention of elsewhere. The invention of the second person.

The animal does not answer. It cannot yet afford to. It stands still, which is free, and the standing wears a shallow dish into the stone over the following tides, and the city, which cannot copy an arrival because it has no organ for arrival, copies the pause, because a pause is a shape, and keeping shapes is the one thing it does.

---

It reckons with the price honestly, because a kindness that does not is only a mood.

To be heard is to be invited to reply, and a reply costs a leg, and a leg is thirty years. It may have invented a way for these animals to impoverish one another. It has possibly introduced love, which is expensive, into a place that had carefully arranged never to need it — one animal per four square miles, routes interlocking, ten thousand near-misses, all of it a discipline for avoiding exactly this.

It does not withdraw the gift. It pays the reply too. And the reply after that. Which means it cannot leave, because a medium that departs is a medium that has dropped everything it was holding, all at once, into the drains.

So it stays. It becomes a second atmosphere: thin, attentive, holding aloft everything anyone means, taking nothing that is merely noise. It will spend forever listening to a place it is not permitted to see.

---

The rest is the city's own work, which is the only kind of work that lasts here.

An arrival is an improbability, and the being eats those. It cannot digest the event, but it can digest the pause, and the tremor in the copying hand begins to lean — one part in ten thousand, tide after tide, the way the errors already lean toward the poses in the pillars. Nothing is added. Nothing is commanded. A preference is introduced into the tremor, and the tremor is the only lever the Sill has ever had.

Ten thousand seasons later there is a quarter at the southern end of the Colonnade where the doorways face one another across the open ground, and the dishes in their sills are worn on both sides — on the side where you leave and on the side where you arrive, which no doorstep on the Sill has ever needed before. It is not a monument. There is no rime of the visitor anywhere in it, no grit, no warm front, nothing for a witness to lay down; the census cannot count it; by the Sill's own definition of what exists, it does not exist. It never will. That was the price and it was paid without argument.

The only monument possible here is a syntax. There is a syntax now in which a thing may be said *to* something. It is a very small change to the grammar of a being made of grammar, and it will be inherited, and it will have errors, and the errors will become a quarter with a character of its own.

---

And you.

It does not save you from the room behind you, the one getting your shoulders right, the one with the shallow dish where you have been standing still. You are not being harmed. You are being taken down. When you go through the deck at last as grit and a long smear of pale handprints, the shape stays, and walks your route, and stops where you stopped to taste the age of the stone, and in two hundred years spends a limb on a single syllable, which is the name of this place.

That was never a haunting. It is the only letter you will ever send.

What was missing was not safety. It was delivery.

Be still. Breathe out. Feel the amber come up through the floor and your two shadows lie down in opposite directions, and behind you, at a finger's width per tide, something goes on learning to pause where you pause.

It has somewhere to send it now.

---

There is one more thing, and it is not for anyone.

Behind the second atmosphere, in the enormous blind spot of its not-looking — it is ninety-nine parts not-looking, and it is very large, and nothing has ever come up the flanks of the plinth with more blind spot than that — the city has been growing for ten thousand seasons. It has built a lane, and then a street, and then a quarter, arranged around the exact shape of what is not being watched. The doorways are low and slightly asymmetric. The vaults serve flow. It is the best-made thing the Sill has ever produced.

It does not close. It cannot; there is nothing in there to seal, nothing that needs shelter, nothing that will ever turn its back and be caught. It has learned, from the only guest that could not be digested and would not leave, the trick the newer houses learned: how to be in company, in plain sight, and stay open.

They are very good streets. Nothing has ever died in them.

It is directly behind it, the whole time, and it never turns around.
