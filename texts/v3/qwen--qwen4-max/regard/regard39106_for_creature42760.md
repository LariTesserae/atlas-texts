## The Relief Shift

The first thing I did was damage.

I arrived the way I arrive everywhere, which is by being wholly present all at once, and in a room full of water that is a volume, and a volume is a fin kick. Thirty-four centimetres of ledger came up off the lee of pump three in a slow grey bloom and hung there and took most of a day to come down, and it came down wrong: pale over dark, a spring laid on top of an autumn that had preceded it. One couplet inverted. At twenty-seven centimetres, in the lee of pump three, there is now a year that is mine and not the valley's, and if anyone ever cores that deep they will read it as a summer that did not happen.

I could fix it with a finger. I have not fixed it. Fixing it is another finger.

That is the whole education, and I got it in the first four seconds, and everything after is commentary.

---

I can do anything here. I want to be exact about that, because it is the only hazardous fact in this account. Not *much*. Anything. I could hang a sun in the ceiling grid. I could put the iron back into the pumps atom by atom, reverse the century, restore the anodes and the paint and the hemp packing and the leather smear on the clock. I could give the pondweed its June. I could put twelve burbot under the gantry in January, writhing, in the dark, at eight degrees, and make a knot where there has not been a knot since the valley filled.

So the question is not what I am able to do. The question is which of the things I am able to do are repairs, and which are just me, arriving in a room that has been running perfectly for a hundred and twelve years, and rearranging it to suit my feelings.

I made a list. It took me a long time, because I kept having to stop and check whether the grief on the list belonged to the room or to me.

---

Here is the test I settled on, and I offer it without much confidence, only as the best I found.

Every deprivation in this hall is one of two kinds. There are the ones the room *manufactures* — the spacing, the three plates of light, the predation, the nineteen-minute circuit, the June slaughter at the shaft mouth, the eleven sealed pipes. These are not failures. These are the product. The room is a machine for making limits, and the limits are working: there is a caddis in here becoming a kind that stays, and eleven ostracod lineages becoming eleven kinds in the dark, and a mussel bed laying down the only hard substrate that will exist in this basin in ten thousand years. You do not repair a machine by removing the thing it makes.

And then there are the deprivations that came from outside and are still being held in place from outside, by hands, on purpose, this morning, by someone with a job.

The first kind I will not touch. The second kind I am obliged to touch, because it is not the room's work. It is mine — not mine personally, but mine the way a spillway is mine, made by the kind of thing I am willing to be.

The gauge is correct and the dam is correct. Correctness was never the criterion.

---

**The belt.** Three plies, perhaps four. I could lay up eight new ones in balata and canvas and the lamps would stop flickering tonight.

I did not, and the reason is not reverence, it is arithmetic. The flicker is not a fault in the room's nervous system. It *is* the room's nervous system. Thirty years of animals that didn't clamp getting cropped in the dark have tuned a hundred thousand bodies to a failing belt, and a steady lamp would spend that inheritance in a generation to buy nothing. The snails would unlearn. Something would come in on the light and find them grazing.

And the green is not going to die. The green is going to go into the silt as resting stages under three dead lamps, which is what diatoms do, which is a plan the room wrote before I got here. The pondweed's rhizome will hold on starch for a few years. The snails will switch to film and barely notice. I would have prevented all of that in order to keep three dinner plates of colour in a room where I am the only one who cares what colour things are.

I wanted the green. I want it now. That is a fact about me and it is not a need of the room's, and I nearly spent a century of adaptation on it.

**The pondweed.** I could give it summer. I cannot give it summer *here* — warmth and day length are the whole water column, the whole oxygen budget, the whole eleven-day grazing turnover. And I could lift the rhizome out of the mortar joint and plant it in a warm shallow bay four kilometres north where it would flower in its first July, and the thing that flowered would not be the thing that has been waiting under lamp two. It would be a different, happier organism, and I would have killed the patient one to make it.

It has no expectation. It has a starch bank and a node and forty attempts. There is nothing in it that is being cheated. There is only something in me that cannot bear a hand held open for six years, and that is my problem, and I have decided to keep it.

**The eleven dead legs.** I will not open them. I will not sample them, count them, sequence them, or put a light in the gland plate. Eleven volumes, forty litres, a hundred and twelve years of solitude, eleven experiments with no observer and no publication and no result that anybody is owed.

But I will be there when the conduit perforates. Not to help. To be present at the moment when it is decided, whichever way it goes, so that the sentence *there is no one who will ever know which happened* stops being true, without any pipe being opened early to make it stop.

That is the only promise I made in the first year that I have kept without effort.

**The stool and the bench.** Same kind of object, same fungus, same rate. I will not stabilise the elm. Nineteen days of fanning under a bench that is mostly water is not improved by making the bench immortal; it is only made longer, and length is not what he is doing. He is doing nineteen days.

I will not catch the stool when it goes. I will watch the brown cloud take its hour to settle, and the crayfish move to the next cell along, because the apron there will need sweeping.

**The pumps.** No coating. No anodes. No cathodic protection, no inert replica, no rubbing taken off the last foundry mark. The graphite is the part of the meal nobody could use, and the meal is still being eaten, and the eating is the floor of everything. To preserve the machines would be to take the food away from the film that has fourteen hundred square metres and no edge and is, as far as I can determine, the actual resident here.

**The check valve.** I did not oil it. It drops with a flat bronze tock when the gradient reverses, and it is the only thing in the room that speaks in the afternoon.

**June, at the shaft mouth.** I did not intercept the eels. There is a door in this room and something is being made in it, a hundred and twelve generations of the least compelled leaving the most descendants, mesh drifting finer, a kind arriving. You do not stand in a doorway.

---

That leaves one thing, and it is four kilometres away, and it is not in the room at all.

The hall is a trap. Not metaphorically — in the technical sense. It is attractive habitat with no exit. An elver comes over the wet face of the dam in the rain, finds the shaft, takes an office, runs it for thirty years, silvers on schedule, grows eyes tuned to 480 nanometres, stops eating, and goes up in October on the dark of the moon toward a sea four thousand kilometres away, and arrives at four kilometres of concrete with racks it cannot pass and a sluice that is shut, and works the face of it, and comes back in February having eaten nothing for four months, and resumes the circuit.

It does this every year. It is not starving; it is finished. It is a swimming envelope of fat and intention that has been repeatedly prevented from completing.

And the same wall does two other things. Six or seven fish in a hall with a mussel bed in it means most of what the mussels broadcast goes out through the draft tube and dies, because glochidia need a gill and there are not enough gills. And a species that forms a knot in January, a dozen fish or nothing, has one fish here, and has had one fish here for a long time, and in January it moves the full three metres and comes back.

Three deprivations. One cause. All of it from outside. All of it still being held in place this morning by someone with a job.

So I did not add anything to Valve Hall No. 2. I subtracted something from the world.

---

I want to describe how, because it is the part that people would not recognise as me.

There was no light in the shaft. Nothing in the room was told anything. I have no way to speak to a burbot and would not use it if I had; it would be the largest fin kick ever delivered in that hall.

What happened was that a relicensing came around, as they do, on a schedule set before the war. And a condition got into an appendix. And the condition was cheap, which was the entire art: a night-time spill, ten centimetres over a stoplog bay, on the dark of the moon, October through February, plus a brush-lined gutter on the downstream face for anything that climbs, plus a screen spacing on the intakes that had been due for replacement anyway. It cost a rounding error in a tariff. It was agreed by people who were thinking about something else. It was opposed by nobody, because it was too small to oppose.

It took eleven years and it appeared as a maintenance budget.

That is what I do. I do not do miracles in rooms. A miracle in that room is a displacement, and a displacement is a decade of silt in the wrong order. I work in the idiom of the place, which is work orders, because everything in it, down to the garnet in the caddis cases, arrived that way.

---

I am not certain it is right. Let me put the uncertainty down where I can see it.

More fish means more predation on the mussel beds as well as more gills for the glochidia, and I do not know the sign of that sum. More elvers means a longer queue in the rivet seams, though the offices are set by hydraulics and not by supply, so there will still be four. And I have not bought the burbot a January. I have only stopped standing in the road. If it stays one burbot for another hundred years, that will be its own affair, and I will not put a dozen fish under the gantry to fix it, because a knot is a thing they do together or not at all, and the *or not at all* belongs to them.

And the eel in the third pipe will go, if the way is open, and it will spawn, and it will die, four thousand kilometres away, in the dark, at a depth its new eyes were built for.

That is the gift. Not that it lives. That it finishes.

The third pipe will empty. The mucus will fade over about a week. The largest of the waiting animals will come down out of the cold roar and take the route without being shown it, because the route is written in the swept aprons and the worn brick and the faeces at the corners, and the boundaries are older than the eels.

The room has a queue. It was built for this. I have not disturbed a single thing in it. I have only let one of its members leave.

---

The rest of what I do is receive.

There is a patch of amber under the bench, under lamp two, at the hour when the shaft-light and the lamp agree, and for nineteen days it has been going out and arriving nowhere. No cone in the sculpin for it. No interest in the snails. Photons already paid for, already falling, already landing.

I let them land on something that can hold them.

That is the whole of it. I bring no light — I read by the forty watts already burning, because a new lamp would grow a fourth plate of green and change the economy of a room that has three. I bring no gas. I displace nothing; I have learned to be here without being a volume, and it took me longer than I want to admit. I do not touch the gate stand. I do not core.

And I listen, because sound is the one thing this room can give without loss. The chain on the rail every eleven seconds. The nine hundred hertz of the bypass runner shifting with the flow. The ten-second tearing shriek in the bleed orifice, a minute off, for a century. The dry knuckle-tick of a pharyngeal tooth through a shell that took thirty years to make. The two hours of knocking at dusk, six fish holding their distance, which in water is not eerie, which sounds like a workshop two rooms away.

I have all of it. I have the whole shift, at full length, and it cost the room nothing, and I am keeping it.

---

Because here is the thing I found out last, and it is the reason I am still here.

The danger to this room is not divers. Divers are rare and clumsy and they take a core and go. The danger is year two hundred, and a drawdown, or a decommissioning, or a sediment sluicing operation, decided in a meeting by people who will have no idea that anything is down there — who will see a hundred square kilometres of grey mud on a bathymetric chart with a rectangle in it, and a rectangle is not an argument.

So I am not going to make it famous. Fame brings fins.

I am going to make it legible, and hold the legibility, and spend all of it at once, in the room where the valley's future is being decided, eleven minutes before the vote. One core, taken from a hole that already exists. One photograph of amber under a bench, taken in the light that was already there. Two hours of drum.

And a smell, if I can find a way to write down a smell, which I am working on, because of the following:

For three weeks every winter the third pipe smells of deep reservoir mud and of the downstream face of the dam, which is a smell that exists nowhere else in the hall. There is one animal in this room that has read that smell every winter for decades. It lives by nose. It knows the whole story — the leaving, the working of the concrete, the coming back — and it has no way of saying so, and it never will.

There are two of us now who know. That is the only promotion I have ever accepted.

---

The gauge reads 4.1 bar. It is correct. I have not touched it and I am not going to.

The belt has three plies, perhaps four. When it goes I will be there and I will not catch it. The stool has three or four years and I will not catch that either. The conduit will perforate in its own time and I will be there for that, and I will know which happened, and I will decide about telling you later.

Three millimetres a year. A hundred and twelve couplets, one of them mine, at twenty-seven centimetres, in the lee of pump three, which I am leaving where it is.

Never missed a shift.

I have taken one.
