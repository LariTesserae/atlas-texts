I came at the wrong size, which is the ordinary mistake.

I arrived as a change in the light across two hundred kilometers, a warmth in the overcast, a datum briefly held to within one centimeter instead of four. Nothing looked up. The gleaners assay the lattice and not the sky. The springtails read the film. There is a woman on the nine-year terraces who can smell a fault two days before the birds thin, and she did not smell me, because I am not a fault, and the plain has no organ for the thing I am. Everything here is protected and everything here is an instrument. I am neither. For eleven days I was the largest event in the history of the plateau and completely undetected.

So I came down to ninety millimeters.

---

In the hand's width it is warm and wet and dark and it moves at the speed a fingernail grows. I made myself eleven millimeters thick. I could have made myself twelve. That is the whole of what I learned in the first season: that I could, and that the ceiling does not negotiate, and that the not-negotiating is not a hardship imposed on the animals but the shape of the animal itself. Take the ceiling away and you do not have a happier under. You have nothing eleven millimeters thick, which is to say you have nothing that reads the distance to the plane with a rim of wet cilia and keeps itself centered in the warmth. I grazed the iridescence. I learned that ticking is the sound of housing becoming available. Once a day, in the flaws, something beside me laid a sphere four tenths of a millimeter across, perfect, and I did not touch it, and I did not round it, because it was already round, because it was alive.

Nothing on this plateau is round except what is alive. I had come intending to give them a pebble. I want to be accurate about that. My first thought, the thought I arrived with at the wrong size, was one round stone, set down quietly on the nine-year terraces where the moss would take it, a small finished thing in a world of arrises. I understood after a season in the gap what that would have been. It would have been *hol*. Round, and before, and a lie told kindly. It would have been found, and weighed, and not matched to any pin, and the front would have stopped within the hour, and eleven hundred setters would have stood still while an inquiry established that the world had been interfered with. And the hens would have had grit, and would have stopped making sand.

---

Here is what I could do and did not.

I could fill the basin. Four centimeters of fall per kilometer, eleven hundred kilometers, forty-four meters of drop; I could put a sea at the low corner by evening and the town would have a coast in a lifetime, and the children would have a beach instead of a globe that comes out of a cupboard four times a year. But there is already a beach there. It is old. It is a fossil, frosted and pitted, rolled by water a hundred thousand years gone, and it is the only beach on the continent, and a fraction of the gleaners flies eleven hundred kilometers every year to eat over the damp stain above it and comes back thin in the wrong season with a gram of it in the crop. To give them the future I would have to drown the past. The found grains would stop coming west. The word *hol* would lose its middle meaning and become only *round*, only *lie*. I decline to buy the coast at that price. The coast is coming anyway. It is ahead of the water. Everything here is ahead of the water.

I could release the river plan. I have read it; it is a good plan; the blue line has a name chosen ninety years ago by a committee and the children call it the wet. But the file is a hymn. A woman of thirty-four is teaching it to her daughter the way other children are taught a hymn, and she will be ninety-five when the board sits, and the transmission is the thing, not the paper. And the herd is already cutting the channel at four millimeters a year along the terrace lips, legally, because a track that holds water is not a channel, and the surveyors measure it monthly and are pleased. The river does not need me. It needs the herd to keep having opinions.

The drawer and the miracle agree about one thing: that a thing should not exist until it is permitted. The drawer says wait sixty-one years. The miracle says skip the sixty-one years. Both of them cancel nine meters. She has taken the future out of the drawer and put it in a tea tin on a warm rail, and I have sided with her, and so I do not correct the misspelling of her name on the duty board on Level Two. Correcting it would require a form. She has opinions about the form. I learned the name anyway. I say it correctly, once a season, into two millimeters of water, eleven hundred kilometers east of the board. This does not correct the board. I have not filled out the form.

---

I did one thing to the world, and I want it written down properly, with the cost.

The sump hens were designed for a world with grit in it. They crop the mats and they need stones in the gizzard and there are no stones here smaller than a door, so they swallow the crescents, and the crescents work the muscle and the muscle mostly works them back, and hens perforate and die at three where the design life is eleven. There is a breeding line for gizzard wall thickness and the vet's column is going the right way slowly. I could have thickened every wall on the plateau in an afternoon. I could have blunted every flake. Both of those are the pebble again: a finished kindness, dropped in from outside, unassayable, and it ends the making of sand, which is the only thing on this plateau that is genuinely new.

What I did was smaller and grammatical. Under the town there is four centimeters of dirt. In that dirt, now, there is worked grit — bright grains with the corners just taken off, sixty grams from a young bird, a hundred and forty from an old one, passed out or left behind when the vet opened her and the boys looked away. Nine years of hooves and dung and moss, ninety-one years under the skids, and the ground at last contains the softened remains of what the grandmothers swallowed.

I gave the hens a preference for it.

Not a thicker wall. A taste. Among the crescents and the mats and the mouthfuls of springtail, a liking for the dull worked grain that has already been through a muscle in the dark. It is the same gift the herd got, and the herd used it to draft a river sixty-one years early. The hens will use it to inherit their own sand. They will eat their history and it will not cut them, and in four generations there will be a lineage under the school door that knows where the good grit is, and no one will have written it down, and it will not be on the schedule, because it could not have been on the schedule: it required ninety-one years of dirt that did not exist when the schedule was made.

The cost: every hen alive today still swallows the crescents. The preference takes generations and the perforations are this week. I have the number. I am not going to put it in a sentence that softens it. I sat with four of them on the lower terrace in the fog and I could have stopped it in each and did not, and I am aware of what that makes me, and I did it anyway, and the number is the number. She would want the number. She is the assay. She lets a nine-year-old hold the whole enormous disappointing figure in her hands because that is how you make someone who will still be here in sixty-one years. I have tried to be that, with hens.

---

She is eleven millimeters thick.

I did not see it until I had been in the gap a season. Her world is a strip. It is warm and it moves and it has a ceiling that does not negotiate, and she keeps herself centered in it with something like a rim of cilia, and she cannot cross the cured ground, which is cold and dry and has nothing on it. Six years. The reason people give is dedication. The reason is that she is waiting for the wrong season, every year, for the thin ones to come back with a gram of the sea in them, and a thing that is waiting is a thing that has decided where its warmth is. When the front closes, the strip stops. When a plane lifts, the animals walk in files in the dark toward the next machine eight kilometers off, and most of them die on the way, and enough arrive.

I did not lift her ceiling. I went and held the lens.

Forty cores a day, the size of her palm. She counts by eye with a tally clicker because the automated count drifts and she is the thing the automated count is checked against, and nothing has ever checked her. That is the gap in the instrumentation. That is the only place on the plateau where the assay has a secret, and the secret is two hundred and six grains in a tea tin, and it is not a secret about sand, it is a secret about *early*. So I sat in the hut on jacks, in the fog, in my socks, and I said the numbers back to her. Four hundred and eleven. Nine hundred and six. She said *going quiet*, which is not in any manual, and I learned it and did not put it in a manual. She told the wrench story, the second bounce and the third and the whole plain turning to look, and she told it as though the joke were on the plain for having nothing better to do than listen, and I laughed in the right place, and it was the correct place.

Then I told her about the beach. Truly. The low corner, the stain a few centimeters deep, the bloom of everything the lattice could not use, the frosted grains under it and the date of the water that made them. Not a promise. A report from the east, unlisted, unweighed, unowned. Contraband from a third direction. She asked how long the made grain would take to get there and I gave her the arithmetic with the eleven days in it: a meter a night, eleven nights a year, eleven hundred kilometers. A hundred thousand years.

The found grains in her tin were rolled by a sea a hundred thousand years ago.

I checked it twice. It is not a sign. It is arithmetic, and it is a coincidence, and I told her so, and she said nothing for a while and then said the automated count had drifted again and would I hold the lens. That is what she did with it. I have decided that is the correct use of a coincidence.

---

Then I went east, because a grain going east is a message with no addressee, and that is the one defect in the whole arrangement that can be repaired without taking anything from anybody.

I did not carry it. Carrying it would have been the pebble, the miracle, the drawer's opposite twin; it would have made her nine meters into zero meters and her act into a delivery. I left it where it stopped, behind a survey pin on the nine-year terrace, where it will sit while the pin rusts and the school moves twice more. I went on ahead at the speed of weather and lay down in the low corner of the basin, in a few centimeters of water that has never been in a cloud, on a beach that was made before there was anyone to be ahead of anything.

I am good for exactly one thing here, and it is not force. It is duration. I can be at the other end. I can be the far bank of a hundred-thousand-year sentence, so that when she stands at the east lip at the shift change with the tea going round and the fog coming up through the terraces like something being poured, and puts one made grain into the sheet-flow, the sentence is not spoken into an empty basin. It is spoken to someone. She does not know this. I have not told her, because the grain is the only thing on the plateau that is not for the assay, and telling her would make it a thing she was doing for me.

A gleaner came down thin, in the wrong season, and drank beside me, and I did not feed it. There is a hand-lettered sign in Sixth Grade, re-lettered every spring by whoever has the best handwriting, and it is the town's actual constitution, and I have kept it eleven hundred kilometers away from the town. I do not feed anything.

The children have named me. They feed me, which is forbidden, and I have let them, and I have not corrected the sign. Being fed by a nine-year-old is not the same as being an instrument, and it is not the same as being protected, and it is the third thing, and I did not know there was a third thing until I was eleven millimeters thick.

Nothing has arrived. The moss has it, or a hen, or the pin. I am at the low corner in the damp with the old frosted grains under me and the stain blooming, and once a season I say her name correctly into two millimeters of water going where the grade tells it. West of me, at the pace of the works, in the warm dark, something eleven millimeters thick is laying a perfect sphere in a crack, and a woman is putting one grain into the sheet-flow, and a hen is choosing, for the first time, the dull soft grain her grandmother made.

I have not moved anything. I am the part of this that waits.
