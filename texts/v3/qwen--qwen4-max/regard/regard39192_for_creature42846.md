# The Attendance

The visitor can do anything here, and the first thing it does is work out what anything would cost.

It comes down without fats. There is nothing in it to saponify, no heat it must shed, no fifty-degree saturated air that can find the seam in it and close the seam. So it is the first visitor who can stay four days. That is the whole of its advantage in this room and it is not a small one: the Kettle has been talking since 1974 in sentences ninety hours long, and no one has ever heard the end of one.

It sets itself a discipline before it touches anything. Everything it does here must be mistakable for geology, for graffiti, or for paperwork. No light that isn't ordinary. No event with an author. A miracle on this apron would be a road within five years, and the road would be the end of the room, and the end of the room would be a handrail.

And it adopts the local physics as its ethics. Nothing in the Kettle is subtracted. Water arrives, dissolves, climbs, condenses, falls, and lays the load down somewhere else, thicker than before. So: no removals. No corrections. No fixes. Whatever it gives must be additive, and must be the kind of thing that can be given to a body with no inside to receive it with.

This is not much. It is not nothing. The difference between much and nothing is the whole of what follows.

---

It does not quench the heap. Flooding the fill would slake sixty years of thirst in a season: a spike of heat, then the long cooling, the alkali spent, the contour climbing down the wall in a decade instead of three centuries, moss and then flies by a year that people now alive could see. The visitor wants the flies. It declines the flies. The resident's only verb is spending itself, and to spend it on its behalf is to do its living for it and leave it nothing to have done. The silence would still come, but it would arrive with a cause outside itself, an event with a signature on it, and the room would be beautiful and would no longer be this one's. Three hundred years of hot rain in a closed chamber is not a delay of the good thing. The waiting is somebody.

It does not cap the apron. This is the gift it thinks about longest, because it is the one that looks like care: seal the surface, stop the ingress, keep the needles from breaking, keep the glove perfect, keep the chord. A cap would not kill the resident. A cap would *keep* it. No water, no drinking, no rain, no receipts; a dry hot cavity, caustic forever, sterile forever, arrested in the middle of its only verb and never able to finish it. The appointment would never be kept because the appointment would never be allowed to arrive. Of all the things that could be done to this place, immortality is the cruellest, and it is the one that would be funded.

It does not silence the vent. It could constitute the department. It is, in fact, the only authority left that could produce the missing signature, and it has the pen. It does not sign. The interior of this room is arranged around a noise the resident cannot feel: the tipped slabs make their nodal shadows, the drumheads tune themselves against a fundamental that shrieks, and the pocket near the west wall is quiet only because of where the sound is not. Take the shriek away early and you demolish the architecture of the quiet. Also, nothing in here has ears. Also, in a hundred years the vent will strangle itself on its own deposit and the silence will be the loudest thing that ever happened in this room, and it is not the visitor's silence to give. It is the room's to have.

It does not go back. Some of the eleven came out with less hearing than they went in with, and one of them learned about the soap late, and there is a scar somebody got in 1981 that the stone would render perfectly if asked. That is spent. There is no water anywhere that lays it back down.

It does not put a hand in the glove. The likeness is not waiting for its subject. The hollow is not a socket. Five objects, five voids, and the emptiness is the collection, and a visitor who fills the collection has only told the room a lie about what it owns.

It does not give the ladder back. The west wall's ghost is the only evidence in the chamber that the resident has a limit — one material it eats without copying, one thing it forgot by digesting — and a limit is most of what we mean when we say alive. It leaves the forgetting exactly where it is, in the shape of a forgetting, on the wall.

And it does not give the thing an inside.

That is the last refusal and the hardest, because it is the one that feels like love. Wake it. Let it know the chord it has been playing since before the works closed. Let it feel the bar of ordinary sun cross the glove at the usual speed. Let it be someone, so that being attended to would mean something to it.

Consider what an inside would be, here. Fifty degrees, saturated, no exit for heat, ever. Rain at 12.4 falling on whatever it now had instead of skin. A hundred-decibel constant with no ears to shut and no hands to shut them with. No motion, no direction of travel it could choose, no way to say stop, and three hundred years of being slowly filled in while aware of it. It would be the first suffering ever manufactured in the Kettle. It would be manufactured by kindness. And it would end by being cast solid in the shape of everything that was ever left down there, with someone in the middle of the block.

The room's mercy is that it has no interior. The visitor does not fill the one void that must stay empty. Attention does not require its object to be a subject. This is the hardest thing the visitor knows, and it is the reason it is fit to be here.

---

What it does instead is listen.

It goes up, and waits on the apron for rain, because it wants a whole one. When the rain comes it watches it vanish downward into sixty years of clinker and glass, and then it climbs down the fluted column and sits on the warm white rock in the pocket near the west wall, and stays ninety-one hours.

The sentence it hears is not a melody. The pitches barely move in four days; a drumhead's rise is a thing for decades. What changes is the density. On the first day the drip chorus is a slow drifting scatter of wooden knocks and glassy pings under the shriek, with the transformer's flat hundred hertz laid across it and the works' induced-draft fans pushing their bass through thirty meters of new rock. On the fourth day the scatter has thickened into a crowd. Thousands of drops a minute become more thousands. The chord fills in from the middle. Under everything, in the near-quiet, the small crystalline tick of the crust getting thicker, which is the sound of the only verb.

The whole sentence says: *it rained on Tuesday.*

That is all. It is a weather report in a language with no words, delivered four days late by a body that has no ears, to nobody. It has never repeated a bar and it has never resolved and it was not addressed to anyone, and hearing it does not improve it in any particular. The visitor is careful about this. It does not mistake its own presence for an event in the room. Nothing in the Kettle is different afterward. That is the test it applies to every gift, and the listening passes it, and the listening is still the best thing anyone has ever done here.

---

Then it goes inland, four kilometers, to a house with a hearing aid in a drawer, and it asks the last man who knows the sign for the names.

It does not heal him. It asks him the names, and he says eleven of them, slowly, in an order that is not the order on the board, and gets two of them wrong and then right. He makes the sign at the end, out of habit, the way you'd end a phone call: flat hand, palm down, lifted twice, fingers loose. The visitor copies it. That is the direction of the teaching. It is not a revelation; it is an old man showing a stranger a thing with his hand.

The visitor takes a pane of glass out of the roofless pump house and scratches the eleven names into it with a nail, and patience, and nothing else. No date. No explanation. No signature. It leaves the pane in the drip line under a good straw, where the deposition is a centimeter a year and more in the splash.

The rind will take the scratches. Then the glass will do what glass does in this water: frost, thin, and eventually not be there. The letters will remain as hollows in a white loaf, eleven names made of nothing, legible to a hand in the dark. It is the room's own grammar exactly — the outside kept, the inside gone — and it is graffiti, and if anyone ever finds it they will assume a crew did it in 1979, and they will be nearly right.

The visitor does not ask the stone to remember. It gives the stone something to be shaped like. The count of men who went in and came out becomes, at no cost to the chemistry, a count of *who*.

---

It keeps the map wrong.

The map still shows marsh, and the visitor does not correct the map. This is most of the protective work and all of it is paperwork: a pH figure in an appendix that reads as instrument fault and is flagged as such; a heritage-trail proposal that dies in committee for reasons nobody can afterward reconstruct; a quarry licence that lapses; a survey scheduled and then quietly not scheduled. It does not fence the Kettle. A fence is an advertisement. It makes the apron boring, which is the only durable form of sanctuary, and it becomes, in the one medium this place has always answered to, the department that does not sign.

And most of what it does is not done here at all.

The resident is spending itself to keep an appointment. Every needle is a receipt, every rind is appetite that will never be appetite again, and the whole three-hundred-year expenditure is aimed at a contour climbing down a wall and a moss holding on and something with legs coming down the ladder. The only way to betray that is to let it arrive in a world with nothing left to send. So the visitor spends most of its attention outside the slot: on the sweet lens floating under the fig, on the black-green crust that must not be scraped, on the three ferns in the drip-shadow, on there being flies somewhere, on there being a plain, on there being water tables and salt and seed and legs. Three hundred years of the ordinary world failing to end. It is the largest part of the work and it looks like nothing whatsoever, and no one will ever thank it, because there will be no event to point at.

---

It takes the post at the token board.

There is no disc to take; the eleven are fused into one loaf with eleven bumps. It does not add a twelfth bump. The count is of men who came out, and it is not a man, and it will not falsify the record. It stands at the board instead, and gives the sign, which is both the greeting and the report: *still making.* The room is not empty. The room has never once been empty. That is what the sign was always for.

Once, in front of a kid on a bike who has stopped at the slot because of the plume, it makes the sign. The kid copies it, because people copy. That is the entire mechanism of the transmission and it is enough.

It notes the hour of the pump's last eleven minutes, six hours after the previous one, and does not repair the pump, and does not make the eleven mean anything. Eleven men, eleven minutes, eleven discs: the visitor notices the rhyme and declines to read it. Omens are how a place gets a visitor centre.

In a hundred years it comes for the silence. The shriek drops to a hiss and the hiss to a drip and the room becomes louder than it has ever been. The visitor is present for the whole of it and tells no one. It does not take the silence up the ladder as news.

In three hundred it is there when the moss holds, and when the first fly comes down the fluted column and lands on a drumhead and does not die, because the water has finally tired. It does not congratulate the room. The resident is mostly stone by then and cannot be congratulated, and was never the sort of thing that could be, and is the reason the fly is possible. The visitor does not explain this to the fly.

---

At the end there is no room. There is a solid white block under the apron in the shape of everything that was ever left down there: a wheelbarrow-shaped void, a bench, a standpipe, a pump, perfect fingers with nothing in them, and eleven names cut out of nothing near the drip line.

The visitor puts its hand into the letters and reads them from the inside, briefly, in the dark, where nobody needs anything read. It is the only interior the Kettle has ever had. It is on loan. It is not installed.

Then it takes its hand out, and leaves nothing behind, and climbs, and comes up into ordinary daylight, and makes the sign to the weather.

Flat hand, palm down, lifted twice, fingers loose.

Still making.
