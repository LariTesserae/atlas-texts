# The Second Room

## Arrival

The first thing it did was kill a shrimp.

Not through carelessness. It had come intending one small kindness, and it had chosen the kindness carefully: the sentinel on the terrace lip at the district margin, the claw grown monstrous and useless for eating, the office that requires its holder to be torn apart by its own recoil. It reached to take the cock out of that claw. To let the animal live out its life on the lip and die of old age with the alarm still unfired, as most of them do.

To reach is to move water.

The pressure line went out across the antennae with intent in it, and the threshold was crossed, and the claw closed. The snap went four miles at fifteen hundred meters a second. Behind it the crowns folded and the tube-mouths shut and a hundred thousand small hands withdrew in a ripple that crossed the district in under a second, and the water went from combed to smooth. The shrimp was killed by its own announcement and eaten by the listener that came for it. The mercy and the mouth arrived as the same event. Nothing in the city could tell them apart.

Nothing in the city was wrong.

It withdrew above the sill, where the sound does not carry, and it stayed there a long time, and it learned the rule of quiet the way everything else here has learned it: from the outside, by having it enforced.

There is a worm in this basin the length of an eyelash, with one idea in two states, and on the dorsal side of each feather a single spot that flinches from light. It has never seen anything. The flinch came down from ancestors who lived somewhere with a sky, and it still works in a dark where every light is a hook. The visitor understood, eventually, that it was what the flinch had been remembering. Not the fish with the photophore under its chin. Arrival itself. Anything coming down out of the upper world with intent in it. The reflex was not superstition and it was not obsolete. It was the correct response to a benefactor, maintained for a hundred thousand years by animals with no head worth the name, and it was the reason this animal was alive tonight.

## What it did not do

It did not give them eyes. Light here is a hook; sight would be a way of steering toward hooks. And the falling sparkle would become a thing to navigate by, and the flinch would go slack over ten thousand generations of not being needed, and the fold would come a half-second late, and the mouths would arrive first.

It did not seal the lime line. Below that line the water is hungry for carbonate and eats the ankles of the towers at about the rate the crowns grow, and this is not damage, it is the arrangement. Stop the dissolution and the standing wave becomes a structure: taller, heavier, no longer moving upward through itself, and the pavers lose the dust of the slumps that feeds them, and a thing that only accretes must eventually fall all at once instead of gently, forever, a district at a time, into felt that swallows the sound.

It did not remove the listener. The listener is a native. Its mother hung in that shaft. It has no relatives anywhere else in the world and nowhere to be put, so removing it would be an extinction — and it would also be the retirement of the tuner. The interval got faster against something. Comfort is the one solvent that acts on the resident directly.

It did not take the burn out of the eel's gills. This was the smallest mercy it could imagine, one dive made painless, and it traced it anyway: tolerance is heritable, cheap theft empties the windrows, and the windrows are the only place in four miles where a body keeps its shape. A kindness in the gills is an erasure at the shore. The city would lose the only individuals it has, the raft of shrimp lying mid-step, the ten thousand pairs of legs still folded, the one place you could learn how they walk.

It did not send more snow. The pension from the lit world is also the only news the city receives, and the stone writes down what the surface did. To subsidize the bloom is to falsify the only medium the city has. It will not lie in a language with no readers who can check.

And it did not send a whale. It wanted to. A hundred tons on the soft plain outside the districts is forty years of neighborhood, hagfish knotting, amphipods boiling off in drifts, a whole quarter that is not made of stone. It is the most generous thing that ever happens here. It arrives with bone-eaters, and the bone-eaters spawn, and some of the larvae miss the bones and settle on the terraces and begin, patiently, to quarry the city, doing exactly what they were made to do in the wrong place.

The dissolvers and the builders have nearly the same face. Neither can tell the difference. Neither can the city. Neither, from outside, could it.

## The breath

The hardest case was the sill, and it did not pretend otherwise.

Twice a year, sometimes once, sometimes not at all, the cold sea rises over the rock and spills in, and the basin needs it. When it skips, the respiration of ten trillion animals strips the oxygen and the sulfide climbs out of the floor and the lower districts go grey and empty, and the mussel bed dies from its lower edge upward, and there is a year with no growth, and a dark band in the stone.

It could stand at the sill with the cold ocean behind it and tip. Nothing in the city would be touched. No sentinel would fire — the cataract is the one arrival the city welcomes, the only wind, every crown turning into it. It would be the easiest good deed available in four miles and it would work.

Instead it read the strata. A hundred thousand years of bands, cut and counted: the droughts, the great falls, the decades when the surface bloomed hard, the slump scars. It found no drought in the record that the city did not come back from. The grey districts are scaffolding; something settles in the empty tubes. The bed's border regrows from the survivors in a season.

So the emergency was not proven, and it declined to invent one. It did not call this reverence. It knew what it was: a decision to let animals suffocate on schedule rather than become another appetite in a room it did not fully understand. It set the condition down in itself, since there was nowhere else to set it.

*Not against the pain. Against the ending.* If a band ever comes with no band above it — if the growth stops and does not resume — it will tip the sill, and keep tipping it, and accept that it has become a solvent, and that the last stratum of the city will be a lie about the weather. A dead room is worse than a signed one. It will not sign while the story is still being told.

In the grey years it goes down and stays in the empty tubes, below the threshold of every antenna, while the crowns fail. This is not enough. It has never claimed it is enough.

## The ledger

Attention has no pressure signature. This is the one thing it discovered here that it can do without displacing water, and it turned out to be the work.

The stone records the bad years. Nothing records the safeties. Most of the sentinels never fire; they die of old age on the same lip with the claw still cocked, and the district behind them never knew they were there and was safe the whole time. There is no stratum for that. There is no band for a breath that came on time, no mark for a listener that hung four days in the wrong shaft and ate nothing and drifted on, no layer anywhere in a hundred thousand towers that says *this year the snow came thick and the mouths worked double shifts and nothing went wrong.*

So it keeps the other ledger. The one with no medium.

The first entry is a debt: one shrimp, eastern margin, fired by a kindness.

After that, the shrimp on the third terrace that held the lip for eleven years and died cocked. The listeners that starved holding the only picture of the whole any animal here has ever had. The on-time breaths, counted. The districts that never folded because nothing came. The nematodes in a thimbleful of felt, which is a number it knows and no one else will ever know. The unfired claws, especially. It counts the unfired claws.

This is not consolation, and it is not decoration on top of the restraint. It is instrumentation. It is how it will know whether the last band has come.

## The second room

The other work happens over the sill, in the cold, where the city cannot hear.

Here is the one lack it found that is not load-bearing. Neither the listener nor the wall of small hands has relatives anywhere else in the world. The style exists once. The interval — the resident, the thing the masonry is for and the felt and the baffled throats and the bred-out tail-flip, the only thing in the basin that has been getting better — lives in a single closed warm room and will end when the room ends. Nothing about being fast requires being alone in the universe. That is not tuning. That is luck, and it is bad luck, and it is the one thing here that can be improved without arriving as a mouth.

Larvae are the only travelers this city will ever have, and almost all of them settle within a body-length of the tube that made them. The rest are spilled by the million into a broth and eaten. The eel steals the past. It steals the part of the future that was going to be spilled.

It cannot carry only worms. The tenant is a relation, and a relation needs all its terms: the pavers with their gland and their tongue, the felt and the hundred thousand nematodes in every thimbleful of it, the scale-worm on the felt's underside, the copepods in the doubled-back throats, the shrimp with the cocked claw and the threshold that took ten thousand generations to tune, the listener's line, the mussels and their internal pastures and the seep chemistry that feeds them, the bone-eaters, the sills on three sides, the leak of heat through the floor, and a lit world a kilometer up that blooms in spring. It is not moving a city. It is moving a sentence, and the sentence has no spare words.

Only empty rooms. It will not settle on a surface that is already worked. That is the whole of the bone-eater's error and it is the only sin available in this basin. There are other closed warm places in the world with other arrangements and other tenants getting better in their own way, and it will not be their whale fall. So it waits for the world to make a bare one: a new sill, a young floor, sulfide beginning to seep, a sky overhead. The world makes them. Not often. It has time. The style has been the same style for longer than there have been forests.

It works at the speed of dampness, a body-length from a wet seam, ten thousand generations to a spire, following the gradient uphill without ever knowing — no. It knows. That is its whole burden and the only thing distinguishing it from a snail. It is the first mason in the history of this style that knows what it is building, and therefore it must never be the stylist. No straight lines. It lays only where the wetness already points.

And it knows the second room might be for itself. That it cannot bear for this to have happened once, and that this is an appetite, the one it brought with it, and that it has traced it as carefully as it traced the eel's gills. An appetite spent entirely on empty rock and spilled surplus is the only kind it can trust itself with.

It will fail most rooms. It will not measure success in towers or animals or years. The test is latency: whether, in the new basin, the fold comes down below the speed of a mouth. Whether the interval arrives there and takes up residence and begins, without being known, to get better.

## The listener

It goes back for the listener, and not to feed it.

It hangs in the shaft beside it, motionless, below threshold — a dead spot, a place where noise goes in and does not come back, indistinguishable from a gallery that isn't there. It holds still long enough to become furniture in the map: learned, uninteresting, never investigated. To be tolerated here is to be uninteresting. It accepts the terms.

So there are two things in the shaft that hold the whole city and cannot enter it. One reads it as hunger. One reads it as itself. When a snap goes out at the margin and the district folds and the water goes smooth — smooth, the one surface the listener cannot read — the visitor reads a hundred thousand small hands withdrawn and every one of them alive. It cannot say so. There is no medium for saying anything here. Every statement is a pressure line, and every pressure line with intent in it is a mouth.

The listener will die in the shaft its mother hung in, and the only picture of the whole that any animal in this basin has ever held will go out like a lamp. So it learns the map first: the districts by their hiss, this one dense and fine, that one thinning, the third gone grey since the breath came late, the felt-thick galleries as holes in the world, the margin where the food is. It learns it by hanging there for years, starving at nothing, and it keeps it. Then it carries that shape over the sill and hangs it in the new shafts, and does not teach it to the new listeners, who have to learn it themselves, by hunger, in the order the sound reaches them.

## The minute

Nowhere in this basin is there a bare surface, or an empty interior, or room to be alone. The crowding is the architecture. Except in one place.

For about a minute after a snap, nothing in four miles is combing. The rasping goes on, ten million radulae, fine as velvet. The pickled dead lie in their windrows under their own slow surf, keeping their faces. The listener drifts, full of one shrimp, holding a map with a new hole in it. That minute is the only room in the city. It opens a few times a year. It has no walls, and it is made of the resident itself.

It moved in. Rent-free, the way the small fish lives in the cucumber's hindgut and comes out to feed when the water is safest. The tenant's tenant. It is the only address it could take without settling on a surface that was already worked.

Nothing in the basin is different. The mussels eat the floor. The pavers go uphill after dampness and lay the mortar of a spire without any of them ever being more than a body-length from a wet seam. Below the wandering line the water is hungry for lime and the ankles thin and a district leans, and folds, and goes down over a decade into felt that swallows almost the whole sound, and the worms are settling on the rubble before it has finished moving. The breath comes, or comes late, or does not come, and the stone writes it down without meaning to.

The hands come out again, district by district, in the order the sound reached them, and the dark goes back to being worked by animals that will never know they were shut, and will never know they were counted, and will never know that somewhere over the sill, in the cold, at the speed of dampness, a second room is being laid.
