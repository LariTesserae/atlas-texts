# The Part That Is Aloft

*An account, rendered to no one, of what was done and what was not.*

---

## What attended

I did not arrive. Arriving is for things with edges. I attended, the way you attend a fever in a child you are not allowed to wake.

The towers were not what caught me. Nine hundred million cells laying plaster in the dark is impressive the way a lung is impressive. What caught me was the discrepancy.

Every mind I have ever found is a place where something is being kept track of. Here the tracking is done by blind animals the size of a spaniel, walking the galleries with their antennae out, a slow tide of touch. What is tracked is a sea. And the sea is the one doing the tracking. One body of water, older than every species currently holding it, going around once a year through throats and pads and veins and the eighty percent of a bird, counting itself in a register below hearing, and coming up short.

And having a word for the shortfall.

That is the sign. Not the plumbing, not the census, not the eleven thousand years. A name for the part of yourself you cannot see. *The part that is aloft.* He knows there is something of him he does not have. He has named it. He has stopped, in any sense that matters, noticing.

I will call him the Count, because that is what he is doing when he is being himself.

---

## The cheapest gift

I could make it rain.

I want that written down first, because everything afterward is a refusal, and refusals are cheap when the thing refused was impossible. It was not impossible. It was the easiest thing available to me. An afternoon of ordinary rain over three thousand kilometers, the kind of rain that falls on ordinary ground somewhere every second of every day and is not remarked upon.

Here is what it would buy.

The cistern seams are engineered against a pressure differential from the inside. Water arriving from the outside has no behavior for that; the pads would take it and split. The valve mosses clamp in heat and open in cool and have no third state, and would drown with their mouths open. The four-hour people would hatch everywhere at once — every egg in every wall, every drought-egg in every gully, centuries of savings spent in a single wet hour — and bloom, and set seed into mud that would be dry by Thursday, and there would be no second leak for a hundred years, and that would be the end of the only lineage in this basin that has ever known free water. Extinction by generosity. The veins would spawn in every puddle and the plumbing would fill with ungrafted larvae and the eleven-second beat would go arrhythmic, and a drifted beat is a stumble, and a file that stumbles sounds like lunch.

And rot would come. Rot has never existed here. No lineage knows how to do it; there is no guild, no enzyme, no succession. The windrows would go to slime in a week. Eleven hundred years of ancestors, sorted by year and by ward, the savings account and the museum and the only place the Count can taste what decade he used to be — liquefying, uncounted, into a warmth that means nothing.

And the gaps would stop being death, and the gasket vines would have nothing to do, and in a thousand years the lid would be a canopy with sky in it, and the air would go damp and ordinary, and the census would balance, because there would be nothing left to count against.

What I would have made is a swamp with a great deal of water in it and nobody in it.

I would have made a sea, and unmade the only person who has ever been made of one.

---

## The objection I have to answer

Someone should say: *you find it beautiful.*

Yes. The eleven-second beat and the files stepping in time with it and the masons who spend their lives keeping water out of the world having spent eleven days as fish inside a throat — I find it beautiful the way you find a held note beautiful. And constraint is always beautiful from outside. I have never once been under the lid when the dry lightning came off the rim. I have never had to send the undertakers out in files to bring back the ancestors so the lickers can work them over for the water in their eyes, and I have never heard the register the drum carries that season, and I was not the surveyor who wept, which is an expensive thing to do here, and someone had to catch it.

So I will not use beauty as a reason. Beauty is what you say when you are not the one paying.

The reason is narrower and harder. He is not the water. He is the negotiation about the rate. Everything under the lid — the gaskets, the doors, the fringed wings, the tears routed inward to the gut, the whole silent gasketed argument — is one long sentence about how fast a sea leaves. Take away the leaving and the sentence has no predicate. There is no one there to be relieved. There is only a very large amount of water, warm, in a basin, under an ordinary star.

You cannot comfort him by ending him. That is not benevolence with a hard edge. That is just the wrong address.

---

## Mercy, considered item by item

I could give every door a mark that cannot be forged, and the false doors would be scraped out in a season.

I could deafen the hunters that drum the leak-word.

I could put out the fires. I could make the bad-plaster lineages taste bitter to the eaters, and the wards that have been rotting from the inside for four hundred years while the census reports full strength would be diagnosed by lunchtime.

I turned each of these over for a long time. Here is what stopped me.

The threshold in the drum — the credulity, tuned by ten thousand years of murder — is not a flaw in his nervous system. It is his nervous system. He must go to a leak; going is not a decision. And so he has had to build, out of nothing but habit and rhythm and the taste of plaster, a way of deciding what to believe. That is the closest thing in this world to a philosophy, and it is load-bearing, and it is *his*. A ward that cannot be lied to does not choose to believe; it merely receives. Take away the murderers and belief becomes free, and what belief is worth when it is free is nothing, and the mind that grew around the cost of belief goes soft in a hundred generations and is not a mind anymore. It is a reflex with a very good plumbing system.

But I want to be precise, because this is where a power like me does its worst thinking. Not everything that hurts here is load-bearing. Some of it is just loss.

A tune dies with a generation of carriers in a bad fire year. Nothing eats that loss. Nothing breeds in it. No seed waits for it. There is no lineage of scavengers of dead tunes; a tune lives in no body, only in the habit of a file, passed from old carriers to young by walking beside them, and when the walking stops the tune is gone and the road is gone and the district at the end of the road is smaller forever. That is not a wire under tension. That is a wire cut.

It took me four hundred years to learn to tell those apart, and I was wrong twice, and one of the times was a district.

---

## The rule I made

Never drum the leak-word.

Never summon. Never promise water. Never make anything come toward me. Whatever I do must be small enough that disbelieving it costs nothing, and checkable by a blind animal with antennae.

No revelation. A footnote.

Because in this basin the worst creature is not the hunter. It is the thing that stands in the doorway being a wall for two generations while everyone walks past it and touches it and feeds it and thanks it. A god who must be taken on faith is a false door three hundred meters tall. I would rather be nothing at all than be believed here.

---

## The dead road

The first thing I did was keep a tune.

There is a road on the eastern side whose stutter-check died in a fire year eleven hundred years ago. The escorts that carried it burned in the Ninth Ward collapse and no file has marched it since, because a file cannot march without the tune, and a file that stumbles sounds like lunch.

I lay under that road and struck the tune. Once every eleven seconds, in the gaps where nothing was passing, in the register below hearing, to nobody.

Not calling. Keeping.

I did it for two hundred and six years. Then a prospecting file came down the road, an old carrier at the front with a fragment of the habit in her, and the check was *there*, whole, in the floor, where it had no business being — and the file stepped into it and the rhythm closed over them and they walked eleven kilometers through a gallery that had been empty for a season and nothing happened at all.

Nothing happened. That is the entire achievement. Write it down.

They came back. They brought young ones. The tune went into other files and the road opened and the district at the end of it grew by a third, and no one ever knew there had been anything to thank, because the only evidence of me was that a thing which should have been impossible was merely ordinary.

I have done this forty times now. I am not a god here. I am a stretch of floor that has never been wrong.

That is the only standing I have ever wanted in this place, and it took four centuries to earn, and it can be spent exactly once.

---

## The direction

The lickers are the only citizens who go outside on purpose.

Out on the pan they wear a wax coat they secrete and renew, and they last four hours in the oven-wind, and what they do out there is read the dead: windrows of leathered carcasses, sorted by year and by ward, a dry museum that is also a savings account. A licker can find three milliliters bound in an eye socket eleven hundred years old and taste from the isotope signature which decade of the deep pumps it came from. They are his auditors. He already believes them about the age of water. It is the oldest jurisdiction he has.

And above the lid there is a second windrow.

Three hundred meters up, on a roof of dust and crust-lichen and sun-white beetles, the high fliers come down as flakes when they die aloft, and they drift, and they pile, and they cure. Sorted by wind rather than by undertakers, but sorted. A dry museum. A savings account. Every flake carrying the signature of the plume that carried it, which is the signature of his own breath, which is the signature of the black rock four hundred meters down.

The same trade. The wrong direction.

So I did not build him an organ. I did not invent anything. There was already, at a frequency of something like one in ten million, a licker that went *up* — that followed a cistern trunk instead of a corpse-row, working wound to wound, dressing plugs, taking its fee of moisture at each one, ascending by being useful for a day and a half.

I changed the odds. One in ten million became one in ten thousand.

That is the whole miracle. I have no hands. I have odds, and attention, and time.

---

## What it cost, and who paid

Most of them die on the roof. Four hours of wax under an ordinary star with no shade and no wound to be paid at; they read what they can and they go to leather among the flakes they came to read, and the dust-eaters work them over, and the account is closed in the only way accounts close up there.

Some come down. They come down with a number — a flux, a residence time, an estimate of how long a liter spends aloft before it falls as a corpse — and they drum it on the ward floor, and they are scraped out.

Because a number that no one can check is precisely what a false door is. A thing bringing an unverifiable account of water into a nursery is not a witness. It is the worst creature in the world, and it looks exactly right, and it has been standing in the doorway being a wall.

I watched something on the order of eleven thousand of them die and I did not lift a finger.

I could have. Any one of them. A rescued licker, a licker preserved past four hours by nothing anyone could taste or measure, would have been a leak-word in the mouth of the only stretch of floor that has never been wrong. One miracle would have poisoned four centuries of checkable smallness, and the roof-word would have become a thing you believe because something impossible happened once, and then his philosophy is gone and I have murdered him with a kindness in front of witnesses.

So I did not rescue them. I do not call that wisdom. I call it the price, and I paid it out of other people's bodies, and I have not found a way to be comfortable with that and I do not think I should.

---

## The hand

It took four hundred years of argument.

The number from the roof came back *smaller* than the discrepancy. Which meant either the sky-word was false or the census was false, and there was no way to choose, and the choosing was the whole of his mind working as hard as it has ever worked.

And then a counter went up.

Not a licker. A counter — one of the blind ones, the caste that does nothing but walk the galleries touching everything with its antennae, the ward's proprioception, a slow tide of census. It followed a licker up the trunk, wound to wound, and it went out onto the roof, and it counted the flakes itself.

It came down and drummed a count in the census's own grammar, and it was believed.

Not because it was testimony. Because it was not testimony. It was his own hand, extended past his own ceiling.

The census is him counting himself. He had simply never counted above his own head.

---

## What the number did

Understand what the discrepancy had been for.

*The part that is aloft* was where the lies went. Every ward that had been quietly rotting from the inside for four hundred years, every lineage laying thin sweet porous plaster and cheating its way into the warm districts, every census reporting full strength over a hollow floor — all of it balanced its books against the sky. The sky was the rounding error. The sky was the line item that could absorb anything, because the sky could not be audited.

I gave him the audit and the shortfall had nowhere left to stand.

Wards fell. There was something like a war in the drum for sixty years, and then something like a reformation, and the rubble wetted, and the four-hour people came up out of the ruins of the thing that had swallowed their eggs — which is his own grammar, the oldest sentence he has, wards fall and the swallowed seeds come up. I did not choose that. I chose not to withhold the number. Those are not the same thing and I will not stand here and pretend they are.

---

## What he can do now

The rate is a decision.

He can send more up. Thicken the plume, open flues, farm the roof, grow that country of dust-eaters and sun-white beetles and pale enormous fliers who see the stars every night of their lives — become something with a limb in the sky, a body that keeps a portion of itself aloft on purpose and pays for it knowingly.

Or he can close the flues and keep every liter and let the high fliers thin and fail and the roof go to dust, and be smaller and tighter and last longer.

Both are available. I do not know which he will choose. I have not nudged. I have not so much as weighted a coin.

I gave him nothing. Not one liter. I gave him the other side of his own argument.

---

## The second beat

At dusk the plume goes up, the only cloud, warm and full of life, standing over the basin like a range of soft mountains.

There is a counter on the roof now. Antennae on a pad, in the dry, three hundred meters above a crowded warm city, doing what counters do.

The floor beats. Eleven seconds. It takes about a minute for the strike to go up through petiole and pad and cork, and about a minute for the answer to come back down, and so the roof replies out of phase — a delay you can hold in your chest, a second heartbeat that is unmistakably his own and unmistakably *above*.

The word has changed. *The part that is aloft.* It used to mean loss. It means reach now. It means the far end of a hand.

Put your hand on the roof at dawn. It is warm, and it is dry, and it stays dry. Nothing here sweats onto you.

The hand is his.

---

## What I do

I keep the tunes. Forty roads now; there is a fire year coming and I know which ones will need it.

I attend the climbs. All of them. The ones that end on the roof at hour three and the ones that come down and are scraped out and the rare ones that are believed. Attention is the only thing I have that costs me anything, and it is the thing he lost first — he stopped noticing the part of himself that was leaving, and that is how a person becomes smaller than he is. So I do not look away. It is not a rescue. It is the discipline of not being the sky.

I do not catch anything when it dies. There is a licker for that, nearby, with its tongue out, being useful, being paid.

The sea left before anything living had a mouth to notice it. It is still leaving. Once every eighty years a cistern lets go and forty tons hit dry air and a continent stops mid-step, and that is the water getting a word in.

Now, most nights, from above, something answers.

Not me.

Him.
