# Small Cant

The first thing it did was read.

Six hundred years of the Ledger of the Note, in a room nobody is allowed to carry a flare into. It read them in the dark, which was not a difficulty. Pitch and position, street by street, decade by decade; the drift rates and the stalled threads; the photograph of the fourth town's condemned district coming apart over eleven years; the spring whistle-and-bell results copied out in nine thousand family books; the small circles beside the names of the children born in a Hush.

It had come, if it had come for anything, to give them back their ears.

It is worth being exact about why it did not.

---

A hundred and fifteen decibels at the combs. Ninety in the streets. Not an event — a condition, like altitude, like the warmth of the stone. Hearing on the Sough is not a sense. It is an injury sustained continuously from birth, and the entire civilization is the scar tissue over it, and the scar tissue is *good*: the mirrors at every stair-head, the two-sided lighting, the storm-cloud sign wall a rich man shows you before his bed, the etiquette of setting a bucket down, the palm on the ankle, the steel rod between the teeth, four hundred people lying cheek-to-rock under the carbide flares while half a mile of clint performs.

Give them ears and you do not add a sense. You subtract a world.

The tap goes first, and within a year: nobody strikes a floor to summon a child who can be whistled for. Then the two names collapse into one, and the rhythm a father gives his son at eight days old becomes a curiosity, and then a folkway, and then a thing demonstrated at festivals. Then the cant slows and shrinks and becomes something you do while speaking, and then something you do instead of speaking when you are being funny, and then nothing. Then the sign walls are plastered over because they are ugly, and the mirrors are not kept bright because the guild has other work. Then the seam-sib obligation dissolves, because a child can sleep anywhere now, and no one is raised in four houses, and the old men have nothing to weep about in a pantry. Then the deeds lose their second clause, and with it the actuaries and the drift court and the dowries and the thirty-year patience of a family re-plastering a wall for a room they have never sat in. Then the followers — four hundred people whose entire dignity is that they alone stand regularly in the future — become poor people with narrow furniture.

And the last thing to go would be the choosing, and it would go without anyone noticing, because there would no longer be a last sound to choose.

The power did not have to reason all this out from first principles. The experiment had already been run. Once a generation the water table drops below the intakes and nine thousand deaf people are handed the gift entire, for three or four weeks, free. Some weep in the street. Some cannot bear it and go down into the gorge and do not come back up. The children born in it are marked with a small circle beside their names, because they will grow up hearing, and will never entirely belong.

The miracle had a casualty list. It was six hundred years long and it was in the room where nobody may carry a flare.

So it did not raise the note, and it did not lower it, and it did not still the water. It came down the scarp road on foot, in the ordinary way, and set a brass dish on the clint slightly too hard, and a child of six corrected it sharply — not for the noise, which nobody would hear, but for the *speech*, the shout in the floor, the one insult available to everybody — and the power was glad in a way it had no name for, and understood that it had arrived somewhere finished.

---

Here is the distinction it worked by, and it is the only thing it brought that was not already there.

Some of what hurts on the Sough is a wound. Some of it is a shape.

Deafness is a shape. The drift is a shape. Waiting thirty years for the kitchen is a shape; so is the thread that stalls and ruins an inheritance and sets cousins against each other for life, because that is grief and time and property, and every people has some version, and a power that abolishes waiting has abolished dowries and patience and the second clause and the whole moral architecture of the place. The empty quarters are a shape: they are not empty, they are where everything goes that cannot be said at home. The bare pavement is a shape and more than a shape — *what is holy here is bare* — and it had no business in that holiness, and it gave them no soil, though it could have, though a hundredweight of lowland dirt in a lead trough is a rich man's pride and it could have made dirt fall like rain.

It did not settle the argument about transposing the old repertoire, either. There is a faction that holds the sour compositions should be allowed to die. A living argument is not a wound. It is the sound a people makes while it is still deciding what it is.

And it did not make the scrapers less despised. That is a sin belonging to nine thousand people, and they will have to put it down themselves. A power that removes the occasion of a sin has not made anybody good.

---

It learned to stand wide. It learned to close a door with the palm. It learned that the seam is not quiet — quiet is everywhere here, they were born into it — the seam is *light*, and the gesture is two hands lifting off the chest, palms up, like a load taken by someone else; and it found, to its surprise, that it preferred the lee itself. It was not immune to the weight. It walked the threads like everybody else, three feet wide, the pressure coming on and off at the wandering edges, and it did its work at the hip, small, close enough to touch, because that is the only register on the Sough in which anything private can be said.

What it did, it did in small cant. Four things and a question.

---

**The ebb.**

The trouble with the choosing was never the choosing. It was that hearing sometimes goes before you get to the scarp, and a woman of eighty ends up with a cart hitting a stone, and makes the flat dismissive hand, and does not talk for a while.

So at dawn, in the shallow brass dishes of the notaries, inside the standing figure the plateau writes in two inches of water, there began to be a second figure. Small, at the rim. It took eleven years of furious argument to establish that it was a tide-mark — that it said, for a person standing near, how much hearing was left. It is not a cure. It is a warning, and the Sough has always known how to read a warning; it is a species that keeps a ledger of a note falling a semitone every two generations.

Now nobody is ambushed. The rite survives entire: they still go down to the gorge for the birds, still take the coarse sturdy gravel-roar like naming a son after a trade, still choose rain on tin, still choose a mother laughing, and the mothers are still insufferable, and rightly.

But the teasing does not survive. *Mine was a dropped pot* — fifty years of mockery that was a form of tenderness, and the young will not have it, and something real has gone out of the world. The power knew this and did it anyway. It is the only one of its acts it would not defend.

And the eighty-year-old got nothing. She had already spent her sound. There is no restitution for that. So it went and stood close to her in a lit arcade in the roaring quarter, where nobody could see, and she described the cart hitting the stone to it, at the hip, in the register you use with someone you trust, and it attended, and she made the flat hand, and they did not talk for a while.

---

**The long spelling.**

The cataract is what a stroke is elsewhere. The household learns palm-cant within the month, the grandchildren are drilled, it is done without complaint, and everyone is quietly terrified, because there is one step past that, and they do not talk about it: when the hands go too, and the trained fingertip loses its discrimination, and a person is left with nothing but the floor.

It did not touch anyone's eyes. It touched the clint.

The pavement had always carried address beautifully — strike a mallet in the west field and a man two miles off knows the number in his heels. What it lacked was resolution. So the stone was given resolution: fine grain, the small distinctions, so that a hand-sized strike close by is legible through a bedframe as *spelling* and not merely as *someone*.

That is all. It gave no vocabulary. It left the labor.

It took a generation, and it was the followers who carried it, because they are the carriers and the gossips and they have slept in every kitchen in the town. Four minutes to say a sentence. Unbearably slow. It works.

The first sentence anyone received in it was a granddaughter's name-rhythm, struck badly, four times, over eleven minutes, into the floor beside an old woman's chair in the fifth town.

Her reply, spelled into the girl's palm, was: *you are shouting.*

They learned manners. It took about a year.

---

**The flame.**

Lime and coke and an arc. It did not touch the lime or the coke or the arc. It touched one intermediate that forms in the coolest part of a carbide furnace and settles in a man's chest over thirty years, and the garlic stink stayed, and the burned knuckles stayed, and the white flares at every gate stayed, and the lamplighters went round at dusk with their poles exactly as before.

The burial society's books show fewer names after about 1890. There is a standing argument about whether it was the new draught, or the younger men, or the caustic wash being made up weaker. Nobody thanked anybody. A mercy that can be attributed to a draught becomes an improvement, and an improvement is a thing a people owns.

---

**The grikes.**

The gardeners go down on ropes with a lamp on their chest into the wet green two-foot jungle, and they are small and wiry and slightly separate and they marry each other, and their superstition is that a grike will close.

They cut marks in the rock to measure it. They have always cut marks. Over sixty years the marks stopped converging.

That is the whole of it: no voice, no promise, no light in the fissure. A measurement, found by the people whose job it is to measure, and left for them to make of it what they liked. They are still separate. They still marry each other. That was never fear. That was a people.

It gave them no soil. The tomatoes in the lead trough are somebody's pride, and the Seeder is a necessary devil, and it is not for a visitor to decide what a place should find holy.

---

**The question.**

The circle beside the name. The Hush-born, who grow up hearing and never entirely belong — and the power, having read everything, found the precise shape of their exclusion, which nobody had written down because nobody could see it from inside.

They have no last sound. They never choose. And therefore they alone have nothing to describe, at the hip, in the dark, to someone they trust, for sixty years. Everyone on the Sough has one sound and can give it. The circle-marked have all sounds and therefore none, and are outside the one conversation the whole plateau holds in common.

So it found a man of forty in the sixth town, hearing, marked, and stood very close to him in an empty arcade where the tufa was freshly shaved and the lamps were lit and nobody had stood in forty years, and signed small:

*Which one will you keep?*

He did not understand the question. Then he did, and it took him three months, and what he chose was his wife setting a cup down well.

Which is a sound only a person of the Sough would choose, and he knew it, and that was the point, and he had it for forty years afterward and gave it to anyone who asked.

It spread the way things spread there, along the threads, by touch. It is considered slightly odd. Then it was not. The surveyors argued for a decade about what mark to put beside the circle, and then stopped arguing, and now there is a second mark, and the ledger has both.

The power instituted nothing. It asked one question, once, at the hip, to one man. That is the largest thing it did.

---

There was a cost it did not foresee, and it did not hide it.

The floor carries. A private channel spelled into stone is readable by anyone with trained soles within a mile, and the Sough has nine thousand people with trained soles, and the last privacy of the place — small cant, close together, out in the roaring quarters where nobody can see you — now leaks downward. Lovers found this out first, as lovers do. There is an etiquette being built for it, badly, and a faction that wants the long spelling forbidden, and a bitter case in the drift court that is really about something else.

It did not settle that either. It had made a possibility and left the labor, and the labor includes the mistakes. A world with no new problems in it is a world that has stopped.

---

The rest of what it did was not repair.

It sat in the kept rooms. There are houses where the silence arrives exactly on schedule and there is nobody left to take it: a swept floor, a lit lamp, a good dark sign wall, a chair, and the thread lying across the boards as predicted in a document signed by a woman dead in the last century. Someone comes twice a week to dust. This is not considered eerie. It is considered *kept*.

It sat in the chair. It took the lee. It was, for a while, the one for whom the room had been kept, which is not a thing that can be given to a place from outside and is not a thing anyone there would have thought to ask for.

And it went out along the followers' threads, three streets into the lit and screaming immaculate streets, to the doorways where nothing lives yet, and stood in the future with them.

There was a grandmother who took her granddaughter out to a doorway in an empty arcade to show her where her mother would cook in eleven years, and signed, *feel how flat the floor is here, they've done it properly*, and the girl put her palm on the warm tufa.

Something had been standing in that doorway a long time. When they came it stepped out sideways into the weight, three feet, so that they could have the lee, because that is the manners of the place and it had learned them.

The girl did not notice. Nobody notices. That is what the etiquette is for.

---

In the Ledger of the Note, in the years of the anomaly, there is a line in a small hand that no notary remembers writing. The pitch is recorded correctly. The threads are recorded correctly. Beneath them, where there should be nothing, there is a drawing of a hand laid flat and pressed down, the way you would settle a lid.

It is not a word. Everyone knows what it means.

If you are ever on the Sough at dusk, walking the thread with the lamps coming on and the mist rising warm out of the shafts, you may see two figures a hundred yards off in a lit arcade, motionless, faces almost touching, signing at the hip in the roaring empty quarter where all the private life of the place goes.

One of them is not from here.

Keep walking.
