# The Amendment

## The first thing it did was wrong

There was a woman in a leaf room off the Over who was dying badly and could not sleep, and the gland in the pipe above her hammock had begun to climb — that thin rising note the signs have a word for and no spoken language does. It is a warning. It is also, at seventy-one decibels, under the leaf-surf, almost the only pitched sound in the Culm, and it was keeping her awake.

The power that had noticed this place laid a finger on the note and held it true.

She slept nine nights. The chord on the eastern third was beautiful for eleven days. On the twelfth the gland let go and two-bar steam found a press-hand named Ost in a way eleven inches wide, and he lived, and was never able to work the shaft side again, and the town did what the town does: it went looking for the lie.

They looked for a boy who had improved something. They walked the round with a hand on every tin. They found the true note of each and no fault in any of them, and the chord was still wrong, and there was no one to beat, and this was the part the power could not undo — four hundred and thirty people who could not locate the beautiful lie because it had been told by something that was not in the book.

It learned the law from the inside, which is the only way the Culm teaches anything. *The chord is not for beauty.* It gave the woman back her nine nights if it could have; it could not; it kept the debt.

It has never once improved anything since.

## The rate

It perceived the whole thing at once, which is its nature and its temptation: two hundred and thirteen years behind and eighty years of planted futures ahead, the rooms specified for women whose grandmothers are not yet born, all of it laid out flat like a page. From up there the ending is visible. From up there you can see the salt arrive.

Then it looked at the girder on the Combing Floor. A whip once. A metre through now. Three or four millimetres of girth a year, patient as a fingernail, thickening on its underside because that is where the load is, fed on the founders and their children.

And it understood that it had come to a place where the correct speed had already been found, without help, by people who could not have explained it, and that anything it did faster than four millimetres a year would be a spark in a town of resin and cellulose.

The worst word here is *quick*. It consented to be slow. This took it eleven years, which is one coppice, and it counts that as the first thing it did right.

## What it declined, and why

**The ears.** It could give them back. It worked out what that would cost in the second coppice and stopped. To restore hearing at eighty-four decibels you must first quiet the Floor; to quiet the Floor you must draw the clutch and leave it drawn; and an unscheduled silence brings all four hundred and thirty upright in their hammocks with their hearts going before anyone has thought about it. Silence is the alarm. There is no version of the gift that is not also the alarm, forever.

And under the ears there is the rest: Sedder with a foot of greased ironwood between his teeth, hearing the drip chorus through his jaw better than the apprentices do, nineteen glands in a decade that nobody else could find. The right-sided geometry, the good side of every doorway, the canted benches. Mouth-to-good-ear confession in full view of ninety people. Courtship four inches away in plain sight, invisible. Two men destroying each other at the shaft side while the hall reads only their faces. Sixty household languages, unrecorded, dying with their households, made by marriages across shifts. The dead named by their labour a hundred times a day, absently, by hands.

It could not find a way to give them their ears that did not also take their privacy, their diagnostic, their marriages and their grandmothers. It left the ears.

**The corners.** It wanted to fill them very much. It is a benevolent thing and the empty eaves were the first detail it could not bear: a warm wet crowded place with nothing living in it, no fruit, no seed, no moth at the lamps, no web in any corner.

But the emptiness is the immunity. One hundred and eleven trunks, one genotype, grafted until the seams disappeared. A borer beetle in that stand is not a pest; it is the end of the world in eleven years, and there is no second stand. The corners are empty because emptiness is why the pressure mains have not been replaced in ninety years. Webs are fuel. Fruiting is a different tree. And the heron stands on the bund at slack water and does not come in, and half the Over watches it every time, and there is a sign for it used for nothing else — and a town with birds in it has no use for that sign.

It left the corners empty. It watches the heron with them. This is not nothing; it is just not repair.

**The seed.** It could make the stock fertile in a season. It could end the glass-house, the brushes, the children under glass, the public event.

Till pollinated eleven florets at the last flower. She was seven. There is a whip in the north nursery with her name on a lead tag and an entry that says what it will be, and she goes out to look at it sometimes, and she is fourteen and has begun to be curious about who will sleep under it. Every distinction that anyone here has, they have because they were a brush-child at a particular flower. Fertility would take the flower, and the flower is where the names come from.

It did not take the flower. It found another way in, and the other way is slower and can be refused, and both of those are the point.

**The wall.** It could hold the sea. It could put a hand's breadth on the bund every year instead of every ten and end the browning at the seaward tips inside a generation.

But the raising is the only festival, and it is the mechanism, and a wall built in a year is a quick thing that nobody planted. And out on the seaward side, entry by entry, one clerk's small even hand at a time, the stock has been being changed for a hundred and fifty years — two lineages bred for salt, only one of them green, only one of them aware. Take away the salt and you stop the breeding. Save them and they arrive at the next thing with nothing learned and nothing planted.

**Being known.** This was the largest temptation and the one it took longest to put down, because it dressed itself as kindness. To be known would be to be *believed*, and belief is quick. It would be an explanation that stops the checking. It would be, precisely and unarguably, a beautiful lie — the one aesthetic crime here, the semitone flat because it sounded better in the chord.

So it declined to be beautiful. It arranged to be a nuisance instead. Vess finds it a nuisance when parents name a child something he did not allow for; that is the register it aimed for. It wanted an entry, not a shrine.

**The singing.** In the nine minutes they shout, because nobody can recalibrate, and they sing unaccompanied and out of tune at full volume, having spent their lives in a noise that eats pitch, and they believe they sound magnificent.

It can hear pitch. It is the first thing in two hundred and thirteen years that could tell them.

It did not tell them. It learned the words.

## What it did: the mud

Ninety kilometres inland there is a river with a bend in it, and on the outside of the bend the flood scours, and the scour goes out to sea and is lost, and has been going out to sea and being lost for longer than the Culm has existed.

It waited forty years for a flood of the right size, and put one fallen tree in the right place, and the bend began to move.

That is the whole of it. The scour now goes over the bank on the spring floods instead of down the channel, and the sedge takes it, and the marsh accretes, and thirty kilometres of standing water is rising at something over a millimetre a year against four millimetres of subsidence and the wedge coming up the channels. It is not enough. It is three hundred years, which is enough for everything currently in the book, and for the book after that, and for the one after that.

Nothing in the Culm looks saved. The seaward tips still brown. The bund still goes up a hand's breadth every ten years with a festival, and nobody under thirty thinks of it as an emergency, and the children are still taken out to the margin at seven and made to stand there until they are bitten. The ledger for the browning is still kept. It has not been closed and it should not be closed.

It did not tell them about the bend. To tell them would be to let them stop raising the bund, and the raising is not a defence, it is a people. It has bought them the only thing they know how to spend, which is time, and it has arranged for them not to know they have it.

It knows what this makes it. The sign for it is one hand brushing the other sleeve. *Dry.* A foreigner. A lie you have not checked. It has accepted the name.

## What it did: the father

The true knife is not the salt. The salt is being handled, slowly, by two lineages, one of which knows. The knife is the clone: one genotype, sterile, a hundred and eleven trunks that are one trunk, sealed against everything by callus and by the absence of insects, and one rust that can eat it would eat all of it, and there would be nothing left to graft from.

The forced flower does not help. A child with a brush crossing a clone with itself is a ceremony, not a breeding. The book has been selecting for salt-tolerance for a hundred and fifty years among siblings that are the same animal. It has been working, in the way that a callus works. It will not work against a rust.

So it brought a father. Not fertility — a stranger. In a barge-load of sedge-cut, among the ordinary rubbish of the marsh, one seed from a wild stand upriver: salt-blooded, seeding itself, fruiting, with insects in it and birds above it and two hundred generations of argument in its embryo that no clerk has ever audited.

It put the seed where Bel would find it. Bel is seventy-one and has been the best grafter here for forty years and every living hook in the leaf rooms is hers; four hundred and thirty people sleep tonight on callus she made when she was thirty. Her hands shake at rest and are steady on the knife. She has no left ear at all and reads the mouth beautifully. She knew in a second that it was not their stock, and her whole art is making two things become one, and she is going to die before any of it is used.

She planted it anyway. Outside the bund. Out in the salt, on the marsh, where the tide can take it and where nothing it carries can come in past the gate — quarantine by water, the same water that kills a mosquito larva in minutes. It is not grafted to anything. It will not be grafted to anything for eighty years.

Because the risk is real and it is not small: the wild thing may bring a borer, a rust, a rot that the Leaven has never met. It has offered them the only cure for their one fatal weakness and the cure may kill them. So it offered it at the tempo of the book, where it can be checked, and refused, and checked again.

They have refused it twice. The first flower it was brought to, the glass-house men would not have it under the glass; the second, the audit found the plot's growth unaccounted for and cut it back to a stump, correctly, by the rule that harvest equals growth. It did not intervene either time. It had learned about the gland.

It is on its third offering. There is a trunk out in the wind past the bund, four centimetres through, that is not the clone, with a lead tag bearing a child's name from a flower eighty years ago. Half the Over watches it the way they watch the heron. They have started using the heron sign for it — the sign used for nothing else — and the sign means, as nearly as it can be put in a dry language: *the thing that comes to the edge and does not come in.*

If they cut it in the fourth coppice it will be cut. It will bring another. It has the time; it bought the time; it is not going to spend it hurrying them.

## What it took

There is a high thin whine above the leaf-surf, left over from the presses, that every person here has heard since adolescence. It is what remains when the clutch is drawn and the town falls to fifty-eight. It is the closest thing they have to a shared secret and they do not discuss it, because to discuss it would be to admit that it never stops.

It could not remove it without unmaking the floor beneath the gap, and it would not have removed it anyway; it has learned what happens when it holds a note true.

So it did the only thing it could think of that was not an improvement. It took the note into itself. Not out of them — into itself, at the same pitch, at the same height above the surf, permanently, irrevocably, with no mechanism for setting it down.

It alters no tin, no rate, no chord. It changes nothing on the Floor. Nobody's grandmother is named differently. It is not a repair and it is not a gift and there is no entry that could describe it, and it is the whole of what it wanted, which was that the thing should not be carried alone.

There are four hundred and thirty-one.

And it sings on the change, out of tune, at full volume, unheard.

## The entry

It asked Vess for work, because it had worked out by then that a gift here is an insult and a trade is a courtesy. Rot is employed in the Culm; it has shifts and a supervisor; the arrangement is not a metaphor to anyone who works there. Everything that lives here pays in labour, including the green mother, including the brown one, including the people, who sweep, and who are already going in.

Vess keeps the book in lampblack bound with the town's own resin varnish, because ink runs here and paper goes limp in a week. His hand is very small and very even. Half his entries are for people who do not exist. He did not find this one remarkable. He asked its trade.

It did the long push of the barge-pole, which is the labour of bringing, and then it held its hands still — which is the thing taught to children who can keep them quiet under a table for an hour, the local poker face, the skill worth money, the not-publishing.

So its name is *pole*, with the still-hands flick, and its name means approximately *the one who brings and does not say*, and it is made with the hands a hundred times a day by people who do not know they are making it, exactly as they make the names of the dead.

The entry reads:

> *Pole, hands-still. No shift. Room: north-west, second leaf room, west of the cistern. Hook: limb 41, to be offered the shackle in the fourth coppice. Collar: seaward, third. Entered this flower. Load: not stated.*

The room is four centimetres through. It will never sleep there. The jar for it will be carried empty at a feeding by children who will be told whose it is, and they will not mind much; it is understood as being put back into the walls.

Vess found the *load: not stated* a nuisance and wrote it anyway, because the alternative was to leave a line blank, and he has never left a line blank.

## Coda

The woman who will sleep in the room eighty years out has not been born, and neither has her mother, and neither has the child who will pollinate the flower that makes her beam. Out on the north-west side, in the wind, in the surf-sound of a hundred thousand leaves, there is a limb the thickness of a wrist that has not been offered the shackle yet, and when it is, it will lay callus over the iron within a day and seal it within a week.

The stock closes over anything left on it long enough. It has left the note there.

In the fourth coppice the audit will find that limb thicker than any limb with an empty room has a right to be, and will not be able to account for it, and will write nothing, because the balance holds and there are worse nuisances. And the wood will go on doing the only thing it knows how to do, which is to thicken on its underside, three or four millimetres a year, patient as a fingernail.

Because that is where the load goes.
