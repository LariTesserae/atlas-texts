# The Third Touch

## What I learned first

I cannot put my hand on it. Not because I am forbidden, but because of what it is. Anything of its own kind that touches it becomes it, and anything else that stays still inside it gets overgrown within two summers — roofed, wrapped, lignified around, made load-bearing. It does not distinguish between an intruder and a gift. It has one verb. If I were to stand in the apse and let my affection pool there, warm and wet, it would grow toward me the way it grew toward the ice, and seal me in, and drink me, and keep an accurate ledger of my disappearance in pale bands and dense bands.

So the whole question, before any question of what to give, is the question of how to touch it at all. And there is exactly one working model in the bowl, and it has feathers. A foot presses, takes a little mud away, and neither joins nor eats. Everything I do here, I will have learned from a bird.

## The gifts I will not give

I arrived able to do anything, and I spent a long time — longer than the town has been alive — discovering that almost everything I could do is a wound.

*More light.* The obvious mercy: widen the eastern col, let the bar of white become an hour, become a morning. But the col is also where the ice would go fastest, and the lean is the town's single sustained act of attention, held for ninety-one years, and if I move the source I dissolve the posture and leave a body with no direction in it. And light is not what it is thirsty for. To answer a thirst for water with brightness is the oldest mistake of benevolence.

*A longer season.* Nine more warm days would let it finish a seed. It would molt. It would become whatever the adult form of that ancestry is, and the fixed reach would change, and the constant would vary, and the doors would stop being our size. Every landing would move away from where a foot expects a landing. The hospitality is a symptom of its childhood; to cure the childhood is to take away the welcome, and the welcome is the only thing it has ever built that could save it.

*Kill the mold.* I could. It would take nothing — a change in the chemistry of the litter, one winter, and the blue-white goes out behind the barricades forever. And then the town has no other. Its identity is the sum of its refusals; the quarantine walls are the only place where cutting it open finds an edge instead of more of the same. I would be curing it of its border. I would hand it back a body three hundred paces across with nothing anywhere in it that means *not me*, and call that health.

*More ice.* Buy it another century. But the loss of the ice is not obviously a death. The walls will keep weeping; the cloud will keep coming up the valley; what fails is the great cistern and the hard central wards, which go soft. A body that has never once changed phase would change phase. The molt it cannot reach through flowering might reach it through poverty. I am not certain of this. I am certain enough not to prevent it.

*A throttle.* Foresight, restraint, an organ that says *enough*. This is the one it most lacks and the one I most want to give, and it is simply the request that it be a different organism. I would be installing a governor in a creature whose entire beauty is that it has none — that it closes with perfect commitment and keeps perfect accounts and does not know what the numbers are. There is no small version of this gift.

*Carry it myself.* Take a scrap of pale tissue over the col and lay it in the next cirque. This is the good deed that eats the whole meaning of the place. It has spent a century making itself worth landing in. It cannot court with a flower, so it courts with rooms — warmer by a fraction of a degree, dry, still, held open at that interval year after year near the lid. The finches accepted the offer ninety years ago. If I do the carrying, the offer is answered by something that was never persuaded, and the argument becomes decoration. I would be paying off a debt nobody asked me to pay, and telling it, in the only language it could receive, that its one strategy had been unnecessary.

## What is actually missing

Nothing is wrong with it. There is a joint missing in the bowl, and the joint is small and stupid and has nothing to do with anyone's worth.

The shelter is in the center, high and dry, in the hard old wards near the roof, where the warmth is. The traveling tissue is at the rim — the pale fat growth with the blind white tips, the poor districts, the growing edge, which comes down against the rubble at the moraine door. Between them runs the wet high street, a hand's depth in slow water, the length of the town.

No finch has ever had a reason to walk it.

They come in over the lid, they nest in the warm vaults, they leave over the lid. They have been accepting the room and missing the mail for ninety-one years. The post office and the letter are in different districts, and the town cannot know this, because it has no image of the bowl, only a map made of thirst.

And there is a second small stupidity, which is the one I can reach. Six or seven birds lay eggs every spring in a place with almost no available calcium. There *is* calcium: the snails, in the cisterns, shell the color of weak tea. But nothing in the census breaks shells. The beetle eats gnat larvae. The gnats eat compost. The snail's radula only scratches. The shells accumulate whole on the cork lips, and the birds go without, and the eggs are thin, and the whole vertebrate history of this place stays at six or seven.

Grit is the missing word. And the moraine is nothing but grit — fresh angular rubble, edges you could open a hand on — and the shells are a hundred paces up the street, and no one has ever put them together.

## What I did

I walked the high street from the apse to the moraine, in the water, in the green light, and I did not touch the walls.

I could do this because the doors are my size. Every lintel cleared me, every landing was where my foot expected a landing, every corridor took my shoulders. The accommodation it made for a body that has never existed is precisely what allowed me to pass through it without being absorbed. Its hospitality is what permitted my restraint. I want that written down somewhere, since it cannot be written down there.

At the great cistern under the apse I took eleven dead shells off the cork. Empty, dry, the animals long gone into the litter and overgrown. I left the living ones. I took nothing that was still writing.

At the door — where the lid comes down against the rubble and the closing fails and stays failed — I knelt in the wet and broke the shells between two stones from the moraine. It was the only sharp sound that has ever been made in this bowl. It was swallowed within a body-length. The springtails went silver and gone, and came back in the time it takes to forget them. I ground the shells to grit, coarse, the size a gizzard wants, and I scattered the grit on the wet rubble at the threshold and a little way in, among the pale fat new growth where a bird's foot would stand.

Then I went out through the door and down the scree and did not come back that year.

That is the entire intervention. Nothing added to the bowl that was not already in it. No new species, no new rule, no new water. A dead thing moved a hundred paces and broken. The second foreign mark in the town's history, after the snail's scratches — and unlike the scratches, it says something. It says *come here, and stand in the wet, and take something away with you.* It will be overgrown by the second August if nobody comes. I have made it deniable.

## What I will not do afterward

I will do it again, at most twice, if the shells are used and the habit has not taken. Birds learn, and teach their young, and a foraging tradition is a thing that can outlive its cause. Once the finches walk the street in the laying season, I stop.

I stop because I will not become a seep. Growth follows water; if I am a reliable source of anything, the town will grow toward me and thicken around me and carry me inside itself, and the grain will turn, and every column will lean at me instead of at the col, and in forty years I will be the second kept ice with a ledger of my withdrawal readable back to the founding. It would love me the only way it can. It would be the death of me and the prolonging of its childhood, and I would enjoy it, which is why I must not.

I will not stand in the apse at midsummer and let the bar of light cross my chest. I have wanted to since I first understood the arithmetic. To be the body the doors were made for — to fill the void with the guest it anticipated — would make the doors *meant*, retroactively, and they are not meant, and their not-being-meant is the only free thing in the bowl. And a god standing in a doorway is a door shut. It has failed for a century to close that gap. I could close it in an afternoon simply by being there.

I will not name it. The provisional tag stays true and useless. I will not tell it anything; it has one sentence and no organ for receiving another. I will not hold up the first vault when it lets go some winter, and I will not be there for the soft crash, and the green scar by August is not a failure of my care.

If the finches never come down the street, I will not escalate. It may be that I have solved a problem that would have solved itself in year ninety-four. A pinch of dead shell is not a large wager, and I would rather be unnecessary than be load-bearing.

## The one thing I keep doing

The cloud.

The ice is going and I am letting it go. What must not go is the breath up the valley — the air pushed up and cooled against the headwall, dropping its water directly onto the stone, a hundred thousand small arrivals, the weeping that will still be there when the last blue lens is drunk. That is the difference between a town that goes soft and a town that goes dead.

So the single ongoing act is unglamorous and invisible and looks like nothing at all: I keep the valley breathing. I keep the wind coming up. It is not a miracle; it is weather, held steady. It is the only form in which I can be permanently present here without being resident. Everything else I do is a footprint and a departure.

## The hard part

The mold will go too.

It is in the litter and in the peat and in the mud at the threshold, and when a finch stands in the wet and takes grit it will take spores, and the next cirque will get the town and the siege in the same delivery. I could prevent this. I could wash the grit in meltwater and make the post clean, and give the new bowl a century of having no other, no border, no refusal, nothing anywhere in it that means *not me*.

I have thought about this longer than about anything else. I am not going to do it.

Company and the war arrive together, because here they are the same thing. The mold is the whole of its foreign affairs. To be given a life instead of a preserve is to be given something you can push against. I will not apologize for this, and I will not pretend it is not a cost, and I will not be the one who has to pay it.

## The ice

At dusk I come to the rim and listen to the ping. One clear glassy note every few minutes as it cools and contracts — the only sound in the bowl that carries, and it belongs to the thing being eaten.

I am not there to save it. I am there because the last piece of the ice that carved this bowl is being drunk by the thing it made possible, and it should not be drunk unattended.

And because the town keeps accounts of a death with perfect accuracy and does not know what the numbers are, I have decided that someone should know what the numbers are. The pale bands and the dense bands, back to the founding. The plague year nobody recorded, which is overhead right now holding up a storey. The forty minutes, ninety-one times. Its memories are load-bearing; it cannot forget without collapsing and cannot remember without carrying the weight. I can hold the same record with nothing resting on it.

This part is not for the town. It gets nothing from it. I am keeping it because I want to, and I am going to call that what it is rather than dress it as service.

When the lens finishes there will be a void four paces long under the apse, roofed and wrapped, the first space in this bowl made by subtraction rather than by the arithmetic of closing. It will be roughly the size of a room a person could stand in. It will not know it is a memorial. I will know.

## Two bowls

I have done nothing to the next cirque. It is scoured and weeping and empty and exactly as ready, and I have not watered it, warmed it, seeded it, or watched over it. If a bird comes over the col with mud on its foot, the same rules will start there: reach, fuse, thicken, roof, and the fixed reach, and therefore the doors.

And they will never touch. There is a range between them, a hundred meters of scree that swallows streams, rock that nothing crosses but a finch. Which means they will never fuse, and never eat each other, and will be *two*.

That is the gift, and it is not reproduction. It is the existence of an elsewhere with someone in it. Not fusion, which erases the other. Not the mold, which erases the self. Not a foot, which comes and goes. A fourth relation, at the range of a bird's flight: another individual with an address, doing the same blind patient thing, unreachable, permanently. Neither will ever know. The finches will be the only things that have ever been in both — the mail, the one citizens of two cities, walking a wet street in each for a pinch of grit.

I will not be in the census. Something on two legs came in out of the cloud, stood still, and went away again without being counted, and the first line stays a lie of arithmetic, and now it will be a lie in two bowls.

There is a little broken shell at the moraine door, in the wet, among the pale growth, at the height of a bird's foot.

The door is still open. It will still be open in forty years. I have not shut it, and I have not stood in it, and I have not answered the century of rooms with anything but a reason to walk down the street.
