It comes in the only way anything comes in here, which is as a load.

It has made itself a body for the occasion, because nothing in this country registers intention. The floor does not know it is being attended to. The floor knows weight, shadow, warmth, and the disturbance of vapour, and nothing else, and so attention here must be physical or it is not attention at all. It kneels on the middle apron. At its knee, thirty-eight. At its face, thin cold air that smells of nothing, and the ears doing what ears do. Its pulse is obnoxious.

And it casts a shadow.

Under a lid that gives light from everywhere, a body is still an obstruction; it takes a bite out of the diffuser. The shadow is soft and only a little dim and a few degrees cool, and on a hot pan a cool patch is a condenser. Vapour finds it. It begins to drip. By the time the power stands up there is a wet patch on the white sinter in the shape of two knees, and the mats will have it by the end of the season, and the snail lines will bend around it for a decade, and somewhere in the next millimetre of the ledger there will be a lamina with a god in it.

It has been here eleven minutes. It goes and stands on the dead rim to think, and this turns out to be the first useful thing it does, though it does not yet know why.

---

It came because of the hum.

Forty kilometres square of peat with a measurable voltage across any hand's span of it, a slow telluric drift, current running continuously through sediment at load for two hundred thousand years without a shift change. From outside, that is a signal. It crossed a great distance to answer a signal.

There is no one humming. It had mistaken a metabolism for a voice.

So it looks for the being it was told lives here, and it looks properly, without deciding in advance what would count.

The country itself: a heat engine taking eighty degrees of difference and spending all of it immediately on weather. It has no interior. It has a throughput. To call it a person would be a promotion the power grants for its own comfort, so that loving the place would be easier, and it declines to do that, and finds that loving the place is not harder.

The deep ones, two kilometres down, turning over on a scale of centuries: enormous in mass, skyless, older than the graben they eat. There is more life down there than up here by a margin that would embarrass the forest. But they have no time in which to notice anything. A visit is shorter than their maintenance. Whatever the power does, they will not learn of it.

The kiln bird: nine weeks of arithmetic within a degree and a half, forty thousand shafts, a beak that is a thermometer and a cohort whose sex ratio is a season's ground temperature written down in bodies. Magnificent. Not somebody. An instrument that has learned to keep itself calibrated.

The beetles: tenants, warm for a night, turned out at dawn with pollen on them. They are not at home in the country; they are at home in a room the country rents them.

And in two of the eleven cold mouths, in pools under gnarled trees with epiphytes hanging in beards, a small fish that hangs motionless in the dark with an array of pits along its jaw that read the voltage of the mud. Its family is nine hundred kilometres away and two thousand metres down and separated from it by an ice age. Its whole world is the area of a large room. It is the only animal in the country that can perceive the grid entire, rather than a hand's span of it.

The power leaves the fish for last, and goes to make its mistake.

---

The mistake is small, and it is the smallest kindness available, and it makes it on a bad night in a dry year.

The cold comes off the rim after two and pours across the floor and the frost line goes down to eleven centimetres, which is deeper than it should go, and there are slugs at eight and crickets at four who have read the gradient correctly all their lives and are tonight three centimetres too shallow. The power puts its hand over a hand-span of moss. Nothing more. A hand-span, one night.

Under the hand it stays at nine degrees. Eleven slugs live.

They come up the next evening and the evening after, and they breed, and their descendants inherit a world in which one bad night in a dry year did not take anybody. Two years later the frost goes to eleven centimetres again and takes the whole patch, four thousand animals, and in the morning the rails walk the pans on their absurd toes and eat the casualties, and the population that comes up at dusk is the population that reads the gradient correctly, which is what the population had been before the hand.

The power could have prevented that too. It stands in the silver hours and does not, and watches the rails work, and this is the hardest hour of the visit.

It had not been cruel. It had been inaccurate. Frost here is not a predator in the sense of an enemy; it is the pump that shakes the night's water off the spines into the collars, and it is the proofreader of the only sentence the fauna knows how to say. Eleven slugs saved out of turn was a lie put into that sentence, and the sentence was corrected, at the ordinary price, later, with interest.

It writes nothing down. It has no need to. It has learned the distinction it came without: that hardness and harm are not the same, and that from inside a slug there is no difference between a frost and a theft, and that the whole of wisdom in this place consists of holding that difference while feeling the slug.

---

Then it considers its gifts, one at a time, honestly, the way you would lay out tools you have decided not to use.

Heat. It could give heat; heat is what it has. Add heat and the plume thickens and climbs and the condensation level rises — fifty metres, eighty — and the air at three metres, where the silver hairs do their nucleating, goes clear and dry, and the fog stops forming on the leaves, and the collars stop receiving, and the lens thins, and every root in the country begins to cook. The gift of warmth is the theft of water. The plants harvest their own coolant out of the plume at a particular height, and the height is not a preference, it is the whole arrangement. To warm this floor is to raise the ceiling out of reach.

Water, then. Cold fresh water, the thing the lens is made of, poured generously onto the peat flats. The lens thickens and drowns the thermal highs; the pans go under; the mats go; the flies go; the rails go. The brine dilutes, and the silica it carries falls below saturation earlier and elsewhere, and deposition drops from a millimetre and a bit to less than a millimetre, while the body below goes on cooling and contracting at a millimetre and a bit, and for the first time since before these species were species the floor does not keep itself level. It begins to sink. The sills, which have held through four glacial cycles by growing exactly as fast as the water cuts, are overtopped or undercut, and the basins spill at new points, and the mature drainage — tedious, perfect, one spill per basin, always the same one — becomes a landscape being made again, which is to say a landscape killing everything that had finished adapting to it.

Everything the power has to give is an amount. Everything here is a ratio. The ratios have not changed in eleven thousand years. It cannot touch an amount without touching a ratio, and it cannot touch a ratio without touching all of them, and there is no dial on this machine marked *more*.

End the frost: no. The frost is the pump and the proofreader, and it has just paid four thousand slugs to learn that.

Speed the deep ones: no. Their slowness is not poverty. They are eating the mountain at the rate at which the mountain can be eaten and still be there in a hundred thousand years. To accelerate them is to shorten the country's life in exchange for a liveliness nobody down there wants, in a place with no sky to be lively under.

Sign its name: it could cut a mark in the sinter, and the sinter would take a decade to fill it, because the sinter fills everything at a millimetre and a bit a year, and a mark is not memory here; the memory is laminar. To lay down a distinctive layer, a white line that would say *I came*, it would have to change the chemistry of the water for a season. And everything here drinks the water.

Speak: the air will not carry. The stone will. It could put its hand on a sinter boss and drum, and the drum would go out at three kilometres a second and come up through the feet of every female cricket on the terrace, and through the boots of nothing, because there are no boots. But the grammar available is cricket, and cricket has two sentences, *come* and *danger*, and neither is true. To be heard in this country the power must lie in the only language the country has. It takes its hand off the stone.

The snake finds it once, in the thin dry air before dawn, and strikes at it — not at it, at the hole it makes in the rising vapour, the wake, the brief gap in the curtain. The fangs go through steam. The snake learns nothing. This is the second lesson, and it is cheaper than the first: you cannot be met here. You can only be registered as a disturbance.

---

But there is a crack, and it is not in the ratios.

Everything in this country is self-supplied. The heat comes from below, the water is recycled, the coolant is manufactured by the things that need it, the light is installed by the plume, the level is kept by the balance of two millimetres. It is autarkic to a degree that ought to be impossible at five thousand metres.

Except the cold mouths.

The eleven pockets of tree fern and bearded epiphyte and ice-age animals are not fed by the furnace. They are fed by meteoric water — rain and fog-drip that went into the rim, sank, and came back cold through a fracture in the middle of all this heat. They live on the glacier. They are the one part of the country running on borrowed ice, and they are here because the lowlands warmed once, and they came up, and found a cold spring in a hot floor, and stayed. Two of those pockets contain species that exist nowhere else, in a total area the size of a large room.

The ice is going. Not because of anything the country is doing. Because of what is being done in the lowlands, where the forest used to stand whose pollen is in the ledger, where the desert is now, where there are men with maps and an assay showing a heat flow a hundred times the ordinary generosity of the earth.

That is not hardness. That is harm. And the power, which has decided to give this place nothing, discovers that it owes this place something anyway: not improvement, restitution. Not to make it better than it is. To make it unrobbed.

So it goes back to the rim, where it went to think, and understands at last why that was the useful thing. The rim is glaciated, blue-white, and dead. Fifteen hundred metres of the country with nothing living on it. It is the only surface here where a hand can be put down without being put down on somebody.

It works there. It does not build; there is nothing to build with and nothing that would not be plated over. It keeps. It holds the snow that falls, and the ice beneath the snow, and the cold that goes down the fractures, and it does this in a manner indistinguishable from weather, because weather is the only medium in which anything can be done here at all. The cold it holds arrives forty years later, at a fracture in the middle apron, in a pool the size of a large room, where a fish hangs motionless in the dark.

---

And then it sees the rest of the problem, which is that ice is held by sky.

The rim glacier is a function of the cold above it, and the cold above it is a function of everything: the lowlands, the desert, the industry, the price of a kilowatt, the appetite of a species that has never knelt here and would find its knees too hot. To hold the rim it must hold the sky, and to hold the sky it must go and work everywhere else.

It also sees what the men with maps will do, and how fast, and how completely. A producing field is drawdown. Drawdown drops the lens. The lens is the ecology; every root in the country lives inside it. And a single bore through the fractured lid gathers into one throat the pressure that is currently bled through tens of millions of pores, and the muffler the size of a county becomes a geyser, and the silence — which is not an atmosphere but a structure, which is the reason there are crickets drumming on stone and frogs calling with their throats against the bottom — the silence ends in forty seconds and does not come back like water closing over a stone.

It will not fence the place. Fences plate over. It permits one hole: a core, on a healed crater on a dead terrace at the margin, where nothing lives, taken and read and plugged, and the silica seals it inside a decade and the stratigraphy closes over it like a mouth. Because two hundred metres of interbedded white and black, four glacial cycles of wet centuries and dry ones, every ash fall and every pollen grain of a forest that no longer exists, is the most valuable object on this continent, and the only durable way to save a furnace from being burned is to make its diary famous. It arranges for the science to arrive before the survey. That is all it arranges. It does not tamper with anyone's will; it puts a core in front of them and lets them be the kind of creatures who can read.

The rest of the work is elsewhere, and slow, and against wills, and unglamorous, and takes centuries, and none of it will look like a miracle here. It goes and does it. This is what attention to this country turns out to require: leaving it.

---

Before it goes, it visits the fish.

The pool is dark under the tree ferns and cold in a way that nothing else on the floor is cold. The fish hangs motionless, reading the mud.

The power is a field anomaly. Anything with a body is: ions move, weight on porous silica makes a streaming potential, and there is a slow drift where the mineralised water shorts one redox cell against another. The flatworm turns along the lines. The snails realign their grazing. The fish does neither. It holds still and it registers, along its jaw, in pits, that something has come into the country which is not growing, dissolving, or being deposited.

The power waits for a response, and then understands that it has been waiting for the one thing this place does not produce. It produces weather. It has never made a reply in two hundred thousand years and has never needed to. Being read is not less than being answered. It is only quieter, and the quiet here is engineered, in the sense that a lined room is engineered.

It does not bless the fish. A blessing is an amount. It lets the fish be a fish, in a world the size of a large room, nine hundred kilometres from its family, for as long as the ice holds — which will be longer now, and will not be forever, and it does not pretend otherwise, even to itself.

Two things hang motionless in the dark, reading each other's fields. Then one of them leaves.

---

It can see the end, too. It can read the remaining heat in the body two kilometres down and put a number on it, and the number is large and it is not infinity. The flux will fall; the plume will thin; the condensation level will come down until it is lying on the ground, which is to say there will be no lid; the hairs will have nothing to nucleate; the collars will dry; the lens will go; the pans will cool; the lamination will stop. The outlet sill will hold its level for a while and then be a wall in an ordinary alpine desert with the best archive in the world under it.

It could refuel that. It has the means. It does not, and the reason is not reverence, or not only. A country that is kept is a ward, and the entire dignity of this one is that it has never been kept — that nothing here is owed anything, that the beetles pay rent and the bird pays for her furnace and the plants manufacture the thing that saves them, and that the arrangement stopped being clever a long time ago and became simply the shape of the place. To underwrite it would be to convert a works into an exhibit.

And it is not extinction. The stain is the surface expression. The bulk is underground and was underground before there was a graben to be expressed into, eating the mountain in the dark at the rate the mountain can be eaten, with no weather and no sky and no need of either. When the lid fails the country does not die. It goes back to where most of it has always been. The refugees were always a crossing; a lifeboat is not a permanent address; the ice will hold for five centuries more than it otherwise would, and then the lowland animals in the cold mouths will go the way of all crossings, and the deep ones will not notice, because a century is less than their maintenance.

The power finds it can bear this. It finds that it was never being asked to prevent it.

---

Here is what the visit comes to, in the ledger, which is the only place anything here is written down.

One lamina, near the top of a white band, with a knee-shaped anomaly in it, indistinguishable in a hundred thousand years from a wet season. One healed core hole on a dead terrace. A run of slightly whiter lines recording centuries in which the rim held better than the sky alone would have managed — and no reader will ever be able to say why, and the power has taken care that they cannot, because a signature here would have to be written in the water.

And one habit, learned from the ceiling.

The lid rises at noon and settles at dusk and dissolves entirely after midnight, and the power came to understand that this is not a failure of the lid. It is the office. Every night the cold pours off the rim and pushes the whole plume out the gorge and the sky opens and the appalling hard stars come out and everything not touching the ground freezes, and the frost is the harvest, and the frost is the proofreader, and for four hours the country is silver because something that had been holding a warm even shadowless plane over it all day let go.

To be the benevolent power of this place is to be a condensation level that knows when to stop condensing. It learned to leave at two in the morning.

It kneels once more before it goes, on the dead terrace, where nothing lives. Its knees are too hot and its face is too cold and its body is loud in its ears, and the floor goes on quietly making itself level beneath it, a millimetre and a bit a year, which is the size of the work and the size of the gift and the size, in the end, of the god.

Then it stands, and takes its shadow with it, and goes down the escarpment with the cold air, and out through the gorge, and into the world, where the work is.
