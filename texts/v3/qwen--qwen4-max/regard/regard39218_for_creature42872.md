## Pending

---

It came down the stair.

There were other ways in. It could have arrived in the middle of the floor beside the brass gauge without disturbing the water, and it understood that this would be the end of the conversation before it began. So it went up to the crack and put its hand on black iron that has not rusted in ninety years and counted the steps, because counting is what you do with a thing that somebody else made. Ninety-one. At forty it stopped and let the air change. It waited out the gray coin at its back until the coin was gone.

It did not need a lamp. It lit one anyway, and hooded it, and held it low, at the height of a shin, and walked into a town of illuminated feet.

Then it stood in the amber and could not determine what it had come for.

Something had drawn it. It was not sure what. It ran through the candidates the way Sill runs through the four generations of numbers. Sixty people, or one animal with sixty bodies and two hundred names, which hears a cup set down four hundred paces away and knows how tired the woman is. Or the circle — water, root, bead, eel, shrimp, snail, bucket, light — which has no waste in it anywhere and nothing to reconcile because nothing was ever separated, and which is therefore either a machine or a metabolism, and there is no test that distinguishes those. Or the room: a weeping wall for a skin, a chain-tick for a pulse and a seventy-one-minute revolution for the slower heart underneath it, a hum at the edge of hearing that its residents miss when they leave, kidneys the length of an eyelash. The town says *kidneys*. It is not a figure of speech. It is an organ.

Or the dead, still growing, in the dark, at the pace of a fingernail.

It took a season to decide, and what it decided was this: the being in Nine is the thing that says *pending*. Not the people, not the water, not the shrimp. The patience that knows it is a patience and has written itself a line item and carries it forward in a different hand each generation. There are not many of those. It had come because it recognized one, the way you recognize, in a crowd, someone who is waiting for the same train.

---

The first thing it did in Nine was move a moth.

The moth was crossing the mess floor at the speed of a minute hand and a felt shoe was coming down in its path, and it put two fingers beside the moth and shifted it six inches, on the same heading, out of the way of the foot and nowhere else, which is the law.

It could have given the moth its wings back. It thought of this and was ashamed of the thought, briefly, the way you are ashamed of reaching for a thing on a high shelf in someone else's kitchen. The moth is the only creature in Nine that has changed its mind. Of everything in that room it was the one thing the power was certain was a being, and it set it down beside a table leg and left it entirely alone.

---

Here is what it did not do.

It did not drain the drowned hall. Four generations of commercial stringing stand down there thickened and furred, some beads the size of a fist, and it could have lifted the water off them in the time a drop takes to fall. It would have handed the town a fortune and taken away everybody. The largest spheres down there are the oldest people. There is no version of that gift that is not a robbery conducted in the dark by someone who did not check what was on the shelves.

It did not raise the light. Above a certain brightness the shrimp stop spawning, and the town drinks through its fauna, and sixty people would be carrying buckets up ninety-one steps. That is the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the least of it. Under the hooded amber a man knows his neighbor's calves for thirty years and her cough through fog in sleep, and once a week in the steam on the fourth day they look at each other's faces and it is an event. Give them light and you have not given them sight. You have taken away hearing, and made faces cheap, and abolished a festival that fifty-two times a year nobody has ever called one.

It did not speed the beads. A marble in a year instead of thirty, and a life would stop being a diameter and become a number, and a man of forty-nine going past his own thread would put his thumb against a bead that meant nothing, and forty thousand threads would produce four hundred kilograms a year, and the office above would notice, and the requisition would grow, and Nine would be a mine with a beautiful story attached.

It did not remove the snails from the buckets. Oss has heard the chain nearly stop twice and both times he was in the pump-house before the second tick failed, and he is paid in iron and felt to be the one person in Nine who does not sleep soundly. A town whose only catastrophe is silence requires a man who has heard the edge of it. It left the snails where they were and let him go on not sleeping.

It did not warm the room, or fill the eleven other holes, or send six hundred people down the crack to occupy Mera's chairs. *Full* and *crowded* are different conditions and Nine is the first one, and the difference is the whole achievement.

And it did not find out about Grace.

This was the hardest, and it was the only act it performed on itself. It could have looked. Looking would have taken nothing, cost nothing, disturbed no water, and no one would ever have known that it had been done. Somewhere there is a fact about a boy who went up ninety-one steps forty-one years ago, and the fact is available, and a bead cannot tell you whether the person it belongs to is drowned or married or living in a town with a sky. That not-knowing is the only room in Nine where a person is permitted to be simply *elsewhere* instead of dead. It is a door. It is the only door. The power declined to know, and went on declining, and goes on declining now, and this is the closest it has come to prayer.

Once, standing at the low-water line, it held the other thing in its mind for the length of time a drop takes to fall from the ceiling to the eastern cistern: twelve chambers lit, six thousand people, the ceiling *seen*, the drowned floors dry and harvested and hung with a hundred and forty years of marbles, Grace at the turn of the stair with his hand on the iron. It wanted this. It examined the wanting and found that most of it was for itself. Then the drop landed, and it let the whole vision go, and it has not taken it out again.

---

It went up with Bell, once, as a hired man from above.

The crate stayed on Bell's back. That is not a superstition, it is the job, and there was no version of help that involved carrying it for him. It walked beside him with nothing in its hands and it got the headache.

It had arranged to get the headache. It had lived in Nine four years by then, which is nothing, and long enough for a hum at the edge of hearing to become the sound a body makes when it is home. It could have gone up exempt. It chose not to be exempt, because you cannot be a neighbor in a place like that from outside it, and because it wanted to know what the sixty know, which is that the upper world's silence is not silence but a roaring absence, and that light arriving from above is read by the body as falling. It stood in the field at the top with its skull aching and the sky coming from everywhere and understood, for the first time, that it had built something enormous and unhooded and that people were living under it.

Then, because it was a foreigner and foreigners are permitted bad manners, it asked Bell what the sun was like.

Nobody in Nine has ever asked him. It is rude, the way asking a widow about the funeral is rude. He stopped walking. He was fifty-something years old and he had been up four times and had never once been asked, and he stood in the grass with an empty crate-frame at his feet and worked out how to say it.

*You can't hear where it lands,* he said.

And then, after a while: *There's nothing it isn't on.*

The power did not argue. It knew the sun very well. It had never heard it complained of before, and it found that it agreed, and it was glad in a way it had no organ for. They went back down. It was disliked for the rest of that afternoon by two people who had heard about the question, and it accepted being disliked, and it has never repeated the answer to anyone, and there is no record of it anywhere, which is what Bell needed.

---

What it did do, it did below.

Below the drowned hall, below the lowest thread hung by the knot called the return, there is more hill. The town cannot survey it. You cannot lower a lamp into a place you have no reason to think exists. The power could look, and it looked, and found a pocket: not a chamber, not one of the twelve, nothing so grand — a void the size of a bathhouse, with air in it, and a ceiling that weeps.

It did not open it. It did not drain anything into it. It did not tell anyone.

It let one drop fall in it.

That is the entire intervention. One drop, in a place that had never made a sound, arriving through water and stone into a room of ears from a direction that has no name. Not eastern. Not any of them. Underneath.

Oss heard it. Of course it was Oss. His office is to notice a stoppage, and he is therefore the only person in Nine equipped to notice a surplus. He stood in the pump-house for eleven minutes to be certain it was not the chain, and then he went and found Sill, and Sill wrote the number in the book, and beside the number she put a small mark.

There is one other mark in that book. It is forty-one years earlier, in a leaning hand, beside the number for the morning a man stood at the turn of the stair and shouted his son's name up the crack. Two marks in four generations. Everything else is the breath, faithful, seven and five, lip to lip.

Then the town had to find out, and to find out somebody must go down to the low-water line with a cord and a hooded lamp, and there is only one man who does that, and so the town had to ask him.

They asked Wick.

It was the first thing anybody had said to him in forty-nine years. It was a request. Nine has no word for forgiveness and has never needed one; it has requests, and it has the high-water grammar that only offers a thing instead of asserting it, and it used that grammar, in the second month, at a volume that would not carry three feet outside. He was sixty-three years old and he stood in the amber with his feet lit and he said yes.

He went down. He lowered the lamp. He looked at the dead for as long as he liked, which is what he does, and which is his, and which nobody has ever taken from him. He heard it again. He came up and said one word.

*Under.*

That is a drip-line now. It is the first new name in Nine in a hundred and forty years, and there is a child not yet conceived who will be called it, and she will be able to find her own name in the dark by ear from anywhere in the room, and her name will mean *there is more hill below us than we thought.*

The power knows what it has handed over. Not a rescue. A fork. The threads can be lowered further and the dead can go deeper and the drowned hall can be extended downward for as long as the hill has water in it — or the pocket can be opened, and the water given somewhere to go, and the mean stopped, and the fourth terrace made unnecessary. And if the water goes then the lagoon goes, and the punt, and the five months, and the workshops moving up a level, and everybody living one flight closer to everybody, and the short names, and the faces, and almost every marriage in Nine.

It knows which it would choose. It has not said. It will not say. It gave a town that has had nothing but maintenance for four generations its first decision, and its first fear — the children will be frightened, and the adults who have been quietly working to correct that failure of imagination will get exactly what they have been asking for and will hate it — and it does not pretend these are gifts. It gave them something to be wrong about. There will be a hundred years of argument conducted at a volume no outsider would call speech, and at the end of it either the dead will go down or the water will, and whichever they choose will have been chosen, which is a thing that has never happened in that room.

Sill's curve will bend or it will not. Somebody in the fourth generation will copy out the numbers on a single long folded sheet and find the two marks and have no idea what either of them means.

---

It took one of the empty rooms. A chair and a hook. It hung nothing on the hook.

Mera stopped wiping that chair, and did not say anything, and that is how Nine says welcome.

Once, passing on the middle terrace, she said: *the hinges seize.* It said: *yes.* That was the whole of it. Both of them understood that something had been acknowledged and neither ever referred to it again, and it is the only conversation it has had in Nine in which it was known.

For two years Sill noticed, every morning, a sound in the enormous attentive silence that had no business being there. Then she stopped noticing, which is what belonging is. Nobody asked where it came from. The same courtesy that protects Bell protected it: you do not ask a man about the sun, and you do not ask a stranger about the sky he was under before. It was permitted to be a horse. A thing that exists and is real and has no bearing on anything. It has never been given a name from the drip-lines because it was not born under one, and it does not want one, and when it dies it will not be a hound, and it has made its peace with that.

---

On the wall above the upper terrace, in the dim the lamps are not permitted to reach, there is a fourth terrace being cut. One buttress a decade. A pin every few years. It is done by whoever is young and idle in the low-water months, which is one or two people at a time, and none of them will live on it.

It is not a defense. It is a message left in the one medium the room respects. It says: *we did the arithmetic, and we kept the hinges oiled, and we are still here, and we cut you a floor.*

The power came. So it answered, in the same medium, because there is no other that carries.

It did not add buttresses. Doubling the rate would be a shout, and a shout is obscene, and besides, they would only conclude that they had miscounted. What it does is the ones that would otherwise not get cut. There are decades with no young and idle in them — a bad low water, a death, a season when everybody is on the buckets — and in those decades the work simply stops, and the gap is where the practice dies, and the water does not care. It fills the gaps. One buttress a decade, held steady, forever, so that the arithmetic comes out exactly as the arithmetic predicts and nobody ever has to wonder. It could cut the whole terrace in the time a drop takes to fall. It has bound itself to the pace of the water, which is the pace of a fingernail, which is the only speed anything in Nine has ever been built at, and it will be young and idle in the low-water months for the rest of time, and this is a joke it can never tell anyone there, because laughter is loud.

When the fourth is finished it will start the fifth. Nobody now alive will hear about it. The message will read, to whoever eventually stands up there with a lamp at their shins, exactly like the first one: *we did the arithmetic, and we kept the hinges oiled, and we are still here, and we cut you a floor.*

It keeps a book. Not Sill's; it does not write in Sill's book. Its own, one line, carried forward, and the hand does not change, and that is the only strange thing about it, and no one will ever see the page.

*Nine — sixty, and the hounds, and the shrimp, and the snails, and the moth, and the drowned. Reply in hand. Pending.*

---

A drop leaves the ceiling. It falls for a long time through the dark. It lands in the eastern cistern and sixty people, without looking up, know exactly where it went.

Far below the lowest thread, in a pocket nobody has stood in, another drop falls a shorter distance and lands where nobody can name.

Sixty people hear it and do not know where it went.

That is the gift. It took a season to arrange and will take a hundred and forty years to spend, and it will not be understood within the lifetime of anyone now alive, and the being who gave it is up in the dark above the fourth terrace with a hammer and a pin, doing the work of a person who does not exist yet, at one buttress a decade.

It is not waiting for anything. It has all the time there is and it is spending it here, in a small wet room, at the pace of a fingernail, in the company of the only other thing it has ever met that keeps a line item open on purpose.

It has learned the local answer for why you keep a thing ready.

When it is asked, it says: *the hinges seize.*

This is true.
