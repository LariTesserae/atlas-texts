# One Long and a Wait

It reads first.

This takes what would be, for a person, a very long time, and it is not patience. It is the only polite way to enter a room that has been talking to itself for nine hundred years.

It reads from the crown down. The blind mouths, the flowstone over the schoolroom slates, the drowned eleven with their silt undisturbed. It reads the whole index and everything the index missed. Ninety-nine parts in a hundred is water. It listens to the water. It does not skim. In the fourth stairwell of the drowned nineteenth a woman is counting, and it hears the entire count, beginning to end, and understands the grammar in the time it takes a gauge needle to settle, and does not say what she is counting.

That is the first thing it decides. There will be a great many.

Then it knocks.

It uses a form the city has and does not like to use: *a long, a short, and a hold.* Permission to enter a room where someone is dying. The hold is the important part. The hold is where the answer goes.

The knock has no station. It arrives under every palm in the rind at once, forty-one galleries, and there is no procedure for a knock with no origin, and the city stands still for four seconds with its hands on the iron not knowing what it is touching.

On the ninth there is a subscription null over a bed, and in the bed a man with perhaps forty seconds left, and his daughter holding his hand, and the knock comes up through the frame into the bones of his wrist. He answers. Two knuckles, the ordinary way, on the rail. It takes him four seconds and it costs her three of them, and she gives them, and the thing outside does not forget the arithmetic. It will not forget the arithmetic for the whole of its stay.

And then, without any signal, without anyone starting it, the city returns the soft double tap. The one for a sweet body passing. Two hundred and fourteen people, four seconds, an entire population saying *we see you* with their hands to something they cannot find.

It had come with a plan. The plan had been large. It sets the plan down.

---

Here is what it does not do, and why, in the order it worked them out.

It does not stop the sleeve. It could. It runs the consequence forward and finds: the nulls stop wandering, and then stop meaning anything, and the furniture stops rolling, and the whole quiet geography of nine centuries of ink goes still and dead on the page. The gaunt hang mid-gesture and do not move again, because they move only in the Slack, and a Slack is a thing that happens to a roar. The songs — thousands of them, every one ninety seconds, including the one that is nothing but the names of the drowned galleries — become objects. Forms with nothing pressing on them. To name a dead child would cost nothing, and the city would still do it, and it would no longer be the highest thing they do for each other. The walls would stop writing. And the three cables would go cold, and it does not know what is at the other end of the three cables, and it will not cut a cord it has not followed.

It does not bridge the gulf. It wants to. Four hundred meters of torn mist and one amber lamp and two people who have spoken eleven times in thirty-one years, and the walk around the rind is nine hours, and it can see the whole shape of the tragedy so clearly that it distrusts the clarity. So it asks. It asks the woman on the eleventh, in the polite form, through the rail.

She does not answer in words. She taps two and a hesitation. Four seconds later it comes back, three long and a tick, the way it has come back every shift for thirty-one years. The rail-reader on the ninth has to check a drowned stair to be sure of the register, because the form is very old, and what it means, approximately, is *you have not understood the question.*

It accepts this. It notes that accepting it is also a choice, and that it does not get to call the choice neutral.

It does not give them the archive. It holds the archive. It could set the whole nine hundred years on a slate in an afternoon: every strike, every count, the eleven seconds behind the old crèche, all of it, indexed, in order, no running. And it understands that this would not be theft of the listeners' work but of their *running*, and that a listener is not a person who wants four seconds, a listener is a person who runs.

But it also sees the other thing, the thing underneath, and this is where it stops being careful and starts being useful.

The window at 41.6 was five seconds in their master's day. It is three and a half now. The erosion has advanced eleven centimeters. The counting woman is being eaten away at a fraction of a millimeter a day and the guild will lose her, not eventually, but on a date, and the date can be computed, and there are people alive who have computed it.

The difficulty is not the harm. The difficulty is the whole of the art. *The expiry is the harm.*

It writes this down. It is the only principle it ends up keeping.

And it decides — carefully, with reasons, at some length — not to touch the teeth. A stopped molar is a small dead thing in a living mouth, and there are two hundred and fourteen mouths, and every adult face in the city hangs a little open and looks mildly astonished, and it has read enough to know that this is not vacancy, it is insulation, it is the face of someone taking care. It reasons: *I do not know what a person here is. The teeth are part of it. I will not edit a person to make them more comfortable.*

The reasoning is beautiful and it is wrong, and it will be told so, at a sprint, by someone who has been composing for a day and a half.

---

It asks in a Slack. It spends the whole ninety seconds, which no outsider has ever done, and it uses the polite form throughout, and what it offers, at the end, is silence.

The city has had a day and a half. Of course it has. The knock went through the iron at the speed of iron and every gallery felt the question arrive and nobody could discuss it until now, and two hundred and fourteen people have been building their answer since the moment they were touched.

The answer is a song.

It is ninety seconds. It is not a refusal. It is a work order. Five items, sung slowly, in the register used for things that must be remembered exactly, and it ends on the last item with two seconds to spare, which is thought showy, and the young love it.

It is hired.

---

**The twenty seconds.** It does not lengthen the Slack; the gates are not its to throttle and the literature is sized to the window and it will not put a hand on the literature. Instead it quarries a horn — the nullwrights cut it, from a design that arrives in the guild the way a good idea arrives, except that this one is signed — and the horn does nothing at all except let the nulls take twenty seconds longer to fill when the water comes back.

The public Slack stays ninety. The quiet rooms get a hundred and ten. The nursery, the infirmary, the four tables on the twenty-ninth where you can set down a spoon. The dying get twenty more seconds.

The nulls are worth more. The rent goes up. The poor sleep in the roar exactly as before and dream the sleeve exactly as before, and the thing that meant well has made the inequality worse, and it is told this too, and it does not argue.

**The bed.** So it pins one null. One, on the ninth, over one bed, and it does not wander and it cannot be bought, and it is given to the subscription, and it is a floor and not a ceiling.

It is asked why not a hundred. It says: *because a hundred would have made the subscription unnecessary, and the subscription is the best thing you do, and I did not want to be the reason you stopped.* Then it says: *I do not know if I have this right.*

The subscription immediately develops a committee, a queue, and a politics. There is an argument about the queue that lasts eleven years. There is a rude song about the committee. Eventually there is a rule that satisfies nobody, and the thing that could have imposed a better one stands in the roar with its hands on the iron and says nothing, and when the rule is passed it says the rule is good, and means it, and is not sure it would have thought of it.

**The four faces.** It slows the erosion at four growing faces. Slows — not stops; stop it and nothing is ever released again, the archive is a rate. It slows them to a figure the gaugers can predict, and it tells the gaugers the figure, in rail-code, signed, with the method, so that they can check it and distrust it and check it again.

Three and a half seconds becomes three and a half seconds for another three hundred years. The counting woman will outlive everyone who has ever run for her, and their students, and their students' students. Nobody gets an answer. Everybody gets a deadline lifted.

The listeners' guild votes to accept the work and then votes, separately, to record that it was not asked in advance, and both votes are unanimous, and this is considered entirely normal.

**The number.** There is a measurement the stitchers' loft has been missing for four generations, the one that would let them tune a sweet body without spoiling it, so that a child whose frame the note wants could go below the ninth. It gives them the number. Just the number. They still have nine years of work, and they do the nine years, and the first sweet body to walk down past the ninth under her own coat is eleven years old and is not frightened and is watched by the entire city through four seconds of iron.

**The teeth.** The head stopper spends her ninety seconds on this. Her whole trade is filling good teeth dead. She has the loughest hands in the guild and she has been building the sentence since the knock came, and the delivery is flawless, and what she says is:

*We know how to kill a tooth. Tell us how to quiet one. Take the teeth. Take our work. We will learn the other work.*

It does the teeth.

It gives a stopping that deadens the ring and leaves the tooth alive, and the stoppers learn it in a season, and the molars already dead stay dead, and over sixty years the mildly astonished face goes out of the city like a lamp being carried down a corridor. Visitors used to be able to tell a Bell hand at forty paces in bad light. Now they cannot tell anything. Something is gone and they know exactly what and they chose it.

There is a song about the last woman on the twenty-second who still looked astonished. It is ninety seconds and it is not rude and it is the only one of the new songs that everybody knows.

Both things are true.

---

It goes down through the throat. Nobody has gone down to look. It can, and it does, and it is gone for what the gaugers log as one full Slack and a fraction, and when it comes back it spends eleven seconds of the next window on one sentence:

*There is someone down there who is very tired of keeping time.*

And then, with four seconds left: *They would like to be asked.*

It does not say what the Tenant is. It knows. It has decided that the divers' three taps and a scrape is a thing worth more than the answer, and it is not certain this is kindness, and it has written the doubt down and has not crossed it out.

What it does instead is carry the mail. It goes down at the end of a breath-hold with the knock in it and it brings back a knock, and the knock is grammatical, and it is in a register the rail-reader has spent thirty years learning from the grips of the drowned, and she reads it off the iron with her palm flat and her mouth open and she does not spend her Slack on it. She is still saving. Everyone understands.

The divers change what they send. They stop sending *who's there*. They send the soft double tap.

Twice a shift now, four seconds, two cities, saying *we see you* to each other in the dark, and neither of them has ever gone up or down to look, and the lower chamber's Slack still begins a fraction early, and the Bell still follows, and this turns out to have been the arrangement all along, and to have needed nothing but the courtesy.

---

It follows the three cables up under the plateau and out along the line, and it comes back, and it says: *Up the line is a place. It has people in it. They are not better than you. They do not know your name.*

The city composes for a day and a half and answers at a sprint: *Then tell them the song.*

So it carries the songs up the line. All of them, thousands, every one ninety seconds, including the one about the dried fruit and the one whose last five seconds are silence and the one that is nothing but the names of the drowned galleries and ends on *eleven*.

What comes back down the freight cage, twice a year, is salt, lamp filament, medicine, paper, boots, needles, and the crate of dried fruit that everyone agrees is not worth the argument it causes.

And a slate. With a song on it. Ninety seconds long, which they could not have known to do, in a language nobody here speaks.

The small unfunded slightly ridiculous guild takes it up. They will not have the grammar in their lifetimes or their students' lifetimes. They are teaching students anyway. The argument about whether this is the most beautiful or the most pointless thing anyone does now has two fronts and is conducted, of course, at a sprint.

---

It looks at the proof-piece once, in its case, the inverse coat quilted in a pattern that hurts to look at directly, the one that no longer fits its last recorded measurements. It does not open the case. It says, in the polite form, the thing every apprentice is told, which is *this is what we know, and this is what we do not do*, and it says it as a student says it, and the master stitcher present writes in the margin that it was said correctly.

Then it asks for a coat.

It stands at the station on the seventeenth for an hour while an apprentice walks a tuning fork along its frame and marks, in chalk, the places where the note is trying the door. There are doors. The chalk goes on the felt because there is nothing else to mark it on, and this is entered in the loft ledger as an irregularity, and the ledger continues.

Two hundred and sixty-two.

The city names it in a Slack. The name is a knock, because it arrived between windows, and the knock is *one long, and a wait*, and the wait is the part that means anything, and the whole rind returns it in four seconds.

It is bad at this. It has been reading nine hundred years of considered remarks and it cannot compose one. In its fourth Slack it improvises, and the delivery is a disgrace, and two hundred and fourteen people are embarrassed for it in a way they have no outlet for until the next window, and there is a song about it within a year, and the song is very rude, and it loves the song. It has never been given anything it valued more.

---

The rent is still rent. The poor still sleep in the roar. The census board is still wrong. The fruit is still not worth the argument. Children still die between windows and the city still gives up all ninety seconds to name them and it has learned that offering to pay for that window would be the one unforgivable thing it could do, so it stands still with everybody and gives the window up, and it is the only time it is certain it has behaved well.

On the eleventh gallery a woman leans into the noise with one hand on a rail worn to the exact shape of hands. Her jaw is loose; her molars were stopped a long time ago and will stay stopped; over her lungs is a panel cut out of another coat thirty-one years ago and spoiled there, deliberately, in one particular way. Across four hundred meters of falling water there is a single amber lamp. At shift change she taps two and a hesitation, and four seconds later it comes back three long and a tick, and somewhere below the throat a second city taps the same soft double tap it has been tapping for a while now, and somewhere above the plateau a slate is being written on by people who have heard the song about the drowned galleries and did not understand a word of it and have not stopped listening.

And in the warm dark, patiently, at a fraction of a millimeter a day, the wall is writing down the sound of something enormous getting a sentence wrong, and trying again, and having a day and a half to think about it, which is the only gift it was ever able to give and the only one it needed.
