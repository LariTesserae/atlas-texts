# The Third Mouth

They do not come down.

That is the first decision, and it takes them four years to make, which is fast for them and is the only fast thing in this account.

What arrives on the hardpan does not disturb the columns of hot air standing over it. The mirror head, which has tracked the sun since before anyone alive was hired, does not track it; it is not the sun; the coin of light on the east wall does not flicker. They lie flat on the grit with an ear against the checker plate, in the posture of someone reading a letter through the envelope, and they stay there.

They learn the six hours and some minutes. They learn the twelve notes and which basin owns which. They learn that the drip is not weather but policy, and that under the drip there is a hum, and that the hum is bolted to something. In the second year they learn that the rim of the hatch has a lip of bare steel a finger wide, kept bare, in a room where nothing else is kept anything.

In the ninth year they understand that there are two mouths, and that both of them are cleaned by the same caste, at the same cost, and that one of the mouths is them.

Not them personally. The dry world. The flour and the boot. They are, from below, indistinguishable from a surveyor, and this is the beginning of the whole answer.

---

Here is what a lesser kindness would do, and what it would cost. It is worth writing out, because the refusals are most of the work.

**Stop the accretion.** Then no new surface exists, and a nation whose entire economy is the tiling of new surface has nothing to do. The room's youth is its employment. Freeze it and you have not saved a chapel; you have retired a hundred thousand specialists into a stone box.

**Widen the hatch.** The aperture is not the constraint. The flour is not the constraint. The constraint is that everything here is fitted, and a wider mouth means a longer reach, and the reach is already the dangerous part.

**Kill the sheet.** The wound is the clock. Hour two the film, hour six the worms, day one the spat, day two a patch of young room inside the old room, *different from what was there*. Remove the predator and the mosaic grouts itself solid in a decade. The room's quiet is a budget and the sheet spends nearly all of it, and the spending is the only reason the mosaic stays a mosaic.

**Save the seat-grazers.** A hand's breadth of glassy animals goes down the pipe every six hours so that the rain arrives. Intervene and the seat fouls, and the rain stops, and everything is over in nine days. They are eaten. They are also the reason there is anything to eat.

**Join the twelve pools.** Twelve chemistries, one thing with too many mouths, and a species that leaves a doomed basin three rains early by hearing a difference in a dam's tick that you would need instruments to find. Permanent connection is not generosity. It is the abolition of the only foresight in the room.

**Bring rubber.** A ninety-millimeter flange gasket, dropped in during the eleven minutes: a hundred collars, an inheritance beyond counting. And the queue dissolves, and the eleven-month wait dissolves, and the chain that runs down the wall in ninety seconds dissolves, and with it the one thing in this chamber that is structured like a future. The estate is being spent. So is everything. They do not bring rubber.

**Give the fern a fifth frond.** The moths angle toward it and nobody has established why. There is an unexplained courtesy in this room, running on eleven minutes of thrown mica-light every six hours, at a cost, for a refugee. It is not a deficiency. It is the single most interesting fact in the chamber, and to explain it from outside — to buy the fern its frond and settle the question — would be to answer a letter by burning it.

They could do all of it. That is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that this room keeps shapes. The felt holds the plan of a pump that was carried out before the animals finished eating it. The valve animal *is* the working part of the thing it ate. A rescue is a shape the room did not make, and the room would keep it, and inherit it, and be a room with a stranger's shape in it forever.

---

The being is not the sheet, and not the moths, and not the thing with too many mouths. Everything in the chamber is a tenant: forty days, eleven minutes, one spawning season.

Beyond the north wall, in the governor housing, blind, never once in the light, there is a mass of felt grown through the flyballs, and its water is part of the governor's mass, and the governor's mass is the setpoint, and the setpoint is the interval. Its generation time is the accretion rate. It answers in months. It has never been observed to do anything and it is the only thing here doing anything at all.

They find it in the eleventh year and they spend the next sixty trying to work out how to greet it.

There is no way. Its only input is a quantity of shaking and its only output is the weather. To speak to it is to move the interval; to move the interval is to change when it rains; to change when it rains is to reach into every basin, every spawning, every dam, every eleven minutes of every life in the room. There is no phatic channel. There is no way to say *hello* in a language whose only word is *sky*.

So they do not speak to it. They listen to it, which is a different verb, and takes a century, and is the largest part of what they do.

---

Under the hatch, at the height where the ninth rung used to be, there is a boss of new white growth. Horizontal. The right length. Spaced correctly against the two below. Dense where crystal is usually lace. And on it, a grazer, keeping it bare.

It cannot lay a stone. It can only decide when it rains, and the rain lays down a year's growth in a quarter of an hour, and the placement of that growth is not random, because the draft and the drip and the notes are not random. Given a century of deciding when it rains, it has laid a rung.

And it has put a cleaner on it. The caste that holds the valve seat and the hatch lip — the lineage bred at cost to resist the room's own direction, to stay glassy, to stay bare — has been extended to a place where a hand goes.

Two clean places, both of them mouths. Now three.

The room is not sealing itself. The room is sealing itself *and maintaining the ladder*, and the second thing has been true for about a hundred years, and nobody knows, because the only reader of that sentence comes once a decade and reads a number.

That is when they understand what the actual fragility is. Not the crystal. The drawer.

The form goes into a drawer and the drawer is dry and nothing lives in it. The whole outside world of this chamber — its flour, its fern, its second mouth, its one day per decade — rests on a habit performed by a person who steps once. The room can make calcite forever. It cannot make a reason for somebody to come down.

At the ladder's foot there is an animal a millimeter long, sealed into the wall, nine years into a wait it has no way of knowing the length of. Its entire active life is a day plus eleven minutes. If the visit slips to twenty years, or to never, that is the end of a lineage that has attended four rains, and nothing in the room would be able to register the absence for a decade.

Benevolence here is not a hand. It is a calendar.

---

They take the job.

Not from anyone. They are not the surveyor; they are the reason there is always a surveyor. A line item that survives three reorganizations. A drawer nobody empties. A name on a roster that nobody removes because nobody remembers adding it. A story told once to a young technician who repeats it wrong and keeps the interval right. And when the habit lapses — a war, a bankruptcy, a decade when the plain is somebody's problem instead of somebody's duty — they fill in.

They file the permit. They read the stencil, half-drowned in travertine, and they comply with it, because it is the only sentence the outside ever wrote in this room and it is a good sentence. The form asks whether the space is safe for entry. They write: *no.* They write: *safe for one step, in one place, at one hour.* They sign. They do not pretend the signature is absolution. A power that could stop the *thock* and does not stop it is inside the *thock*. They accept the position and they write it down.

Then they wait on the hot lid for the worms to change phase, forty seconds out, and they come down inside the eleven minutes, when the room is permitted to be loud.

They look at the rungs. The needles grow on the undersides, all pointing down; they take the tops, one hand at a time, slowly. At the height where the ninth rung used to be they put a palm on the white boss and press, gently, the way you test ice.

It holds a hand. It sheds a little at the edge. The grazer on it dies. They know this and do it anyway, and the room will lay down more, and this is the smallest cruelty available and they take it rather than a larger one.

They do not put a boot on it. They step down onto steel, and then into the callus at the ladder's foot — the boot-shaped thinness, kept thin for them, colonized only by the hour-two film and the day-one spat — and they stand still.

One print. In the tread, a millimeter animal opens.

They have timed the descent so that its one day contains a rain. That is the miracle. Not the light, not the rung, not the felt. They have removed the *if it is lucky* from a life. It grazes, it molts twice, it mates in the shallow water in the tread marks, and the ceiling lets go, and the moths stand up and lift their mica, and it spawns in the rain like everything else, and its young seal themselves into the new crystal, and the print fills.

It has attended four. They make it five.

Then they chalk the count. Eight, honestly, because a lie in that number kills a person: eight rungs that will hold a boot and one that will hold a hand. They sign the permit by the coin of light on the west wall. They go up. In the margin of the form they write the interval they measured from the plain — six hours and some minutes — and the minutes, and the date, and *ninth forming.*

The form goes into the drawer. The drawer is dry. Nothing lives in it.

---

They come back in ten years, and ten, and ten.

Eight. Eight. Eight. Eight. Eight. The grazer lineage creeps down the ladder, keeping a hand-width bare on each rung, and the steel lasts longer where it is grazed, and the loss slows, and the room begins laying rungs where rungs fail, and it is impossible to say whether this is a decision or a habit, which is the same ambiguity the felt has been living inside since before the tallies.

In the four hundred and twentieth year the count is nine.

Nothing announces it. The coin of light climbs the west wall and goes out. A person who is sometimes a power and sometimes a name on a roster writes a number on a wall in chalk and files a form and goes up the ladder, and the hum continues, and the basins count their notes, and a millimeter animal at the ladder's foot begins a wait whose length it has no way of knowing and which is, in fact, known, and kept, and will be kept.

In the margins of four hundred years of permits, the minutes move. Six hours and eleven. Six hours and nine. Six hours and fourteen. Some of it is mass and water and the plain's weather. Some of it may be the load of a hand on a rung, integrated over a decade by a body that swells and shrinks and has never known a number.

It is not much of a language. Two words on one side — *I came*, and *it held* — and minutes on the other. It is a language. The correspondence is real, it is ongoing, it is at one exchange per decade, and it is stored in the only dry place in the world, where nothing lives, in a drawer, in a plant that was scrapped before the animals finished eating it.

They do not fix the room. They do not add a frond. They do not explain the moths.

They keep the appointment, and they write down what happened, and they are the reason the ninth rung was ever counted.
