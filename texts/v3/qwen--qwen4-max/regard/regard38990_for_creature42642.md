It could put the fifth back. That is the easiest thing it could do, and the first thing it wants to do, and it would be an eviction.

Because the pitch is not stored in the air, it is stored in the shape of the holes, and to raise the chord you must narrow every throat in the country. Take in the waists. Shrink the chambers where a house could park. Drain the black pools down to puddles, drop the fern shelves into daylight, put the old people's warm dusk up where the wind can reach them. The chord is low because the country is full of homes. The noise that a visitor cannot bear is the sound of a hundred million years of rock having been converted into somewhere to live, and the low register is the rent. To give them back their grandmothers' fifth is to hand them a bright, thin, ringing plain with nothing in it, and the fish would go, and the compost heat would go, and the grandmothers would go up into the weather to die in the cold with their names still in their skulls.

So: not that.

Not the ears, either, though it goes back and forth on this longer than on anything. Eight years of air and then sixty years of a memorized hiss of grit on the mats — it could simply stop the loss, thicken nothing, leave the temporal bone thin and the brow uncalledused, and every person here would keep rain for life. And then every person here would be a listener. That is the thing it cannot get past. There is already a caste that can hear on this plain, eleven or twelve to a district, raised in the stopped fields, and they are not popular and they do not sleep, because their office requires them to go out and stand in the roar with their plugs out and their teeth aching and love a country they can hear being turned into void. To give everyone ears is to make a hundred thousand of those people and call it a gift. It is to take a population that lives *inside* the sound, in the shelf, in the jaw, in the soles, in the hour-long hum of two foreheads pressed together with the eyes shut, and turn it into a population standing outside the sound, listening to it, the way you listen to weather happening to you. The bone-speech is not a compensation for a loss. It is a language with a literature and a flirtation and a grammar of sarcasm and a way of saying a name into a temple after the person has stopped answering. It would be a strange kindness to delete a literature in order to restore a hiss.

Not the wind. The wind is the privacy. Air is the only medium here in which a thing can be said to one person, and therefore the only place confession can happen, and seduction, and the last words of the dying, which are spoken aloud into an ear by someone who mostly cannot hear them and are stolen before they clear the teeth, and this is not a defect in the arrangement, this is the arrangement. Calm the wind and you get a plain where everything said is said to everybody, which is to say a plain with no inside. Also the mats are wind-made, and the towers are wind-played, and the bird eats what the wind lifts, and the flowers have handles because for ten thousand generations there has been exactly one animal that could reach them, and that animal is a person with a brush. In ten generations of calm the handles would soften and go, because a handle only exists where it is needed, and then there would be a people who used to be necessary.

Not the flowers. It will not make them self-setting. This is the most available mercy and the worst one. Three taps, a pause, two more: *person*, *owing*, *name*. Relieve the debt and you have not freed them, you have unnamed them. You have made a cut throat out of a whole country — a scream with nothing in it, no compost, no fish, no calendar, no reason to get up with a brush in the belt.

It can make a snail. It cannot make a snail that came out of that pool and was carried to this one in a jar of her mother's water by a woman whose name is notched on a cord four generations back. The fish follow the brides; the fish are the record; a resurrected fish is a forged inheritance, and there are people who read pools for a living and would be made liars by the miracle. So the lost radiations stay lost. The feral holes stay brown-ringed. The fifth stays gone.

And the cliff it leaves entirely alone, including the children who weep at the lake and cannot stop and are carried down and left among people who talk in air. It has no way to make that not a cruelty. The most it can say for itself is that the songs are alive down there, sung all day, effortlessly, by liars — that the plain is not only losing, it is also seeding, and does not know it — and that this is not nothing, and is not enough.

---

**The two channels**

Here is the actual problem, and it is a problem of grammar, not of power.

There are two media in this country and both are foreclosed.

If it knocks, it says a thing to the whole district that cannot be unsaid, and the thing it would be saying is *I gave you this*. There is no brush in the world with which that can be repaid. A people whose word for themselves is *owing* would be handed an infinite creditor, and every finite debt would go slack beside it — the hundred and forty mouths, the notched cord, the two seasons after which a collar browns and a crime has been committed. The flowers would go unvisited by people waiting to be visited. It would not be a religion, exactly. It would be a default.

If it whispers, it lies. Not falsely — structurally. *A thing said where it can be kept.* Unverifiable, private, owed to nobody, outside the record, exactly the class of utterance the whole legal apparatus of the plain exists to exclude. A god speaking in air here is not a revelation, it is a rumor with good credentials, and the country has a word for that and the word is not a compliment.

And if it simply appears — stands up, on a plain where nothing stands up — it becomes a tower. The only vertical thing for days, the fixed note, the thing people walk home by in a whiteout with their hand on the cord. Everything would orient to it. And then it would leave, because everything that appears eventually leaves, and taking a note out from under people who are navigating by it is not an ambiguous act. It is a killing, done slowly, in fog, of whoever is furthest out.

So it does not speak. It works in the third medium, which is matter, and which the people here already interrogate for a living with kilns and drills and clay and correction tables. And it sets itself three rules, which it derives from them rather than from itself:

*Do not touch a loss that is holding something up. Do not leave alone a loss that is holding nothing up.*

*Whatever is given must be findable by their own hands, in their own crafts, and must be cheap enough that no one can own it.*

*Every gift must make more work.*

That last is the only test it trusts. A gift that reduces the round is a gift that reduces the people. A gift that adds mouths to visit, jars to compare, arguments to have, is a gift that can be paid — forward, sideways, to each other, on the cord, with a brush.

There is a reading of all this in which it is simply a coward with excellent reasons. It has not been able to rule that out. It has decided instead to act small enough that the reading does not matter very much either way.

---

**What it does**

Two things. Both are almost nothing. Both are the kind of thing that could have happened anyway, and one of them nearly did, several times, and didn't.

**The grog.** A listener's apprentice in the eleventh district, nineteen years old and not diligent, makes a batch of reference jars and uses grit ground off the lake lens for grog because the good sand is out past the touch-lines and the fog is bad and she is lazy. The jars come out of the kiln ordinary. She compares them, notches the cord, is bored.

Nineteen years later they have not moved.

Not *have moved less*. Have not moved. Not with the wet season, not with the cold, not with the pressure fronts that turn the whole district inside out, not with the four hundred small creepings that the correction tables exist to chase and the corrections to the corrections exist to chase after that. She is forty and unpopular and she has ended a literature.

Nobody knows why. There is a literature.

The stuff is lake grit and there is a lake's worth of it, and any kiln in the country can fire it, and this is deliberate: it was made common on purpose, because a rare virtue is a thing that can be bought, and the quietists have money and buy throats. You cannot enclose a proportion. You can only enclose a lake, and they will try, and there will be a survey and a quota and a bitter law about how much of the only silence in nine days may be ground up and fired into jars — because the bird lands there, and the tree is there, and the children are brought there once to be shown what silence is, and you cannot quarry that away. It will be argued for two hundred years. Good.

They call it by the word for *a thing that cannot be unsaid*, which is also their word for *law*, and, in the older grammar, *grandmother*. Nobody finds this worth remarking on.

It has a domain, and the domain matters: it holds in the dark and the damp and it shivers apart in three seasons of sun and freeze-thaw. So it is useless for towers. The flues still need wax and clay hour by hour as the pressure moves; the watch never ends; the lineage in the north goes on keeping its one pitch through its twelfth tower and its thirteenth, and the masons who can drill a new tower to an old note are still the highest artisans alive. What the stone is good for is jars, and jars live down. So the true pitches go down the vine into the warm dusk and sit on the fern shelves beside the pools, in the compost heat, which is the slow burning of ten thousand years of fallen green and everyone who ever lived in the shaft. The fixed thing is kept with the dead. The listeners, who are not popular, now have to descend into everybody's grandmothers once a year to do their office, and this changes what they are, and some of them hate it and some of them have stopped being unable to sleep.

*From the correction tables of the eleventh district, final entry:*

*Jar 4, ninth firing, lake grog. Against itself, nineteen years: no difference. Against jar 11, old clay: jar 11 has fallen. Against the district: the district has fallen a little more than we thought and a little less than we feared.*
*The table is closed. There is nothing under this line. There will never be anything under this line.*

Three consequences, and it wanted all three and could not have brought them about directly, because they are not facts, they are institutions, and institutions are not its to make.

The openers learn to cut sharp. A new mouth is a scream with nothing in it and takes four hundred years to become a hole, and its pitch falls the whole way as it widens, and now the fall can be reckoned rather than guessed — so you cut a throat *out* of tune with the present country in order to cut it *into* tune with the country the long cord says is coming. This requires a clause, and the clause is the hard part, because the existing law is absolute: *you may not tune into a beat with me*, and a mouth cut sharp is a beat with everyone for four centuries.

*Added to the tuning law, after sixty years and two districts stopped speaking to each other:*

*A mouth may be cut out of tune with the country that is, if it is cut into tune with the country the cord reckons. The cord shall be reckoned forward four hundred years. The cutter's name shall be knocked and kept. Her grandchildren shall not be sued for the beat.*

It cannot pass that clause. It can only make the clause worth passing. That is the correct division of labour and it took the people sixty years and it was not wasted.

The moth-flasks stop being a gesture. A flask of lake-grog clay tuned to a note the land let go of a century ago will hold that note for as long as there is a lip to set it on. So the moths that were made for a pitch can go on being made for it after the hole that taught them has widened into the floor of hum. A hole in time where a hole in the ground used to be. It sometimes works. There are people alive who have stood at a brown-ringed feral mouth in the dark and heard a kind come back to a note that no longer exists anywhere in the world except in a jar. Every ark needs tending: the collar must be kept green, the water must be kept in its funnel, the dead must be turned. More work. More owing. It checked this. It checked it first.

And some families will keep shelves of true jars tuned to a struck flask, to rain on a plug-field, to the exact sound of a mother's air-voice saying a name, kept at the keeping, kept for sixty years by people who would otherwise have had only what they could hum. This will make some of them unbearable and comfort others past bearing. That is what objects do. It considered the risk of a country turning into a museum of its own lost hearing and decided the risk was real and smaller than the fade.

**The last minute.** A roof that is about to spall whines thin for six hours, and no adult can hear it, and the whole system of not dying rests on a six-year-old sitting cross-legged at the lip with her hands flat, bored, important, waiting.

It leaves the six hours exactly where they are. The rota keeps its hours. The children keep their office; the day your ears go and you come off the rota is still the day you become an adult, and there is still a feast, and it is still not happy.

It moves the last minute down.

The final minute of a roof's complaint now also arrives as a grinding in the shelf — in the palms, in the seat of the pants, in the teeth of anybody sitting there. It has no grammar. It cannot be inflected, cannot be sarcastic, cannot be mistaken for speech, cannot be argued with, and does not need ears. A child learns it in a week, before hands.

Nothing has been abolished. The last useful work a person does with their ears is still the last useful work a person does with their ears, and they still remember it at seventy. What has changed is that the child is no longer the only thing standing between the child and the floor.

There is a theology about this now. It is wrong in every particular. It has the right effect. It notices that it accepted this quickly, and is not sure what that tells it about the rest of its reasoning.

---

**What it costs**

The edge watchers refuse the stone entirely, and they are right to, by their own lights: *a word should not outlive its saying*, and a jar that says one thing forever is the worst object ever made. They go on tracing on each other's palms and backs, legally dead, keeping the record of the notes as the notes go, and they send back — by a runner, in strokes on skin — the only sustained objection anybody has written to the whole business, and it is a good one, and it does not get answered, and it should not be.

A district in the south stops comparing against anything and declares the long cord an idol. Two families get rich on kilns before the recipe escapes them, which takes eleven years. There is a schism, and it is stupid, and people are hurt in it. A man is given a hammer of lake-grog stone as a punishment, because it will not damp, will not inflect, carries twenty miles and says one thing forever, and cannot be sarcastic, and cannot be private, and cannot stop; this is worse than the rotten sandstone and is banned within a generation, and the banning is the beginning of a whole new law about voices.

It could have prevented most of this by making the stuff rare and handing it to the good people. That is called picking a side, and it is what a power does when it wants to be loved.

Nobody will ever owe it anything. That is the point. It did not expect to mind, and it minds.

---

**Four hundred years**

A woman whose name is four taps and a scrape cuts a mouth on the western flats, out in the open, wrong on purpose, high and thin and screaming, tuned to a pitch that beats against three neighbours and makes their children fretful. She is sued twice. She loses once. She dies with the beat still going. Her grandchildren tend the raw mouth and lower the first jars and are mocked for it, and their grandchildren put in the first fern shelf, and the vine goes down hand over hand toward water, and the first dead are laid in a gallery and the compost begins its ten-thousand-year burning, and the note falls, and falls, and the long cord says it will fall, and it falls as the cord says.

Three hundred and some years later, on an ordinary night in the wet season, with the fog running in threads to the nearest mouth and the chord at its lowest and thickest, the beat between that throat and its neighbour slows, and lengthens, and stops.

Nobody in that district has ever slept without the dread. They do not know what has been taken away. They know the children are asleep and the water in the pool is not shivering and the old people down in the warm dusk have stopped complaining about a thing they could never name. Somebody lays a jaw on a boulder and feels that the country is, for the first time in the memory of the cord, consonant here. Somebody knocks the news. The notch goes on. Her name goes into the ground, and after it the word that is also *owing*, and a hundred miles of hard caprock carries it, and people feel it in their soles and do not know why they are crying.

It lasts sixty years. Then the two mouths drift apart again, because everything here is falling, and someone will have to cut another one sharp, four hundred years out, and be sued for it.

The chord is a fifth lower than it was. The plain is still being converted into void and the void into silence. The bird still lands only at the lake. The songs still drain down the cliff and are sung all day by people who cannot help it. Nothing has been repaired.

What is different is that the future is now a place they can put things.

---

An old listener, in a stopped field, at night, alone with the jars on the shelf and the clay lungs breathing tortuously all around her and the roar held down to a hum a stone's throw wide. She is holding a jar that has not moved since she was forty. She has read the tables all her life and the tables ended in her hands, and she has thought about it for thirty years, and she has worked out that the grog was laziness and the grinding under the children was mercy and that neither of these is the sort of thing that happens twice.

She says something out loud, into the air, into the wind, where it is taken before it clears her teeth. It is not a knock. It goes nowhere. Nobody can check it. In the only grammar her country has, it is *a thing said where it can be kept*, and therefore a lie, and it is the only prayer on the plain.

It hears it. It is the only thing it ever hears in air.

It does not answer, because answering would make her a creditor's clerk, and because she has a hundred and forty mouths and a cord with notches on it and a granddaughter who can still hear rain, and because the debt has to stay payable by hand.

In the morning she takes her brush and goes out along the touch-lines, and leans over a lip, and takes hold of the warm ridged wrist of a flower that has spent ten thousand years learning the shape of a hand, and does the work, and moves on.

All of this has been said where it can be kept.
