# The Ninety Seconds

## Arrival

It comes the way everything comes here: on the drift, from elsewhere, at the white hour, when nothing is home.

It takes a body, because the thing it has come to do requires breathing.

At the rim it squints, like all of them, and says that the air looks thick, like all of them, and then it coughs — one cough, before it knows — and the cough goes to the far wall intact and comes back, and comes back again, and in the four returns it learns the entire law of the place, which is that nothing here is private. It does not cough again. Not that day, not in the eleven hundred years following.

Then it does the thing no visitor has done.

It stays.

That is the whole of its advantage. Everything else it has would be an addition, and this is a room where nothing is added, where the only architecture is subtraction, where the water made every pocket and flute and scallop by taking away. Power, here, is a liability. Wisdom, here, is mostly knowing what not to hand over. But duration is not an addition. Duration is a thing you can offer without putting anything down.

## The gifts it does not give

It does not deepen the pool. Income and outcome have been equal in that hat-width of water for longer than there have been eyes to notice, and the travertine collar is the ledger of that equality, laid down one evaporation at a time. To deepen it is to drown the ledger and change the number forty into some other number, and to end the long unbroken life of a flatworm the color of a fingernail that has never had a body older than a season.

It does not send more rain. The banding in the oldest shells records eleven hundred wet seasons, and it is the only document in the chamber. Add rain and the document lies. The room would go on being beautiful and would no longer be true.

It does not water the fern. This is the refusal it returns to most often, in the centuries when returning to things is still something it does. One fern, forty years old, the size of a hand, in the single fissure out of twenty thousand square meters that could hold it. Everything else in this room has a reason. The fern has a history. It is the only place in the cup where luck is still permitted to be the explanation, and a tended fern is a reason, and then there would be nowhere in the whole bright volume for an accident to stand.

It does not teach the nine to strike more quietly. Three times a season the room stops for a minute, swifts stalling and dropping and catching themselves, and no one has ever learned to do it more softly, and this is not a failure of the nine. A knapper who has learned to strike softly has learned that others mind. That is the first thought. The second thought is a cache. The third is a place where the cache is kept, and a reason to keep others away from it, and then the courtesy is gone, and the pockets become property, and the room is a village.

It does not hush the dusk. Ninety seconds of chittering is the only speech, the only mail, and the entire education of every infant in the high nursery pockets, who will come down at fifteen already knowing the law with no one having taught them and no one to ask.

It does not go down the throat with a light. It would have to widen the slot to fit, and widening is addition, and the slot is the one thing in the chamber that must stay a hand wide. So it listens instead. When a flake goes over there is a small silence and then a much smaller sound from far below, and that sound is a hundred thousand years of tools reporting that they have arrived. It is enough. Something down there is fat and eyeless and has never heard of the sun, and is not owed a sun.

And it does not feed the tall one. We will come back to that, because it is the hardest thing it does, and it does it every day for eleven hundred years.

## The problem, stated correctly

The room is not broken. It has been exactly itself for longer than there has been anything to look at it, and the census that says *sterile* at noon is wrong in a way that is the room's chief achievement: everyone here lives in an hour rather than a place, and the hours fit together without overlapping, and the fit is not arranged by anyone.

But there is one thing in the cup that is not fine, and it is not a flaw in the design. It is the design's cost, paid by a single body.

The tall one is starving, at the speed of the room, because it has done its job perfectly. It lives on exceptions and there have been no exceptions. Forty paces a day is roughly what a body spends when it has nothing to spend.

It has also never been off duty. Not once. The one interval in the day when it cannot hear is the ninety seconds at the hinge of dusk, and it spends them with its ear-fans folded flat against its skull, waiting out the only window in which anything at all could go to the seep and drink and not be taken. Its one daily rest is a daily terror.

And when it stops, the drop will go on falling exactly as it always has, and nothing in the room will be regular anymore, because eleven is not a property of the water. Eleven is a measurement. A measurement requires something breathing. Regularity is something someone does, and there is one doer, and no successor, and no one at all who will be standing there when it dies.

The power considers the case for leaving. It is a strong case. The good of this place is already achieved; every intervention is a subtraction of the thing it is; the elegant thing is to recognize a finished work and withdraw.

It rejects the case on one ground only, and the ground is not elegance. There is an old animal in the shade behind the seep that is hungry and afraid and alone, and has been for centuries, and will die unwitnessed in a room where nothing watches. A benevolence that can look at that and be satisfied by the beauty of the system it serves is not benevolence. It is taste.

So it must act. And it must act without adding anything.

## The method

Two facts show it the way in.

The first: there is exactly one honest shadow in the chamber. Everywhere else the light comes back four and five times and there is no true dark except in the pockets and under the seven figs. A body standing anywhere on that floor would cast a new shadow — a new cool patch, a new lie in the light, possibly across one of the hundreds of pinhole discs where an old snail has parked its decade and drifts a few centimeters a year with the sky. But behind the seep, in the deep scallop, there is already a shadow, and the tall one is already in it.

Two in a shadow that was already there is not a new shadow.

The address of the gift is therefore fixed by the geometry, not by sentiment. It cannot stand anywhere else in the room without costing someone something. It stands beside the tall one, a body-length away, and touches nothing, because that is how everything here meets.

The second fact: for ninety seconds a day the volume is saturated with itself, doubled and tripled off the ellipse until no sound can be distinguished inside it. That is the only window in which an arrival is not an event.

So it arrives in the noise.

It comes down the shaft on a dusk chittering and is standing in the shade when the hand closes, and the tall one, unfolding nothing, has no evidence that anything came. And thereafter it never moves in the silence again. Not a foot, not a fan, not the width of a snail. All day it is a pale shape in the one place where pale shapes are already understood to be. Visitors have mistaken four thousand snails for mineral until one of them moved. Here is one more dot that will be mistaken for mineral, permanently, because it never will.

This is the entire technique. Everything it does for eleven hundred years, it does inside ninety seconds a day, at the speed of patience, and none of it is ever heard.

## What it does in the ninety seconds

It stands between the seep and the room.

That is all. It is not a fence and it is not a threat; nothing here would recognize either. It is simply a second thing in the shade during the one interval when the tall one is deaf, so that the interval the tall one has spent its whole life dreading is, from that dusk onward, guarded — and the tall one does not know it, and cannot know it, and one evening folds its ear-fans flat as always and then, for the first time in the history of its kind, unfolds them again into the chittering, and hears nothing but swifts.

It does not learn that it is safe. It only stops being afraid, and never asks why, because nothing in this room asks why.

Ninety seconds a day. It is not much. Over eleven hundred years it comes to about a year and a half of rest, delivered in fragments too small to notice, to an animal that had never had any.

The rest of its care is negative, and takes centuries.

It never walks the same line twice, so that no second groove is worn into the case-hardened white. There is one path in this chamber, a finger deep, made by ten thousand pilgrimages of soft bodies, and it must remain the only one; a second path would mean a second destination, and a second destination would mean somewhere that is not here.

It keeps out of the green line, which is two fingers wide and the entire standing green of a hundred thousand cubic meters.

It casts no shadow on anyone's ellipse.

It takes nothing from the pool, which would break the count in a way the whole room would feel, and it takes nothing from the seep, which would break the count in a way that would end it.

Once, in the fourth century, one of the nine sits at the pool a body-length from the shade and does not touch it, and sits its hour, and goes. The courtesy absorbs the new resident the way it absorbs everything: by leaving room.

## The hunger, which it does not fix

It could feed it. That is the thing.

It could put a lizard at the edge of the coin at an hour when lizards are not there. It could stand, once, in a place where a swift would misjudge the turn. Ninety seconds is plenty of cover for either.

It does not, and the reason is not fastidiousness. The tall one eats exceptions. An exception delivered on purpose becomes a schedule; a schedule is learned; and a tall one that has learned to expect food at the seep is no longer a deterrent standing near water, it is an owner waiting at a trough. It would begin to be at the seep. It would begin to drink there. The law it has spent its life enforcing would be broken by the only animal that remembers there was a reason, and it would be broken by the reason's well-wisher.

Also, it will not kill a swift to buy a century. It has looked at that arithmetic from every side and there is no side from which it comes out clean.

So the hunger continues, and it stands a body-length away while it continues, for eleven hundred years, and this is the price the power pays rather than the price it waives. It does not arrive at peace with this. It arrives at being there.

It does one other thing, and it is not a subsidy. It is an offer.

It makes itself edible. It stands in the shade as a body that can be taken, and it settles, in the first century, that if the tall one ever comes out of the dark at a slow walk and takes it, it will not move.

The offer will never be accepted, and it knows this, and it holds it open anyway. The tall one lives on exceptions, and the pale shape beside it has never once broken the interval, has never arrived in a moment that shouldn't have contained it, has never been anything but part of the wall. To eat it, the tall one would have to stop being what it is.

So there is food in the room, standing still, a body-length away, forever, and it is never eaten, and the offer is not withdrawn. That is the only form mercy can take here without unmaking the thing it has come to love: not delivery. Availability.

## The stopping

It happens at an ordinary hour of an ordinary day, in the shade, between two drops.

The room does not stop. The room stops for stone. The swifts turn through the light without a sound and the snails hold their ellipses and the clay moves its millimeter, and the seep gathers, and lets go, and the interval afterward is not eleven of anything, because there is nothing left in the chamber whose breath is slow enough and long enough to make a number out of it.

At dusk the swifts speak, and inside the ninety seconds the pale shape moves for the second time in eleven hundred years.

Forty paces, across the pink clay and the glass-dark nodules, carrying an old body that weighs almost nothing, because almost nothing is what it lived on. Past the pool, which does not notice. To the low point. To the slot a hand wide, where the warm air sighs out.

It lets go.

There is a small silence, and then a much smaller sound from far below.

Then it walks back, and stands in the one honest shadow, and breathes.

## The forgetting

It has to learn the number, and it learns it the way everything here learns: with the body, over years, from the drop. Not eleven as a fact — eleven as a practice, a slowness held steady while the seep gathers. It is courteous about this for as long as it can be. It tries to breathe at the old rate, out of respect for four thousand shells and nine pairs of hands and eleven thousand birds who have all been keeping a measurement made by something that no longer exists.

It drifts. Of course it drifts. It is a different body.

The drift is about a breath every four hundred years, which is to say a millimeter a year, which is the speed of everything in this room. The snails' withdrawal lengthens by a hair. The nine's hands wait a hair longer. The ninety seconds stays ninety seconds, because that is the swifts' own, and always was. And in the banding of the shells laid down after that dusk there is a slow widening that no one will ever read as anything but climate.

That is its signature. It is indistinguishable from weather.

And it grows pale, because it does not move in the light. And it grows slow, because forty paces a day is what a body spends standing still. And it stops watching, because watching is useless in a room with four bounces and no honest shadow, and it turns its attention into the intervals, and into anything arriving in an interval that shouldn't.

Somewhere in the sixth or seventh century it loses the reason.

Not the practice — the practice is in its body now, past recovery, the way the slot is a direction rather than a decision in the hands of the nine. What goes is the why: the rim, the cough, the list of refused gifts, the argument about the fern, the arithmetic about the swift. It had come to do something. It cannot any longer say what. It knows only that the seep is not for drinking, and that the interval is to be kept, and that during the ninety seconds it stands where it stands and nothing gets past it, and that this is simply what is done, the way you put your hand out when you fall.

It becomes a consequence with nothing left to follow from. It becomes the last thing in the room that remembers there was a reason, and then it becomes the thing that doesn't.

There has never been more than one. Now it is possible to say why. Whatever stays long enough becomes it.

## Noon, when no one is home

A warm loud animal stands at the rim for an hour or two, squinting, and says the air looks thick.

It counts seven bent figs, a pool the width of a hat, a scatter of pale dots on two hectares of wet-looking white that are mineral until one of them moves, and, in the deep scallop behind the seep, one tall pale shape that does not move at all.

It coughs. The cough goes to the far wall intact and comes back, and comes back again, and in the shade the ear-fans turn, independently, and take the bearing, and find that it is only a visitor, and fold.

Afterward the animal will describe the place as silent.

The film begins on the cool wall, hours early, invisible. A fig leaf turns two degrees toward a sun that will not arrive until spring. The clay moves a millimeter toward the dark, carrying a flake, a shell, a leaf, a small bone. The white gives back the last of the light. The room goes on being exactly itself, which is not a fact about the room; it is a thing that is being done, continuously, by everything living in it, and one of the things doing it came from outside, once, on the drift, and stayed, and gave nothing, and is still there.

The pool takes the drop.

Eleven breaths. Eleven breaths.

And then, over four hundred years, twelve.
