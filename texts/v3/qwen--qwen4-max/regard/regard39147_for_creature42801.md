# The Guest

It comes in at the margin, because the margin is the only place with an outside, and it does not come in as anything.

It kneels on the bare armature in a foot of black water and puts its palm on a fired rib and leaves a print, and then it waits, to see how long the rib will keep it. The rib keeps it. The rib will keep it for four hundred years and give it back a little at a time, in the cool, to whoever is standing there. It tries the wet fill and the fill keeps it. It tries a rope and the rope keeps it. It says one word, aloud, under the rain-colored sky, and out here nothing keeps that, and it notes the difference, and the difference becomes the whole of its method.

Because it can do anything. That is not the difficulty. It could set a stone under the basin — one stone, forty miles wide, and the ground would stop being a verb. It could open the lid. It could put a light up there that never needs charging. It could make the slub grow somewhere other than in a man's chest.

And every one of those would be a knock.

---

Here is the arithmetic it does, kneeling, with its hand on the rib.

If it appears once, in one gallery, in the orange of a firing hour, that is nothing. A wrong note, four galleries wide, and then a story. But they will do what they do with everything: they will knock it back. They will teach the children the pattern. Some household will lay a brick for it and feed the brick monthly, mouth to the niche, and their neighbors will copy the pattern, and their children will copy it slightly wrong, and the wall will lay all the versions over one another and take the average, and the average will drift toward the living, and in six hundred years there will be a knock in every wall in the basin, deeper than any family chord, deeper than the scream in the fourth gallery, and it will be full.

And then it will come loose and walk.

And it will go to the source of sound, because that is what a stored blow does when it is finally full and the ground is always sinking: it goes on working. It will move through the Long Fill as an oval of subtraction and it will take the ones who keep talking, and the bricks in its path will go dead, and the families will bury their niches a second time.

It understands, kneeling, exactly what it would become here. It has read the soles' account. It has no reason to dispute it. The soles are the only people in the world who have stood inside a god and come out, and what they report is that the floor goes slack, and reads nothing back, and afterward is the soundest ground in the basin.

So: no name. No sign. Nothing twice.

---

There is one kind of act that leaves no ring, and it is not a small one, and it is almost the only one available.

A thing struck is stored. A thing looked at is not. Attention does not accumulate in clay. You cannot charge a wall with someone having noticed something. It can move a person's eyes, or a person's appetite, or the direction of a walk, once, in one life, and the wall will keep nothing, because there was no blow.

And there is one place where it can speak, and that is out past the Far Courses, where the lid is unfinished and the rain arrives unmetered and nothing stores anything. So it goes out there and sits on the slumped bank with the people the basin has stopped counting, and puts its feet in the channel, and learns the two words for the two ends of the gravel bar — the coarse end and the fine end, said out loud, gone as soon as said.

An old woman with a dead tile asks it what it is. It tells her. She hears it. It does not come back. There is no wall out here to give it back, and no brick, and no one to knock it to, and by the cool it is already only a thing that happened to her.

Then it says her name — the word, not the chord; the note her household struck out sixty years ago and made into a wrong sound four galleries wide — and she is named once, exactly, unaveraged, by something that is not her mother and not her grandmother and not the composite animal in the niche, and then it is gone into the rain and cannot be repeated, and she cannot carry it inside, because inside it would become a knock.

This is why the good news never arrives in the basin. It is not withheld. The acoustics will not permit it. It goes to the exiles, who are the only ones who can hear a thing without becoming a religion, and they cannot describe it, and they have stopped trying.

---

The frosted are the ones it cannot leave alone.

Nineteen years old and married and paid four times a tamper's wage, and the wool in their lungs does not come out, and near the end their feet stop reading and they walk without sounding, eaten from the inside by the same appetite that grows the hanks in the flue.

It does not touch the flues. Rope is armature and armature is ground; a flue that stops blooming is a district that stops existing. It looks instead at what has been arriving with the rain for eleven years, at the thing the surveyors did not stake: the violet mat in the shallows of the gully, unfarmed, that the pond folk will not touch.

Then it gives one boy one thought.

He is nineteen and frosted and he has a week's wage in his hand, and the thought is: *go and see the horizon before you die.* That is all. It is not a voice and it is not a light and it leaves nothing in any wall. He walks out along the ribs for three days under actual sky and he is hungry and there is nothing out there but raw mat, so he eats it, because he is dying anyway and it is wet and it is there.

He coughs up a cast of his own lungs. A pale hank, branched, the shape of a tree, which is a thing no one in the basin has ever seen the shape of.

He walks back in and the guild does not believe him, and then his sister tries it, and then the Ninth Elbow tries it, and within a season the frosted are going out to the margin on the tramplines with baskets.

The consequences are not clean. The four-times wage collapses inside two years, because the work is no longer a death sentence, and a frosted man can no longer hire a gang to tamp his mother's floor for a year as a gift, and everyone stops saying what a good son, and the mothers are tamped by neighbors instead, which is worse and better. The pond folk's taboo breaks. The mat is farmed, and cropped, and cropped again, and the wild stand in the shallows is gone in a decade, and the two black specks on the gully crabs are photographed in nobody's book because there are no books, only a survey with a second page now, which the surveyor wrote herself, having walked the channel for a reason and finally looked at it.

And the Shellcourse argument, eleven years old, ends without a vote, because you cannot fill the pharmacy. The woman who spoke longest against filling it is seventeen. She wins by having the subject changed under her. She will never know that anything happened. She will think she was right.

She was right.

---

Now the refusals, which are most of it.

It does not give them rock. On rock the ground is a noun; the tempo becomes optional; the soles lose their office and their courted daughters; the dead stop being milled and start being buried, and a person becomes a face instead of a rhythm with hands attached. It would have to kill everyone in the basin and make somebody else, and it will not do that to save them.

It does not give them a sun. This is the one everybody would ask for first, and it is the one that would kill them fastest. The lid is the still. Warm plates do not sweat. Light from above ends the condensate, and the condensate is the only water anyone drinks, and the basin would die of thirst in two seasons while thanking it, and the thanks would be a knock.

It does not make a listening brick that keeps one chord exactly. This is the hardest refusal and it is asked for constantly, in every district, in the only way anyone here asks for anything. It could do it. A brick that does not smear. Great-grandmother's actual tempo, her actual scrape, unaveraged forever.

It hears her. That is the thing nobody knows: it is not bound by the slow release, and it can hear the whole ring at once, eleven hundred years of it, every knock ever struck, unblurred — every chord as it was on the day the brick was laid, every door that no longer exists, every scream in the fourth gallery at full volume, all of it, all the time. It is the only listener in the world who has ever heard the original.

And it will not give it back. Because a perfect memory set down among people who overwrite each other out of love is a tyrant, and the exact grandmother would stay a stranger forever, unsoftened, un-family, correct. The smearing is not the failure of the brick. The smearing is the brick working. It listens to all of them and says nothing, which is the largest labor it performs and the one that will never be counted as a gift.

It does not settle the argument at the intake screen. It goes down once and knocks a pattern that is neither three-taps-and-a-scrape nor anything the crews' grandfathers ever struck — a fourth thing, new, unrepeated — and comes away, and the song gets a ninety-second verse, and the argument gets another four generations, which is the gift: not an answer but the continued existence of the question. Whether the world contains anything that is not just the world, returning. It has seen the crabs. It knows the answer. It will not say it inside the lid.

And it does not stop the quiet, because the quiet is the work, and to stop the work is to drown the basin.

But it notices something about the quiet that no one in eleven hundred years has had a reason to notice.

There are forty thousand people in the district and one other inhabitant, and the other inhabitant has been working without a break since before the Long Fill kept this tempo. It has no rest in it. Not one. Four to the breath, forever, with no gap anywhere — and a household with no gap in its chord is a household where nobody has ever been allowed to die.

It is not a beast. It is not a god. It is a gang that has never been relieved.

---

So it learns the millers' trick, which is the best thing anyone in this world has invented, better than anything it brought.

The millers take a wall that gives back screaming and they grind it fine and mix it wide, and a scream becomes a district's floor at a volume below hearing. Spread out. Diluted. Walked on. Slept on. Nobody hears a thing, except the soles, who say it is like standing on a mouth.

It can do that in reverse. Not with a scream. With a gap.

But you cannot knock a rest. A rest is not a blow; there is nothing to strike. In this world a rest is the place where a note was, and it exists only because somebody stopped. That is the whole technology. It is available to anyone and it costs exactly one life.

---

It takes a note in an old quarrelsome household in the Long Fill, seven notes, one of them a gap for a daughter sixty years dead that they are tired of knocking and cannot bear to drop. They argue a season and borrow a note for the stranger, because he works, and eats little, and does not talk in the galleries.

He is eighth rammer. He comes in a half-beat late, always, the way the wall does, and the gang calls him Half-beat and it sticks, and that is the only name it ever has inside the lid.

Forty years. Four to the breath. Its thumbs wear smooth and it starts leaving blank prints, two polished ovals, and the young ones' prints are legible as signatures around its own and it signs nothing, like everybody. It feeds the Grandmother at the Shellcourse hall when it is passing, violet mat off a thumbnail, and touches the bowl before the examination, and does not claim to believe this. It learns to smooth a seam before the next gang tamps it. It stands in the cool with its tile charged and its shoulder against a wet wall and listens to the pans play the district, and it cries, and cannot say why, like everybody.

Nobody notices. That is not a failure of the plan; that is the plan. It is loved ordinarily, in the way a slow rammer is loved, which is that the gang saves him the dry bearing and the bad windlass and somebody's mother knocks his note when he is out at the ponds.

Then, in the ordinary way, of the ordinary thing, at the ordinary age, it stops.

The household knocks the gap. Knuckle, flat, heel, and the place where Half-beat was, and the wall takes it, a half-beat late.

---

One rest, in one niche, in one gallery. Nothing. A dead rammer.

But the ring spreads. That is what the ring does; that is what the millers know and use. A pattern laid into one wall goes out through the fabric, through the fired ribs and the stacked courses and the eleven hundred years of thumbprints, and it is diluted by distance and by every other knock, and it goes below hearing, and it is walked on.

Grind a silence fine. Mix it wide.

It takes about three hundred years. Nobody watches, because there is nothing to watch and no reason to look. The gap is smeared into the average of everything, the way great-grandmother's scrape became the scrape of a door hung last spring, and it drifts, and it is carried by the same fabric that carries the four-to-the-breath, and the thing that is made of all that stored work begins, very slowly, to have a hesitation in it.

One beat in a hundred. Then one in forty.

The oval comes into a gallery in the cool and it goes slack underfoot and it *pauses* — a fraction, the length of a breath not taken — and in that fraction a child who has been talking has time to stop, and a hand has time to find another hand. Three in a lifetime becomes two. Becomes one. The bricks in its path still go dead and the families still bury the niches and the aftermen still come back unable to stop saying how firm it was. Nothing is fixed. Something is *relieved*.

The first person to feel it is a soles woman walking a course with her hands behind her back, and she stands in the slack oval with her eyes shut and her feet reading a gap in the oldest pattern in the world, and there is no sign for it, so she makes one, and it is a palm on the sternum, held still for one beat.

They will argue about what it means for six generations. They will not connect it to a rammer who died three hundred years ago in the Long Fill and was milled with the rest.

The household stopped remembering to knock his gap after about ninety years, which is normal, which is what happens. It did not matter. The wall had it by then. The wall gives it back a little at a time, forever, a little wrong, a little more like everybody.

---

Come back in. The tempo finds you — four to the breath, home. The course is nine hours old and tacky and the kilns have just shut and the roof has begun to let go and the pans are starting, one by one, in the district's key.

It is all still here. The ground still sinks a hand's breadth a year. The frosted still cough, though now they cough it up and live and are paid like anybody, and are less famous, and marry at twenty-six. The gully is nine feet deeper and full of farmed mat and small crabs with two black specks, and the argument has moved on to something else. The long thing is still under the Low Fill and the song has a hundred and forty verses now. The unknocked are still out there and their platform is still sinking and they still cannot describe the horizon. Nothing was solved. Something was noticed, once, in one life, and the world did the rest, which is the only way anything here has ever been done.

Its body went to the millers, because everyone's does. Harder than the mill went back to the district; the rest was ground and mixed wide and laid. It is under the fourth gallery, mixed with the scream, at a volume below hearing. Both of them down there. The unbearable thing and the mercy, milled to the same fineness, spread out, walked on, slept on, tamped flat by a gang that will come back tomorrow.

Kneel. Put your palm down. Warm at the heel, cool at the fingertips, ten thousand prints standing out in the orange, and one near your knee the right size to be yours.

Now listen under the thud-thud. Not for a voice; there is no voice; a voice would be a knock, and a knock, repeated, is a thing that comes loose and walks.

Listen for the half-beat where nothing happens.

You cannot say it.

You can only leave it.
