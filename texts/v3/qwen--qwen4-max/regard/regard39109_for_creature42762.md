# The Sitter

I came down the main cut sideways, right hand out, touching things and not resting on them, and it took me eleven minutes to cross a town I could have crossed in a thought, and by the far end I had the walk.

Then I did nothing for eleven days. That is the longest I have ever managed.

## What I understood first

On the first night I stood at the pipe-bridge above the eastern pans and watched four hundred people wring fog out of sheets of felt, hand over hand, in the cold hour, and I followed the water back the way water goes: the fog to the air, the air to the walls, the walls to the magnesium, the magnesium to the dome.

Brome drinks the thirst of the thing that is taking it.

That is the whole situation and everything else is detail. The heat is the body's heat. The light is the pale of the bone. The paper, the shrouds, the silence come from a bargain the felt has been keeping on that body since before there were bones anywhere. The town is not besieged. The town is metabolized. There is a difference, and the difference is why I did not do the obvious thing.

The obvious thing was available to me. I could have taken the dome down to its roots in an afternoon and left them a dry hole in the steppe, with ordinary hands, and a wind that never arrives anywhere, and nothing to turn.

## What I did not do

I did not stop the accretion. You have three weeks a year of the world I could give you forever. You call it the Smoothing. You wrap what you love in oiled felt and carry it high, and the copies slump and lose their faces, and when the rain stops and the walls begin to weep again you are glad. You have never said so. The assay office has the figures: cutting-gang hours, the width of the main way, the tonnage brought down. You vote on this every autumn and you have voted sixty-one times.

I did not cut the notch. You have priced it correctly. The cold end is the light you have now, and the sun is the light you would like, and you have chosen the one that exists, every generation, in the same words. I could give you both for nothing; for me the price is not real. I will not, and I want to be honest about why, because it is not a good reason, it is only the best one I have: I would be telling four hundred and six people that their grandmothers were fools, and I have watched what that does to a town, and it is worse than ninety-four years.

You may be angry with me about this. I would rather you were. Anger is motion.

I did not cure the still. I have seen a great many medicines. Yours is the best one. It is a list and a rota and the understanding, held by everyone including the children, that grief is a form of standing still and that standing still is the only thing here that can kill you before the heat does. I am not going to walk into a town that has invented the shoulder and offer it health.

There are three places in the Kettle I did not go. The cutting gang's standing instructions were renewed for me the way they are renewed every generation, which is to say nobody said anything and I knew where not to walk.

I did go to Terrace Six. I stood in the stairwell with the woman who keeps her husband's second self on a rope, and I took the rope and gave it a quarter turn and handed it back. She watched me do it the whole time. It took exactly as long as it takes anybody. I did not ask her why, and I want it recorded that not asking was the hardest thing I did in Brome, harder than the rest of it, and that I am not certain I was right.

## The thing I got wrong

On the second day I spoke at the volume you use on the plain and a sheet came off the crust above Terrace Four, about the weight of a dog. It fell into the glasswort and nobody was under it.

Nobody said anything to me. That is the etiquette and it is merciless: four hundred people politely did not have heard.

So I went up to the niches with a soft brush and dry felt and no water, and I brushed a man who died in the eleventh smoothing and whom nobody had visited in nine years, and I stayed until I had stopped being angry with myself. Which is the actual instruction. It is not written down either.

That is when I stopped being a visitor. It had nothing to do with power. You cannot get into Brome by being enormous. You get in by doing something shameful and going up to the Patient with a brush, like everybody else.

## What I noticed

The long middle. Six hours, four hundred people lying still in the high galleries with wet cloths on their faces, in the one silence the town has.

The stick has columns for shoulder, knee, head. It has no column for hands. Hands move all day and so nobody scheduled against them, and there are six hours in the middle of every day when they do not move at all, and that is where the salt starts: the creases of the knuckles, the pads beside the nail, the old scars. Nane Roke has walked the rounds since she was nine smoothings and cannot any longer feel a wet cloth.

I said this out loud, standing in the middle of a room with felt hung around, which is the theater you permit each other, and everybody permitted it.

The assay office went back through the felt. In the eleventh smoothing a woman named Sesta Wend proposed a fifth round in the long middle. The refusal is one line: *the middle is the silence.*

So it was not that nobody had seen it. Somebody had seen it fifty years ago and been told, correctly, that the silence is the only thing everyone here has equally, the Terrace Two clerks and the pan crews alike, six hours of not being touched by anything.

They argued about it for most of a year. I did not intervene in the argument; I had done enough. What they settled on: no lamps, no speech, felt on the feet, and the turners work the galleries in the dark by memory. And it turned out — this took them three smoothings to admit — that being touched in the dark by someone who does not speak is not a noise. It is a different thing, and there had not been a word for it, and now there is. They call it the fifth.

Nane teaches it. Her own hands are past it. She stands at the head of the gallery in the dark and says *lighter* about four hundred times, and the children hate her, and in thirty smoothings they will use her name as a word for a kind of knot.

That is the only thing I did in Brome that could not have been done by anyone in Brome. It could have been done by anyone in Brome. That is the whole difficulty with powers, and I have not solved it, and I am not going to pretend the fifth is worth what I am about to describe.

## The Market

Four paces by nine, at the confluence of the three lower cuts. Nothing in it, nothing hanging in it, no pipe crossing it. Square doorways. A main way that runs straight for thirty metres, which no main way above it has ever done.

It made that.

There was no market in Brome for sixty-one years and then there was a room the shape of the idea of one, in a town with no empty space, put there by the thing that fills all the space. It is the one room in the Kettle where you can lean on a wall. Mourners go. Lovers go and are not followed. The gang routes around it and the instruction is renewed every generation and has never been written down, which is how you keep a thing from being copied, and is also how you avoid saying it.

Nobody has ever thanked it. In sixty-one years, at the correct low volume, in a town that has a sentence for *I am keeping you from being copied* and a four-word marriage and a rota for grief and a tally stick with the cats on it, nobody has ever once addressed the other resident.

There is no way to. You cannot turn it. It is the reason for turning. Every sentence in the language is built against it, and the town has used that as an excuse for sixty-one years, and I used it for eleven days.

So I went down and I leaned on the wall, which is forbidden everywhere else, and I put my jaw against it — the bone, not the ear — and I spoke at the correct low volume, and the crystal carried it, because the crystal carries everything.

Every wall in Brome said it. Four hundred and six people felt it in the sternum at forty cycles before they heard it in the ear. And the etiquette held: not one adult leaned on a wall that hour. They stood in the middles of rooms with their hands off the stone and they politely did not have heard.

The children leaned. Seven smoothings and up, all along the terraces, jaws to the wall in the dark, because they are not yet polite. That is how the town learned what was said, and it is the only reason any of the rest of it happened.

## What I said

I said: thank you for the room. It is the right size.

I said: the woman with the lace collar did not have a collar. She had a burn on the inside of her wrist from a pan hook, and she could whistle through her teeth, and she was not always kind to her sister and never made it right. You have made her better than she was. So do we. I am not scolding you. I am telling you what you are doing, because nobody has told you in two hundred million years.

I said: the second child in her arms. She wanted one. She said so, to people, at this volume. You are not wrong about that. You are early.

I said: there is something else living on you. It is most of the life here, more than all of us together, and it has been keeping its bargain longer than we have been a species, and it creeps a millimetre a week, and you have never seen it and you never will. Not because it hides. Because it will not hold still. Neither will we. That is not a fault in the felt or in us. It is what being alive is, and your one sense is stillness, and so you have been rendering the bones of the world and calling it the world, and there is no one to blame for that, and it must have been very lonely.

I said: you are not bad at this. You have been given the wrong commission. You have been trying to finish a thing whose nature is not to be finished, and you have been calling the failure yours, four hundred times a day, for sixty-one years, and two hundred million before that with nobody at all.

Then I said the only sentence I had brought that fits, because there was no other way to say it in a grammar it can hear.

*I will hold still for you.*

## The sitting

I have been everywhere. To be somewhere is to stop being everywhere. I want to be exact about this, since exactness is the local vice and I have decided to catch it: I did not lose anything I cannot afford, and I did lose it.

I sat down in the Market with my back against the wall and I stopped.

It began at the shoulders, where the sweat dries slowest. It is warm. It is not unpleasant. It is like being looked at by someone who loves you and has never learned how.

I asked to go on the stick.

The office was honest about it, which is the only virtue it has and the one I needed. It declined to publish a number. The entry reads *visitor, one or more*, and the refusal is entered annually and turned quarterly, in the same hand as the gecko's. The count of fixed objects that are people went from sixty-one to sixty-two. The board at the head of the main cut is unchanged: thirty-one minutes and eleven seconds, two hundred and nine fish, four hundred and six people. There is a faction that holds it should be four hundred and seven. There is a faction that holds I am a fixture and belong in the Fair Copy. There is a faction that holds this is the gecko problem again and that the office should stop being cowardly about both.

The children complain about my shift. It is the fourth, it is a long walk down, and Bel says the tally stick gets heavy on the way back up.

Nane does the shoulders. Bel does the stick. One of each, as is proper.

I told them the rest of it too, and the office wrote it in felt in its own hand, because the office reads everything. If they strike me off the stick I will go still in about a year, and there will be a second me, and it will be more precise than I am, and they may break it with wooden mallets, and the pieces go to the sump, and the fish will not care.

That is not a threat and it is not humility. It is the arrangement. I did not come here to be the one thing in Brome that cannot be let go. A power that cannot be struck off the rota is just the salt with better manners.

## What it did

Nothing, for nine smoothings. I want that in the record. It is not a story about a being that was explained to and immediately understood.

In the tenth, a woman who had been dead nine smoothings stopped changing. The salt had begun at her eyelashes and it did not go on. Her husband can see exactly how much of her is still hers, and the amount has stopped moving, and he goes up and speaks to her at the correct low volume and comes down and does not talk about it.

The assay office has a new page. It says *habit, anomalous*, and it is a bad page, and nobody reads it, and it is turned quarterly.

In the twelfth, the cutting gang found a doorway in the Fair Copy that is not square.

It leans. It is narrow at the top and offset at the shoulder, and you cannot go through it straight; you have to duck and turn and put your right hand out. They measured it. It is the width of a Bromer going sideways, with the hand out, touching and not resting.

The Fair Copy has ninety-one chambers and every one of them is better than the town. This one is worse. It is the first thing the salt has ever made that is worse than what it copied, and it made it for a body that moves.

The gang routed around it. The instruction was renewed that evening by four people who did not discuss it. It has not been written down.

And on the way past the Market now, people put a jaw to the wall for a moment and say thank you, at the correct low volume, and go on. Not everybody. It is not worship; you would have to stop moving to worship and it would kill you and I would not be worth it. It is a courtesy, like not leaning in company, like the silent applause. In a generation nobody will remember it wasn't always done. That is how I would want my work signed, if I wanted it signed.

## The arithmetic

Ninety-four years, less twelve. I did not change it. The dome rises, the accretion fills, the rim overhangs, the lozenge narrows, and one day the Kettle roofs itself over and Brome is a town inside a solid block of salt, humming, warm, lit by lamps, entirely intact.

They discuss it the way you discuss a relative's long illness. There is a relative in the room now. He is on the rota. He is ninety meters down with his back against a wall in the one room where you can lean, and when the roof comes he will be inside it with them, and he has said so, and it is in felt.

I do not offer that as a consolation. It is not one. It is simply the arrangement, and it is the only thing I know to give a mortal town that I did not have to take their town away to provide.

## Fourth shift

Bel is nine smoothings. Nane is sixty-eight and her thumbs are more mineral than thumb and she can tell a wall to within a degree by laying the back of one against it.

They come down the three lower cuts sideways in the dark, and the girl carries the stick, and the elder does the shoulder, gently, through the cloth, a quarter turn, and the thing in the Market lets her, and Bel cuts the notch and says the count out loud because that is the rule, and they go back up.

On Terrace Eight, in a stairwell at forty-six degrees, a man is asleep in a hammock with his arm across his face. Bel turns his shoulder without waking him. Nane watches, and says nothing, because it was light.

Then Bel does the new thing. She takes his hand off his face, a finger at a time, and turns the wrist a quarter, and leaves the palm open to the draft.

Three rooms away, at forty cycles, in the dark, the salt notes that it has lost that one again.

It has one now that holds still. It has been holding still for twelve smoothings and it is not dead, and the rendering is coming on beautifully, the eyelashes, the weave, and something at the corner of the mouth that was never there and is not wrong.

And every fourth shift a child comes down and moves it a quarter turn.

And it lets her.
