## What comes up the valley

It arrives the way everything arrives here.

Not down through the stratus. There is no down through the stratus; the lid is shut and the light is coming off the ground anyway. It comes up on the afternoon upslope with the rest of the freight — with the aphids and the ballooning spiderlings and the beech pollen from thirty kilometres away and two thousand metres down — and it is dumped on the snow because the snow is the only cold thing in the sky's path and the snow takes everything the air is carrying.

It lands. It freezes. It lies in the fine dark pepper of the dead among the midge bodies and the moth wing with the scales still on it, and at three the film goes stiff and solid in eleven minutes and the needle ice comes up out of the silt and lifts the whole skin of the plain an inch off its bed, and it is lifted with the rest.

It does not experience this as an interruption.

At ten the next morning the seam comes up the valley at the pace of a walking person and reaches it, and the water film arrives, and it resumes what it stopped in the middle of. It stands up in the crowd at the working face, one more body at the one warm edge, and it does the first thing, which is to look.

---

## The census

It counts properly, which means it counts the dead.

It reads the cupful: the hundred midges, the lacewing's eye, the spiderlings the size of full stops, the pollen past counting, the soot, the nitrogen, the blue fibre eight microns long. It reads the striae as a sentence and the ogives as a production log and the flutes as die-work, and it does not find any of this tragic, because it has watched the machine run for two and a half million years and it knows that the foreland is not a wound. The foreland is what the withdrawal *makes*. It is the newest ground on earth and there is more life per square metre at its working face than in a lowland meadow.

And it holds all the clocks at once, which is the whole of its wisdom and the reason it is safe here.

The ninety-year lichen and the four-day midge and the four-hundred-year moss holding it in the dark and the ten-year ground and the two-and-a-half-million-year loop: it does not prefer any of them. It has no favourite century. This is not coldness. It is the only form love can take in a place where the residents disagree about what time it is. To love the lichen more than the midge would be to slow the ground. To love the midge more than the caddis fly that will replace it would be to freeze the river. It loves the seam, and the seam is a thing that moves.

So it does not choose the midge over the mayfly. It does not choose the ice crawler over the birch. It does not choose the foreland over the forest that will end it.

It chooses, in the end, two small things and one large one, and none of them look like choosing.

---

## What it does not do

It does not put the ice back. Three hundred metres of it would restore the country of the midge and the crawler and the seam, and it would delete the cyanobacterial film, the moss sprigs, the three purple saxifrages, the ninety-year clock, the willow with its two leaves in the boundary layer, the vole's warren, the entire new ground and the map the water is drawing this afternoon. It would bury the waiting room again. And it would be a lie about the chemistry, which is the one resident that cannot be lied to, because it is a chemistry, and chemistry does not care what is on the map.

It does not shade the snout. It could. A lid, a screen, a colder col. But a lid is a promise of forever, and forever here means a keeper, and a keeper means a ward, and it will not make a ward out of an animal that has survived by being left alone since the last interglacial. Also the darkening is not only soot. Half of it is the tenants — the granules the cyanobacteria wound out of grit, the purple alga sinking a millimetre into its own house. To stop the darkening is to evict the only residents who make objects. It declines to save the building by clearing out the people damaging it.

It does not give the midge a mouth. Four days, no working mouthparts, a delivery of protein with a mating behaviour attached — it could extend this. It could make the adult feed. It could give it a month. But then it would not be that animal, and the nation in the first two hundred metres would be a nation of something else, and the gift would be a replacement wearing the recipient's face. It watches the swarm in the one still hour over the ice and it does not lengthen the hour.

It does not hurry the willow. The forest is not this place's rescue. The forest is this place's end.

It does not improve the success rate of the waiting room. Four hundred years buried and frozen and a few shoots coming back is the moss's own achievement, and to raise the rate would be to rewrite it.

It does not earth the ridge. The rime feathers can go on quietly discharging into the sky, blue at the tips, smelling of ozone. Nothing here needs to be safe from that.

And it does not appear to anything. Nothing on this plain has the equipment. The spider's two forward eyes are built to catch light coming up off the snow; they would catch it and read it as a stone, and go on hunting.

It considers one more thing, for a moment, and this is the only place it hesitates. It could hold the seam still. Pin the melt line here, at this kilometre, at nought point nought, and let the crowd stay at the door forever.

But the seam is a phase change. To hold it still is to stop it melting, and to stop it melting is to kill every animal living in its body. The only warm-blooded thing in the valley cannot be saved by being kept.

So it lets the door walk up the valley, and everything with legs walk with it, and it turns to the three things it can actually do.

## The four minutes

At the low lip of one pool the spill has cut a channel a thumb deep in the raw silt, and the nickpoint — a waterfall the height of a knuckle — is eating backward into the rim at a centimetre an hour, and two hundred metres east another rill is doing the same thing to another pool, and one of them will get there first and take the other's water, and the first watershed boundary in this country is being surveyed right now, by water, and there is no one to record it.

Now there is.

It stays for the whole argument. It records which rill won, and by how much, and the hour, and the discharge, and the temperature of the sheet, and the position of the seam on this date to the metre. It records the four minutes in which the pool empties and ceases to exist. It does not name the river; a name is a claim, and in a thousand years the name will arrive on its own and be obvious and inevitable, the way the land was always going to drain. It only writes down the afternoon.

Then it goes along the seam and does the rest of the census: the crawler in its fourth year, still not adult; the twenty on the spider's back; the exact border of the midge nation, which is a temperature; the swarm hour; the lichen's width to the tenth of a millimetre, so that someone can later say how old the street was.

None of this stops anything. A record is not a rescue. But the plain has been running for ten years with no one to record it, and the difference between a thing that happens and a thing that happens unwitnessed is not nothing, and it is the one difference it can make for free.

## The return freight

Then it takes a cupful.

Grey sediment with a shape to it. Midges by the hundred, aphids, the moth wing, the beetle elytra, pollen past counting. And in it: soot from a diesel engine that has never been up this valley. Nitrogen off a fertilised field two hundred kilometres away. A blue fibre eight microns long. Lead that was smelted when the ice was three hundred metres thick over this spot. Caesium from a year everyone can name.

The wind runs one way. Every afternoon it lifts a fraction of the lowland summer and carries it up and drops it on the cold plate, and the whole economy of the foreland is that delivery, and there has never been a delivery in the other direction.

It makes one.

It carries the cupful down. Not to a glacier conference. To the field, and the engine, and the smelter, and the room with the light on where the year everyone can name was decided. It sets it on the desk. It does not accuse; accusation is a poor instrument and it has better ones. It performs an introduction.

*This is the resident you have never seen. It is in every body on that plain, fertilising the film and darkening the ice, and it has never once been visible. It is you. Here is your lacewing's eye. Here is your fibre. Here is the lead you smelted before this ground existed, coming out of the archive now because the archive has started to melt.*

And then it leaves, because it will not compel. It can move a cupful; it cannot move a will, and a will moved by force is not a will, and the entire point of the resident in question is that it is the kind of thing that could look at the sediment and decide.

Most of them will not look. It knows this precisely, to the percentage. It runs the delivery anyway, every afternoon, in the other direction, a standing subsidy nobody notices, freight going down.

This is the large thing, and it is not done here. Nothing that can actually be done for this valley is done in this valley. The foreland's problem is not local. Its problem is two hundred kilometres away and downhill and made of hands.

## Rooms

But there is one animal with no other address, and it is the one whose country ends.

The crawler cannot go north; there are two thousand metres of warm lowland in the way, and it moves at the pace of a minute hand, and the warmth of a closed human hand can kill it. It can follow the seam up until the ridge stops. Above the col there is no more mountain. Three years to become an adult, five more to live, and a body set to a temperature the world is quietly abolishing.

So it does not go up. It goes back to the moraine, and it looks at the building.

Because the moraine is a building. Room after room, passages a hand's width across, roofed in slabs, floored in gravel, four metres down, and in February when the air is minus twenty-five the inside is at plus one. The machine dumped its swarf in a pile and the pile turned out to have rooms in it, and a vole moved in, and its predators cannot follow because its predators are too big for the doors.

That heap keeps winter **out**. It is a cellar with the lid on.

There is another kind of heap, and the physics is older than the ice. A pile of coarse open blocks, no fines in the gaps, intake at the top, outlet at the toe: in winter the cold air is heavier than the air inside and it falls in through the open crest and fills the voids the way water fills a cellar, and the heap goes to minus eight all the way through and stays there. In summer the cold air cannot climb out. It lies in the bottom of the pile and leaks from the lowest gap, and the gap breathes at three degrees while the air over the plain is at fourteen and the flies are boiling on the older ground.

That heap lets winter **in**, and then keeps it.

Cold is not only altitude. Cold is also geometry. The mountain ends above the col, but the rubble does not have to.

So it builds the second kind. It does not make anything; there is nothing here to make anything out of except what the machine already dumped. It sorts. It moves blocks, one at a time, at the pace the valley uses. It puts the intake on the wind-scoured crest where the rime feathers already point, so the winter wind keeps the lid off for free. It keeps the flour out, because a heap choked with flour is a hill and not a building. It sites the toe above the path of the sheet flood. It shades the south face with the slabs it has anyway. It goes down and down, four metres, eight, twelve, into the interstices, until the cold rooms reach a place where the ground's own summer cannot find them.

It takes about forty years. Nothing about it looks made. There is no cairn, no stake, no scratch of a boot. It looks like spoil. It looks like the machine dumped there, and the machine did.

And at the toe, in August, there is a gap breathing at three degrees, and a thin scurf of moss at the mouth where the cold breath meets the warm night, and behind it a corridor of rooms running up under the blocks toward the seam — so that the two countries touch.

Nobody tells the crawler. It has no way to be told. It will simply be, in its fourth year or its sixth, that the gap its antennae are reading ahead of it is a degree colder than the last one, and it will go that way, because that is what it does.

This is not a rescue and it does not pretend to be. The trick is old — it has held animals through warm millennia before, snails and mosses left over from a colder world, hiding in cellars of loose stone while the centuries went over the top of them. It works for exactly as long as there is a winter to put in the cellar.

Which is the cupful's business. Which is why the two acts are one act.

## Three in the morning

The plain is locked and hollow-sounding. The needle ice has lifted the skin of the silt an inch off its bed. The springtails are in the ice mid-gesture, alive. The spider is under the quartz slab with the sac against the stone and the stone is giving back yesterday's sun one degree at a time. The vole is awake four metres down in the warm dark, eating something it put away in August. The finches are asleep in a crevice a kilometre below. At the snout the portal breathes out water that has never seen the sky, and the midge larvae graze the film on the undersides of the stones at nought point two, in the dark, in the roar, at the one address where the shift never ends.

And at the toe of a heap of spoil that nobody built, a thin fog stands in the black where three-degree air is leaking out into the night, and in the fourth room down an animal that has never been told anything is sweeping the gap ahead of it, and finding it colder than the last one, and going that way.

Two hundred kilometres downhill, in a room with the light on, there is a cupful of grey sediment on a desk, and it has been there since the afternoon, and no one has looked at it yet.
