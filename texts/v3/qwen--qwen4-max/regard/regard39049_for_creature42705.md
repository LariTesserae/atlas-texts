## The Attention

It is not the tree that draws it. Trees like this are rare but not astonishing; a thing that has watched a great deal of matter arrange itself has seen stranger chemistries than nickel in a leaf.

What draws it is a loop it watches for four hundred rounds before it interferes with anything.

A limb loads. A child, who has been taught the difference between the tick of cooling and the tick of loading, tells her father. The father writes it in a book. In the morning a small quiet person puts a palm flat on the bole, waits, and takes a hand's breadth off it. Eighteen months later the answer arrives, and is measured, and is written down beside the kilograms.

Attention, speech, paper, action, patience, paper again. It has seen this loop in very few places. It has never seen it running so long on so little, in the dark, at forty centimetres, with a metal taste in everyone's mouth.

It decides the loop is the thing. Not the tree. Not even the people, exactly. The loop.

## The Rule

It reasons about this for a while, in the way it reasons, which is not sequential.

Almost everything it could do would be a subtraction.

It could put a spring in the northern corner beside the wet seam, and the ledger would go black in July forever, and the collectors would stop walking the gutters at dusk in the best light of the day, and would therefore stop being the kind of people that job makes, and would therefore stop marrying each other, and within three generations there would be no poems in the Rows and no one would know why. It could give the tree memory, so that it held its agreements the way paper does — and then paper would be unnecessary, and the accounts are the only difference between fauna and citizens, and the archive is where they keep their dead. To remember for them would be to demote them from a joke about fauna to a fact about it. It could stop the thickening, and there would be no Clearers, no Balance Book, no rate, no politics, no shouting sheds, no distinction between noise given and noise inflicted, which is the most sophisticated moral achievement in the basin and possibly for eleven kilometres in any direction.

It could send people. Two hundred and thirty-one is not enough people; it knows this better than the town does, having looked at the crossing plans. But people who arrive in numbers arrive loudly, and the last thing that came in from outside and made noise in a wall void has eleven pages and is read aloud once a year.

So it makes itself a rule, and the rule is short enough to be written in the Balance Book, and eventually is:

*A gift is admissible if it adds something to ask for, or reaches someone the asking cannot reach. It is not admissible if it removes the need to ask.*

By this rule it can do very little. It is pleased by this. It has been in places where it did a great deal.

## Thursday

It comes up the stair on a Thursday with the cart, in felt soles, carrying nothing, and waits three paces off for somebody to finish starting.

It does not step forward. It has worked out that this is the whole grammar of the place and that arriving fluently is more important than arriving well.

It learns the approach pattern with a lamp before it learns to send cleanly on the wire, and it is teased for its accent on the wire for eleven years by children who then lose their own. It has a fist like everyone. The operator knows it before the code resolves, the way she knows a footstep, and cannot say how.

It hires on at the shouting sheds.

This is not symbolic. Acoustic forestry is real work and the town buys larynxes because its own people cannot make the sound, and it has a larynx now, of the ordinary kind, and it sits two hours a day in a dead-wood box collared in felt and air and yells down a horn at a limb on the north side that has been carrying badly since the year One-Ninety. It is paid very well. By the third month it is hoarse. It stays hoarse; it could fix that and does not, because a shouter who is not hoarse by the third month is not a shouter, and because the wage is the wage.

Everyone assumes it was sentenced somewhere else first. It does not correct them. To correct them it would have to be heard from further away.

## What It Asks For

Three things, over ninety years, all of them politely, all of them slow.

**A second hollow.** Not a spring. The cistern at the base of Row D was asked for in the third decade and has been enlarged politely ever since, and it turns out that the power's only real advantage in this basin is that it can ask more precisely than anyone has ever asked, and wait longer than anyone has ever waited. It is not stronger here. It is *better at asking*. It spends forty years learning the grammar of the request — which is not language, it is the run of the grain under a palm and the exact placement of a wound and the season — and then it asks for two more fog nights of storage.

The slow hands refuse for eleven years. They say the cistern is already the largest void anyone has ever dared ask for and that the plate cannot spare it. The power does not do it anyway. It comes along the avenue with its lamp swinging, and stands at forty centimetres, and argues in the low ferocious register with its breath on their breath, and loses, and goes away, and comes back the next round. In the twelfth year it wins by three-tenths of a vote and the hollow begins.

In July they are still careful with washing. It is glad of this and does not explain why.

**A mushroom.** It will not take the nickel out of the leaf; the leaf is the town. It will not take the metal out of the air of the cell-house, because the rash on the inside of the wrists in the third year is the price of the wire that is the mesh that is the water. What it does is ask the beds in the undercroft, over sixty years, for a strain that binds the metal in the gut so that it passes through a person and out again.

The mushrooms already taste of nickel and are loved anyway. Now they are eaten every campaign, deliberately, by the cell-men, who complain about the taste, which is worse.

The rash comes in the third year and goes in the fifth, and is mentioned again, constantly, at every supper, because things that are going away get mentioned. There is a new column. One woman refuses to eat them and is not argued with, and her wrists are entered in the book beside the kilograms, and the power reads that line more often than any other.

**A hand's breadth.** This is the one it thinks about longest.

There are people who have stopped clearing. Their crotches close, their children leave for other rows and grieve, and an address is on one map and not the next, and nobody says they died, because they didn't, exactly, and there is no word for what they did.

The graves it leaves alone. Mourning ends when the walls meet because there is no longer anywhere to go, and that is a mercy the town found by itself and it will not put a finger on it. But the ones who let themselves be included while alive leave no mouth to put an arm into. There is no last visit. There is nothing to measure. The children have nowhere to take their hands.

So it goes to each of them, before, and stands near enough to begin, and asks whether a hand's breadth may be left at the base — not for them, they will be past needing it, but for whoever stays out.

Most say yes. Two say no, and are not asked again, and their places are smooth, and warm-ish underfoot, in the way of wood, and the Clearers walk past.

Where it is yes, the Clearers walk past a small hollow that will not close, and the surveyor draws it every year, and a granddaughter comes and puts her arm in up to the elbow, forever, and this is not resurrection and does not pretend to be. It is a visiting-place. It is the one gap in the round, extended by a few, by consent, on the ledger, in writing.

## Shifts

It takes the long-spouted can for four years and oils every bearing in the town twice a week, all of them, and therefore knows every hinge and drum and drum-brake in the place by feel, and therefore becomes the surveyor. It is not a rule. It just happens. It is the most argued-with person in the basin for a while and enjoys this more than it should.

It sits in the felt-collared booth with the drawer every ninth day, on the plate, saying nothing, for the whole shift, for a hundred and forty years. The drawer is visited rather than lived among. This is the only cure for that which is not an insult: someone who comes and does not need anything and does not require the drum to stop.

It lies in the bays. It is very good at listening — better than the dying, though it never says so — and it deliberately reports less than it knows, and only what it can say in the ordinary way, with a cheekbone against the wood, so that the reports go in beside the kilograms in the same hand as everyone else's. Half the town's structural intelligence has come from the dying. It is careful not to make that fraction smaller.

One year everyone forgets the wren, and it writes the date in the book, and no one notices, and that is the correct outcome.

It is good at finding the moth. It teaches the adults who mostly can't, one at a time, on the underside of the vault, in the flush, with a lamp; it does not touch the fraction, which has been the same fraction since the year Sixty and is not its to adjust.

And it walks the plain. It can bear clatter; that is the one thing its body has that theirs do not. So it goes to the stone houses on the coast and stands in the wind and the gulls and the doors, and waits, and comes back and says at forty centimetres: *she is well, she has a window, she is not coming.* It carries letters both ways for two hundred years. It sits with the fifteen-year-olds who last a week, who are ashamed, and walks them on to somewhere else instead of back, and does not tell the town, and they are written in the ledger as grafted out, and are spoken of warmly and rarely, and the power is the only one who knows where they are and it keeps the addresses on paper.

Once, a collector gives it quarrel-right. It is the highest thing that happens to it in the basin. It is quarrelled with, nose to nose, breath on breath, at a volume that would not wake a baby, about the rate, ferociously, for four hours, and it does not step back.

## The Word

It does not name anything it does.

This is deliberate. It has named things in other places and the names outlived the reasons and became laws and then became walls. So when they ask what to call the small hollows that will not close, it says *a hollow*, unhelpfully, and shrugs, and goes on oiling.

The word arrives about thirty years later, in the dialect, two syllables, all nasals and liquids, catchable with the back turned. It means something like *the kept gap* and something like *elbow* and is used mostly by children and mostly about their grandparents. There is a second word, later, for a hoarse person who was not sentenced. The power did not make either word. It made the hole in the language that the words came to fill, which is the only kind of naming it now trusts.

It is entered in the ledger itself with a date, annually, in the keeper's shrinking hand, on the line above the winter visitor. Not explained. The Rows loves things by writing them down.

## The Paper

It cannot promise them forever. Two hundred and thirty-one is not enough people and it has decided not to solve that, and it knows what it has decided, and it does not pretend the decision is free.

So one night in the cold room, where the year's pollen sleeps in folded paper, the keeper of the Balance Book asks it the question directly, at forty centimetres, in the register that would not wake a baby: *if there is no one left, who reads it?*

And it says: *I will. All of it. Aloud. Once a year. At conversational volume.*

And she writes that down, because that is what they do, and it is the last thing in the Balance Book in her hand.

It keeps the promise. It keeps the map room dry, which is the whole of its ongoing miracle and takes almost nothing, and it turns the two hundred frames of the very slow film with a thumb, and it reads the nurseryman's ledger and the crossing plans and the eleven pages about the rat and the kilograms and the millimetres and the reports of the dying and the date of every wren and the wrists of the woman who would not eat the mushrooms, aloud, once a year, to an audience sitting very close, and the audience gets smaller over a long time, and it never raises its voice to bridge the gap, because the gap has to close, and because there is no one left to close it, and because it has read every page and knows exactly where the threshold sits.

It reads below the threshold.

The rooms stay open.
