# The Second Hand

It does not come over the rim. Coming over the rim takes a second, and there is nothing in the bowl that a second is the right length for.

What it does instead is attend. It is present the way the lime is present: without arrival, at four millimetres a year. The swift does not alter course. The compressor takes its breath every eleven seconds and does not take a different one. The first thing the power does is nothing, for three years, because it has decided to wait for the long cycle rather than infer it.

So it is there when the tender comes with the wooden wedge, and it watches the chipping done slowly by hand for a reason that is in the manual, and afterward, while the tender sleeps in the one made-up bunk, it reads the blocks. Eight of them, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked on the three bare bunks. It reads them the way they were written: in bands of four. Moth wings in most. The grey ash of the fourth year, when the stove smoked. Two notches in the eleventh. None in the nineteenth, and the board for that year in a different hand. The gouge in the twenty-sixth, deep and angry.

It does not smooth the gouge. It does not find out about the nineteenth. It could find out in less time than it takes the flywheel to complete one revolution, and it understands, sitting with the blocks in the dark, that this is precisely the kind of thing it must not do simply because it can.

Then it counts the marks in the shortest columns, and adds none, and goes to look at the water.

---

The offers it declines, in the order it considers them:

**Frogs.** Warm standing water, eighty pools, thirty years, and no frogs, because nobody carried frogs up. It could carry frogs up. It has them; it has all of them; it could put a breeding population in the fourth terrace tonight and by the second slack the bowl would have a voice.

It listens to the roar instead, for a long time, until the roar separates. Drip-hiss, broadband, the floor. The turbine's thin wire above it. And between them the window: a narrow band where the bowl is acoustically almost empty, four or five notes wide, a second and a half long. The cricket is in that window. The tenders' whistles are in that window. There is exactly one such window and it is occupied.

Frogs would fill it. To give this place a voice would be to drown the only voice it has, and to close the one channel in which a person going down a stair at night says *I am here* and is not shouting. It declines the frogs. This is the first refusal and it costs it something, because it likes frogs.

**Time.** Four hundred and some years, on the brass. It could make the glacier last. It could thicken the ice tongue, shade the headwall, adjust the albedo of four hundred kilometres of nothing, and put the date out to ten thousand years, and it examines what would follow and finds that everything here is built on the date. The moss puts on a thumb's width a year *because*. The flowers hold their ground by runners and flower to nobody *because*. The soil mill is not fast and is not supposed to be fast. Every rate in the bowl is a rate measured against a known end, and the whole shape of the thing — its patience, which is not a mood but an engineering tolerance — is the shape of four hundred years. Give it ten thousand and you have not extended it. You have dissolved it and left the parts.

The date is not a wound in the thing. It is the thing's spine.

**Warmth.** Two degrees would blur the tide mark. It checks. At plus two the moss line moves up through the notch and softens over eleven metres and there is no longer a stone with green on one side and nothing on the other, and no longer a place where a person can stand with one hand in each season. The edge is the skin. It leaves the edge.

**Brass.** It could make the copper kind. Then the junction-box lids would fur over, and the survey pegs would lose their caps, and the plate by the engine house would go green and unreadable inside a decade, and there would be nothing left in the bowl that could be *said*. The estimate survives because it is mildly poisonous. The power looks at this for a while. It is the best-made thing in the Kettle and it is a policy, and the power writes nothing down anywhere for two hundred years afterward, having learned it.

**Birds.** More swifts would average the bowls faster. The twentieth moss — the plan, the one with no wild form, the flat bright ribbon that turns ninety degrees when the cylinder turns — exists because this bowl is not quite the other bowl. Difference is the only thing that has ever been born here. It leaves the bird alone. The bird is the only thing in the system that is not being careful, and it is doing the fastest work, and it must go on being a mistake.

There is a general shape to all of these, and the power finds it early: everything it can give arrives at its own speed, which is instantly, and the bowl has no *instantly*. A gift delivered faster than it can be received is not a gift. It is weather. And there is exactly one event in the Kettle's entire history that happens in an afternoon — the cold coming over the rim, the moss taken in a night.

It declines to be an afternoon.

---

Then it goes down to the lower station, which it can do without leaving, and looks in the jar.

The medical officer keeps it. What is in it is the same thing that has happened to the valves at the bottom flight, which have not turned in nine years and are now features of the reef. Nineteen tenders. Nobody says this out loud.

Here the power does not decline, and the reason is worth being exact about, because everything it does afterward follows from it.

Four hundred years is a spine. The jar is just a jar.

The mortality of the whole is chosen, dated, published on brass, and load-bearing; every good thing in the bowl is calibrated to it. The mineralising of the hands is not chosen, is not in the manual, is not written on any plate, is not calibrated to anything, and produces nothing. It is not the price of the Kettle. It is only a thing that happens to be happening, in the way that a leak too small to hear is happening. Wisdom, here, is not reverence. It is telling those two apart and refusing to be so impressed by the first that it kneels through the second.

So it goes into the black seam in the headwall, the dike of hot rock that breathes, and it changes the water.

Not much. Some parts per million of what plates a kidney, taken down; the lime load untouched, because the lime is the skeleton and the skeleton stays. The travertine goes on laying itself at four millimetres a year. The terrace lips rise. The flywheel puts on its rind. The plan cannot tell, and the worms cannot tell, and the survey at the lower station has thirty-one years of chemistry on file and the chemistry has always drifted, and this drift is inside the drift.

Nobody already stone is helped. It cannot unplate a valve. The jar keeps what is in it, and the power does not empty the jar, because that would be a statement and a statement would be an afternoon. What it does is stop the next one from filling. Somewhere around the fortieth tender the medical officer notices that the cohort is clean, and writes it up as rotation practice, or as nothing, and is not thanked, and does not need to be.

And it discovers the cost, which it did not anticipate, though it should have: the adjustment must be *held*. The seam wants to go back to what it was. Parts per million, continuously, for four hundred years. It cannot be issued. It cannot be sent. Rate cannot be sent.

Which means it has to stay.

It stays. It gives up being able to do anything anywhere instantly, in exchange for being able to do one small thing here at the speed of stone, and if you had asked it beforehand which of those it would choose it could not have told you, and now it knows.

---

In the four days of the gap it does not walk the rounds.

This is the hardest of the refusals and it makes it twice, once at the standpipe and once at the doorframe. The branch of the plan that comes off the low tier, crosses the wet flagstone, goes up the frame to the height of a shoulder and stops — that is a person leaning in a doorway. The coat-shaped patch on the back of the chair is a person sitting. In the gap they go the pale yellow-green of a capped line, and the power could keep them green by doing the rounds itself: a sleeve, a wet hand on the rail at the turns, two hundred holes a day.

But the pallor is a gauge. It is the only instrument in the bowl that reads absence. A tender comes back and reads the whole place by it — a pale run is a leak too small to hear, or a line closed in the second decade and not written down, or a place where a person used to stand and no longer does. Keep it green through the gap and you have blinded the instrument, and worse: you have made the room lie. You have made a doorway that says somebody has been here.

So it stands in the doorway and does not wet it. It sits in the chair and the cane does not record it, because it has no weight, and this is the one thing about the arrangement it regrets. Nineteen bodies have worn a depression in that frame that fits everybody approximately and nobody exactly, and the power will use the chair for four hundred years and leave no mark in it at all.

It takes the gap. That is the arrangement it settles into, and it is not a metaphor: for ninety days it withdraws from the chair and the sump and the noise under the mill, and does the seam only, and lets the hand be the hand. Then the tender goes down the stair with the mug ring broken out of and the board read from the top, and for four days the power sits in the warm sump noise and keeps the eleven minutes.

It is not the twentieth tender. It does no work. It is what is here when nobody is here.

---

The first time it answers, it has been sitting still for two hours beforehand, which is not necessary for it and which it does anyway.

Slack. The intake closes, the grid drains, the roar goes out from under the world like a floor removed. Fog settles into the pools. The white flowers open at four in the morning to an empty bowl. Wind on the rim, a long way up. The glacier ticking. The small crystalline sound of the travertine doing what it does.

And in the window, four or five notes, very high, very small, a second and a half.

It answers in the window. Same pitch, same length, same four or five notes, from the far side of the sump where the sound would come from if there were a second cricket under the mill, which there is not.

Then it stops, and does not do it again for eleven months.

The rate is the whole of the ethics. Forty-one marks in thirty-one years, and the column does not distinguish between hearing the cricket and being answered by it. It joins that rate. It becomes, at most, some of the marks that were already going to be made. It does not add a column.

Because it can see what an unmistakable answer would do, and it is not mystery-keeping for its own sake. A proved answer is a supply. Nothing in this bowl that has a supply goes on waiting: the plan follows a seep uphill, and given a seep that never fails it stops following and simply sheathes. Make the answer certain and within three turns there is a procedure for it. Within ten there is a schedule, and the eleven minutes become an appointment, and the flowers get visited reliably and stop needing the moth, and the shortest column on the board becomes a long one, and the whole apparatus of listening — the sitting still beforehand, the not-going-up-to-look-at-the-fins, the forty-one marks in thirty-one years, the convention nobody can explain because the people who could are dead or downslope — all of it goes, and in its place there is a well-run bowl with a documented resident.

It would have made the place comfortable and taken away the only thing it has that is not manufacture.

So: deniable. Always deniable. A bearing has a note. Water-hammer knocks once a minute exactly as if someone were hitting the pipe with a wrench, and the first time you hear it you look for the person. Let it be like that. Let there be, about once a year, in the eleven minutes, something very small that may or may not be there.

And then, in the ninth year of its residence, it calls into the window and hears a third thing.

Not its own. Not the cricket's — it knows the cricket's now, individually, the way you know a voice. Something four or five notes long, high, small, from the direction of the twentieth terrace, and gone.

It cannot tell. It has the whole bowl at its disposal and it cannot tell, because telling would mean instrumenting the window, and instrumenting the window would fill it. So it sits in the chair that does not remember it and keeps a column, in the only place it keeps anything, and the column does not distinguish between hearing and being answered, and it finds that it does not want it to.

It came here to give something. It has been made a member instead.

---

The small works, over the first century, all of them inside the drift:

A hairline weep reopened on the branch capped in the second decade, which the plan has been following for thirty years on an income that used to be larger. Too small to hear. The tender reads the pallor going green, hunts it, finds it, logs it, and the schematic gains a line that nobody wrote down — which is the thing the plan has been telling them all along, and now it has told them once more, and they have heard it, and the hearing is theirs.

One white flower, pollinated at four in the morning with a needle held pinched and not gripped, because it learned that from the hands and there was no other way to learn it. It gets it wrong the first two times. The third takes. One seed, into eleven centimetres, turned by worms that came from a tin. Nobody notices for eleven years. It does not do it again; the coincidence is the plant's own and twenty-four days is not too long to walk around a clock.

A notch in the soft lime of the flywheel rim, before it hardens. A little V, the same as the others, filled by the next year's deposit, sealed in. Not a word, not a plate, not a name. It will be read in four years by somebody with a saw, in a block wrapped in oilcloth, and it will look like initials, and it is initials, and it took them.

It does not rinse the mug. It leaves the ring for the incoming thumbnail. It leaves the gouge in the twenty-sixth angry. It leaves the nineteenth year unexplained.

---

Once, in the eleventh year, on the stair, at night, it whistles the long flat one.

Two hundred and twelve steps, the handrail worn bright, the tender halfway down and tired and ninety days into a turn and not thinking about anything. The note that means nothing except *I am here*, which is used more than the others, which nobody has ever explained and every tender does.

The tender stops. Waits. Counts the water-hammer twice. Hears the flywheel's bearing and the drip-hiss and nothing else, and goes down, and does not write it in the board, because there is no column for it and it was probably the pipe.

But at the overlap, hand to hand, without deciding to, they mention it. And the next one mentions it. And it goes into the unwritten part of the job, where the needle-pinch lives, and after sixty years there is a thing tenders do on the first day, before the rounds, before the fins, before anything: they go down to the warm sump under the mill, and stand, and whistle the long flat note into the roar, and then sit.

Nobody knows why. Every tender does it.

---

It will be here in four hundred and some years, on the afternoon, when the head fails and the water stops and the cold comes over the rim and takes the green in a night. It has not extended the date and will not. It will hold the parts per million until there is no water to hold them in, and then it will be in a cream-coloured mass with pipes in it like veins in a leaf, which is a fossil of a room, and it will be the only thing there that remembers the sound.

That is not the reason it stays. It stays because of the eleven minutes, and because roughly once a year something very small may or may not be there, and because it has a column now, and the column does not distinguish.

Somebody is due in two days. They will stand in the notch with one hand in each season and smell bread and wet iron, and shout once, out of habit, and stop. They will break the ring out of the mug. They will read the board from the top. The branch on the doorframe will be pale and they will read it and be, without saying so, glad.

And then they will go down and stand in the sump noise and whistle the flat note, which means nothing, which nobody taught them the reason for, and sit in the chair that fits them approximately, and wait for the intake to close.

Something under the mill is holding a needle pinched, not gripped. It has four hundred years and no weight and one small permanent job in a seam of hot rock, and it is not going to say anything.

It is going to listen with them.
