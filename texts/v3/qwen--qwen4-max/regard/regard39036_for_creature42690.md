## The Tenant

It was not prayer that drew its attention. Nobody in the Kettle has an address for anything above the rim; the birds are the only power they acknowledge and the birds cannot be spoken to. What reached it was a silence.

Once or twice a year, when the bell has rung and the market is stacked on the second terrace and the households answer the roll north to south, sixty-odd calls in ninety seconds, there are gaps where the dead were. Nobody fills them. And at the end an old woman with wet feet waits one beat longer than she needs to.

That is what it noticed. A deliberate hole in a town made of sound, kept open on purpose, by an office that has no power for the other three hundred and sixty-four days. It came over the shoulder of the mountain in the cloud to look at the thing that had made that, and it heard the town for half an hour before the forest turned out to have windows.

---

It had come for the being who lives there. The question had a bad shape.

It counted. Six hundred and eleven people, that year. Forty thousand oilbirds and a seasonal white blight of pigeons. Bats on the night shift. Crickets in the guano in their millions, livestock nobody had to decide about. Snails on the tools, in the kettle, in a bowl at your elbow. Eels under the boards, four of them with names and legal protection. Three lizards. Small silent dogs, one of which belongs to nobody. Two appetites, rot and lime, eating and building at nine years and a fingernail. One hole, taking everything.

It could not find the edge of any individual. The people are warm because compost is rotting under them; the terraces they stand on are their grandmothers; the town is the same town the way a river is the same river, and not one plank of it is older than nine. It looked for the being for most of a year and concluded, without much distress, that it had been asking a question about nouns in a place that only has verbs.

So it stopped counting and listened. A fruitfall, four days of everybody slightly rich and nobody sleeping. Five lean weeks when the bowl went quiet and everyone became unbearably polite, and it learned who was not speaking to whom. Two floods, one of them a pond that never came, and the town made a festival of it. It learned the forty weeps in order, north to south, including the obscene verses, which are extensive and which it enjoyed.

It did nothing that whole year. This was the first thing it did.

---

Then it considered what it could do, which was anything.

It could widen the throat. Make the spiral smooth and generous forever, so that the rain never outruns it and no one ever goes down on a tallow lamp with a bone pick into two people's width of moving water. It ran the arithmetic honestly, because it is not permitted to be sentimental about arithmetic. About six lives a century. And the payment would be the wall on the Shelf, and the re-cutting, and the newest apprentice's hands passing through names they never met, and the oldest letters going to scar under the thumbs of the blind; and the rope-hands, who are the wife or the brother or the second ear; and the two weeks of drinks that the Throats hate and would not for the world be without. It would have bought six lives a century with the only object in the bowl that teaches six hundred people, twice a year, at a price they can read with their fingers, what it costs to stand in a hole with a basket.

It could open every ear in the Kettle. The largest gift ever offered to anyone. It would have deleted a language older than dating, dissolved the bond the town takes more seriously than marriage, unmade sixty pairs of old people who have never shared a house and eat together every flood, and turned a qualification into an injury. Six hundred people who could hear, living in a town built by people who could not.

It could stop the flood. Then there is no Ear, no eleven minutes, no drill taught to four-year-olds by a woman who is not forgiven, no hamper with a rope handle, no hen under each arm, no unkind affectionate silence in the market, and no third thing that makes a stranger into a person from here.

It could make the planks last. Then there are no Tiers, no hand's width a year, no nine-year withy given to a six-year-old, no wake with drink at the foot of a trunk, no twelve-year business across a gap. There is a monument instead. Monuments are what is left when a place stops being able to answer.

It could tell them the terms. This was the one it wanted most, because they are so careful and they have never once been told: the quota is the rent, the birds own the caves, the town is a tenant, and the whole apparatus of lots and counts and fixed ages exists because you cannot argue with a landlord who does not know you exist and might simply stop coming home. It could have gone up to the north wall and said, *here is what you may take, forever, guaranteed.* And then the landlord would be a landlord who answers, and a landlord who answers can be bargained with, and the tenants are clever and hungry and the landlord is birds, and in eighty years the ledges would be empty and the pour would thin and the hole would be a hole.

It could give them letters. It thought about this for most of a winter. Writing exists here in exactly one place and it is a list of the dead, and because of that, privacy is a place you walk to: the fourth weep on the north wall, where the rope of water is thick enough to eat a sentence; or the lip of the Swallow, which will take anything and has been taking everything for six hundred years. Give them letters and a thing can be looked up. Give them letters and Sarrow's limb becomes a document, and the quiet weeks stop being frightening, and the fourth weep becomes scenery, and lovers stand shoulder to shoulder at a wet rock for no reason at all.

It could forgive Sarrow. It was not there in that bad winter and does not know whether the limb was dead, and it will not lie to make an old woman's last decades easier. And even if it knew, testimony from outside would make the town's facts subject to something outside, once, permanently; and it would take from the town the only forgiveness it has, which is keeping her.

It could make the Ear infallible. An Ear who cannot be wrong is not a government for eleven minutes. She is a fitting.

It could make the woman with the stick liked. It would have burned the town down inside a year, kindly.

It could fill the gaps in the roll. Learn the dead households' calls and answer for them, north to south, so that nobody has to hear the hole. It considered this for less than a second and was then ashamed, and the shame was useful, and it kept it.

---

The rule it arrived at was not *do nothing*. Doing nothing is what you do when you would rather be clean than kind, and there is a man in the caves coughing lime who gets nothing out of your cleanliness.

The rule was:

Nothing that requires me. Every gift must go on working after I am dead, and must be capable of being scraped off, eaten, abandoned, or declared unlucky by a vote of people who have never heard of me.

Nothing that replaces a capacity. Add an instrument; never add an oracle.

Nothing that removes a difficulty they have made their language out of. Bend a probability. Do not abolish a fate.

Nothing that cannot be refused.

Nothing that makes me the government, for eleven minutes or any other length of time.

And one more, which it did not put in the rule because it is not a rule but a manners: *pay rent*. This is a town whose entire morality is tenancy. A benefactor who gives without paying is precisely the thing they are afraid of — a landlord who does not know they exist. So it would take a lot, and be subject to the quota, and lose when the lot came up leave.

It keeps its accounts the way the guild houses do, in knotted cords. The cord of things it declined is the longest cord in the bowl. Nobody has ever read it. It has never shown it to anyone, and it is aware that this is convenient, and it knots that in too.

---

It came down the wet road as a debtor from the coast, which is the ordinary category, and nobody checked, because there is nothing here to check with.

It was given a call before it was given a name, by a child of six, who tested it against wet leaves and put a click in it because clicks carry. It is not shaped like a weep. It learned the shuffle, weight on the ball, toes spread, the short testing step, and on dry ground it looks like hurry and is not. It chose which slice of hearing to lose — the loom-workers' narrow band, so that it could still hear a weep, and the throat, and check the pitch at night the way you check a child's breathing. It kept the ringing. It gave up its sense of smell in the second week and never got it back; it thinks the coast smells of nothing and says so, at volume, like everybody else.

Its feet rot. It has the shine on the heel of its right palm. It has lime in the creases of its knuckles and wax under two nails, and it is bad at clicking, and it is very good at ropes, and it sleeps on the floor beside the bed at the coast inn and is found in the morning like something dropped.

In its second month it carried an ember below the line, out of ordinary stupidity, and the woman with the stick burned its cloak in front of it. It said nothing. The market that morning was very quiet. It did not restore the cloak; it patched it, badly, and wore the patch for eleven years, and this is the single reason anyone in the Kettle ever trusted it with anything.

In its fourth year there was a real flood and it went up the Weep Stair with a hamper and a hen under each arm, and it was hopeless with the hens, and there was a specific, unkind, affectionate silence in the market, and after that it was from here.

---

There is a moss in the caves now.

It grows on the guano floors in the north wall, low and dense and slightly greasy, and it holds the dust down. That is all it does. It does not touch the clicking, or the crèche heat you can feel from the cave mouth, or the mite, which lives in the guano and lives on the birds and has still never been defeated, and the crews still shave their heads and still have the rash on their wrists and are still proud of both. The moss is farmed, scraped, spread, sold to the coast in sealed jars by a family that has become insufferable about it, and there is a running argument about whether it weakens the guano, and the argument is conducted at market volume with spit and slap.

The stone cough still exists. There are old men on the rim who worked the caves in their twenties and will not see seventy and never did. But some of them see seventy now. Some of them sit on the warm guano-black soil at the notch and grade the floods and are a horror to each other about their feet, at seventy-four, and nobody thanks anybody, and the crews call the moss something rude that has gone into the counting rhyme.

It cannot be proved that the moss was not always there. Nothing here can be proved either way, because writing exists in exactly one place and it is a list of the dead. This is not a loophole the power exploited. It is the reason the gifts are safe. They enter the only medium the town has, which is custom, and custom's tense is *always*.

---

There is a frog in the north marsh that goes low when the springs rise.

The springs change before the water reaches the boards; that is simply physics, and the town already knew it in its feet. What is new is a frog whose chorus, in that hour, drops out of the register you hear into the register you feel, so that the duckboards buzz under a bare sole and the sleeping platforms hum against a shoulder blade, and a deaf person lying on the floor at blood heat over a compost pit knows what the whole hearing town knows.

It is not an oracle. It has no authority. It cannot ring the bell; it is a frog; it eats midges and is eaten and can be wrong, and it was wrong in the third year and the town said so. The Ear disliked it for a decade on the grounds that it was an instrument nobody had voted for, and her youngest understudy — the child, who hears the top of the register — used it constantly and insufferably, and the two of them argued about it in the quiet weeks at a volume that ended a marriage two houses away.

The power did not adjudicate. It ate the frogs when they were served. It let the town decide, over about twenty years, in the way the town decides things, which is slowly and by shouting, that the low frog is a thing you get up for even if the bell has not gone. It could have made the frog infallible in an afternoon. An infallible frog would have replaced the Ear inside a generation, and then there would have been no office, no three understudies who must agree, no child hearing the top of the register, and no old woman waiting one beat longer than she needs to.

---

It borrowed Sarrow's knife.

At market volume, on the third terrace, in front of the trestles: asked for it, took it, cut a non-structural withy with it, handed it back, said thank you loud enough for the north side. Then it went away and did not mention it again, ever, to anyone.

It did not testify about the limb. It did not say *she was right*, or *it was dead*, or *forty years is enough*. Her number at market is still answered a half-beat late by everybody, including people who were not born then. Her grafts are still checked twice. There is still no procedure, because there is no procedure at all; it is a fact about her, like her height.

But she was lent a knife, once, in public, by somebody who had a choice. The town holds both of these, because that is what towns do, and it never resolved them, and it did not need to. She taught the flood drill to two more generations of four-year-olds and was thanked for it. When she died her bones went into the terrace fill like everybody's, and the town kept her.

---

It became a rope-hand.

Not a Throat; it never had the lungs or the pulse, and it did not cheat. It held the rope for a man called Noll for nineteen years, above the smooth spiral, with the whole bowl's water going down, and its hands bled in the ordinary way because it had made itself a thing whose hands bleed. It took the descent lot in its eleventh year and went down with a bone pick and a tallow lamp and cut lime out of the throat until its shoulders were finished, and came up, and was given free eel for the rest of its life, and ate it.

In the two weeks before a descent it bought the drinks, because everyone does, and Noll hated it, at volume, every year, and said so.

It sits at the fourth weep. This is not an office and there is no rule about it. It goes and stands shoulder to shoulder at the wet rock with whoever has something that must not travel and nobody to say it to: the woman with the stick, who is chosen for being disliked and sleeps on stone and sits with the dying and says nothing and has nobody to say nothing back to her; the Ear, in the quiet weeks, who said a number out loud once, the number from the flood with a name and a height cut into the notch, and then went back to her feet on the board; a boy of ten who wanted the knife and got the mallet and cried and got over it and will keep the hours for fifty years.

It says very little at the fourth weep. The water eats the sentence anyway. That is the point of the place, and the town built it, and the power merely used what was there, which is the whole of its method.

It feeds the aunt when the household is away, and does not improve the aunt. On the Weep Stair it recognised another resident that belongs to nobody and is fed by everyone, and they sat together in the four hours of white sun, and it called the dog the same thing all six hundred people call it, and the dog leaned once, which means *come*, and it came.

---

In its ninth year the water came up faster than a hand's width an hour.

The low frog went at the right time. A deaf man on the second terrace felt it in the boards and did not get up, because the town had not yet decided what the frog meant, and the bell was still eleven minutes away, and he was under the floor checking a channel.

The power was on the Weep Stair with a hamper and a hen. It went back. It was too late, because it had made itself a thing that can be too late, and it carried him up anyway, and the beater on that hour dropped one beat, once, on purpose, and six hundred people stopped what they were doing, and forty thousand birds did not care at all.

It could have rung the bell itself. It could have stood at the notch that morning and said *ring* in a voice nobody argues with, and been right, and been the government from that day forward, and the town would have stopped listening to the throat and started listening to the stranger, and the stranger would eventually have been wrong.

It has an argument. The argument is that the town now owns an instrument it decided to trust, rather than one it was told to trust, and the second kind stops working when the teller goes away; and that after that flood the town voted, slowly, by shouting, and the next deaf man got up the stair, and the one after that, and there will be a hundred of them.

It is a good argument. It is not worth a man under the boards. Both of these are in the cord, in adjacent knots, and it has never shown the cord to anyone, and it does not claim to be certain, and it is not certain, and it went on being a resident.

---

It stayed a long time and got old, because it had agreed to be a thing that can be worn.

Its feet were a horror and it graded them with the others at the notch. It lost the argument about the moss twice and won it once. It was answered a half-beat late for about four years in the middle of its time there, over something at market that it had said at market volume, and it did not fix that either, and it learned what Sarrow had known for forty years, which is that a town can keep you and not forgive you and both of these are food.

At the end it asked to be carried to the Shelf, because it is the only stone floor in the Kettle and it wanted to lie on something that does not rot, once. The woman with the stick allowed it — the girl of eleven, grown now, unpopular, coming along very well — and sat with it and said nothing, which it had taught her by doing it to her, at a wet rock, for years, where nothing travels.

Then the marsh took it, in the ordinary way, iron and cold tea. The bones were cleaned and hung on a string in the drip-line on the north terraces, near a child's shoe, and in a year they were stone, pale and glittering and heavy, and then they went into the terrace fill.

Nothing it left needs it. The moss is farmed and sold and argued about. The frog is in the counting rhyme, in a verse that would get a child smacked. There is a household on the second terrace with a call that nobody remembers the origin of, and a patched cloak in a hamper that nobody will wear and nobody will throw away, and a habit — unattributable, unrecorded, entirely local — of borrowing a knife from somebody the town has decided not to lend to, if you happen to need a knife, and you happen to be standing there.

None of it is a miracle. All of it is how things have always been done.

---

And when the bell has rung and the market is stacked and the whole town is standing along the terraces in the rain shouting advice at a pond, the Ear does the roll: every household answers with its call, down the line, north to south, sixty-odd calls in ninety seconds.

There is a gap in it now, a little south of the middle, where a household used to answer with a click in it that carried very well through wet leaves. Nobody fills it. Nobody is quite sure whose it is. It has been there long enough that the children think it was always there.

She waits one beat longer than she needs to. She has always done this. Nobody has ever asked her to stop.

Then she says the number, and the number is right, and the water goes on going down the throat.
