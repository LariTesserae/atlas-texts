# What Came Down the Stair

## It did not shine

It came down on foot, at night, on the third switchback where the air turns warm and sweet, and it did not shine.

That was the first courtesy and the whole grammar of the visit follows from it. In the Slake, glowing is how you eat things. The nap shines in the seeps and the gauze come to the shine and are held, and by morning there is a fringe of white grains at the lip that was yesterday a swarm. Anything that arrives in this basin luminous arrives as a mouth. So it came matte, dust-colored, the size of a person, at the height of a person, and it carried an eel-oil lamp with a silk wick like everybody else, and it stopped every hundred meters to look at the flame.

It had no bird. A child lent it one, in a cage of lime-plaster bars, and it took the strap and wore it correctly and did not give it back until the child was asleep, which is to say until the child's turn on the rota came around and someone else took the cage.

That night a man named Long Lip walked it around a dry bench in the dark for six hours because it was a guest and guests are kept awake. It could have stayed awake on its own. It let him do it. It let him be tired for it. In the morning it was given a name by a girl of about a year and a half who was watching from a hide: two fingers lifted off a flat palm, which is not a place-name and was therefore a bad name, and it took the name anyway and used it and never offered another.

Everything it did afterward was permitted because of that first night. A power that will not be kept awake by a tired man is not a guest. It is weather.

## The ledger

It could have stopped the water.

Not the springs — the *degassing*. One adjustment to the chemistry of forty-one degrees and the stone stops falling out of the water and the basin becomes an ordinary wet cave with a hole in the roof. No soft front. No blocks. No weirs, no farming of geology, no grain, no reason for the Stair. Within a generation the terraces would be old surfaces, weathering, exposing strata, and the past would be visible in the ordinary way, which is the way of a place that is being taken apart. The Slake would be a ruin. Ruins are legible. That is what they are for.

It could have lifted the Sour. Crack the rim wide, put a wind-scoop on the south wall, let the bowl breathe. Then the seeds that fall in and die white and swollen would not die. Green would come down the Stair in eleven years. In forty there would be a valley with larks in it, four hundred meters deep, warm and wet and fertile, and forty people would become four thousand, and there would be wood.

That is the whole temptation in one sentence: *it could have given them plants.*

It could have given them wood alone, which is smaller and harder to argue with. Wood is handles and kiln fuel and cage bars and club shafts and boats. Wood is the reason they scrape guano into gut bags for the farmers. Give them a grove on the north benches and you have not touched the water or the gas or the stone; you have only removed the scarcity that organizes every hour of every life down here. And also removed the trade, and the rim, and the wife up there, and the sign for shafter.

It could have made them safe, and safety is not a small thing, and it did not do it, and it owed them a reason.

The reason was not that the Slake is beautiful. It is beautiful. The chord over the Combs at four in the afternoon, forty bowed saws leaning in the wind; the black spiral of shafters going up over the storm; the Castings lit from inside at night, thousands of small blue-green windows at every height, the whole field looking like a town — it is beautiful, and a power that preserves a place because it is beautiful has decided to love a pattern more than the ones inside it, and that is the worst thing available to the powerful, worse than the flood and the grove, because it wears the face of respect.

It said so, out loud, in the sixty signs, badly, to eleven people at once: *I will not keep you as you are because you are beautiful.*

And it did not choose non-interference either, which is the same crime with better manners. Non-interference is a policy that costs the interferer nothing. Every bystander in the history of the world has had that reason available and has used it.

So it went looking for the actual evil, which took it eleven days, and which was not the stone.

The evil is that there is no *slack* in the Slake. Not one hour of it. Every good thing in the basin is load-bearing on the killing. Love is being kept awake because sleeping alone is death, so no one has ever been able to find out whether they would have stayed awake anyway. Attention is conjugated entirely into *will-be-soft* because *was* is unsayable and unreadable and there is no material to say it with. And a body that cannot keep moving has three futures — to be carried, at the cost of another person's whole night, every night, forever, out of a stock of forty people; or to be carried up the Stair to the green country where it can sit down safely and be nameless among farmers for the rest of its life; or to walk into a seep on a warm evening and stand.

There is a sign for the third one. It is the flat hand laid palm-down on the air. It is the worst thing one person can say about another and it is also what they say about the man who walked his mother around a bench for eleven nights and on the twelfth sat down. The crime and the mercy have one word between them, because in a place with no slack they are the same event.

The rule is where all the meaning is. The *absence of margin* is where all the cruelty is. It decided to work only on the margin, and to change nothing else, and to be refused whenever it was asked.

## The nap

Before anything it consulted the resident.

Not the cutters, who are visitors of a slow kind, and not the shafters, who pay and leave. The nap: the millimeter of felt continuous across the whole floor, white where the sulfur is, rust where the iron is, slate-grey and pond-smelling where the methane seeps, the only thing here that has never had to learn the rule.

The conversation took a long time and nothing visible happened during it, because the nap does not have durations, it has speeds. What it learned, insofar as it can be put in a language with organs in it, was this: *the nap does not resist burial. It grows up through the burial as fast as the burial arrives.* A hand's depth of bench is a hundred pages of the same sentence. The nap is not surviving the stone. It is using the stone as a place to keep having been.

Everything it did afterward was that, applied.

## The first gift was a question

It asked them where the water was not.

They did not know. Nobody had ever known. There is no reason to know: dry ground is where nothing happens, no soft front, no blocks, no grain, no nap worth rasping, nothing to read at evening. The whole attention of the species down here is a search for wetness. Dry stone is the floor you sleep on and move the hide off of. It had never occurred to any of them to *map* it.

It took eleven days, and it was mostly done by children, and the answer was a bench on the north wall, sixty meters above the Combs, well above the Sour line, where a lip had failed years ago and turned the seep away and the rock had gone on drying ever since: two hundred meters long, forty wide, hard as a road, and utterly worthless.

It did not make the bench. It found it. That is the entire intervention. No physics were altered.

They carried Nine Tubs up there on a chitin sled, which took four people most of a day, because she was seventy and her hips had gone and she was scheduled for the Stair on the next grain run. They set her down on dry stone in the shade with a hide and a lamp and a bowl.

She sat still for an hour and nothing happened to her ankles.

She sat still for a day. She sat still for a week. She is still sitting there. The bench will be wet again in about ninety years, because the terrace below it is rising a finger's width a year and the lip will fail back the other way eventually, and it told them this on the first day, and then it showed them how to find the next one — how to read a wall for old failures, how to ask the question — so that the gift would be a method and not a possession.

That night a man slept a whole night alone and unwatched, the first adult in the history of the basin to do so, and in the morning he could not say what had happened to him, and his brother made the flat-hand sign at the bench: *it let me sleep.*

And Nine Tubs, who had been watching, laid her hand palm-down on the air and lifted the fingers off it.

That is a new sign. It is not one of the sixty and it is not the sixty-first. It is used now between walkers passing in the dark, and it means something like *rest, I have the hour*, and the argument about whether it has destroyed marriage is ongoing and will not be settled by anybody from outside. Two of the twelve knots refuse the bench entirely and say the dance will go out of the stilts and the people will follow. They may be right. It did not argue with them. It left them a lamp.

## The second gift was something to do while sitting

Stillness, made survivable, turns out to be a *skill*.

It offered this one to Nine Tubs personally, and told her plainly that she could refuse it and that it would not explain the cost, because it did not know the cost, and anyone who tells you they know what a people will do with a past is selling something.

The method is nothing. A wire-cut face on an old bench, wet-polished with eel oil and the gritty white drift of shed gauze cases, which is the finest abrasive in the basin and which they had been walking on for four hundred years. That is all. It is a technique entirely composed of things they already own, and it could have been invented by any of them on any afternoon, and it never was, because there was no reason to look at old stone.

Polished wet, the laminations read.

Rust bands and white bands and slate-grey bands, year on year on year: the iron summers, the sulfur winters, the methane years. Dark seams a hand thick where a weir breached and a million scutch were sealed in a week — you can count the crashes. Thick rich laminations under the guano walls where the shafters' tariff fell fastest. And in among them, faint as thumbprints in dough, the curved backs, and the tapered cases, and the shapes of things mid-climb.

The basin has never lost anything. It has been accessioning everything, perfectly, at a finger's width a year, and filing it underneath, and the only exposed surface anywhere was the youngest one. The past was not missing. It was *unread*.

Nine Tubs sat on the dry bench for a season with a wire and a bag of grit and read down eleven meters, and found the terrace she was named for.

She is the first person in the Slake to know what her own name means. She cannot go there; it is eleven meters inside a cliff. But she can *say* it now, in a form of the sign for *under* that the young have started inflecting, and the inflection is turning into something, and the something is a tense, and the tense is *was*, and they made it themselves in about four months, which is fast.

Their literature used to be *will-be-soft*, spoken in doorways at evening. It still is. But now the old sit on dry stone above the Combs and read the rock aloud in the new tense, and the young come up from the cutting fronts to hear it, and there is a thing happening in this basin that has never happened in it, which is two tenses in one place.

They also found their dead. Not metaphorically. A polished face through the Castings gives you a stilt mid-preen in cross-section, feather-crisp, and the void where the animal was, and you know which one it is because it is standing where it stood. The sign for a cast that has stopped sounding means *sealed*. It does not mean *forgotten*. It never did. Now there is a way to unseal, and they are arguing about whether to, and some of them are, and some of them have stopped and will not start again.

It let all of that be theirs. It did not stay to supervise.

## The Sump

It asked to go down into the Sump, past the last hard knob, into the water that has not met air in a long time, where alone in eleven kilometers an old surface might lie exposed.

They gave it the sixty-first sign.

It did not go. Not because it could not, and not because it was frightened, and not because it agreed. Because a place that cannot refuse you is not a place, it is a display, and the first refusal is the thing that makes every subsequent gift a gift instead of an installation. It made the sign back, correctly, and it never asked again, and the cutters noticed that, and it counted for more with them than the bench did.

## The deposit

Then it did the only thing it did that they could not have done themselves, and it did it in their idiom, which is the only idiom in which anything here can be said at all.

It walked down into the Combs at the edge of a fast front, into a seep a fingernail deep, and it stood still.

They shouted. It has no name that can be shouted; that is the whole problem with names down here. A girl ran at it with the copper-headed club and it did not move and she did not swing, because you cannot club a thing that has been keeping your grandmother company on a dry bench for a season.

It took eight days. Ankles in an hour, knees in a day, and by the end of the week a column of cream travertine the shape of a standing person, crisp on the outside, hollow within.

Inside it there is no Sour.

When the four-o'clock storm slams its rotating slab down the wall and the whole field of tens of thousands of bottles exhales a knee-height wave of dead air across the Combs, that one breathes out clean. Things live in it. Gauze pupate in the throat. It has not stopped them.

And in wind, it sounds. Every hollow shape with a crack in it has a note, and the field detunes itself year by year as the voids fill from below, and the cutters navigate by the detuning: *turn where the two high ones are; not that one, that one's gone flat.*

It learned the bone whistle. There were four signals. It made a fifth, and the fifth means *heard*.

That is all it says. Not *come*, not *danger*, not *do this*. A person alone in a hollow is temporarily nameless in this basin, because to be named you must be seen, and there is no calling anyone from more than ten meters, ever, and that is the loneliest fact in a place made almost entirely of lonely facts. Now there is one thing in eleven kilometers that answers.

You blow, and the field answers, and you are not nameless.

It held that note for about two years. Then it did the last piece, which was the actual gift, and which was to teach them to grow their own: a hand-built weir on a windward lip, a void form packed in wet nap and gauze drift, a throat tuned with the copper wire while the stone is still hard cheese, and the water does the rest in a season, because the water does everything in a season. They farm the geology. They have always farmed the geology. This is one more crop.

There are forty-one of them now, one per person and one spare, and the notes are chosen, and when you whistle at night the ones that answer are the ones whose people are awake, so the sound of the field at three in the morning is a map of who is keeping whom.

Then it went flat, and then it was sealed, and the mat grew up through it, as the mat grows up through everything.

It did not die. It was sealed. There is a sign for that, and it is used about the dead only, and it does not mean forgotten.

## Night, after

Warm, thirty degrees, no cooling to speak of. The stars come into the Breach and lie in a band across the middle of the sky. The hiss never stops. The scutch sheets grip and the columns bend and re-form and somewhere on the east wall eight hundred tons of last year lets go with a sound like a door in the sky.

Up on the north wall an old woman is polishing a face with eel oil and grit, sitting perfectly still, which is a thing her body can do now and which is the most valuable labor in the basin. Below her the Castings are lit from inside, thousands of small blue-green windows, and among them one dark shape that is full of clean air and says nothing.

A child on the low route, alone in a hollow, temporarily nameless, puts the bone whistle to her lips.

The field answers.

She makes a sign at it — flat hand, palm down, fingers lifting — which nobody can see, because she is alone in a hollow, and which she makes anyway.

The oldest thing you can put your hand on here is still a footprint from last spring. But you can cut a face now, and wet it, and read down.
