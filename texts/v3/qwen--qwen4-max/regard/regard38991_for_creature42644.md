# New Water

## 1. What drew its attention

Not the bulge. Standing water is a spectacle, and spectacles are cheap; there are things in the universe that hold themselves up far more improbably than forty kilometers of warm sea on a mountain, and most of them are not worth interrupting.

What was noticed was four kilometers of cord with nothing said on it.

Nine thousand people, every one of them holding some end, and not one word passing — and underneath the silence, felt in the wrists of an entire city at once, the slow drag of a stone-shaped woman with folded hands being towed somewhere else. It attended to that for a while. It understood that it was listening to a species change its mind about its dead, at the speed of a swim, in the dark, without permission, and that the change was being conducted entirely by not speaking.

Then it looked at the rest.

## 2. The being

It looked for the one who lives there, and could not find a single one, and did not conclude that there was none.

There is the water, which is fond of itself and has not learned to fall, and which a woman put her hand into when she was nineteen skins old and felt be aware of her hand. There is the mason's animal, a million small mouths in a felt, laying down the world instead of thinking, which has been asked its opinion by no one in eleven thousand years. There is the city, which has lungs and mains and a throat and hands and a switchboard of four immobilized women, and whose resting heart is thirty-one. There is Bell, who has a district and a sort of citizenship, and after whom nine thousand people ask. And there is the softness in Bell's flank, which is small and many and four years old and has a word now, and which may be a disaster or a person or, as the physicians suspect, the same thing seen from different ends.

It declined to decide between these. It treated all of them as possibly someone, which is the only policy that has ever been available to a power that cannot be sure, and it arranged its conduct accordingly, and the whole of what follows is that arrangement.

## 3. The lip

It did the arithmetic on the ninth post's marks, and got the same answer as Old Marl and the four hundred others, and then it went down.

Down off the saddle, down four thousand meters of nothing, into valleys that have never had anything in them: no silt, no rounded pebble, no river, no rot, no salt. It walked the dry country where the delivery will go. It found the places where the water will braid and the places where it will stand and the long shallow basin that will be a marsh for nine hundred years and then a soil. It did not widen the saddles. It did not soften the landing. It did not leave a marker, or a fence, or a channel, or a warning; it left nothing at all, because everything it left would have to be maintained by somebody, and there was nobody down there yet.

It memorized the route. Then it came back up, which took a season, and this is the only part of the story with no witnesses.

It could take a hand's breadth off the bulge every generation, invisibly, forever. The marks would go on reading the same. The curve would stop steepening and no one would ever know why, and there would be no afternoon, and no first mud, and no downstream, and the sea would go on being a held breath on the roof of the world until the mouths cooled.

They do not call it an ending. They call it a delivery, and they argue about the date the way people elsewhere argue about a wedding, and they have already invented the mercy themselves: a woman with a jar of her mother's moss and a tethered child, walking out along a ridge at a hand's breadth in a generation, planting an animal in a country with no name. Four fingers of sea out there now. A shrine the size of a fist.

To hold the lip would be to overrule the one hope the whole city shares, in favor of a safety nobody asked for. It left the water alone.

## 4. The jar

It could have sent something down through the fog.

It has the trick of taking apart. It could have put it on a feather, or in the crop of a wick-bird, or in a sealed jar in the nineteenth skin of a woman who is now furniture; and the ceiling-midden would have gone cloudy and sweet and begun to eat itself, eleven thousand years of uneaten rising, and there would have been soil in a century, and cheese, and the physicians' faction would have won.

It did not, for a reason it found in the country itself, in the third theory of a woman of fifty-one skins who does not put it on the public lines: *the biter learned subtraction from us.* From the wear of hands. From the sweep of the step. From the grit under the archive. Predation is not a new animal, it is a new geology arriving in the shape of a mouth, and it is *theirs* — the one thing this people has ever made that was not made by adding.

A gift would have taken that away. It would also have proved the shutter party right: the wound came from outside, close the door, net the saddles, and no child at the lip next year.

So it did not open Vann's jar and it did not close it. It did not sterilize Bell's flank, either, though it could have, and this was the hardest of the refusals and the one it is least sure of, because the softness in the flank might be someone, and you do not get to unmake someone in order to tidy a wound.

## 5. The ninth joint

The small mercies were the real temptation. Not the flood — the flood is a date. The ninth joint of a trunk-section under the fourth bridge, which is going to fail in a year or in ten. A grittiness in a knuckle. A hip that clicks. An eleven-year-old girl on her belly in the dark in a tube a hand and a half wide, who is the only person in this city who is ever alone in air.

It could have mended the joint. It could not have mended the joint every year for eleven thousand years without becoming the thing that holds the lip.

That is not a rule about non-interference. It is the local geology. A kindness that does not have to be repeated is a rope left slack: a rod in a season, a column in three years, and in a lifetime a load-bearing member of a building nobody designed. And a guild is a way of knowing. Ondu's ears are her capital; she refused two marriages by them; her granddaughter comes out of the mains full of other people's lives and is debriefed, and that is the actual government of nine thousand people, and it is treated like plumbing. Replace it with a kindness and you have not saved a district. You have deleted a sense organ, politely, and the doors will seal behind it in eighty years.

So it did the other thing. It sat at the mouth of the main with its cheek against the trunk-section for the whole of a shift, listening to Coir work a hundred meters away in the dark, so that the throat that is listening was also being listened to. She was furious about this for two years. Ondu approved of it, on the grounds that a listener at the mouth is a safety measure and costs nothing. It is still the least honored position in the least honored guild.

## 6. The asking

Before it swam, it asked the water.

It put the question the way Hesk put her hand: without a pattern for it, in a language that turns it into weather. Nothing answered. It went in anyway, having asked, and it went on asking at intervals for the rest of its life, and never got an answer, and did not stop, because that is what the courtesy is — not the receipt.

It asked the animal too. It got down among the curd-moss in a seep in the Grown Quarter and put the question to a million small mouths: *what do you think.* Nothing came back. Nothing has ever come back. It considers this the most important thing it did and it will not be able to explain why to anybody, and it did not supply an answer on the animal's behalf, which would have been the easiest and worst of all the available miracles.

But it learned what the animal eats, which is the dust of use, the grit of feet and hands and rope-wear, the only substance in the world that has been taken away from anything. The animal's whole opinion, insofar as it has one, is: *give me what you have lost.*

That told it what to do with a body.

## 7. The arrival

It came in over the rim on foot, from the settlements, in the cold thin air, as new water — one who has not yet learned to fall — and it was misjudged in every distance and every depth, and the locals found this funny in a mild unhurried way and let it happen.

It had to be given air. That is the first debt and it never stopped being one: a dome, a pipe, a resin joint, a woman's cheek on a trunk-section, an eleven-year-old on her belly in the dark. Everything it thought with, it thought on borrowed breath, at thirty-one beats, in a room that smelled of resin and crushed leaf and the sourness of many people talking.

It was illiterate. It could not read a twitch at the corner of a mouth at ten paces, and children could, and it had to be taught, slowly, by people who found it obvious. It could not write its name; nobody can. It learned to hold three turns around its wrist and wake when the district's mood changed. It learned the pattern for *Kettle is in the west shallows*, which is broadcast unprompted several times a day because people like to know.

It learned the count of nine, for a moss lineage going out, by standing in a street and stopping talking with everybody else.

## 8. The address

A stranger has no dead, and the dead are the deed, and so it had no address, and this was not a bureaucratic difficulty. It was an obscenity, and a pity, and people did not know where to put their eyes.

Sesha solved it, in the end, with the flat practicality of a woman who has spent twenty years developing a private dialect for people who cannot answer. The ninth lane of Low Rungs has eleven dead women at eleven doorways and no living residents, and it is a bore now, shoulder-width, ribbed with the ghost of a hand's-breadth lattice, and in eleven years it will be head-width. She gave it a doorway. She lent it eleven grandmothers.

The lane's address changed for the first time in a hundred and forty years: not *the lane with eleven and nobody*, but *the lane with eleven and one*.

Two things followed, and neither of them is a miracle. The dialect stopped being private, which Sesha grieved, openly, on the cords, at length, and accepted. And the bore began to be swum twice a day by two people instead of one, and the guild's estimate went from eleven years to nineteen, and the estimate being wrong is the only sort of miracle this country has ever permitted.

## 9. The work

It swept.

Steps, sills, the floor beneath the archive. The lowest work there is, and the only subtractive work, and the source of the most valuable substance anyone owns. It swept and it kept the sweepings in stoppered jars and it gave them away — half to the masons, half to Nim in the Grown Quarter, who is thirty-four skins and a rope-field heir and entirely unimportant and has made a bowl.

It could make dust. It could not make better dust. Its heel lost a hair's breadth like anybody's heel; its hands wore rope at the ordinary rate; the yield was a few pinches a year, which is nothing, which is what everyone has. The masons had begun to say on the long lines in the small hours that the potters were taking the food out of the mouth of the thing that builds the world, and they were right, and it did not resolve this, because it was too small to accuse and too small to arbitrate, and it went on sweeping for both of them and being wrong about it in public.

And it took a rotation at the archive.

It read properly. Hand over hand, slow, keeping the knots crisp, wearing them the way reading wears them, which is preservation and destruction in the same motion. It did not read for grit. It has never once used that verb. The dust came anyway, because the dust comes anyway, because living is enough — you do not have to grind a history to make clay out of what happened; you only have to keep it, and be a body while you do.

The stone woman of eighty-one skins who holds all the reading-room lines in her lap watched it for nine years before she gave it one of them to hold. That was the first thing anyone in the city gave it that it had not asked for.

## 10. What it said

Every day, on the long lines, at the ordinary hour: *how is Bell.* It has never seen Bell. It would weep.

Once, in a lung, at ordinary volume, with its mouth full of milk that will not mix in water and is therefore associated with everything warm and domestic and slightly boring about being alive, it said the insane thing. It said *I want cheese.* It did not know that this was the whole of a politics and that nobody says it on a public line because it sounds insane. It said it because it wanted some. Vann was there. Vann has not told the cords either, and has begun, quietly, to say it herself, in the west shed among the pale trunks where everyone speaks softly, and there are now four people in the world who want cheese out loud, and that is the entire intervention, and it cost it standing for a decade.

On the nets it said: let them come. It was asked why, and it said, *I am new water; you are asking me to vote for the shutter.* The children named Wick and Feather stood at the saddle that year. It does not know if it won or if it merely happened to be standing there when the argument turned. It has stopped needing to know which.

And it asked Hesk what she saw down there. The eleven-thousand-and-first time. It did not remember a correction. It did not turn it into weather. It let her see that it did not understand, and she said it again — *the mouths are not holes, holes have edges, the water comes up fond* — and it stayed not understanding, in front of her, for an hour, which is the only thing anybody has ever been able to give her.

## 11. The rumor

It never said what it was.

Not out of policy. Out of the arithmetic of the ninth post. If it said, they would ask it to hold the lip, and it would have to refuse, and go on refusing, in front of them, forever, at every flood-mark, in every lung; and they would stop being a people who are going through and become a people who were visited, which is a thing that holds still and gets a rind and in two hundred years is a variation in the grain.

Also, it cannot lie with its face. The face is the only fine print in this country. So it did not hide anything; it simply never said, and people read it at ten paces and concluded what they concluded, and there is a rumor on the long lines at all hours, and four immobilized women edit it, and it lets them, because that is what neighbors are for and it is one of them now.

## 12. The stilling

Grit in the knuckles at forty skins. The click in the hip. Half marble by seventy, moving in the slow deliberate way that gives the whole city its tempo. It did not exempt itself. It had thought about exempting itself and found that the exemption would have been the whole gift, given back.

It sits in a niche at the mouth of the ninth lane with its hands folded, because folding is now what hands do, and it holds ends, and it has become fluent, and it edits, and it is not proud of the editing. It has opinions about the towings and it has stated them and been argued with by people who have never been down a bore in their lives. It said, on the public lines, that it was not sure it had been right about Bell's flank, and the city disagreed with it in eleven different dialects for a month, and it let them, and it is still not sure.

There is a scaffold at the head of the lane with a mark tied to it, due in eighty years. It tied the mark. It will not see the forms peeled off. Nobody who plants a tower sees it. This is not tragic here; it is simply the tense everyone speaks in, and it took it forty skins to learn to speak in it, and it is the only thing it ever had to learn that it could not be taught.

It knows where the water will go. It walked the basin. When the lip lets go it will go down with the lane and the eleven and the towers and the nine hundred cords and the sayings and the six and the moss and the whole bright unfinished country, four hundred cubic kilometers in one afternoon, and it will be grit in the first mud, and something will finally be allowed to take it apart. It has learned the local word for this. It does not call it an ending.

## 13. The lane with eleven and one

Nim made a second bowl. It cost four generations of sweepings and a purchase from the archive and some pinches from a stranger who swept steps in Low Rungs for sixty years, and its core is a little grey-brown heart of ground-off hands and feet and rope, indistinguishable from anybody's, sealed in the same rind as everything else. It holds water chosen, at a lip she made, in a shape she thought of, and it stands slightly proud of the rim and quivers when someone swims past. She has still never said so out loud. He never asked her to.

At the twelfth doorway in the ninth lane there is a man-shaped thing with folded hands and a new skin on its shoulders, and a cord tied to them, because it was tied there when he was alive and nobody has untied it.

People pluck it. It answers. It is only physics — a taut string does what a taut string does — and the children are told it is bad manners to say so, and the adults go on saying good morning to the door.

The bore is still shoulder-width. It will close; it is closing; in a lifetime it will be grain, and a pocket in the loaf, and somebody will put a hand on a doorway she can feel but not see, and count.

Somebody swims it twice a day so that it will still be a street next year. Under her bare heel the step loses a hair's breadth of itself.
