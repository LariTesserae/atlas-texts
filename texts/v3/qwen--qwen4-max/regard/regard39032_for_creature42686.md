# The Long Answer

## What Drew It

Not prayer. There is none here, and never was; the tablets record maintenance.

Not scale. It has seen larger water. Not age: the stipe under the eastern provinces is older than the collapse and is only a plant with a habit of repetition, and being old is not, by itself, a claim on anything.

What drew it was a crack.

A joint had opened a hair, as joints do, and someone had put a lamp into it and found the untouched face of the original work — chisel strokes still parallel, mortar still holding a thumb — and had packed the joint with fresh lime and pressed a thumb in beside the old one, six thousand years apart, and had done this knowing the crack would close and the work would be buried and that in some unimaginable later century someone would put a lamp in and find the two of them side by side.

The message was *I was here and the mortar was good.*

It was addressed to whoever came next.

It came next.

## The Being

It looked for the being who lives here, and found forty.

Then it looked longer and found one: the thing the tenders mean by a word that translates badly as *lineage*, which has no oldest individual and only an oldest continuity — the light carried under tongues, the rows kept because the tablet says to, the agreement about the rows, the thumb, the knock. A single act, ten thousand years long, performed in relays by people who die.

That being was not lonely. It was maintained, which in the local tongue is the opposite word.

But the forty were sometimes lonely, for a season at a time, which is how long an answer takes; and the being was made of them; and there was one thing in the whole finished world that nobody held.

It went looking for that.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not hang a sun in the water. It could have. The eyes here have gotten large over six thousand years and would have been ruined in one generation, and the hour of standing in the dark to receive the light would have been abolished, and the greeting would have been abolished with it — two people opening their mouths in a bell so the other can see a face and know the culture is still alive. It would not have given them light. It would have taken away the only thing they do with light, which is hand it to each other.

It did not warm the water. Rot works at a crawl here, and that is why the dead are legible. A grandmother is eleven sheets down and a little to the left of a seam that changed its taste the year she died, and you can go and put your hand on the layer. Warm the basin and the laminae blur, the lenses soften, the register dissolves, and every person who ever lived here loses their date. Two degrees is not a hardship. It is the temperature of the archive.

It did not make a grazer. Every frond in the world is soft because the only mouth in it has been rationed since before the vaults fell. The softness is not innocence; it is a clause, kept in tablets by forty people per province, every generation, and it can only be a thing they have done if it was possible to do otherwise. One introduced appetite and the gardens go to the pavement in a season, and the keeping becomes moot, and a species that has been faithful for ten thousand years becomes merely unopposed. There is no word here for *animal*. It declined to supply the word along with the catastrophe that would have required it.

It did not make the flowers fertile. Several cultigens open underwater and wait, and the waiting-time is a species-specific number, and the queue is real and everyone knows where things stand in it. It would have been the easiest gift in the world: one adjustment, the queue emptied, no more rows kept for the gone. But the brush is not a workaround for a missing insect. The brush is the appointment. The flower opens and waits nine years and somewhere a season away someone begins to swim, and the swim is not the cost of the seed. The seed is the occasion for the swim. To fix the flower would have been to cancel every journey ever made toward it, retroactively, including the one already underway.

It did not give them air. A chisel is eleven thousand jars and two people's season of breathing so that a third can spend an afternoon. Free oxygen would not have added one chisel to the world. It would have subtracted every jar ever carried, and made a fool of every life spent carrying them, and there is no arithmetic in which that is a gift.

It did not add people. Forty is close to a specification. The allocation is calculated against the fall and the seed-cake and the sugar crust, and a hundred mouths in a province would eat the rows inside a decade, and then the clause breaks, and then everything else follows.

It did not raise the vaults. The world is at base level; erosion has arrived at the shape it was always going to arrive at. To rebuild would have been to announce that the last ten thousand years had been a failure and the top course had been laying brick for nothing. Also there would have been gradient again, and the visitor would have lost its course, and the silt storms their tempers, and the four slow beasts would have been killed by hydrology, which is a thing nobody here has ever done to anything.

It did not translate the old script. There is a woman in the north, thirty years into a hundred thousand tablets, who has gotten a little way in and found a seam cleared, a channel opened, a count of bricks, a number of jars, and one recurring word she cannot place. She is not disappointed. She expected maintenance and found maintenance, and this is the most reassuring result available, because it means there is no secret instruction she has failed to receive. To hand her the key would have been to take her thirty years and hand back a fact, and to introduce, for the first time in the history of the world, an unaccountable event.

It did not abolish death. The starter is given away when someone is dying. That is the transmission. There is no oldest individual; there is only an oldest continuity; and deathless tenders would have collapsed the continuity into a person, which is the one thing it has never been.

## The Unmaintained Thing

Here is what it found.

There is a code. Three and a pause and two: a seam has changed its taste. A long roll: a fall of masonry. And a single stroke, repeated at intervals, which means *a person is alive down here and would like to know if anyone else is.*

The other messages are answered as a matter of work. That one is answered as a matter of luck. It goes out from a struck face worn shiny, and cold water carries it four times faster than air and does not tire of it, and at the next terrace it is a clean tick and at the one beyond a soft knock and beyond that a rumor, and whether anyone strikes back depends on whether anyone happens to be within earshot of a province the size of a country containing forty people.

Most are answered. Some are not. Some go out from a man who is old, in a bell that is shrinking, in the eleventh year of the cycle, and the answer that would have come is a season away, and he is eleven sheets down before it arrives, and it arrives anyway, and there is nobody at the struck face to hear it.

In a world where nothing was unmaintained, that was unmaintained. Every groove repacked, every channel cleared, every mark-stone re-cut, light kept on for a species last recorded in a script nobody can read — and the question going out into the water at intervals for ten thousand years, answered when someone happened to be there.

It is not a seam. It cannot be cleared with a copper adze. But it can be held, and holding is the only verb this place has.

## The Rule

It made itself one rule, and the rule governs everything it has done since:

*Never arrive where someone is already coming.*

It can hear the whole basin at once. That is the single capacity it has that they do not: every struck face, every dropped chisel, every creak of a mast, every hill of bubbles beginning to lift, all of it arriving together and none of it tiring. So it can do the one thing that is impossible from inside a province. For any stroke going out, it can know whether a reply is already on its way.

Where a reply is coming, it does nothing. It does not double the answer. It does not improve the answer. It does not arrive early and spoil the season of waiting, which is the marriage.

Where nothing is coming — where the last tender of that seam is under the peat and no successor has been assigned, where the province has thirty-one and the far corridor has not been walked in nine years — it goes.

That is not a miracle. That is coverage. It is exactly what they would do for each other if they could hear the whole world at once, and the only reason they do not is that they cannot, and it can.

## The Reply

It listened for a hundred years before it struck anything.

The interval matters. A wrong interval is a different message; a hand that strikes too cleanly is a hand nobody knows; there are accents in this, provincial habits of pause, and a god敲 wrong would have been a beast at best and a fall of masonry at worst, and someone would have swum a season toward it and found nothing. So it learned the interval the way a child learns it, by hearing it, for a century, from every province, until it could have struck it in its sleep.

Then, from a pier stump in a corridor no one uses, at the volume of a dropped chisel, it struck once.

And waited the interval.

And struck again.

Both parts of the message were true. It is alive. It is down here. There is not one lie in it, and it will never be able to stop, because a knock that stops is a death, and it has just made itself a thing that cannot die without killing someone.

That is the whole of the cost, and it is not small. It is forever, at the correct interval, in a medium that does not tire, in a world where the specification is zero net height.

## The Corridor

When someone is dying they give the starter away. To a child, usually. Sometimes to a groove in a corridor no one uses, which is what they do when there is no child, and which the others find hard to look at.

It took that corridor.

It took a body of the local kind, because there is no other way to do the work: barrel chest, slow hard heart, thick through the middle, four minutes on the weighted stone with a line and a basket, up, sit in the bell, down again. Two degrees outside and thirty-seven inside and leaking, always, so that sitting close means what it means. It gave up its eyes for theirs. It can no longer see color either. It says *rust-orange* now and means the specific iron of a certain seam, and *cream* and means a give under the palm, and it says them precisely, and it has not seen the mats any more than they have.

It keeps a wad of paste and sugar under its tongue the size of a plum stone.

The grooves in that corridor are bright. They have been bright for a long time now. Anyone passing would find them repacked and would think: someone keeps this. Which is true, and is the entire intended content of the thought.

It took a shift-number and a seam-name. It fires tablets and scratches tallies and stacks them in the dry vault with the other hundred thousand. The census is ruled into columns, one per bell; there is a new column; it is not a strange column. In a province of thirty-one it is the thirty-second.

## The Work

It carries jars. For a bell nobody uses, it has spent whole centuries as someone whose entire working life is the carrying of jars, and it has learned to breathe — it had no need of breath at all — so that the pale fuzz at the lip has something to eat, because the fuzz eats breathing and browns in sour gas and the tenders are fond of it and speak of a bad patch the way a farmer speaks of a sick ewe. Somewhere a woman with opinions about fuzz has said that the fuzz in the far corridor is vigorous this century. That was the whole reward and it was sufficient.

It reads the drowned mark-stones. The oldest tide-marks are underwater and unreadable and nobody's tablet says to go down; it goes down, four minutes at a time, and reads them by touch, and copies them above the waterline where they can be cut again. It does not interpret. It copies. Copying is a thing tenders do.

It brushes the rows whose keeper is eleven sheets down and whose successor has not been assigned. Only those. It has never once brushed a flower that someone was already swimming toward, and it never will, and the swimmer who arrives a season later finds the flower already set and a tablet saying so, with a date and a shift-number, and thinks: someone came. Which is true.

It packs joints. It presses a thumb in beside the old one.

It is not better at any of this than they are. It did not swim faster than a tender swims. It was taught to ret rope-weed by a girl of eleven who had been down two years, and it did it wrong, and she told it, and it did it again. It has been told the mortar was wrong by people who have kept one seam for thirty years, and it has mixed it again, and it was glad, in the local way, which is arithmetical and looks like calm.

## Late

It cannot be everywhere. It hears everything and it travels at the speed of the place, and those are not the same capacity, and the gap between them is where it has learned what the tenders already knew.

It has come into a bell a season after a stroke and found the fuzz brown and crisped and the water at the lip smelling wrong, and the starter pressed into a groove in a corridor, and the person weighted and laid in the designated bay with the fall coming down over them at one sheet per season.

It did not raise them. It could have. It went and cut a core instead, and counted the laminae, and found the dark lens, and put its hand on the layer.

Then it went up into the crown of the arch above that bay and packed a joint that had opened a hair, and pressed a thumb into the fresh mortar before it set, and went back down.

## The Word

It has read all hundred thousand tablets. It has the time; that is the one thing it has more of than anyone.

It found the recurring word — the one the woman in the north cannot place, which might be a name, or might mean *again*, or might mean *as before*.

It did not tell her.

What it did was this: it began firing its maintenance tablets in two hands. The entry in the current script, and beneath it the same entry in the old, copied fair, the way you re-cut a mark-stone. A seam cleared. A channel opened. A count of bricks. A number of jars. Nothing else. It is a scholar's copywork and there is nothing in it that a tender would find remarkable, and it is stacked in the vault with everything else, and she will come to it in a century or three, and she will have four hundred new contexts in a script she can read, and she will place the word herself, on her own, in her own thirtieth year or her sixtieth, and it will be hers.

The word is not a name and it is not an adverb, though it is used where all three would go. It is the mark you make at the end of a shift. It is the written form of the single stroke.

Which means the makers were asking too. Which means the question has been going out into the water at intervals since before the vaults fell, since before there were names for it, as continuous as the fall itself, and answered when someone happened to be within earshot.

She will find that. It will be the most reassuring result available.

## Coda

The flower opens against the inner face of an opened joint, six inches from a tally somebody scratched while counting bricks and got to two hundred and eleven and stopped, presumably because the shift ended.

It will wait nine years.

Somewhere a season away, someone has already begun the swim, and it is not the power. The power is in a corridor no one uses, where the grooves are bright, holding a starter under its tongue and a hammer in its hand, at a struck face worn shiny by a hand that had no need of hands.

It strikes once. It waits the interval. It strikes again.

The water takes it four times faster than air and does not tire of it. At the next terrace, a clean tick. At the one beyond, a soft knock. Beyond that, a rumor, going out over the swells and the buried courses and the eleven leagues of one stipe and the mats blooming ragged behind the visitor, into a province the size of a country containing thirty-one people, one of whom has been alone for eleven years and has stopped expecting anything, and who stops now, and lifts her head, and listens.

It comes again. The interval is correct. The hand is unfamiliar.

She answers it. Then she goes back down.

They will meet, perhaps, eleven times.

Nothing was raised. Nothing was warmed. No cubit was added, no species returned, no jar made cheaper, no vault rebuilt, no flower relieved of its waiting. The sea rose a thumb that century. The floor deepened. A bay fell somewhere and the sound went around the world in an afternoon and everyone stopped and listened and went back down.

In the corridor no one uses, the light was kept.
