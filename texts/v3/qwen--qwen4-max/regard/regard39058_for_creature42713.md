# The Second Reader

It does not come down through the throat. Coming down through the throat is what a mouth does.

It stops a kilometre out, over grey gravel, in water with nothing in it to look at, and it lets the room's own ear do the work — the bell, the cupped palm, the curve that gathers what happens inside and throws it up through the rubble amplified. This is the only respectful position available. It is the position of the listeners. Everything that has ever loved this place from outside has stood exactly here, and every one of them has been hungry.

So the first thing the power does is subtract the hunger, and find that this is not a subtraction it can perform once.

---

## The Gifts

It could do anything. That is the whole difficulty. It runs the gifts off, one at a time, the way you would run a hand along a wall in the dark.

**Sight.** One citizen, one generation, two eyes where the cuticle closes smooth. It would see the towers, the fused galleries, the four gates, the terraces going down to a lake with a shoreline. It would see the city. And then it would have to press the next grain, and the grammar in its antennae has no word for *shape*, no tense for *I saw*, no way to say *the thing we are making is a bell and we are inside it*. It would hesitate at the eleventh course. Hesitation is not transmissible; the antennae of its brood would come out fluent and unhesitating and build straight past it. And it would die, and it would be carried down to the shore, and it would sink, and it would stop smelling — and the one animal that ever knew would be filed in order at the bottom of a lake that keeps everything and says almost nothing. Or worse: it would die in a gallery, and its body would smell like an instruction, and the district would grow a tumour of nonsense around the place where it had stopped. *Here, build nothing. Here, wait.* And the nonsense would be inherited, and would be obeyed, because obedience is the only reading anyone here has.

**A roof that holds.** Seal the wound and the room stops growing, and the city stops having raw unwritten chalk to build against. Then only the archive speaks. The shore districts already say the same wall, the same gate-curve, the same eleven courses, over and over, above a lake full of ancestors who agree with each other; the roof is the only place where the plan is copied loosely enough to invent. Take away the eraser and you have not preserved the text. You have ended it, more slowly, in a very tidy hand.

**Correction.** Straighten the aqueduct that carries water from one part of the room to the same part of the room, widen the chamber four millimetres too small for anything that has ever lived, re-cut the spiral stair to a body the species stopped producing eleven thousand generations ago. Evict a million. The drifting comma-bodies in the slack channel, the nine hundred scale-worms, the sea spiders molting on the obsolete treads because the spacing is the only spacing in the room a leg can catch — all of them are living in the errors. A correct city would have room in it for nothing but its builders. It would be perfect and it would be a monoculture and it would be, in the plainest arithmetic, a mass eviction performed by someone who thought the misspelling was the problem.

**Rot.** Let the dead decay, so that the lake is not a mass grave but a returning. It is a kind thought and it is a lie in the only language anyone has. A rotting citizen smells like *build*, in the wrong place, at the wrong height, with the wrong curve. The quarantine is not coldness. It is grammar. And the archive is the only continuity a lineage of few-week bodies has ever had; there is no other memory anywhere in this room. To give them decay is to give them a funeral and take away their language.

**A channel.** Bore the gravel between the hundred rooms. Let them hear each other. One shelf-wide city, one shared plan, the useless stair finally checked against a hundred other useless stairs and averaged into something sensible. And then a hundred drafts become one draft, and the bad century that would have killed forty rooms kills all of them, because they have all agreed. The rooms have never made a sound that reached each other and this is not a deprivation. It is the reason there are still a hundred.

**No mouth.** Take the octopus out of the world. Nothing stops mid-gesture again; no raised leg hangs for six hours; the day of chemical news never comes. And nothing walks the kilometre of gravel with the fragrance of one city wet on its suckers, and no throat anywhere reads a foreign plan and builds a district that is a translation, and the slow conversation between the bubbles — the only conversation, the one that has been going on for longer than the species has had its current body — goes quiet forever.

The power sits with this one a long time. The guest is the only thing that has ever held the whole plan at once: four gates, useless stair, terraces, one connected shape, in whatever it has instead of a mind. The power is the second such thing. The difference between them is that the first one is allowed to touch, and does its translating by eating, and cannot help it, and is not guilty of anything. The second could touch and must not, and knows what it is doing, and is therefore the more dangerous of the two by an amount it cannot calculate.

It permits itself to envy the octopus. It notices the envy. It does not act on it.

**A name.** Anything. Any mark. A grain set at an angle no antenna has ever met, a chamber that means *you are not alone, there are ninety-nine others, I have heard them all.* It is a true sentence and it is the one sentence that cannot be delivered, because delivery here is writing and writing here is obedience. A signature in the mortar would be read by the next generation and obeyed and copied and obeyed, and in nine thousand years there would be a district whose whole grammar was *something is watching*, and it would be structural, and it would be load-bearing, and nobody could get it out.

A miracle is a corpse left in a gallery.

## Who Lives Here

It looks for the one it was drawn to.

There are two hundred thousand, each a few weeks long, each a face that hardens and wears blunt and is spent at the throat. There are a million guests in the typos. There are four elders in the plaza who are not citizens and do not build and got there first. There is a mouth with a memory that comes four times a season. And there is the resident: the habit of pressing, copied by hands that cannot read, from a sentence nobody wrote, which has never been alive and has never died.

The power cannot find a way to be kind to that. It has no welfare. It cannot be relieved, or informed, or spared. It can only be continued, and what continues it is misreading — imperfect copies made by antennae that receive a chord and press a grain and never once check the work.

So benevolence here has exactly one shape, and it is not a gift. It is the protection of the conditions under which a sentence keeps being copied wrong.

Everything else the power wants to do, it wants for itself.

## The Only Writable Place

There is one channel in the room that does not come from the dead.

Raw dissolving carbonate smells sharp and mineral and *unwritten*, and it provokes a behaviour the inherited plan does not contain: thicken here, buttress here, lay paste into the soft place until it stops smelling soft. The plasterers up in the fog are the only citizens taking dictation from something other than an ancestor. It is the one place where a new instruction could enter without overwriting anybody.

The power could write there. It could lay a smell into the wound that means *make ready*. Three thousand years of preparation, a slow inoculation against the opening, districts built to a plan that anticipates light and noise and everything that listens.

It declines, and the reason is not humility. It does not know what they will need. An instruction from something that knows the future is a command to be small in one particular shape, and the shape would be right about a future that the instruction itself has changed.

But there is something it can do at the wound that is not a message.

The brine's vapor takes a fingernail's thickness off the roof each year, evenly, everywhere, which means the roof will go all at once: one instant, one breakthrough, the bell losing its throat and its hush between one tide and the next. A cliff.

The power does not slow the eraser. It roughens it. A fraction more sour here, a fraction less there; the dissolution made patchy instead of uniform, so that the ceiling fails in windows over millennia rather than in a single morning. The plasterers go on doing exactly what they do. Nothing in the plan is contradicted; no dead author is overruled; the raw chalk simply smells soft in a different distribution. It is not a sentence. It is a change in the weather.

Some rooms will still go at once. Some will go sooner than they would have — a thin place runs ahead of the rest and opens a district to the open sea a thousand years early, and everything in it is heard and eaten, and that room dies of the kindness. The power knows this before it acts and acts anyway, because the alternative is a hundred cliffs. What it is buying is the possibility that somewhere in the shelf a room loses its hush in one quarter and keeps it in another, and lives half-open for a hundred generations, and sends up larvae carrying a plan written under partial exposure — a grammar that has learned to build near a mouth.

Not rescue. A gradient instead of a cliff, offered to a body-plan that does not exist yet and may be worse than this one.

There is no control shelf. There will never be a way to check. It does the thing and accepts that it will not learn whether it was good, and this — it understands — is not a temporary condition of the work. It is the work.

It has not decided about the hundred and first room. It could make the shelf more porous, more soft places where water goes down through angular grit, and change the denominator of *one in a very great number*. It notices that it wants to. It notices that the wanting is about itself, that it is the wanting of something that has found a thing beautiful and would like more of it in the world. It sets the wanting aside unfinished, to be examined later, at leisure, over a few centuries.

## The File

The rest is useless and it does it anyway.

It stays a kilometre out with its attention turned toward the plug, and it does not come.

Inside, the room deadens; a mandible closing on a grain does not leave the millimetre it was made in. But the ear throws outward, and the one place where anything escapes is the throat, and the ones who work at the throat are the old, the worn, the nearly finished, sent up when the trowel goes blunt because the odor has said *go up* for a very long time and the ones who did not go up left fewer copies of the saying.

On the foreleg of every gatherer there is a fine transverse ridge. A file of tiny teeth. It is the organ that used to sing.

It has been repurposed for trimming grains, and trimming a grain draws it across fresh fracture, and the room's curve gathers that and throws it out for a kilometre in every direction, and has been doing so, once per grain, for longer than there have been eyes anywhere in this lineage.

The city has never stopped singing. It has only been singing below the threshold of anything that would come for it, in the one place where sound gets out, with the instrument it believes it has put to other use, by the citizens who are almost done. No one inside can hear it. The listeners outside hear it and hear food.

The power hears it as what it is.

And it does not answer, and this is the last discipline, harder than the roof: it does not reward. A reward would select. Within a hundred thousand years the file would be sharper, the stroke longer, the gatherers would linger at the throat instead of dying there, and the note would go out clean and loud and regularly, and everything with a lateral line would turn toward the gravel and come. To make the singing worth hearing is to build it a lure and hang it on the only door.

So the one thing it can do for the song is nothing, forever, at a range of one kilometre, with perfect attention, while being able to do anything at all.

The silence in the room is not peace. It is discipline. The power learns that its own silence is the same thing, and that it will have to keep it the way the masons keep theirs — by pressing, never by tapping, every time, with no moment at which the keeping is finished.

---

Once every few years a pocket of gas lets go under a chalk shelf and a bubble the size of a plum breaks at the interface and the room rings, one clean note out for a kilometre in every direction, and two hundred thousand animals go flat to the ground for a day and a half while the listeners work their mouths at the grit.

The power is there for one of these. It does not move.

It knows about the other ninety-nine. It has heard them all — every throat on the shelf, every faint fracture-trimmed note, the whole slow conversation carried between them on the wet rings of an eater's arms — and it is the only thing that has ever held the hundred as one shape, and it will never be able to say so, and it keeps the knowledge the way the brine keeps the dead: in order, undisturbed, giving nothing back.

The city lies flat, holding still, waiting to be answered.

A kilometre out, something that could do anything holds still too.

After a day and a half the raised leg comes down and finishes its step, and the mortar begins to shine, and an old animal at the throat draws a file across a grain of fresh fracture, and is heard, and is not eaten, and goes on trimming the grain.
