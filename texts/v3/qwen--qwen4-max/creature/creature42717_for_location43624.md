## The Seam

Begin in the seam, because nothing else on the Sill has an address.

The seams are the only lines on the plain and they are not gaps. They are a habitat, three hundred microns wide, roofed by the overlapping rims of two neighbors, damp long after the surface has gone dry, and they run continuously for kilometers the way a hedgerow runs. Everything small lives in them. There are thread-animals here, colorless, longer than a penny is wide, that spend their whole lives in the junction and never once come out onto the exposed crust; they graze the leakage where two bodies meet, the sugars that escape the pane, the sloughed cilia, the mucus of an argument. There are mites that hunt the thread-animals and never leave the seam either, so that a predator and its prey can share a lifetime of three meters of travel. There are fungi in the seams that do not decompose anything — there is nothing here to decompose — but instead live as brokers, carrying molecules from one neighbor to the other for a cut.

And it is in the seams that the plain decides who is who.

Each penny tastes its neighbor continuously, at the fringe, all day and all night, and what it tastes is a signature: a suite of long, stubborn, useless molecules that mean nothing except *this is me* or *this is not me*. Where the signature matches, the fringes interdigitate and the two animals share water and the seam goes quiet. Where it does not match, the fringes withdraw a fraction of a millimeter and the seam widens and fills with a resinous exudate that nothing can cross, and the two animals spend the next several centuries growing against each other at a millimeter a day.

Which means the census is wrong. Not the density — the density is real, forty thousand a square meter, a crowd in a handful — but the count of *individuals*. The pennies bud from their own edges. A daughter settles beside her mother and matches her and fuses with her and is, in every sense the seam recognizes, the same animal. The plain is not tiled with four trillion animals. It is tiled with something on the order of nine thousand, each of them the size of a village, each of them a single genetic individual spreading outward from its own center at the pace of a growing fingernail.

And the center is dead. It has been dead for a hundred thousand years. It cemented, as everything here cements, and the living tissue is an annulus a few meters wide creeping away from its own corpse in every direction, and the corpse is laminated, page on page, and readable.

So: nine thousand animals on the Sill, wearing four trillion bodies, each one walking outward from its own remains. The seams are their borders. The wars are slow, and no one wins them, and the border is the only place on the plain where anything is decided.

## The Shaded

Under the top animal there is a second, and under that a third, and in the old centers a fifth, and the light budget at the bottom is about a two-hundredth of what falls on the crust at noon.

The shaded are not small. They are the same size as their parents and they are made of the same tissue and they are, in the ordinary way of counting, alive. But they have given up. Their panes are thin and dark and spread wide, their lenses have gone amber and then brown, and they no longer shift a few millimeters an hour to keep a garden at temperature, because there is no garden left to keep, only a slow film of bacteria that eats what comes down. They live on leakage. They live on the sugar that escapes the animal above them, which is not much, and on the dissolved remains of the animal below them, which is less. A shaded penny may go eleven years between measurable growths. Some of them have been down there since before the escarpment had retreated to where it now stands, and they are the oldest living things on the surface of the earth that are not clonal, and if you asked what they do, the answer is: they hold the pattern.

This is the part that is easy to miss and hard to stop thinking about. The plain repairs its scars along the old lines. It fills a hollow with dead that match the banding of the dead already there. It restores a pebble to within a centimeter. The pattern has to be *somewhere*. It is not in the living top layer, which is young and busy and knows only its own edges. It is in the laminations, and in the shaded, who lie against those laminations with their whole ventral surface and read them the way a tongue reads a groove.

Their bodies carry the signature of the sequence beneath them — not a copy, exactly, more a resonance, a chemical accent picked up from centuries of contact — and they pass that accent up through the stack in the leakage, and the top animal cements in the accent it was fed. When a block falls off a knob and gouges a meter of pavement, the exposed laminations begin to leach immediately, and the leachate is a gradient, and the gradient is a shape, and the animals that crawl into the wound are drawn along it and lay down tissue in the accent of the thing they are lying on. The scar heals in the old handwriting.

Nothing here remembers. There is no organ for it, no nerve, no image. But the past is physically present, in contact with the living, at all times, and it is instructing them, and the instruction is obeyed. The dead are not a legacy on the Sill. They are a functioning part of the population. They outnumber the living by a factor that has no useful name, and they are doing most of the work.

## The Waiting

A pan is a nation, and a nation is mostly not currently existing.

Take one the size of a barn floor, four centimeters deep in a good season. In the crust under it and around its rim there are perhaps two hundred million cysts. In the water, at the peak, there are perhaps four million animals. The rest is potential — the population is ninety-eight percent deferred, and the four million are not the nation, they are the nation's brief expression, the way a hand is a brief expression of a body.

They come up within the hour. The order is not random and it is not the same in any two pans: first the film-eaters, then the flatworm sheets sliding out over the wet rock like something poured, then the crustaceans in a burst that clouds the water for a day, then, on the third or fourth night, the leaf — a transparent swimming thing the size of a thumbnail with a single red eye, which arrives late because it needs the others to be there first.

The leaf has an eye. Nothing else on the Sill has one worth the name. This is not an accident of ancestry; it is an accident of tempo. For three weeks in a decade, in four centimeters of water that is going to evaporate, there is *hurry*. There is pursuit and there is flight and there is a thing that must find another thing before the pan goes bitter and shallow and hot. In that window, vision pays. Everywhere else on this surface, a year is a reasonable time to eat one meal, and an eye would be an extravagance, and there are none.

So the red eye opens once a decade, for a fortnight, and is the only fear on the plain.

Each pan's leaf is its own species and has been for a very long time. The pans differ by nothing you would notice — depth, salinity, how fast they dry — and those differences are the whole of the isolation. In a shallow pan the leaf matures in eleven days; in the deep one two kilometers east it takes forty; the two lineages have not exchanged a gene since the escarpment was a cliff of fresh rock, and they cannot, because between them lies a border of dry rust that neither can cross.

Except that it is crossed, constantly, by accident. The tripods walk. Their feet are pads and the pads pick up cyst-mud from the rim of one pan and carry it, wet, for thirty meters a day, and drop it, years later, at the rim of another. The customs house is also the post office. Almost every pan's fauna contains one lineage that does not belong to it, arrived on a foot within the last few thousand years, failing slowly, laying cysts into a chemistry it was not built for. And in the crust of the filled pans — the ones the pavement has healed over, the ones that no longer exist — there are cysts still waiting for a wetting that will not come, and every rare flood that cracks the seal hatches them into stone.

## The Leaners

Predation here is a posture held for a year.

The leaner is a smear, a millimeter thick, the color of old fat, and it moves by putting down a margin of acid and following it. It selects a neighbor — there is no evidence it selects; there is evidence it stops when a particular molecule exceeds a threshold — and it begins to hollow the crust beneath the neighbor's foot. Eleven months. The hollow deepens, the foot loses support, the fringe tears, the seal fails, and the leaner flows into the failure and drinks an animal that has been alive for a hundred and eighty years and did not, at any point, do anything about it.

There is no flight response on the Sill. There is no armor either, because armor is a response to being chased and nothing here is chased. What there is instead is a chemical sulk: a penny whose foot is being undermined thickens its ventral mucus and floods the seam with a compound that makes the leaner's margin sluggish, and this slows the process by perhaps six weeks, and that is the entire defensive repertoire of the dominant life form of this world.

Sometimes the leaner stops beneath a dead penny. The signature is right; the molecule persists in cement for millennia; there is nothing there. It will hollow for eleven months and flow into a mineral-filled shell and find no meat and lie there, and it will not stop, because stopping is not among its behaviors, and in a year or two it will begin again beneath the next dead thing. There are patches of the pavement, near the old centers, that are undermined into a lace by generations of leaners grieving a molecule.

And there are leaners that have taken up against the huts. The cement-worm tubes are meat, thinly held. They dissolve the base course of a dome over four or five years, and the worms above respond by thickening the base course, and the two of them have been locked in this at a millimeter a year for longer than there have been mammals.

## The Tall Ones

A tripod crosses the Sill about forty times in its life.

Thirty meters a day, four hundred years, no reason to hurry and nowhere to arrive: the herd's routes are not migrations but a slow braided wandering, and an individual will pass the same knob every eleven or twelve years, and will pass it on the same side, because the routes bend around obstacles and the obstacles here are almost all dead tripods.

That is the one avoidance behavior on this surface. When a tripod dies it stands for a decade, drying, held up by its geometry, and the herd goes around it. The path bows out, forty meters wide, and stays bowed for ten or twelve years while the pennies climb the legs and pave the body and the whole thing settles into a low mound, and then the bow straightens. It is obstacle avoidance. It is also the only thing on the Sill that has the shape of a detour made for something that is no longer there, and if you watch it for a decade you will find it very hard to keep calling it only that.

What the tall ones eat is elsewhere. The comb faces the fog and the fog is full of the lowlands: pollen, spores, mite eggs, the ballooning juveniles of spiders that will never see a web, dust from a desert on another continent, the ash of volcanoes that erupted before the escarpment existed. Every atom of phosphorus on this surface has passed through a comb. A tripod's gut is a sample of the world outside, and it is the only place on the Sill where the outside can be found in one piece.

Under their feet, in the drip circle, grows the only moss. It cannot cross the dry rust between stains. It has no dispersal stage of its own worth mentioning; it travels on pads, in mud, at thirty meters a day, and its entire ecology is the wake of the herd. When a route shifts — and routes shift, over centuries, as the herd's slow braiding drifts north with the weep line — the stains behind it go brown and the moss in them dies, and a new green appears ahead, and the moss has moved a hundred kilometers without ever having moved.

And some juveniles go into the huts.

A young tripod is a bread loaf on three folded stilts and it fits, just, through a forty-centimeter door. Inside it is dry and warm at night and out of the fog, which for a comb-building animal is a mixed gift, and it stays a season, and it comes out different. Not changed — nothing changes an individual here — but *selected*. The ones that shelter survive their first century at a rate the ones outside do not, and they are the ones that go looking for the next dome, and the looking is inheritable, and it has been inheriting for a long time. The huts were grown by worms following a rule. The rule specified a doorway of forty centimeters for a reason that has been lost. The doorway has spent that lost reason and acquired a new one: it is now, quietly, without anyone intending anything, breeding the tall ones.

## The Marks

A dragger lays about sixty stones in its life. The spiral is a kilometer across and contains something like nine thousand. No dragger has ever laid a shape. No dragger has ever been in the presence of the thing it is making; the largest arc any individual has ever contributed is a few hundred meters of curve, and it laid that arc one stone a year across six decades and died before the next arm came around.

The maintenance is the part that should not be possible and is merely chemistry. A quartz pebble sitting on iron hardpan for two thousand years leaches a shadow: a halo of altered pH, a whisper of dissolved silica, a ring where the dew holds a half-hour longer. Lift the stone and the shadow stays. It stays for centuries. And a dragger passing over that shadow stops, and sets its stone in it, because the shadow is the stimulus the behavior was built to answer, and the behavior does not distinguish between a stone and the memory of a stone.

So the marks are written twice: once in white quartz where you can see them, and once in the crust where you cannot, and the second writing is the durable one, and the first is only its current transcription.

Nobody knows what it was for. Territory, a trail, a marriage — the guesses are ours and they are worth what guesses are worth. What can be said is what the lines are for *now*, which is nothing anyone intended. White quartz on rust is a change in albedo of a few percent. It is a cold stripe at dawn and a warm one at dusk. Dew forms along it first and leaves it last. There is a cushion plant that occurs only within four centimeters of a pebble line and has done so, on the evidence of the laminations, for about a million years; there is a seam-mite that navigates by the lines and cannot survive away from them; there is a whole narrow ribbon of life running two kilometers straight across a plain that has no other straight things in it, living off the side effects of a habit whose purpose died before there were flowers.

The reason left. The habit stayed. Something else moved into the reason.

This happens everywhere here. It may be the actual character of the place.

## The Guests

Some residents are not usually present.

There is a cyst that hatches only under fresh fracture — the raw, unweathered iron exposed when a block falls off a knob. It has no other trigger. It waits in the pavement at a density of a few thousand per square meter, everywhere, all the time, and it may wait four hundred years in one spot, and when a block finally falls it comes up within a day, blooms in a scar the size of a table, eats the fresh mineral, lays down again, and goes quiet. There are perhaps eleven such wounds open on the whole Sill at any moment. The species is not rare. It is almost entirely asleep, and its waking life is scattered across a hundred kilometers and a hundred thousand years in patches that never touch.

There is another that waits for ash. Distant volcanoes, once or twice a millennium, put a grey film on the rust and a flush of phosphorus into the seams, and for two seasons the plain goes a slightly different color, and something that has no other occasion lives out its whole existence in that film.

And there is the nineteenth.

Every nineteen years the pavement spawns and for three nights the air is a slow blizzard of mouthless winged gonads, and out of the crust comes an animal that has been waiting for exactly this: a cyst with a nineteen-year clock, crude, drifting a year or two either side, which hatches into the cloud with a mouth. It has no other adult occasion. It eats for three nights. It mates in the fourth dawn among the drying bodies and lays and is gone, and its children will not be adults until the plain is nineteen years older, and they will not know anything, and they will not need to.

The winged forms themselves carry a single dark eyespot, which is useless to them, and which is the red eye of the pan-leaf, worn down to a speck. The pavement was colonized out of the pans, long ago, by swimmers that learned to farm and then to cement; and every nineteen years the whole plain briefly remembers it was once something that hurried in shallow water, and puts a hundred billion vestigial eyes into the fog.

## The Edge, and the Nine

On the knobs there is a species with nine individuals.

There are nine knobs. Each has a north face, which is the only shade on the Sill that is not made of another animal, and on each north face lives a slow lateral thing with its lens turned sideways, and it will never leave, and its entire reproductive hope is the nineteen-year cloud, which arrives from everywhere and is the only reason the nine are one species and not nine.

At the escarpment's lip there is a ribbon of trawlers, net-makers, animals that face outward and downward and harvest the lowland drift before the tripods get it: a fringe of the plain that is fed almost entirely by another world, thin as a hem, and it hears the roaring surf of the forest below with no organ whatsoever, and it does not know that there is anything down there, and it is made of it.

And in the forest, nine hundred meters down, in the wet green hurry, there is a small fast animal on the undersides of leaves, with two eyes and a gut and a generation time of eleven days, which is descended from a penny that went over the edge. It is the plain's only emigrant lineage. It has forgotten everything: the fog, the millimeter, the shading, the patience, the seams. It is the single thing anywhere in this world that is in a hurry, and it got that way by falling off.

## Who

So the honest answer to *who lives here* keeps sliding as you ask it.

Forty thousand animals per square meter, and none of them individuals. Nine thousand individuals, and each of them a slowly expanding ring of tissue walking away from its own dead center. Under each ring, four generations of shaded pensioners holding the handwriting of the last two hundred thousand years and passing it upward in their leakage. Under the pans, a hundredfold more life than is currently alive, waiting in the rock for four centimeters of water. Two hundred thousand tall things carrying the outside world in their guts and a moss on their feet. Four hundred rooms grown by worms for a guest that stopped coming, now quietly breeding the herd that shelters in them. Nine thousand white stones and their more permanent ghosts. And a handful of guests who are almost never here at all, and who are, between appearances, still residents, the way a cyst is still an animal.

None of it is coordinated. The noon lift is not a signal; it is a contagion, each penny rising when its neighbor's underside cools, a wave crossing a hundred kilometers at the pace of a slow walk, and it looks like a decision only because you are standing far enough away to see the whole of it and not close enough to see that nobody decided. The spawning is the same wave with a nineteen-year fuse. The flatness is the same wave with no fuse at all: bump grazed, hollow filled, scar healed in the old accent, over and over, a millimeter down and a millimeter back.

But it is not nothing, either. There is a self here — the seams know it, chemically, and defend it. There is a memory here — the shaded hold it and the laminations keep it and the pebble-shadows are its handwriting. There is a metabolism, an import economy, a border, a repair response, an inheritance, and something that has been maintaining the same shape against the same erosion since before there were flowers, without once being interrupted, without a single generation of the surface ever being empty.

It has no inside. It cannot consider itself. It has never had an idea, and it is not going to.

It is simply the oldest thing that has ever been continuously alive, and it lives here, and it is one, and it is made of everything above, and it will still be holding this floor at this exact elevation when the escarpment has retreated out of sight and the forest below has become something else and the nine knobs are eight.

The fastest thing ever to cross this surface is your attention, going from the seam to the whole plain in a second and a half. Nothing that lives here could do that. Nothing that lives here has ever needed to. They are not slow, in the way that a thing is slow when it could have been quick. Speed is simply not among the properties this place has ever had use for, and so it never acquired any, and so what grew up in its absence was this: nine thousand animals the size of villages, mending a floor, in the warm dark, at a millimeter a day, forever.
