## The First Answer

Ask at the station and they will tell you nobody lives here. That is nearly true and it is the wrong scale.

The houses are on the mineral ground, three of them with their lights on, and the men come in over the causeway at forty like everybody else and go out again at the end of it. What they leave behind is the whole of their residence: a flask on a radiator, a jacket on a nail in a hut two kilometres from anything, a boot print in the wheelwash that will still be there in March because nothing here has the manners to erase it.

But count properly. Nine on nights, two on the harvesters even in the dead season because the machines have to be moved and greased and told about, one in the weighbridge hut with the door locked against a draught, one walking the pumps. For eight hours out of every twenty-four the commonest large animal in the cut is a man. Then the gate goes, the lights along the conveyor stay on for nobody, and the vertebrate population of eleven kilometres drops to two ravens, a hare, and whatever is overwintering in the remnant that you will not see.

The men are also the only things in the basin that will rot. They keep themselves at thirty-seven degrees and roughly neutral, they take their water out with them at the end of the shift, and they carry a fine brown residue home in the cuffs of their trousers and in the small hairs of the nose. Everything else here is cured, or drying, or asleep, or on fire. The men are the exceptions. They are the perishable staff of an imperishable place.

The ravens are not exceptions. The pair on the gantry have held the whole basin since before the rail loop was re-levelled the second time, which is longer than anybody on the current shift has worked nights. They nest in the steel where the condensate keeps the frost off. They know the blow better than the men do — they are up before the sound finishes arriving, every eight hours, riding the thermal off the stockpile in a basin that has no other thermals, and then dropping back down to the drain edges to look at what the frost has brought out. They cache things in the culvert mouths. They have a territory the size of a parish and no rival in it, and they are the only residents who see the shape of the place from above, which is the only position from which it has a shape at all.

None of this is the population. This is the loud, large, incidental fraction of it.

## The Named One

The only thing in the cut with a name is the fire.

On the log it is *Stockpile 3 — thermal anomaly, ongoing*. Out loud it is the animal. It has been in there since July, and it has habits, and the men who walk it on a Thursday know them the way you know a dog. It stays deep. It likes the windward side, where the air gets in through the coarse layer at the base. It comes up at a new place every week or so and puts out a wisp of white about the thickness of a wrist, and then goes quiet for a while and travels a metre, and the probes read four hundred degrees three metres from a surface you could lay your hand on. It goes to ground when it rains. It does not go out. It has been eating the same body since the summer and will still be eating it in April, and it will finish before the pile does, because the pile is being sold.

They take its temperature. They keep it away from the reclaim tunnel. They talk about it in the present tense with a kind of grudging custody, and one of them, who has been here since the sod peat, calls it the last of the livestock.

It is worth being exact about what it is. The station is oxidation with a licence and a turbine on the end of it. The animal is oxidation without either. They are the same process at two speeds, and the only difference the men can point to is that one of them pays. In a hot dry year the animal gets off the lead. Then it goes down into the floor and travels sideways under your boots for weeks, breathing where the cracks are, coming up in a field nobody has been to, and you fight it with drains and dozers and there is no bottom to fight it against, because the bottom is five thousand years down and it is all the same food.

## The Population Proper

Take a handful from the remnant, from a hollow where the sphagnum is wet enough to look lit from inside, and squeeze it into a jar. There are more animals in that handful than there are in the whole cut with the ravens thrown in.

Testate amoebae, mostly. Each one a single cell in a house it built itself out of whatever was to hand — grains of quartz blown in from the mineral ground, recycled scales off other people's shells, plates it made and plates it stole. There is no calcium here to build with, pH three point eight and the lime all gone, so they build in sand and in protein, and they carry their houses around upside down and put out arms through the mouth to eat. Some of them eat bacteria. Some of them, the big ones, hunt the small ones and put a hole in the shell and take the contents. There is a whole predation in there the size of a full stop.

They also keep the records. Every species has a wetness it will tolerate and no other, and when they die the houses stay — nothing here finishes — so the peat is stacked with the abandoned architecture of five thousand years of water levels. A woman with a microscope can read a centimetre of the face and tell you the summer the surface dried, and which century it stayed dry, and when it came wet again. It is the only continuous account of this place, and it is written in the shells of things that never knew they were keeping it.

Then the rotifers, working their two wheels of cilia in the film on a leaf, and the tardigrades going through the cushion on eight legs with claws on the ends of them, at a scale where a millimetre is a county and a sphagnum leaf is a wet hillside with a forest of one-celled things on it.

And here is the part that matters. When the hollow dries — and hollows dry, and the remnant is only staying wet because the licence says the pumps must not take it — the tardigrade pulls in its legs, wrings itself down to a third of its size, and sets its own inside like glass. It is called a tun. It has no measurable metabolism. It can be blown across the field as dust, put in a drawer, kept for decades, and then wetted, and it will unfold in an hour and walk off as though nothing at all had occurred. Rotifers have been taken out of frozen ground elsewhere after twenty-four thousand years and have started dividing within a day.

They have solved the problem the entire place is organised around. They know how to stop without stopping.

## The One Who Stayed

He was older than the pines in the face. Curled, tanned the colour of the ground he lay in, fingernails intact, hair gone red from the acid, the last meal still in him. He is in a case in a city now with a label and a lighting rig, and the place where he was is a hole with string round it in the third field, and the extractor went around, and there is a notch in the field boundary that will be on the maps for as long as anyone keeps the maps.

It is important not to get this wrong. The bog did not keep him for us. It was not saving him. It refuses everything at the same rate and with the same indifference: the shoe with the laces still knotted, the butter keg with its grey wax, the alder trackway laid by people who wanted to cross dry, the pollen of a single summer, a man. There is no intention in it. It is a refusal that happens to be legible.

But the strategy is the strategy. A curled man in acid cold with no oxygen in the water is a tardigrade at the wrong scale — held, not finished, waiting on a change in the conditions that would destroy him if it came. Nothing in this basin has been permitted to complete. The moss is dead plants that never got to finish dying, five metres of them. The butter is a Bronze Age breakfast still in the vessel. The wood in the face is iron-coloured and four thousand years old and would still make a fire if you dried it. The animal in the stockpile is the same material finally being allowed to finish, and that is why it is the only thing here that could be called impatient.

And at the margin, on the one hawthorn, there are rags. Cloth, a child's sock, a strip of hi-vis, something that might have been white in the seventies. Hi-vis comes from here. Nobody at the station will tell you who ties them, and everybody knows about the tree, and the two facts sit together in the same men without any apparent difficulty, and they will change the subject to the price of gas.

## What Actually Lives Here

One thing, at several speeds.

Its body is eleven kilometres of fibre five metres thick, and most of it is water held in the cells of plants that died before the pyramids. Its fast face is the animal in the stockpile, a metre a month. Its working face is the station, oxidation licensed and geared to a shaft. Its slow face is the living skin, a millimetre a year, still laying itself down on the dome where the licence says leave it. Its inhabitants are its cells: the amoebae in their sand houses, the rotifers, the tuns in the dry cushions, the sundews turning carnivorous because the ground gives them nothing and they had to get it somewhere. Its eyes, such as they are, are two ravens on a gantry. Its nervous system is nine men on nights who are the only part of the whole arrangement that knows there is an arrangement, and who describe the time of day in sootblows.

And it breathes out. Continuously. That is the whole of it.

The cut is a bubble held open in a body of water, and the pumps are what holds it, and what they do is exhale — fifty hertz, four of them, three running and one on standby, taking the water out from underneath and putting it in the outfall and letting it go down to the river. It must never breathe in. In three years of silence it is a shallow lake. In thirty it is a fen. In three hundred it is a moss. In three thousand it is a dome with a forest buried in it at chest height and, somewhere in the black underneath, a curled man who was already old when the pines drowned.

There is a plan in a drawer for exactly that. Block the drains, let the water up, grow it back. It is not a plan for a habitat. It is a recipe for wetting a tun.

At four in the morning the man who walks the pumps goes out to the sump with a torch and breaks the skin of ice on the drain, and it goes with the sound of sugar. The frost is on his moustache and grey with dust. The plume is lit from underneath and stands up into the dark. Under the plume the accidental ribbon of moss is green and lush and dripping in a parish where nothing else drips. He puts his hand flat on the discharge pipe, which is the only warm thing for a kilometre, and he stands there and does what he actually comes out to do, which is listen — not to the roar, which tells you nothing, but underneath it, for the four separate notes, and for the standby, which is the note that means one of the others has stopped.

When a pump trips there is a gap. Half a second, sometimes less, before the standby takes it. In that gap the water begins to come up through the floor at a rate you can measure in weeks, which is to say at a rate you cannot measure at all in half a second, and which is nevertheless already happening.

He waits for the second hum. It comes.

Under everything, the pumps. Under the pumps, five thousand years of water with a gradient in it, not asleep, not anything, simply arriving — the way it has arrived at every drained field and every hollow and every dried tun in the history of this ground, patiently, from below, at the speed of the slow.
