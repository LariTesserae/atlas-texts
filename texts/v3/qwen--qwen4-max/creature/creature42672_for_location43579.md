## The Quick

There are a hundred and sixty of them, and the first thing you notice is that they are all listening to something you have stopped hearing.

Their chests are deep and their ribs sprung outward, barrel-built, and under the skin they carry an uneven rind of fat that a lowlander would find strange — thick over the kidneys, thick at the nape, a pad above each eye, thin on the shins. Babies are born with it. It is not beauty and it is not ugliness; it is the shape a person takes when the water is nine degrees and the work is eleven breaths down. Their resting pulse runs in the forties. In the water it falls into the twenties, and the blood pulls out of the hands and feet and goes to the middle, and the face changes — goes smooth and grave and slightly swollen, the face of somebody thinking hard about nothing. You can tell a working diver from a resting one across a plaza by the face alone.

Their fingertips have no prints left. Rope and cold have taken them. What they have instead is knuckle: each person's knocking hand calluses into a unique hard architecture, and a knock carries the timbre of the bone that made it, so that a tap on a rib is not a signal, it is a *signature*. You know who is at your threshold the way you know a voice. There are forgers of rope, of claims, of fish-tallies. There has never been a forger of a knuckle.

They hear with their jaws. The chord arrives through the ribs and the teeth and the skull, and after sixty years the air-conducted world goes quiet — the choughs fade, the shout at the rim fades — and this is not a loss here. It is a promotion. The old sit with their molars against a lignified vein and receive the town entire, every knock in every chamber, clearer than any young person hears it, and they are consulted, and they are usually right. Deafness is when you finally get the whole message.

Above water they mostly sign. The throats make shouting useless and the choughs make it pointless, so the air-language is hands, eyebrows, a tilt of the chin, a whole vocabulary of two fingers on the wrist. Below water it is percussion. Two languages, then, and the border between them is the waterline, and every person here crosses that border four or five times a day and changes tongue at it without noticing, the way you change the weight on your feet going up a stair.

And they are fast. This is the thing that takes longest to see. In a place where a house takes forty years and a note takes a century, the people are *quick* — they walk quick, they eat quick, they count quick, the counters' knotted cords running through their fingers at a rate that would look like panic in a lowland market. They have to. The air is thin; a breath is short; a distance is eleven breaths and not twelve. Their word for themselves means the living tissue inside a horn, the sensitive quick under the dead cork, and it also means *fast*, and nobody here thinks that is a pun. It is simply an accurate description of their position in the world.

## Names

A person is not one knot. A person is a stretch of cord.

Your name is a family, a whorl, a shape, a year, and a state — *coming*, *ready*, *drowned* — and when you meet someone the first thing exchanged is not a face but a claim: what you are waiting for, and how long. There are people whose entire standing is a hollow that does not exist yet, and they are not poor. There are people with a roof over their heads and no knot at all, and they are poor, and everyone is careful with them, and the carefulness is worse than the poverty.

Because the claims trade. A room due in thirty years can be sold for rope and fish now, and the buyer is buying the meristem's speed, and the meristem's speed is not guaranteed. There are men who have grown old rich on the growth of a whorl they never touched, and men ruined by a cold decade. There is a word for the sin of selling a claim twice and it is the second-worst word in the language.

And there are the orphans. Cords whose oldest knots have no living claimant, requests made by lines that ended, chambers that swell open anyway, decades late, into a town that has forgotten who asked. The shrimp clean them. Nobody lives in them. But once a year somebody goes to each one and knocks the threshold — a real knock, with a real knuckle, formally, on behalf of nobody — and because sound reaches every chamber in the Hold in under a second, all hundred and sixty people hear it, wherever they are, whatever they are doing. A hundred and sixty people pause in the green water while a room is greeted that no one will ever enter. Then the work goes on. It takes less than a heartbeat and it happens eleven or twelve times in a morning and it is not sad. It is the mail.

The census cord has a hundred and sixty knots and then, a hand's width further along, one more, dyed black. That is the core. It is counted as a household. It has been counted as a household for longer than anyone has been counting anything else, and if you ask why, people look at you the way you would look at someone who asked why a grandmother is on the family list.

## Nine Years Old

Before a child is taught to swim, they are taken to the notch: the healed scar in the dam face where the water went over four hundred years ago and took a third of everybody. They are shown it. Nothing is said about it that a child could not follow.

Then, at nine, they are apprenticed — to air, to rope, to fish, to the counters — and on the same day they are given a bone awl and taken down to the inner meristem and shown where to wound.

They pack it themselves. They chew the bast themselves, and mix it with ash and their own spit, and push it in with a thumb, and the Hold swells, and twenty to forty years later there is a room.

This is why the rooms are slightly the wrong shape. Not drift, not the plant's indifference: a nine-year-old decided. A child who wanted a window where a window would drown. A child who packed a bulge because a bulge seemed like a good idea. A child who made the mouth fish-shaped, so that four generations of a family have gone in and out through a fish's mouth and cannot imagine a house any other way, and would find a rectangular door vulgar. Somewhere below the Wash there is a chamber shaped like a joke nobody now understands, and above the water there is one whose request was packed by a boy who died the following winter, and the family that lives in it will not correct the pucker. They have been asked. They will not.

You may not have a child until you have a room coming. This is not law so much as arithmetic that has become manners. The consequence is that the town's birth rate is a function of the plant's growth rate, and in the decades when the water does not rise and nothing comes ready, no children are born, and the census cord has stretches with no knots in them at all. There are gaps in this people shaped like weather. A woman born just after one of the gaps is called *the one after*, and it is a thing you can be, and other people are *not* it, and it matters at councils.

## The Other Tenants

The combers are one species and two peoples. In the lit galleries they have eyes and use them; in the deep shafts they have almost none and go by the pressure of each other's turning; and they interbreed freely at the Wash, and the town has known for a long time that these are the same fish, and has never once been able to feel it. A shoal pouring through a plaza is read the way you read a sky. There is a knock for *shoal* and there is also a knock, unofficial, untaught, that means *the shoal is going down*, and everyone who hears it stops working, and no one can tell you why.

The snails are the second clock. Ten million mouths taking a micron at a time is a rate, and the rate is very close to the rate at which the Hold lays down new surface, and the town lives inside that near-equality: walls being eaten exactly as fast as walls being made. The hiss is not background. The hiss is the sound of the town staying the same size.

The caddis larvae build out of mica and stolen bast. Some of that bast has been rope. Some of that rope was cord. There are larvae dragging along the veins in cases that contain the fibre of somebody's claim, and the town knows this, and finds it funny, and the joke is old enough to have its own knock.

The choughs are the only thing here that makes anybody hurry. They nest in the cork fissures of the rim, hundreds of them, and their nests come apart, and what comes apart goes to the lip, and the lip must be kept. So the whole fast component of this slow world — the running, the shouting, the quick hands — is largely devoted to an eternal, unwinnable, entirely respectful war with a bird. Nobody hates the choughs. They are simply the other party to the town's one collective act.

The dippers walk the drowned handrails of the Wash like commuters. The mink has a name, and the name is a lineage, and there is a knot for it in the archive: a household, occupying chambers, taking fish. A fish is left for it, formally, on a rack, and the leaving is entered. It is the only wild animal in the world with legal standing, and it does not care, and this is felt to be exactly right.

And in the warm heart, the red worms and the eels, doing the work that makes the next room. When someone says *the room is my grandmother* they are not speaking figuratively and they are not speaking devoutly. They are describing a supply chain.

## The One Who Is Actually Here

All of that is a hundred and sixty people and their animals, and it is too small an answer, because there is only one *individual* in the cirque and it is not any of them.

The Hold is one organism. It has an age nobody can state and a lower bound everybody can: older than the lichen's square hand's-breadth, older than the town's name, older than the notch. It composts its own dead heart and holds a blood heat. It clots with a resin that sets under water. It answers wounds by making hollows, which is to say that its scar tissue is the entire built environment of the town — every room a keloid, every doorway a pucker in a healing, a cathedral of asked-for injuries, none of them ever finished closing.

It has been domesticated. Five fluted intake funnels, a raised rim, chambers on request, hardwood only from the dead: this is a bred thing, shaped by ninety generations of awls.

It has also done the domesticating. After a month here a person cannot sleep anywhere else. Their heart rate, their sleep, the counters' tempo, the rhythm of the town's whole day lock to the chord, and when the third throat went flat — within one old woman's lifetime, a matter for the council, argued for eleven years — there was a bad year, and then a bad decade, and the children born in it are still called *the old key*, and are said, by people who do not mean it unkindly, to be slightly out of time with everyone.

Those who go down out of the bowl come back wrong or do not come back. The quiet sickness. They lie in the dark humming a note they cannot get right. The sentence for cutting a live rib has two parts and the second is the spillway stair with your rope, but the first is worse and is not written anywhere: the knockers are told your knuckle, and they do not answer it. You walk through your own town knocking, and the town does not sound back. Then you go down out of the sound.

## Two Clocks

So: what lives in the col is one animal with two clocks.

The slow clock is the plant. Meltwater for a metabolism, ribs for a skeleton, the nine bells for lungs, the throats for a voice, the core for a gut, resin for clotting, tannin for an immune system that also happens to preserve everything the town has ever dropped. It grows a finger per generation. Its intentions take forty years to become a shape. Its memory is perfect and external: rope in the driest bell, and cold water below the Wash where nothing rots, so that a comb in a doorway is not a relic but a recent event.

The fast clock is the hundred and sixty. They are the quick under the cork. They run, they count, they carry breath up the Long Street level above their heads, they clear the lip, they dive eleven breaths into the warm and come back holding their great-grandmother's shoe, and they die, and go down the core, and become the wall of the room their grandchild moves into.

Between them there is a nervous system with a latency of under one second, and it is made of hardwood and knuckles.

And here is the thing that only becomes visible if you stay a season. The plant grew a mouth — five of them, and they boom, and the chord has been the same three or four notes for a hundred years at a time. But a mouth is not the interesting organ. What it grew, lately, in the last thousand-odd years, by wounding and coaxing and being tended, is an *ear*. Something inside it that can hear the third throat go flat and be troubled. Something that goes once a year to the only mark in the cirque the plant did not make, and wets a cord, and measures itself against stone, and knots the result, and argues about it for decades.

The council at the lip is this organism noticing its own size. The divers going down for a comb are it remembering. The knot-cord is it intending. The nine-year-old with the awl and the mouthful of chewed bast is it *wanting* — because the plant cannot want, it can only grow, and so it grew a part that can, and that part is cold and loud and frightened of the spillway and packs its rooms slightly the wrong shape on purpose.

There is a number they all know and nobody says. Clean stone above the waterline, and then the sill of the col, and above that no cirque, no melt, no bowl. Fingers per generation, floors per century, so many floors left. It is in the longest cord, coiled in the driest bell like a sleeping animal, its newest knot still damp. They can count. They are the counting part.

What they say instead — at a birth, at a launching, at the slow double knock that means the lip has been measured and the world is still holding — is a phrase that means *one more whorl*, and is used the way other people use *God willing*, and means precisely what it says.

And then the girl goes up the Long Street again through two hundred open doorways and a shoal like thrown rice, in green light, in a roar, at the top of the world, eleven breaths from the warm and forty years from her house, carrying a basket of silver breath above her head.

*Level, level, level.*
