Ask them what they are and they will take your hand, turn it palm up, and knock on it: three taps, a pause, two more. That is a name. It is also, in the older grammar, the word for *owing*, and no one here finds the pun worth remarking on. They are the debt. They are the animal the flowers are waiting for, and they have known it for so long that it has stopped being a discovery and become a chore, a calendar, a religion, and the reason a person gets out of bed with a brush in their belt.

**The body.** They are born hearing. This is the first thing to understand about them, because everything else in a life follows from the loss of it. An infant here has perhaps eight years of air — eight years in which the world arrives through the ears like it does for you — and then the plain takes that away, gradually, from the top down, the high frequencies going first, so that the first thing a child loses is the hiss of grit on the mats and the last thing is the human voice. They know this is happening. They prepare for it the way other peoples prepare for winter. There is a season in a child's ninth or tenth year called the keeping, when the family takes it around and makes it listen: to its mother's air-voice saying its name, to rain on the plug-field, to a struck clay flask, to the wind failing to be a sound. Memorize these. You will have them for sixty years and you will not get them again. Some children are diligent and some are not, and you can tell which were which, decades later, by what they can still hum.

What replaces hearing is bone. The skull here is dense, the temporal bone thickened, the jaw heavy and squared; there is a callus on the forehead just above the brow ridge, and another on the heel of each hand, and the pads of the feet are like a second set of palms. They hear with all of it. Two old people who have been married fifty years will stand in the wind with their foreheads pressed together and their eyes shut, humming — not speaking, humming, the larynx still working, the sound going nowhere in the air and everything through the frontal bone — and this is conversation, and it can go on for an hour, and it looks from a distance like a kind of sleeping upright. Mothers do it to infants. Lovers do it with teeth. When someone is dying you put your mouth against their temple and hum their name-pattern until they stop answering, and then you keep humming it a while longer, because the bone stays good for a little after the person does not.

Between the brows and the humming there is a whole ladder of registers, and a person's age is where they live on it. Children use hands: an elaborate, fast, beautiful sign that is the everyday tongue, useless in fog, useless in the dark, which is why every child also learns the touch-forms, the same language degraded to contact, spoken palm to palm. Adults use stone. Adults carry a hammer.

**The hammer.** A rounded chert cob, palm-sized, worn to a groove by the thumb. It is given at naming and it is buried with you, and it is not a tool, it is a voice box. Struck on the caprock it sends a tap for miles through the continuous hard skin of the plain, and everyone within those miles feels it in their soles, and anyone who cares to lays a jaw against a boulder and reads it. The code is dense and old, with grammatical inflections carried in the *timbre* of the strike — where on the stone, how damped, with what part of the hammer — so that a fluent speaker can be sarcastic through the ground.

A child's first hammer is a chip, and it taps high and faint, and everyone can tell a child is talking. Over a life the stone wears and its knock deepens and coarsens. You can hear a stranger's age in their tap the way you can hear it in a voice. To lose your hammer is to be mute; to have it taken is a punishment still on the books in some districts, though it is considered barbarous now, and the usual sentence is a stone of soft rotten sandstone that will not carry past a hundred paces.

And so: air is private, rock is public. This is the inversion that governs everything. Anything said in stone is said to the whole country and cannot be unsaid; law, price, gossip, marriage, the location of a fall, all of it goes into the ground and any child with a jaw can listen. Anything said in air is stolen by the wind before it clears the teeth, and is therefore the only speech that can be trusted with a secret. Confession, seduction, and the last words of the dying are all spoken aloud, into an ear, by people who mostly cannot hear it. Their word for a lie translates as *a thing said where it can be kept*.

**The house is not the house.** The turf-roofed prow on the surface is a shell for sleeping and getting out of the weather. No one would call it home. Home is a throat, and a household is a vertical thing, and their maps are not plans but sections, drawn on clay in profile, showing the lip, the fern shelves, the water, the galleries, and the names of who is at each level.

At the lip: children, on watch. A roof about to spall gives a warning, a rising thin whine in the last hours, and no adult can hear it, so the listening rota is staffed by the hearing — six-year-olds sitting cross-legged at the mouths with their hands flat, bored, important, waiting to hear the ground start to complain. When a child reports a whine the whole family comes up out of the shaft with the cords and the jars and the grandmother's chair. This is the last useful work a person does with their ears, and everyone remembers doing it, and the day your ears go and you are taken off the rota is the day you become an adult, and there is a feast, and it is not a happy one.

In the middle: the gardens. Two body-lengths down the roar is a hum, four down it is warm dusk, and the whole economy is in that dusk. Fern hearts, tubers in the wall pockets, mushrooms in the side galleries, snails farmed on the liverwort, blind fish in the black pools, the starchy hearts of the wool rosettes, the fat pale grubs of the moths. The vine that grows downward hand over hand is the rope crop, trained and pruned and spliced, and it is also the ladder, and it flowers twice — pale reflectors at the mouth, small wet bells near the water — and pays resin at the top and nectar at the bottom, and is the hinge on which the two worlds swing.

At the bottom: the old. Nobody who cannot climb lives above. The elderly go down and do not come up; they sit in the body-warm dusk by the pool with their feet in the water, tended by grandchildren who bring down food and carry up water, and they are not thought of as retired but as *ripening*. When they die they are laid in a gallery, and the gallery is where the compost is, and the compost is why the shaft is warm. Every shaft's heat is the slow burning of ten thousand years of fallen green and everyone who ever lived in it. You go down to be warm, and the warmth is your grandmothers, and this is not a metaphor to them; it is the sort of thing they say to children who complain about the smell.

The fish record the marriages. Each pool has its own blind fish, its own snails, its own small radiation of forms found nowhere else, because a throat is an island and has been for longer than there have been people. But the fish move, because brides move, and a bride brings a jar of her mother's water and her mother's fish, and so the genealogy of the fish across the district is a shadow of the genealogy of the women. *The fish follow the brides.* There are people whose whole craft is reading this — who can look at a pool and tell you which family married out of it four generations ago, and who are consulted, quietly, in disputes about inheritance.

The moths fly only below the lip. They are pale and heavy-winged and they have a ceiling: a hard terror of the mouth, an inherited sense of where the moving air begins, and any individual that loses it is taken by the wind and eaten by the bird. They find their home shaft by its note. They are tuned. And the plain is going low, and has been for as long as anyone has measured, and so there are moths now circling in the dark above a hole that no longer sings the pitch they were made for, and there are people who build clay flasks and set them at the lips and tune them to notes that no longer exist in the world, in order to call in the last of a kind. It sometimes works. It is the only kind of hunting here that anyone considers holy.

**The flowers have handles.** This is the thing a visitor notices last and cannot stop noticing afterward. The pale reflectors at the mouths are not shaped like flowers. They are shaped like tools. There is a grip — a ridged, dry, warm wrist of a stalk, exactly the diameter of a hand, angled so that a person leaning over the lip takes hold of it without looking. There is a landing shelf that fits a palm. There is a gland at the base that weeps the resin, and the resin is the wage. Ten thousand generations of hands have selected these plants for handliness, and the plants have selected the people for diligence, and the arrangement is now obligate in both directions: the flower cannot set seed untouched, and the people cannot make resin, pitch, glue, caulk, plug-seal, lamp, or medicine without the flower.

So the round is walked. Every household holds a set of mouths, and every mouth has its season, and you go from throat to throat with the fiber brush and the smear, leaning over the lip, taking the wrist, doing the work, and the plain gets to have sex. It is not romantic. It is a debt calendar, notched on a cord, and the notches are why the word for a person and the word for *owing* are the same three taps.

The worst crime in the country is not murder. It is letting a throat go unvisited. A hole nobody works for two seasons has a collar that browns and lifts; its flowers set nothing; its garden starves back; its dead go unturned; its note changes as the walls dry. There are feral holes all over the plain, brown-ringed, abandoned when a family died out or a district was driven off by dissonance, and they are dangerous and sad and full of the last snails of their kind, and there are people who rob them for old resin, and everyone agrees that these people are what the word for *not-owing* means, and it is not a compliment.

**Tuning.** Two mouths a little out of tune with each other produce a beat, a slow throb in the ground, and if the beat falls near the rate of a resting heart it makes people ill — a dread with no object, a nausea, children who will not sleep. A single roof spall can open a note that beats against a neighbour's and make a hundred generations of gardens unlivable. There is a whole law about this. You may not tune into a beat with me. You may not widen your throat to steal my consonance. You may not turn a thread of fog-water out of my funnel into yours; the catchment boundaries are surveyed and marked and argued over more bitterly than anything else, because water here is not a river you share but a private funnel of stone, and stealing it is stealing a garden's whole blood.

Arbitration is done by the listeners, and the listeners are the strangest people here.

They are raised in the stopped fields with their ears plugged from birth — a stopped field being a few hundred mouths capped with fired-clay baffles, tortuous little clay lungs that let a shaft breathe without letting it sing, so the garden below stays alive and the surface goes quiet enough to talk in. Keeping a field stopped is the labour of centuries; plugs blow, plugs crack, plugs silt, and a bad plug that seals rather than baffles will suffocate a throat and rot everything in it. Inside the quiet, people can *hear*, and so inside the quiet there is audible music: singing, clay flutes, a struck jar, the whole ordinary human acoustic world, existing in a stone's throw of relative silence and nowhere else. Every village has its songs and its songs cannot leave. Carry one past the plug-line and the wind takes it apart before the second phrase. To marry out is to lose your music. To emigrate is to become a person who once knew a tune.

The listeners keep the jars: sealed, stopped, tuned flasks of fired clay, reference pitches, the only fixed things in a country where every voice is dropping. Once a year the district's throats are compared against the jars, and the difference is notched into the long cord, and the long cord is the reason everyone knows the chord has fallen a fifth since their grandmothers. The jars themselves creep — clay is not forever — and there are correction tables for the jars, and corrections to the corrections, and a small bitter literature about whether anything at all can be held still. The listeners are the ones who can tell you exactly how fast the world is going quiet. They are not popular. They also do not sleep well, having been raised in quiet and needing it, and being obliged by their office to go out and stand in the roar with their plugs out and their teeth aching, listening to a country they love being converted into void.

Two parties have grown out of the long cord. The quietists hold that the roar is a wound and the silence is the healing, and they plug, and they buy up throats to plug them, and they are patient and rich and widely hated. The openers cut new mouths — which can be done, with fire and water and a great deal of time — to put voices back up in the range where voices used to be. A cut throat is a scream with nothing in it: no compost, no ferns, no fish, no snails, no name, no dead. It takes four hundred years to become a hole. The openers know this and cut anyway, and their grandchildren tend the raw new mouths and lower the first jars and are, in a modest way, the only people on the plain doing anything about the future.

**The towers.** One vertical thing for days, drilled full of flues, screaming a fixed note into the weather. It is not a monument; it is an instrument being played continuously, forever, by a family on a rota. Flues are plugged and opened with clay and wax as the temperature and pressure move, hour by hour, to hold the note true, because people out in the fog are walking home on it and a lapse kills them. The watch never ends. There is a lineage in the north whose entire recorded history is the keeping of one pitch through eleven rebuilt towers, and the masons who can drill a new tower to an old note are the highest artisans alive, and when one dies the whole district feels the news arrive through its feet.

**At the edge** the caprock is broken and will not carry a tap, so knock-speech fails, and the wind takes everything else, and the highest voices left are up there thinning toward nothing. No one lives there. The watchers who tend those last throats are struck off the rolls before they go and re-named when they come back; while they serve they are, legally and in every ordinary sense, dead. They speak by tracing on each other's palms and backs. They keep a record of the notes as the notes go, in a script of strokes on skin and on clay, and their whole theology is that a word should not outlive its saying, which is either the deepest thing anyone here believes or a symptom of the altitude.

**The bird** trawls the updrafts. Everything the plain exhales — grit, spores, spiders, the moths that lost their ceiling — goes up in the trembling columns and is harvested by that enormous slow mouth going over, and the bird carries the plain's emigrants down over the lip to the cloud floor and the trees, and this is the only export the country has. It lands at the lake, on the unbroken lens, where there is no roar and the water laps and a child is brought once to be shown what silence is.

There is a tree there. One, in the lee of a boulder, low and wide and espaliered by the wind, older than any lineage, planted by the bird's droppings some unrecorded year and kept alive by the lake and the absence of noise. Everyone has seen it or will. Cuttings are taken and cuttings die. The children who weep at the lake and cannot stop are carried home, and most of them recover, and the ones who do not are, eventually, taken down the cliff by the resin-carriers and left in the forests with people who talk in air and are therefore liars, and who sing, all day, effortlessly, the songs that cannot be carried out of a stopped field. The plain's music is all down there. It has been draining that way for centuries, along with the water and the moths and the noise.

What stays is the round. Up before dawn, when the fog is running in threads to the nearest mouth and the mats are stiff with dew and the chord is at its lowest and thickest; down the vine with the jar; up with the water; out along the touch-lines with the brush and the resin, taking hold of the warm ridged wrist of a flower that has spent ten thousand years learning the shape of a hand, doing the work, moving on, owing. At night, a jaw on the stone, feeling the district's note come up through the shelf into the skull, which is how they fall asleep and how they know they are home. Somewhere out there a roof lets go, and a new note enters the world, and by next season there will be a green collar around it, and someone will knock the news into the ground, and a child at the lip will have heard it coming.
