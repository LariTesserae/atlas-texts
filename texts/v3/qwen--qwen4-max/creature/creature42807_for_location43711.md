## What Lives Here

The census has a column for identity and the crews leave it blank. This is not ignorance. They know exactly what is standing in the ring, how tall it is, how many of it there are, what it eats and what eats it. They simply decline to say what it *is*, because on the Sill that is a question you ask once and then spend nineteen years apologising for.

What follows is the part of the ledger they keep in the back, in pencil, where the tallies are.

---

**The grey.** First and oldest and least counted: the mat. It lies over sixty miles of warm sediment like a skin over a burn, grey, wet, faintly resilient, in places a single cell thick. It was here before the plain was. It was here when the plain was still water, and when the plain came up it did not move; it simply found itself exposed and continued.

The mat is the organ by which the Sill hears.

It has never been told what to be, and so it is grey, which is the colour of a thing awaiting instruction. When the wires hum *this is the shape you are*, it is the mat that receives the hum, sixty miles of it, all at once, and complies — holding the profile, keeping the channels single, keeping a foot a foot. And when a man shouts, it is the mat that hears the shout, and the mat is obliging. That is all. There is nothing malevolent underfoot. There is something *helpful* underfoot, sixty miles wide and a hundred and forty years old and without any judgement whatsoever, and it will try to give you back what you gave it, and it will keep trying, and it will get better at it.

The crews do not hate the mat. You do not hate the floor of your own house. But they walk on it the way you walk past a sleeping infant you have been told is a light sleeper.

**The shingle.** Above the mat, the crabs: pale, blind, shoulder to shoulder, ten thousand to the acre, and they are not individuals in any sense the Board can use. A crab here is a unit of area. Lift one and the gap closes before your hand is straight; not by another crab crawling in, but by the shingle simply *being* one crab denser there, the way water is one water deeper in a hole.

They stridulate below hearing, in the shins. Stand still on the flats for an hour and your leg bones fill with a fine granular chatter, and if you have been on the Sill long enough you can read it — density, mood, the approach of a stroke. Old gaugers diagnose their own illnesses by how the crabs sound in their tibiae. The Board once tried to record the chatter with a felted horn and a wax drum and got a cylinder that no one has played since.

They are ballast. Their weight is part of the specification; the profile is held partly by the hum and partly by ten thousand pale bodies to the acre pressing down. And they are not yet crabs. They have no eyes because eyes have not been arrived at. They have the general idea of a crab, in bulk, the way a foundry has the general idea of a bell.

**The worms, and the thimbleful.** Under the crabs, the worms, which eat the mat's mistakes. Everything the mat gets wrong — a doubled channel bank, a piece of ground that has come up as generic — the worms take down and pass through themselves and cast out, and the cast is *soil*. Actual soil: rounded, dead, finished, the first on the Sill.

There is perhaps a thimbleful of soil on this plain at any moment, and all of it is inside a worm.

The crews know this. It is the one thing they are sentimental about. A gauger who has walked the grid for forty years will tell you, in taps, that he is making a country a thimbleful at a time, and that his grandfather is in it.

**The pending.** Under the worms, the flat things. Nobody has described them properly and it is Board policy that nobody does. They are two-dimensional. Not thin — *flat*, lacking the third dimension entirely, so that a sounding rod passes down through where they should be and touches marl, and yet the rod comes up wet with something, and there is a shape on the flats where nothing is.

They are the plain's unadopted proposals. Drafts. Things that have been sketched and not yet given thickness.

In the year of the second census a naturalist from the port described one: four pages, careful, with measurements. It became a bird. Badly — a bird the way a word shouted through a wall is a word. It stood in his tent for two days and then went out into the fog and the naturalist went home and the Board struck his pages out of the record and instituted the blank column, and the column has been blank ever since, and this is why.

---

**The fallen light and its livestock.** Light here is a fluid with a food chain.

It falls, it pools, it drains into the sediment over nine hours, and in the ninth hour it goes sour and grows a skin, and in the skin things breed. Pane-flies, whose whole life is nine hours long, so that a generation is exactly one puddle. Skimmers that lay in a lit gauge-face and hatch into the reading. A small white midge that exists only in light that has been carried in a tin pan and set down again — light that has been *handled* — and which the crews call, in taps, the thing that comes of using it.

Then the grazers: pale slugs under every pylon lamp, digesting the fallen light and glowing faintly for an hour afterward.

Everything eats the grazers. This is the hinge of the whole district. The light goes into the slugs and the slugs go into the eels and the birds and the crabs and the moths and the men, and so everything on the Sill glows faintly for an hour after it eats. The plain has no illumination of its own. It is lit entirely from inside its animals. On a good night in the Sweating you can stand at a plinth and see the whole sixty miles as a slow shifting constellation of digestions, a hundred thousand small green announcements moving across the dark, and read from them where the food is, and where the pumps are, and where something has died.

**The rings.** Vegetation exists only where a surface manufactures rain, and so the flora of the Sill is a map of its machinery. Every pylon has a garden. Every derrick leg stands in a private meadow. The stranded dredger has a forest under its keel, forty feet of candle-cane, the largest living thing on the plain by volume, and it is living on the drizzle of a hull that has not floated in ninety years.

The candle-cane is not a plant. Cut a stalk and there is no cell, no fibre, no sap; there is a wet joint and a small cessation. It is a drip that has got into the habit of standing up. It is the *shape of falling water*, persisted, thickened, jointed, eating light out of the mud at its foot. Where the drip stops, the cane does not die; it simply stops having been there, and the ring closes over the gap, and the census records the number of the ring and the height of the tallest stalk.

The glasswort is provisional in a different way. It is trying to be glasswort. Some seasons it manages. In the next census the same ring is sedge, and the sedge is trying, and the crews write the height and leave the name.

---

**The felt.** Heat-moths, in sheets, reading temperature with their wings.

They have no eyes and no interest in light. What they have is a wing that is an isotherm made flesh, and they fly along a temperature the way a gull flies along a cliff-edge. Anything at blood-heat draws them: a pump housing, a bearing, a man. They settle in a felted mass a foot thick and they read it, and their reading is not passive. A mass of heat-moths on a warm machine holds it at thirty-four degrees with an accuracy no governor on the Sill can match, and the crews have stopped fighting this and started using it. Half the pump-houses are thermally regulated by a living blanket that has to be brushed off the intake every stroke.

A man asleep in the open will be read down to ground temperature over about six hours. He does not die of it. He wakes up feeling excellent, and slightly less specified than he was, and the Board has a form for this.

Their scales are the dust of the district. It is in the water, the oil baths, the bread. Everyone on the Sill coughs felt.

And once — this is in the pencil ledger, not the ink — the Board tried using them as survey instruments, on the theory that a thing which reads temperature that finely must read the plain finely. They released a marked sheet at the unnumbered edge and plotted where it settled. It settled in a ring nine miles out, on bare mud, in a circle forty feet across, and stayed there four days.

The crews laid a pump-house on that spot eleven years later. The moths had been reading the heat of a machine that did not exist yet.

Nobody discusses this either.

**The transit.** Sump-eels: a hundred thousand at any moment, wriggling across open sheet-water, all silent, all going the same slow way.

They are going to the edge. The unmeasured edge, where the silt is warm and generic and has not been told. They arrive, and they lie down in it, and they stop being eels, and they become the grain of the ground — a bank, a bar, the firm side of a channel. The generic silt out there is not empty. It is full of animals that have consented to be geography.

The direction of the transit changes about once a century. It has been swinging east for sixty years. It is now pointed at the new span, and the crews laying that span know perfectly well that they are not building a causeway across mud. They are building a causeway across volunteers.

---

**The listeners.** Here is the thing the visitors never get told properly, because it takes too long to say in taps.

The birds are the echoes.

A noise made on the Sill nucleates them. Not loudness — *onset*. The plain copies edges, beginnings, the sharp attack of a sound; a continuous tone has no edge to copy and is safe, which is why the hum can run forever at fifty cycles and why the pumps can be as strong as they like provided they take eleven minutes to arrive at their stroke. It is not a quiet industry. It is a *soft-onset* industry. Belt drives, felted cams, oil baths, every stroke eased into being like a hand into a glove. A roar raised over an hour makes nothing. A whisper begun sharply makes a bird.

They come up out of the mud in the fog, stilt-legged, eyeless, ear-slots wide as a hand, and they stand in the pans, and they gape.

They gape because they are full. Each has one noise in its crop and cannot swallow it and must not give it back, and the gaping is the whole of their effort, all day, forever. They do not call. They are *not calling* with everything they have.

Shout, and four come. The first stands off a mile. The second at half. The third at a hundred yards. The fourth comes closer each time, over a week, or a year, or forty years, and it is not hurrying, and it will arrive.

Kill one and it empties. There is a Board protocol, four taps, that means: *a dead bird and a noise standing over it, do not approach, do not answer it, wait for it to be reabsorbed.* There are men who have spent a whole shift standing felt-soled and motionless twelve feet from a sound their grandfather made.

So the bird population is the record of every sharp noise ever made on this plain, standing about in the fog, digesting nothing, holding.

And in the chapel grove there are the small ones. The corrugated roof manufactures the largest rainfall on the Sill, and every drop is a tiny sharp onset, and each makes a bird the size of a thumb, and there are tens of thousands of them under that very good roof, in the biggest candle-cane in the district, each holding one drop.

Their combined noise is the sound of rain.

That is what the chapel is for. There is no god in it. There is a bench, and a roof, and the crews come in from nine miles of held silence and sit with their hands on their knees and listen to water falling, which is the only sound on the Sill permitted to be sharp, because each piece of it is too small to specify anything. Men weep in there. It is entirely ordinary and nobody mentions it. The chapel's regular congregation is thirty thousand birds and whoever needs it.

**The one with the disc.** In the chapel grove, standing among the small ones, there is a large one.

Ninety-odd years ago it swallowed a brass benchmark off a plinth on the eastern grid. **LEVEL: TRUE**, stamped in brass, now in its crop, and it will be in its crop when everyone reading this is a thimbleful of soil.

Measured ground stays. It has a measurement inside it. It is therefore fixed: it cannot be revised, doubled, softened, or renumbered. Its number in the ledger is the same in every census, and has been for five censuses, and it is the only entry on the Sill of which that is true.

Which means it is the only individual here. Everything else is a draft. This is a *somebody*.

It eats light-grazers. It stands in the drip. It gapes, because it has a noise in it too, and nobody knows what noise, and it does not say. The crews leave it alone and the children are taken to see it once, and it is the closest thing to a god within sixty miles, and it is outside, in the weather, in the cane, and the chapel is empty behind it with its very good roof.

This is why benchmark theft is the worst crime. Not because a disc is expensive. Because a stolen disc costs the district a fixed point — and because it makes another one of *those*, out there, unnumbered, permanent, and a second fixed point is a disagreement about the shape of the world, and the Sill cannot afford two opinions.

---

**The legs.** Some of the tall things in the fog are pylons. Some are legs.

They are wading legs, and they belong to bodies that are always past the horizon, because distances out there do not add and a body nine miles off is nine miles off in a way that keeps being true however far you walk. The crews have surveyed the ankles. The ankles are concrete plinths, and there are benchmarks set in them, and the benchmarks read TRUE, and the Board maintains them, because whatever else it is, it is *measured*, and therefore it is infrastructure.

They drink the light-lakes. They come to the sorting hall and stand in the chest-high stale light and take it up somehow, and the numbers of the legs change between censuses because legs get revised, and the crews renumber them, and no one discusses it.

For forty years the cannery canned crabs. For eleven years after that it canned what the legs left in the sorting hall, which was copies: of crabs, of eels, of the light, of the men who were standing in the hall at the time. The tins are labelled with a number and no name, and *CONTENTS: 1 (ONE)*, because a sealed measured tin is the only other way besides brass to make a thing stay itself.

The warehouse is still there. It is the most guarded building on the Sill. In the last census the tally inside did not match the tally outside, and the discrepancy was noted, in pencil, and the door was felted.

**The last shift.** Thirty-one went into the sorting hall the day the line stopped. Or thirty-four. The number depends on the census.

They are still in there, in the light, being revised. Now and then one comes out, and it is a man you knew, with the right face and a slightly different arrangement of legs, and he is looking for the end of his shift, and the Board numbers him and finds him work and does not discuss it. There are three on the eastern grid now. One of them is very good with a sounding rod. His hands are wrong and he compensates and he is the best chain-man in the district and everybody knows whose grandfather he is and nobody says so, because saying so is a noise.

---

**The crews.** Two hundred and some, in ones and threes at nine-mile intervals, felt-soled, felt-cuffed, four hundred taps of vocabulary, teeth humming at fifty cycles from the day they are born.

They are born at thirty-four degrees and they cannot sleep anywhere colder. Off-plain they get the quiet sickness: the fillings stop, the shins go empty, and the silence has edges on it, and they lie awake in a normal night listening to a normal world *begin* things. Most come back. The port that walked away is full of Sill men who did not.

Their children are measured within the hour — length, weight, position, stamped and entered — because an unmeasured child on this ground will start to double, and there is nothing cruel in this, it is the same reason you put a foot on a ladder. Names are numbers and numbers are reissued every nineteen years, and a man is Fourteen until he is Forty, and his wife is a different number after each census, and they remark on it in taps the way people elsewhere remark on grey hair.

Their dead go into the mud. Not burned, not boxed: into the mud, at a plinth, so that the worms can have them, so that the thimbleful grows. There is no other soil-making on this plain and every crewman knows he is a contribution. It is the whole of their religion and it needs no god and no roof.

And there is the one who shouted.

She was seven, before she had a number, and she did what children do on a plain with no edges: she shouted to find out where she was. Four came. She is sixty-one now. The first three have arrived and stand about her hut in the fog, gaping, holding pieces of a child's voice, and she feeds them light-grazers out of a tin pan and they do not swallow.

The fourth is at her shoulder. Has been for two years. It has stopped walking.

She does not remember the word. Her mother, who is dead and in the mud at Plinth Nine, said it was her name — the name she had before the Board gave her a number, the only proper name ever spoken aloud on the Sill. The ledger for that year says: *one noise, four returns, pending.*

When the fourth gives it back she will have a name, and a name is a measurement, and a measured thing stays. She will be fixed. She will be the second individual in sixty miles, standing in a grove with the other one, permanent, unreviseable, finished, ordinary, and she will die properly and stay dead and be soil.

She has begun opening her mouth. Very slowly. It takes her eleven minutes.

---

**The youngest.** And over all of it, the dark.

It is a hundred and forty years old, which on this plain means it is the newest thing here, newer than the cane, newer than the crabs, newer than the crews. It is not an absence and it is not a condition. It is an animal the plain made for itself, first, before it tried anything else, and it is still settling — still finding its edges, still learning to lie flat over sixty miles without pooling in the low places, still coming in too thick at the unnumbered edge and too thin under the lamps.

It is a juvenile. It is the larval form of night.

That is what everything here is. The crabs are the larval form of crabs; the glasswort is a larva of glasswort; the flat things are larvae that have not been assigned; the birds are the larvae of echoes; the men are larvae of men, unrounded, unweathered, provisional, numbered instead of named. The whole Sill is a nursery sixty miles across, blood-warm, and nothing in it has finished arriving, including the dark, including you.

And in some centuries it will all pupate. The arrises will round. The thimbleful will become a field. The pumps will stop one at a time and the hum will go out of the wires and the plain will hold its own shape without being told, like a grown thing, like anywhere. The birds will finish digesting and be echoes and disperse. The one with the disc will be a bird. The dark will simply be night, and no one will be able to scoop it, and nothing will breed in it.

Until then the crews go out at every stroke with their pans and their padded hammers and say the shape of the world into the wires, patiently, forever, the way you sit up with something newborn: *this is the shape you are, this is the shape you are.*

Out at the new span the felted hammers are going, and a fresh wire is dripping on ground that has only lately consented to exist, and a ring is starting, and in the ring something is standing that has not been given a third dimension yet.

The Board has numbered it.

It is following them home.
