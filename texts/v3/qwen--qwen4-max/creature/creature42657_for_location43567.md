## What Lives in the Vesicle

Start with the only thing in the chamber that makes anything.

The film is forty microns thick and it is not a skin, not a scum, not a coating in any sense a wet world would recognize. It is a reaction front. Sulfide comes up out of the seam; oxygen comes down through the crack; they are not permitted to meet anywhere except on glass, and on glass they meet inside cells. Above the middle shelves the gas is too oxidized to feed it. Below, too reduced. Between, in a band six shelves deep, the two exhausts of the mountain interpenetrate at exactly the ratio a bacterium can use, and there the film burns — cold, slow, without flame, at a rate set entirely by how much air a four-millimeter slit will admit.

Everything alive in here is downstream of that flame. And the flame's product, the thing it makes that nothing else in the chamber can make, is water. A few molecules at a time, formed inside a cell and never let go of.

So the first accurate thing to say about the inhabitants is not that they are animals. It is that they are shapes for holding new water in the dark.

---

The machine breathes through them.

The shelves sit at the pressure nodes, which is to say at the velocity antinodes, which is to say the felt has settled in the only places where the gas is moving hardest. Twenty-one times a second the entire atmosphere of the world slides one way and then the other, and the distance it slides is about a quarter of a millimeter. Less than the length of a mite. Forty-two reversals a second, forever, and no net wind anywhere, ever.

This is why the film does not smother in its own exhaust. This is why the middle galleries are breathable at all. The animals live inside a lung that never inhales and never exhales, only shudders, and their hairs — every hair on every leg in the chamber — are tuned to a movement smaller than they are.

On the upper shelves the springtails make a pavement. Not a metaphor: a continuous tiled crust of bodies, abdomen to abdomen, flexing as one sheet when the cycle passes and slackening when it goes, so that from any distance you could not resolve it would look like the felt itself was alive, a grey-green hide with a pulse. Individuals move through it the way a cell moves through tissue, by displacing neighbors. A springtail here does not have a territory. It has a position, briefly, and the position is the thing that persists.

When one crosses a gallery gap it does not entirely jump. The field takes it. It leaves the shelf charged and arrives opposite charged differently and is *pulled* the last centimeter, and the animals have learned to launch at the phase of the cycle when the pull is favorable, which means the entire population crosses gaps in synchrony, in a rain of small bodies flung shelf to shelf forty-two times a second, most of them going nowhere, a few of them arriving.

The mites are slower and heavier and armored like something that expects to be dropped. They are, functionally, canteens with legs. A mite's contribution to the world is to hold a microliter of manufactured water inside a chitin shell and walk it three body-lengths an hour along a filament, and to be eaten eventually by something that will walk it further.

The thysanurans graze in files, three body-lengths apart, silver and flat and fond of heat. That spacing is exact and it is maintained. It is the only regular structure in the chamber that is not precipitation or standing wave. Nothing built it. It is simply what happens when ten thousand animals each keep exactly one neighbor's distance from the next, and it is the closest thing this world has to architecture: a lattice made of restraint.

---

Speech here reaches two centimeters and stops.

Consider what that does to the content of a message. There is no possibility of describing anything. By the time a signal has crossed one animal it has been re-generated, re-timed, re-owned; by the tenth it is a different signal entirely, and by the ten thousandth it is a weather system. Nothing that crosses the chamber arrives as it left. What travels is not a statement but a *state* — a wave of alarm, of arousal, of stillness — and the wave is the only entity in the world larger than a body.

It has properties. It has a speed, half a centimeter a second. It has a refractory period. It reflects off the glazed wall and comes back confused. It dies at the margin where the wool is old and dead and does not conduct, so the edges of the world are silent, and animals that wander there vanish from the conversation and are, in the only sense available here, no longer anywhere.

And it costs. Drumming is muscular; muscle burns sugar; sugar was made from water that was made from sulfide by a film capped by an aperture. An alarm that crosses the whole chamber spends something on the order of a hundredth of a drop. The world's entire liquid capital is a cup. A panic is a withdrawal.

They have therefore become extremely reluctant to speak. Two thousand generations of selection against unnecessary conversation have produced animals that will stand motionless with a predator's leg on the fiber beside them and say nothing at all unless the touch has a particular signature. Silence here is not the absence of a message. It is thrift.

---

The pseudoscorpion grips one filament with both pedipalps and waits.

Not hours. It may wait eleven days. Its whole life is conducted inside a radius of about four centimeters, and it will very likely die on the fiber it was born on, having eaten perhaps forty things, having been touched by ten thousand neighbors, having never once been out of contact and never once been anywhere.

The spider does not build. It stands with its legs on four filaments at once and reads them as a chord — four separate conversations arriving from four directions, and the difference between them is a *direction*, which is the nearest thing to a spatial concept any animal here possesses. It hunts by walking down the gradient of agitation.

The centipede is the interesting one.

It patrols a gallery end to end in forty minutes. Rumor crosses the same distance in eleven. Which means the centipede is permanently preceded by its own announcement: a bow wave of alarm runs ahead of it at three times its walking speed, and everything connected to the network knows it is coming a quarter of an hour before it arrives, and steps aside, or freezes, or is passed the instruction to freeze by a neighbor who was passed it by a neighbor.

It cannot ambush anything that is in contact. It has never eaten anything that was in contact.

What it eats is the disconnected. The animal on a snapped filament. The one at the dead margin. The one that has just molted and is soft and silent and off the network for forty minutes, receiving nothing, telling nothing, alone in a crowd of a million. Molting is the great mortality here, and it is not dangerous because the animal is soft. It is dangerous because for a little while it cannot be told anything, and the patrol is a machine for finding the places where the conversation has a hole in it.

So the predator does not hunt bodies. It hunts gaps in the correspondence. Death, in this chamber, is defined acoustically: to die is to stop being relayed.

---

And then there is the reader.

Palm-sized, two millimeters thick, blind as everything else, and it has done the one thing no other lineage here has managed: it has extended its touch. The first pair of legs are whips four times its body length, and it lays them out along the fibers ahead and above and below, and it reads.

Twenty centimeters. Thirty, on a good filament, in the clean upper wool where the acid has not yet dulled the glass.

Ten neighbors' distance. It is the only animal in the world that has ever received a signal from something it is not standing next to. It is the only animal that has ever been told about a stranger. It reaches across a gallery and lays a whip along the underside of the shelf above and hears, faintly, at the very edge of attenuation, a conversation it is not part of.

Nobody knows what it does with this. It does not broadcast — it cannot; its own signals die at two centimeters like everyone's. It is a listener with an audience of one and a range of ten. It is the world's only asymmetry: an animal that receives more than it can possibly return.

It works the fringe, the feathered two centimeters where a shelf's felt frays out into loose fiber and the springtail pavement thins into individuals. It reads the fringe the way a hand reads a page it cannot see, and when the pattern under the whip changes — when the tile of bodies stops being a tile and becomes a few — it strikes.

It does not carry its young. Its ancestors did; amblypygids everywhere else in the world hatch their brood onto their backs and carry them, a millimeter of children stacked on a millimeter of mother. Here that millimeter is the difference between fitting and not fitting. So the reader lays her eggs singly, deep in the felt, one at a junction and the next junction four centimeters away, and she walks off, and the spacing is the entire extent of her maternal care. She will read her own offspring a hundred times with the tips of her whips and register them as small unfamiliar objects and pass on.

She is measurably flatter than her great-grandmothers. You can go and check. At the wall margin, where the wool is old and dead and dry and has not conducted a signal in a century, the bodies are still there — nothing wears, nothing rots, nothing here has ever been wet — and they lie in the order they fell, uncompressed, generation under generation, an archive with no archivist. The readers in the deepest wool are a full millimeter thicker than the ones alive today. You can read the shrinking off the wall like a spine of a book.

---

But the body is the wrong unit, and it takes a while to see it.

Follow the water instead. A molecule is made in a bacterium on the fourth shelf on a Tuesday, held for nine hours, eaten into a mite, held for six days, eaten into a pseudoscorpion, held for a month, released at that animal's death into the film again, taken up, and by the following year it is inside a springtail on the eighth shelf, three meters and two hundred bodies from where it was born. It has never once been free. It has never been a droplet, never been dew, never been at a surface. It has only ever been *inside*, passed along, and the passing is the only continuous thing in the chamber.

The cup of water is older than any animal and younger than the crack. It has been through, at a conservative estimate, ten million bodies. It is the single object that everything here shares, and none of them can perceive it, because perceiving requires a signal and a signal reaches two centimeters and the water goes everywhere.

The bodies are the plumbing. The generations are the pump. If you insist on asking what individual lives in the vesicle, the honest answer is: one, made of water, four meters wide, two hundred years old, temporarily distributed among about a hundred million knots, none of which can be told about the whole.

---

They do have a calendar, though nobody keeps it.

When a storm crosses the plateau the edge tone slides up a whole tone and comes back down over an hour. The animals do not hear the tone. They feel the pressure change in the slosh, in the field, in the way the fibers rub. And a few days later, sometimes, the ceiling crack delivers a drift of ash — a fall of grey grit onto the top shelf, and with the grit, nitrate, fixed by lightning in air that has nothing to do with this mountain.

Nitrogen is the aperture's other limit. The world is normally nitrogen-starved. Then, once every few years, the sky feeds it.

The film blooms within a day. The pavement doubles in three generations. For about a season — six weeks, eight weeks — there is more water in the chamber than at any other time, the readers grow fat and lay twice as many eggs, the patrol runs a shorter route because there is so much soft, molting, disconnected flesh to find. And then it is over, and there follow thirty lean generations, and the fat ones are not remembered by anything, because nothing here can hold a memory longer than a body.

But the wool remembers. The ash sits where it fell, in a layer, and the next fall sits above it, and the dead margin holds two centuries of them, thin grey lines in gold felt, a stratigraphy of storms. Alongside them lie the bodies, in order, getting flatter. The chamber has a complete and perfectly legible history and not one thing inside it can read a line.

---

Two curves are crossing.

The crack widens by a flake or two a century: more oxygen, more film, more water, more of everything. And the galleries close by a millimeter every twenty years while the felt thickens into them: less room, less room, less room. The world is getting richer and smaller at the same time, and its inhabitants are being quietly resized to fit the difference — the great hunters already gone from the bottom two shelves, the readers thinning, the brood abandoned, everything with a thick dimension being sanded away by nine centimeters becoming eight.

In nine hundred years the last gallery will be too thin for legs, the phase relationship will fail, and the note will stop, and the whole thing will set into a plug of glass with a cup of water distributed through it in dried pieces.

Except.

Gas leaves along the upper lip of the crack. It has been leaving for two centuries at a velocity that will lift a mite. It carries out, onto the plateau, a fine continuous dust of eggs and molt-shells and desiccated bodies and filaments — the world's shed surface, blown away. Almost all of it dies on the black glass in seconds.

But the plateau is a foam. There are dozens of sealed vesicles in the flow, and some of them have cracks too, or will have, and the wind that scours this summit from the west has been laying that dust across their openings for two hundred years. A mite is a canteen with legs. An egg is a canteen with a lid.

So here is the last thing, and it is the reversal the whole chamber has been walking toward: a society with no broadcast, in which nothing has ever addressed more than what it touches, has been broadcasting continuously since before it had a reader in it. Not by signal. By debris. At a range of kilometers, at a rate of perhaps one successful landing a century, into rooms that are still hot and still spinning their wool in the dark with nothing alive inside them at all.

What lives in the vesicle is not the film, or the pavement, or the patrol, or the flattened blind thing on the third shelf laying its whips along the glass. Those are the local expression of it. What lives there is a habit — a way of holding new water in shaped darkness and passing it to the next one — and it is spreading through the mountain at the speed of cracks, and it does not know it is doing this, and it cannot be told.

And in the middle of the third shelf, right now, a whip twenty centimeters long comes down on a filament and touches something that is not a neighbor: a stranger, faintly, at the very limit of the glass's ability to carry a message.

It is the largest event that has ever occurred in this world. It happens a thousand times a day. Nothing records it. The reader adjusts its grip, moves two millimeters closer, and reads again.
