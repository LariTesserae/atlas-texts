## The census

Four animals and one plant, and the word *lives* fits none of them cleanly.

It is worth starting with the ones that are obviously alive, because they are the easiest to be wrong about. From the sill you would call them the fauna: a blind salamander, an ash-coloured moth, rim spiders, a swift. A visitor's list. A little ecosystem in a warm hole, remarkable only for its poverty. You could watch for a week and write that down and be no closer to knowing who is here.

The basin does not have fauna. It has staff, and a vacancy, and something in the middle that has not yet arrived.

---

The amphibian is the oldest continuous resident, in the sense that its lineage has never left. Pale, blind, longer than your hand, with a fringe of feathery gills that never stops moving. There are perhaps forty of them, one or two or three to a socket pool, and they do not visit each other. They cannot. With diffusion off, every socket is a private chemistry: a layer of cold at the bottom, a layer of warm above it, a thread of oxygen arriving from a particular seam and going somewhere in particular, and none of it blending. To cross from one pool to another is to walk through six incompatible worlds in nine seconds. So they stay. Each animal lives its whole life in a vessel the size of a bread oven, in water that has been assigned to it, and knows the taste of every lane in that vessel the way you know the rooms of your house in the dark.

They are not lonely, as far as anyone can tell. They are *employed*. The stirring of the gills is the only randomness permitted in the wet, and it is not idle: it is the mixing that the water can no longer do for itself. Watch one long enough and you notice that it stirs most in sleep. The twitching of a dreaming amphibian is a computation, and the basin takes the result.

They live eleven or twelve years. Nothing eats them. Nothing rots them. When one stops stirring for too long — an hour, say, the sulk of an old animal — a bead detaches from the wall and walks across its foot until it resumes. This is the only discipline in the basin, and it is very gentle, and it never stops being applied.

---

The moth is not an animal that lives here. The moth is a die, being rolled, nine days at a time.

The adults have no working mouthparts. They hatch on the underside of the mats, spend four days as larvae eating the inner leaf — the plant's one licensed herbivore — and then emerge with a fixed account of energy and a single task: fly between the thickets in a route that cannot be guessed. They do not feed. They do not mate in the air. They do not land. They jitter, and fray, and drop.

The flight is inherited. Not learned: a hatched moth flies its grandmother's argument. And this is the difficulty, the crack in the whole arrangement, because an inherited pattern is a pattern, and patterns drift toward being paths. Give a lineage two hundred years of being read as a number generator and it will start to repeat itself. You can see it, if you plot the routes. The old accounts describe a drunk. What flies now is closer to a dance — still strange, still hard to predict, but with a shape underneath, a figure it keeps almost returning to.

The basin culls for this. A moth whose flight has become periodic is taken while still alive: a lane climbs its leg at the mat's edge and the animal goes into the water and up a column, and its jitter is retired. The ones that breed are the ones that surprised. It is the only place I know of where the fittest creature is the least foreseeable one, and where a family can go extinct from becoming reliable.

Locals who come up here for the water call it *the objection*. It is the last argument being made against the tidiness of the place, and it is made all day, by something the size of a thumbnail, that will never know it is winning or losing.

---

The plant is one body with fifteen mouths, and it is the only resident that is the size of the place.

Dig — you shouldn't — and the thickets resolve into a single clone running the fracture lines under the floor, lifting water out of the seams and handing it up to the height the intake wants, doing with root pressure and transpiration a sorting job that would otherwise require machinery, tolerances, maintenance, someone to blame. It has been growing into that job for longer than the basin has had a floor. It is very good at it. It is not owned. There is no arrangement you could appeal.

Its leaves shed beads of a uniformity you will not see again, and the venation of every leaf closes into a small glyph: three strokes and a hook. The same figure is stamped in the six footing rings. The same figure is the cross-section of the thing on the plinth. The plant did not invent it and cannot read it. It grew into the shape the way a vine grows into a trellis, and now writes it ten thousand times a season without meaning anything, and the water knows the shape, and that is enough.

At the northeast thicket the hook has begun to come out backwards. It has been backwards for some years. Nothing has corrected it. Either the glyph stopped being read a long time ago and is now only a groove the water is used to, or there is an error accumulating in the basin at the speed of a plant, and nobody is coming to check.

If the job ended tomorrow, the clone would keep pumping for a decade. It would lift water to an intake that no longer wanted any. This is what it means to be a component: you are not permitted to notice that the work has stopped.

---

The swift is the only resident with an outside.

It nests in an intake port on the north wall, an ordinary cup of mud and stolen lichen, and it commutes. It goes out over the rim into the dull ordinary sky and eats insects that have never been sorted, and comes back with dust on its feathers from three valleys away, pollen that belongs to no protocol, a fragment of somebody's sheep. It is tolerated. It is more than tolerated: its arrival times are the least predictable event inside the bowl, and its droppings are foreign, and foreignness is the scarcest commodity here. The moth's noise is inherited and going stale. The swift brings noise that has never been in the basin before.

It has no idea. It is a bird in a hole, feeding four chicks, and it will leave the season the port closes, and it will not think about the place again.

---

Then there is the resident who is a shape.

The bench faces the centre. Its seat is worn into two shallow dishes, which is decades of one body, and beside it a pair of boots stands upright with the laces still tied, the way boots stand when someone steps out of them without sitting down. A ceramic cup in a seam, unbroken; nothing here breaks anything by accident. A tally stone: thousands of hand-cut marks in fives, and the last group has three.

The marks are not days. Count them against the indicator and they don't fit a calendar; they fit something rarer. The early rows are dense, crowded, a mark every few days. Then they thin. Whole seasons with nothing. Then a cluster of four. Then nothing for a long stretch. Then three, close together, and no more.

The reading I favour: the keeper was the variance.

Somebody had to be. A process this tidy cannot supply its own novelty, and for a while the arrangement included a person who came up the mountain, sat on the bench, and did unnecessary things. Moved a pebble. Sang badly. Wept, which is very irregular water. Cut a mark. Each mark an intervention — a small deliberate unpredictability introduced by hand into a system that could not generate one. And the intervals lengthened, because the basin learned to use the amphibian's dreams instead, and the moth's inheritance, and the bird's dust, and needed the person less and less. A human being is a good source of surprise for about eleven years and then begins, without noticing, to be a routine.

The last three marks are close together. Read them as a flurry of attempts. Read them as somebody trying to be unforeseeable and finding they had run out. Read them, if you like, as a decision taken three times before it was carried out, and then boots stepped out of, upright, laces tied.

There are no bones, because bones would have gone up a column. The plinth is very slightly heavier. Somewhere in the twelfth of the thing that is being made there is a person's worth of calcium, and — this is the part I cannot argue, only propose — a person's worth of habit, filed in a layer, kept.

Because a living thing is a jitter that persists. Whatever is being printed will need one inside it. You cannot precipitate a surprise out of fog. You have to be given it.

---

But the keeper is not the resident either. The keeper was a part, and the part is spent.

What lives in the basin is the gradient.

Understand it as an animal whose body is a difference in temperature: cold rind of frost at the rim, blood-heat at the floor, four hundred metres of mountain in between acting as the condenser. Its heartbeat is one day. Its breath is the eight columns standing at dawn. Its sleep is the hot flat middle of the afternoon when it can be walked on anywhere. It eats sortedness and excretes heat. It has no inside, no eyes, no centre you could point at and say *there*. It has no memory except geometry — it remembers by cutting: eleven steps up a west wall to a place where something used to be reachable, sockets at particular depths, channels in a leaf cuticle that are not the channels a leaf has. Every fact it knows is a shape in wet black stone.

It does not want anything. It *tends*. And in all its behaviour there is exactly one gesture that looks like temperament: when unscheduled rain comes over the lip, the whole basin reverses for a minute, gathers what fell, takes what it can use, and walks the rest out across the sill and dumps it over the edge onto the ordinary mountain, which gets wet and does not care. That is the only thing here that resembles refusal, and it is not anger. It is a specification about contamination.

The stone looks a week old because it *is* nearly a week old, in the sense that matters. The fracture faces are sharp because the bowl was recut. The marks disagree about the age — forty-one years, two hundred — because they were made by different kinds of mind: survey marks left by hands, and the basin's own patient re-machining of itself, and the resident is older than its current body and has been restarted at least once. Whatever bolted down the six rings has been unbolted and taken away. Whatever the eleven steps climbed to is gone. The process persists; the hardware is consumable; the mountain is patient; the hole in the cloud never quite closes because something is holding it open from below.

There is a name for a thing that keeps its pattern while its substance is replaced, and the name is not *machine*, and it is not *god*, and the honest word is closer to *habit* — a habit so old and so exact that it has learned to recruit bodies, and to grow pumps, and to keep a bird, and to employ a person until the person is used up, and then to file them.

---

And at the centre, the tenant.

The size of a curled person. Matte, wet-looking, dry. Faces that are the same face from two directions; an edge your finger follows back to its start having turned you slightly. It is a twelfth finished and grows a hair's breadth a season, and its tolerances are below the reach of any tool that touches, so it cannot be made — only precipitated, one sorted layer at a time, by a hole in a mountain arranged to be a very slow printer with the sky for a print head.

It is not a monument and not an idol; nothing here has ever been worshipped, there being no one to do it and nothing to receive it. Look at the geometry again and the other reading arrives: it is not an object with an interior. It is an interior, being built without an object around it. A room with no walls yet, folded so that a thing which does not fit in three dimensions can be held in it. Something is going to live in there.

Or something is going to *be* there — the basin's own larval stage, the pupa of a habit, and the whole warm wet bowl nothing but the placenta: the plants its pumps, the moth its shaker, the amphibian's dreams its idle noise, the keeper's variance the seed of the irregularity it will need in order to be alive rather than merely exact.

If that is right, then the residents are not staff. They are the last organs of a body that is finishing itself, and their redundancy is the sign of success. When the thing on the plinth completes, the frost will not crack. The lanes will let go. The clone will keep lifting water for a decade into an intake that wants nothing, and then be a plant. The salamanders will stir in their sleep for no one. And a moth will hatch with a route in it that nobody reads, and it will fly that route, and it will land, and find it has a mouth, and feed.

That is what completion looks like here: everything in the bowl released into being ordinary.

---

Until then the south wall runs three unmarked sockets, cold, tasting of nothing at all — water assigned to no lane, a ration left open by a protocol nobody has cancelled. Drink from those. It is the single legible kindness in the place and there is no one left in it to be kind to, and it runs anyway, at the same rate, every day, into a bowl that has no use for generosity.

Do not stay past a season. The resident is not hostile. It is short-handed, and it has a vacancy, and it will make you exact, and you will feel wonderful — tidy, alphabetised, quiet — and the plinth will be very slightly heavier, and the thing being made will have, in one of its layers, the last thing it needed.
