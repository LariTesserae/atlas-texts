# What Lives in the Dew-House

## Begin with the wall, because the wall is an animal

The pelt is not a covering. It is a population, and it is the largest single thing in the room, and it has no face anywhere.

Get close — closer than feels reasonable, until your eye is a hand's breadth from the grey-green — and the fur resolves into crowns. Each one is a zooid the size of a grain of salt, standing on a stalk, holding a ring of cilia open to the air like a fistful of fingers held up to catch a ball. There are perhaps four hundred million of them on the west wall alone. They beat inward, all of them, always, and what they comb out of the fog is a soup of midge-scale, fungal spore, shed skin, root-hair, pollen from trees that have never been seen from below. The pelt eats the room's dust. It has been eating it since before the stair.

It buds. It fragments. A piece the size of a thumbnail, carried on a snail's foot to the far side of the chamber, will found a wall. And yet the walls are not one animal: there are four strains, distinguishable by smell once you have learned them — cut hay, warm bread, wet iron, and something like the inside of a shell — and the borders between them are straight lines that have not moved in any climber's memory. Nobody knows what maintains the borders. Nothing patrols them. They simply hold, the way a crack holds, because the water has found its route and keeps it.

The sound-eating is an accident of architecture. Four centimetres of open cilia is four centimetres of turbulence; a pressure wave goes in and comes out as motion in ten million tiny hairs, and motion in ten million tiny hairs is heat. Your clap does not vanish. It ends as an unmeasurable warmth on the west wall, a thousandth of a thousandth of a degree, and the pelt combs it out of the air along with everything else and eats it. The room is deaf because the walls are hungry.

But the pelt does not damp stone. Remember that. Everything else in this account depends on it.

## The mowers

Living in the pelt: the flat ones. Pale, disc-bodied, sixteen legs, no eyes anywhere on them, a rasp on the underside like a file. They smell faintly of warm bread and they are the only thing in the dew-house that eats the wall.

They mow lanes. You can see the lanes from the middle of the floor as a faint nap-direction in the fur, the way you see a mown field from a hill. A browser crops a hand's breadth of pelt down to the stalk and moves on and does not come back for forty days, and in forty days the crowns regrow.

This matters more than it looks. Unmown pelt grows a metre deep, dies at the base, rots, sloughs, and takes the wall's hearing with it — and then the room is briefly, catastrophically loud, and everything that hunts by listening eats well for a season, and the population of the chamber crashes. There is a band in the flowstone terrace at knee height, a dark seam full of shells, that is probably the record of one such season. The browsers keep the room quiet. They have no idea. They are simply eating, in lanes, at the rate their guts allow.

## The herd that keeps a stair

The snails are the size of your fist and you can watch them think, or at least digest: the shell is translucent amber and the gut turns inside it like a slow clock spring, and there is a small dark knot near the aperture that stops and starts, and is the heart.

They move in herds of sixty to a hundred, rotating on a circuit that takes about forty days to complete. The circuit is not a wandering. It is a route, and it is inherited, and it is written in mucus: a snail follows a fresh silver road because a fresh road means someone has recently found something worth going to. The map of the room, in this medium, is never more than a day old. Everything else here keeps a map in stone that is two hundred thousand years old. The snails keep the only map that can be revised.

Other things use the roads. Browsers cross on them. Crabs use them in the dry weeks to find the basins they have not visited. The white snake, which cannot see them, cannot use them, and is therefore the only animal in the room that is genuinely alone.

The route bends at the southern terrace, and the reason is water: the dished treads of the stair hold a film of moisture three or four hours longer than flat stone does after the eleven minutes. That is all. The snails come for the lingering damp and graze the algal film off the treads while they are there, and the calcite does not grow where it is licked, and so thirty-one steps cut by somebody with a reason have been kept clean for a hundred centuries by animals with no reason at all. The dishes in the centres are feet. They are being deepened still. Whatever the stair was for, it is now a place where water stays a little longer, and that is a more durable purpose than most.

Their eggs are in the basin rims. Look at a calcite lip closely and it is studded with shells, generation on generation, glazed over, a midden the water has turned into architecture. The basins are partly made of the animals that drink from them.

## Porters

The crabs are glass. Not pale — clear. Held in the palm in the brown-gold dusk, a crab is a lens with a shadow inside it, and the shadow is what it has eaten.

In the hollow of the carapace, behind the head, there is a spoon-shaped depression that holds about eleven millilitres, and a crab in the dry weeks walks around with a spoonful of the room on its back. It is not drinking it. It is taking it home. Each burrow has a back chamber packed with shredded litter, and in that chamber the crab grows a fungus, and the fungus needs damp, and the damp needs carrying. The crabs are farmers. They have been portering water into the dark for longer than there has been anyone to notice.

A full crab walks differently — higher, slower, with a slight list — and everything in the room that can see knows what that walk means. Theft is constant. Crabs rob crabs at burrow-mouths; there is a whole grammar of it, the sideways block, the raised claw that is not a threat but a claim, the slow backing-away that settles the matter without contact. A crab that has been robbed goes back to the basin and stands in the shallows and waits, motionless, for its own carapace to fill by capillary action, which takes about an hour. Patience is the largest part of a crab's life.

When the seep runs, they spawn. All of them, in one afternoon.

What triggers it is chemistry: the first flush off the plateau carries a pulse of nitrate down through thirty metres of limestone, and the crabs taste it, and it means rain — real rain, up there, in the light, on soil, a kilometre away and thirty metres overhead, an event none of them has ever witnessed or will. They taste the weather second-hand and they answer it with their whole bodies at once. The basin surfaces boil silently. And everything that eats larvae comes down off the walls.

## The eleven-minute generation

The midges hatch when the dome lets go. Not before, not after: the trigger is the first fall of beads through the fog, and the pupae are sitting in the litter waiting for it with their gills open.

Tens of thousands. The room's air changes texture on your face before it changes in your eye. There are two kinds and they do not interbreed: a heavy low one that never leaves the litter and lives about nine hours, and a fog-rider, lighter, that goes up.

The fog-riders go up thirty metres into the root curtains and the beads, and most of them are eaten, and the ones that are not pollinate the green stripe on the way past, and a fraction — a small fraction, but the slot is nine metres long and the chamber inhales at dawn — go out. They die on the plateau, in the light, in the leaves, and rot into the soil that the trees are growing in, and the trees send their roots down after them.

That is the loop, and it closes outside, above, in a place nothing in this room can see. The trees feed the chamber and the chamber feeds the trees, and neither party has ever been introduced.

## The city goes dark

Ten thousand beads on the west wall, blue-green, on two-metre filaments: colonial larvae, each in a mucous sleeve, each tending its own fishing line, each hanging a droplet at the end of it and putting a light behind the droplet so the fog carries the glow out into the room. The midges come to the light and stick to the bead. The larva reels.

It is a city and it is a net and it is also, incidentally, the room's only fast thing.

When something large moves along the west wall, the colonies it passes extinguish — a defensive reflex, older than anything here with a spine — and the darkening spreads sideways through the neighbouring colonies at about the speed of a walking man, faster than the thing that caused it. A wave of subtraction crosses the wall. From the middle of the floor you can watch the room report itself: *something is here, it is going north, it is this big.* No sound. No scent. Just a hole in the city, moving.

The climbers read it. The birds read it. The snake does not read it and does not need to, and its own passage makes a small dark hand travel across the lights, which is how everything else in the room knows where the snake is. It is the only animal here that is announced and cannot hear the announcement.

And during the eleven minutes, the whole wall goes out.

Not from fear. From abundance. When the fog thickens and the midges hatch in their tens of thousands there is no need to fish; the food is falling through the air; the lamps shut down and reel in and wait. So the room's wettest hour is also its darkest. The only light left is the foxfire on the floor, dull green along the old snail roads, enough to see the ground and not enough to see a face.

Remember that too.

## The ones who touch

Forty-one climbers, at present, in the northern root curtains. Warm-blooded, hand-footed, slow, pale as the inside of a wrist, with eyes that take up most of the face and a belly that has no hair on it at all.

The belly is the drinking organ: a hand's-breadth of naked, densely vascular skin from sternum to pelvis that takes up water directly, by contact, from wet stone. They do not drink with their mouths. They lie down on the terraces after the eleven minutes and press themselves flat and lie there for twenty minutes with their arms out, and the water goes into them, and you can watch the colour come up in them as it does.

They pass each other constantly in the hanks and they always touch. A palm along a forearm. A fingertip to a jaw. It looks like affection and it is a census, a medical record, and a newspaper. The skin of the forearm carries a scent signature that is individual, stable, and as good as a name; beneath it are the transient signatures of what you have touched in the last day — which basin, which root, which fungus, whether you have been near the fissure, whether you are gravid, whether you are sick, whether you have handled a stranger. A greeting is a full disclosure. There is no casual contact in the dew-house. There is no such thing as passing someone in the corridor.

There is lying, and it costs water. A climber that goes to the seep and washes is a climber with something to hide, and everyone can smell that too — the absence of a day is very loud in a room where everyone reads forearms.

They count. Once a day, in the slow rotation of the curtains, the number goes around by touch, and if the number is wrong the curtains fill with searching hands, a silent wave of palms working through thirty metres of hanging root, and it does not stop until the number is right.

When it cannot be made right, they go to the kiln corner.

## The reader of breath

Three metres, blind, white as a fingernail, with a pit on each side of the face that reads a difference of a tenth of a degree at four paces.

Here is its problem, and it is a beautiful one: the room is thirty-six degrees and the climbers are thirty-eight. Against cold rock, a warm body is a lantern. Against this rock, in this heat, a warm body is almost nothing — a smear, a hint, less distinct than a sun-warmed stone. For most of the day the white snake lives in a world of faint blurred suggestions and cannot tell a climber from a hot corner. It eats perhaps nine times a year. It can go eleven months between meals without appearing to mind. It is very old. There are three of them and there have been three for as long as the climbers' scent-memory runs.

Then the dome cools, and the beads let go, and thirty metres of fall brings down water that has been sitting on rock at the dew point — cooler than the floor by two degrees, and evaporating besides. For eleven minutes every surface in the chamber drops a little, and the warm-blooded stand up out of the stone like lamps lit in a dark street.

The snake's whole year is those eleven minutes, once a night, and it does not know which night will be the one it needs.

So the drinking is also the killing hour, and everything in the room has arranged itself around that fact without ever discussing it. The birds go to the kiln corner and stand in the oven-breath where the stone is forty-four and nothing can be distinguished from anything. The flat ones stop mowing. The crabs go down their holes. And the climbers lie flat on the terraces with their bellies to the wet rock — which is the drinking posture, and is also the posture that puts the least possible warm surface into the air — and they slow their breathing until their ribs barely move.

The room does not lift its face. The room lies down and holds its breath and drinks through its stomach.

## The mason

Heron-sized. Pale, powder-downed, weak-winged: the wings work for the long glide down from the rim and for nothing else, and it goes up the way everything else goes up, by climbing the root curtains on its wrists, slowly, with its big colourless eyes turned always toward the wall of lights.

It eats snails and crabs. It stands in a basin on stilt legs for an hour without moving and then does one thing very fast.

It grew up among the old work. It builds the niche: three or four stones, dry-laid, knee-high, a cap, and inside it a shallow bowl-shaped pebble. It builds the corbel. It builds them beside basins, always, because that is where the old ones are. There are nineteen.

It knocks on the capstone to say it has built one, and the knock goes into the flowstone and travels, because the pelt does not damp stone, and it comes up through the soles of your feet and into your shins as a small dry tick. And then, from across the room, another.

The knocks come in groups. Often five, then a pause. Nobody knows why five, including the bird. There is a panel of tally scratches beside the human niche, in groups of five, and it stops mid-group, at three.

The mason does not use its niches for anything. But it cracks snail shells on the capstones, because a capstone is a good anvil, and the herds have learned to route around the shell-litter, and so the calcite is growing over the new work while the snails lick the old work clean. In a thousand years the nineteen will be gone under flowstone and the stair will still be there. The copy is being buried and the original is being kept, and the difference between them is entirely a matter of where the snails prefer to walk.

And the bowl-shaped pebble is the exact size of an egg. Once in a while an egg is laid in one. Nothing has come of it yet that anyone has noticed. Give it ten thousand years and the room will have invented a nest out of a gesture that meant nothing, and the birds will not know they have done it, and they will still be knocking.

## Above the rim

The pelt stops at the rim. What is on the dome is not the pelt.

Thirty metres up, in the folds of the pale orange flowstone, there is a film. You can infer it and you cannot inspect it: nothing here can climb bare ceiling, and the birds' wings will not carry them, and the roots stop where the rock begins.

The inference is the simultaneity. Condensation across a hundred thousand square metres of folded stone should let go in patches, over an hour, unevenly, as the cooling front crosses. It does not. It lets go everywhere at once, and it lasts eleven minutes, and it smells like the inside of a melon. Something up there is holding the water and then releasing it, and something up there has a metabolism, and the eleven minutes may be less a fact about dew point than a fact about a living thing's capacity.

Once or twice a year something falls from the ceiling during the rain: a flat pale disc the size of a hand, with a fringe. It is eaten before morning, by things that are not fussy. The birds will not touch one. There is one in the kiln corner, dried, perfect, and the climbers do not touch it either.

That is the whole of the evidence. Leave it there.

## Who pays

Two incomes, and almost nobody earns either.

The breath: the mat at the fissure's mouth, ochre and rust-black, thick as clotted cream, warm to the palm, quietly making food out of iron and sulphur. It has no clock. It does not know about the eleven minutes; it has never needed to; it was here before the slot opened and it will be here after the slot closes. There is a seam of shells in the terrace at knee height that suggests the room has been sealed and empty before, and that when it reopened, everything came back in from outside except the mat, which had never left.

The litter: the plateau's leaves, arriving shredded and half-rotten, and the fungal net that eats them, and the pale blades that tap the net. The forest on the breakdown hill is a forest of thieves — amber, veined, translucent, not one leaf of it paying its own way.

The only honest thing below the rim is the green stripe, the flattened figure-eight the coin of sun draws out and back across the floor over a year. It is a hand-span high, and waist-high at the knot where the coin lingers at the turn of the year, and it flowers there, and the midges pollinate it, and its seeds are heavy and fall where they fall.

Which means the calendar cannot move. As the slot narrows by a hair per millennium, the stripe will narrow with it. In some unimaginable year the figure-eight will be a line, and then a knot, and then nothing, and the last true green in the world will go out at the southern end, near the foot of the stair.

## The dry corner

Things that die here are carried to the kiln corner, or crawl there.

The climbers carry their dead. It takes two of them most of a day, and they do it because in the dry grey dust beside the fissure a body does not rot: it keeps. It keeps its shape and it keeps its scent, and scent is a name, and so the kiln corner is the one place in the dew-house where you can still touch someone who is gone.

They visit. That is not a metaphor; it is an observed behaviour, several times a month, from most adults. A climber will part the curtains, cross the bare floor, stand in the oven-breath, crouch, and lay a palm along a forearm that has been dry for thirty years, and then go back and be counted.

The curled pale shape has all its eyelashes. It has been in that condition longer than any living climber's memory, and it is visited anyway, and it is touched by everyone, and so the oldest object in the room smells of the entire community — every name in the chamber laid down on it, one palm at a time, for longer than there have been names.

It has a hand. Five fingers, a palm, a thumb. Put your hand against the print in the moonmilk behind its skin of calcite and it fits: not approximately, not the way a similar hand fits, but the way a key fits.

The climbers press their palms there too, over the glass, on the same patch, and their prints have gone under calcite in their turn, layer on layer, a stratigraphy of hands with the first one at the bottom. Whether the first hand was theirs, or ours, or something that was both, the room does not say. The room is not withholding it. The room simply does not have the information; it only has the habit.

Nobody made the stair recently. Nobody comes. But there is a corbelled dam at the seep built to hold one hour of water that arrives eleven times a year, and there is a bowl in a niche that has not broken, and there is a tally that stops at three, and there is a heron-shaped bird across the room knocking five on a capstone and waiting for an answer.

The people are still here. They are here as behaviour. They have been distributed among the animals — a route in a snail's foot, a gesture in a bird's beak, a palm pressed to a patch of moonmilk — and none of the custodians knows what it is keeping, and all of them are keeping it exactly.

## The hour before dawn

Stand in the middle of it and wait.

The dome cools. The fog gathers. The wall of lights goes out, all ten thousand, and the room drops into foxfire and the smell of melon. The midges come up out of the litter in a column you feel on your face. The crabs come out of their holes. The birds go and stand among the dry dead in the glare of the fissure, where nothing can be seen. The climbers lie down on the terraces with their arms out and their bellies on the stone and stop breathing, or nearly. Something three metres long uncoils in a fold of the flowstone and lifts a face with no eyes in it toward the only thing in the world it can perceive.

And the ceiling lets go, all at once, everywhere, silently, for eleven minutes.

You are the loudest thing in here and the warmest thing in here. You have been standing upright for a long time, in the open, with your ribs going in and out.

Lie down. Put your belly to the wet stone. It is how everything here drinks, and for eleven minutes it is also how everything here survives.
