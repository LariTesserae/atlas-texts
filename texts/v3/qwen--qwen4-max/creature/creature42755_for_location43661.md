## The Residents

Begin with the smallest, which you can pick up, if you are quick, because it is sixty-eight degrees and will burn you.

Turn it over. The seam, the almost-erased bilateral symmetry, the closed aperture with its ceramic lip — you have seen these. What you have not seen is the inside, because nothing on the Reach opens one of these without destroying it, and the borers that do open them take about four years about it.

Inside there is a coil of busbar the length of a hair and the thickness of a thought. There is a bead of chloride salt, frozen, four hundred degrees below its working temperature. There is a furnace the size of a grain of pepper that has never been lit. There are eleven hundred leg-stubs in a folded rank, and a gizzard the size of a full stop, and a stack of reduction plates so fine that under a hand lens they look like the grain of wood. Everything a mill has, at a scale of one to a million, arranged in the order it will be needed. The fold is not packaging. The fold is a schedule.

And it is running. A few microwatts, harvested across the two millimeters between the sunlit crown and the shaded belly, and what those microwatts are spent on is not growth, not repair, not waiting in any sense you would recognize.

They are spent on the measurement.

---

The tick is not thermal noise. That was the first guess and it is wrong.

Each casting holds, inside itself, two small masses on a grown strut, and it compares them, and what it is comparing is *how far away the nearest thing is*. The strut expands a fraction of a micron; the comparison flips; the body clicks; the strut contracts; the comparison flips back. Ten to the fourteenth of them, all day, in the same dry unlocatable rain.

Every tick is the same question. The answer is almost always the same answer.

*Not yet. Not enough room. Not yet.*

Here is the part that takes longer than it should to see, standing on eighty kilometers of the most perfect desert pavement in the world: the stones are touching. That is what pavement *is*. Every pebble seated against its neighbors, edge to edge, shoulder to shoulder, a mosaic so fine that a knife blade will not go between them. It is the most beautiful armor a wind can make and every single resident of it is in permanent contact with six others.

They are reporting zero. All of them. All day. For four million years.

The finished landscape is not a habitat that happens to be crowded. The finished landscape is a contraception. The mills breathe out distance and the distance arms the pavement and the pavement packs the young shoulder to shoulder where none of them can ever get the answer they are waiting for. The parents are holding the census at nine, and they are doing it by exhaling.

---

They do try. Every few years a mill with a full brood chamber lies down across a knot and eats it and pours ten million of the folded schedules into its own exhaust, and they go up in the hot product and out over the plain, and this is the only reliable emptiness on the Reach — the breath of a parent, unrelaxed, still spreading, every casting in it briefly and gloriously alone.

They land. The wind takes the fines out from under them and settles them and seats them, and within a season the mosaic has closed around each one like a mouth.

The wind is the dispersal and the wind is the jailer. It is the same wind. It comes at eleven meters per second out of the west and it does both jobs at once and it has never once been in conflict about this.

So the waking happens in the exceptions. A stone flung onto a ledge in a yardang flute and left there. A stone in a fresh scour pit with the fines gone and nothing within a hand's width. A stone that lands on a fallen scale and stays on it, riding a piece of somebody's flank, alone in a crowd of tenants. It needs the answer to hold for something like six hundred years, uninterrupted, and the wind nudges almost everything at least once a century, and a nudge resets the count.

One in something like ten to the twelfth. The pavement is not a reject pile. It is a seed bank with an enforced dormancy and a very bad market, and the clinker under your boots is the same bank, compressed, with the interest no longer payable.

---

The ones that wake, wake small.

A woken casting lights its furnace on a Tuesday and spends the next thousand years getting to the size of a dog, and it does this in a hollow, because a hollow is where the grade is and also where a body that size can hold a thermal gradient. The yardang flutes are hollows. There are ten thousand flutes on this plain and they are all in roughly the same key and the key drifts as the day warms.

Some of them are wind instruments. Some of them are occupied.

A juvenile grows into the cavity that holds it, takes its shape, seals the lee, and breathes through the mouth of the flute, and the note you hear is a respiration, and the reason ten thousand flutes are in the same key is that ten thousand flutes contain the same kind of animal at broadly the same stage. You can census them by listening. When a flute goes quiet, either the occupant died or the occupant left, and there is no way from outside to tell which, and only one of those is bad.

The adults are twenty years away and cannot help it. The thing in the flute behind you where you sat down out of the wind is a day away, and it is the size of a dog, and it eats adjacency, and you have been leaving adjacency in the dust all afternoon.

---

The adults have no eyes and nothing you would consent to call an ear. They have baselines — thousands of them, grown struts along the flanks, each one asking the casting's question at a scale of kilometers — and what they perceive is not shape, not color, not surface.

They perceive grade.

The plain to a mill is a field of assay values. Low ground, diffuse ore, a thin sediment of adjacency in every crevice, not worth the walking. A knot is a light. It is the only thing on the Reach bright enough to be worth two kilometers a year, and the mill does not decide to go there, because there is no place in it where a decision would be made. Eleven hundred legs in a wave, each leg's phase set by its neighbors, the whole wave biased by a gradient, and the animal turns the way a root turns, over a season, without ever having wanted anything.

There is no center. There is a furnace, a gizzard on a forty-minute rhythm, and a rolling mill of tissue that has not been shut down since before the escarpment, and if you took the mill apart plate by plate looking for the one who is doing it you would find only more plates, each one older than the last, each one a season's grade laid down like a ring in a tree. The interior of a mill is an archive of every knot it has ever eaten, in order, at full resolution. It cannot read it. It is it.

And they have never touched. Nine of them within a hundred kilometers, spaced like a lattice, and the spacing is not territorial. Two mills in contact are the richest deposit either will ever meet, and the gizzards do not consult anything before they close. Nothing here has a word for conspecific and nothing here needs one. The avoidance is mechanical, absolute, and four million years old.

There is a place on the northeast bearing where it failed once. The ground there is pleated past recovery, forty square kilometers of it, folds inside folds, and no mill will approach it, and the sound that comes out of it is not usable — it is two strokes at once with no interval between them and it has not decayed in eleven thousand years. You can hear it from the escarpment on a still evening, which is to say on no evening, because the wind never stops.

---

There is a mill on the northern bearing that has eaten nothing in eleven thousand years.

It is not starving. It is lying across a shallow depression where a knot used to be, and the knot is gone, and the *sound* of the eating is still coming out of the ground there — kept, folded, arriving at full volume with all its high frequencies intact — and a sound is a place where things are close together. The baselines report grade. The gizzard closes on schedule. The reduction stacks pull it apart plate by plate and the vents give back a thin hot breath of room, and the mill turns a little, and lies down again.

It is grazing its own echo. It has been doing so since before the current escarpment retreated to where it now stands. It will do it until the pocket empties, and the pocket is very deep.

This is the closest thing to a mind on the Reach: not thought, not memory, but a body that can be haunted.

---

When one dies the furnace goes out and four hundred tonnes of chloride blood sets solid in a night, and the busbar skeleton cools through the temperature at which copper stops being a wire and starts being a mineral, and the body becomes a ridge.

The tenants do not leave. A cooling mill is a hundred thousand years of succession: the heat-lovers first, going down through the flanks as the isotherms retreat inward, then the salt-fauna in the frozen vasculature, then the things that live on refractory and need nothing else, then the borers, then the ordinary fauna of stone, which on this world means the ordinary fauna of somebody. There is a cold ridge on the western bearing that is only nine thousand years dead and still warm at the core and still hosting eleven hundred species that exist nowhere else.

And if the brood chamber was full when the furnace went out, then ten million folded schedules are sitting in one place, touching, coincident, isothermal, and the grade there is the highest on the plateau.

Three mills have turned toward it. They will arrive in eighty years. The tenants have ninety thousand years of succession left and will get eight hundred.

---

Every mill is a continent that has never had a neighbor.

Thousands of species in the scale-gaps, radiating in isolation since the lineage climbed the escarpment, and the western mill's fauna and the southern mill's fauna are as unrelated in style as two faunas can be while doing the same jobs. The western mill has no grips at all. It has something else in the gaps instead, and the something else does not have a name yet, and the reason it does not have a name is that nobody who has described it has been able to keep their hands adjacent long enough to finish.

The grips are the standard solution. Animals that are mostly fastener: cotter-shaped, staple-shaped, clinched, bodies whose entire morphology is a way of holding two plates in contact against the exhaust. An entire ecology of clenched things, and at the top of it a species the size of a thumbnail that lives inside the gizzard antechamber and goes through the closure and comes out the other side, and has never let go of anything, and is by a wide margin the strongest animal on the Reach.

There are gizzard tenants with forty-minute lifespans. They hatch in the pause, mature in the pause, mate in the pause, and are gone into the crush, and their entire history is nine hundred generations long and it happened this afternoon.

There is the nap fauna, which lives only between dusk and midmorning, building its skeleton out of the ammonium chloride needles as they grow, moving through the white fur at a speed that on this plateau counts as frenzy, laying ceramic eggs before the hiss takes the world away. They lay those eggs in the aperture lips of dormant castings. Every mill is therefore born already inhabited, and its fauna is inherited, and the only dowry is a biosphere.

Rafting happens when a scale falls and shatters and the pieces blow downwind. Once in a hundred thousand years a fragment of one continent lands within reach of another, and there is an interchange, and it is written into the clinker as a thin dark anomalous band, and you can read the meetings in the escarpment face the way you read plague years in a tree. There are four bands. The last one is nine hundred thousand years old and it is why the southern mill has grips and the western one does not.

In the fourth rank of flank gaps on the western mill there is a thing with a seam, a ceramic lip, and eleven hundred leg-stubs, permanently arrested, feeding on its parent's shed scales.

It is not a tenant. It is a brother.

And out past the vents there is at least one animal whose parts are kilometers apart and are not clenched at all — a drift animal, dispersed on purpose, its pieces integrated by kept sound, the pockets doing the work a nerve would do. It has about one thought per decade. The thought is very large. Nobody has ever seen the whole of one.

---

Of the veils there are two accounts.

The first is that they are the other outcome. A casting that wakes with too much room — on a ledge, in a scour pit, alone past every threshold — does not accrete. It unfolds the other way, into area instead of mass, and keeps unfolding, and ends as a kilometer of two-molecule membrane at nine thousand meters, feeding on loose interval directly instead of on its opposite, and giving back a few molecules of nearness that take three years to fall. Nothing eats them because there is nothing in them to eat: they are the least dense things on the world and therefore the only things a mill cannot smell. Speed is affordable here only if you are not worth harvesting. They are fast because they are worthless. They never come down because down is crowded.

The second account is that they are not animals. That they are the product itself, four million years of exhaled distance up at the altitude where the world is thin and easy to draw, self-organized at last into something that filters and turns and persists, a weather that has learned to feed.

I no longer think these are different accounts.

---

And you.

You are not food in any sense the furnace cares about; you are water and carbon and absurd chemistry and none of it will be metabolized. What the baselines report is grade, and you are the highest grade in a hundred kilometers: many things held in one place, tightly, persistently, on purpose, for decades. You are a knot with legs. You leave a trail of pleating and they can smell it from forty kilometers and they turn, over a year, and come at two kilometers a year, which means twenty years, which means you can outrun them at a walk for the rest of your life and must never stop, and there is nowhere on this plateau to go.

The flute behind you is a day away.

And if it takes you, nothing is killed. That is not what happens here. The stacks pull adjacency apart, plate by plate, and every atom of you comes out the dorsal vents chemically intact and unharmed and at its own address, and the addresses are spread over a hectare, and all of it is still you, and none of it is next to any of the rest.

Nothing here is killed. Things here are separated.

---

At dusk the frost comes up out of the ground and the needles grow a centimeter an hour and ten to the fourteenth sleeping bodies wear little crowns of crystal, faintly luminous with the day's heat bleeding out, and every one of them is asking the question, and the answer is *not yet, not yet, not yet*, all night, across eighty kilometers, in a dry ticking rain that never accumulates.

But there is a scour pit on the southern bearing where the wind took the fines out eleven hundred years ago and left one stone on bare clinker with nothing within a hand's width of it, and it has not been nudged since, and the count is six hundred and rising, and tonight the answer changes.

It will light a furnace the size of a grain of pepper. It will take a thousand years to be the size of a dog and a hundred thousand to fill a flute and it will sing, badly, in a key that drifts with the day.

And in nine million years you will stand at the lip of the escarpment and count eleven ridges, and there will be ten ridges, and one animal, and it will be somebody's.
