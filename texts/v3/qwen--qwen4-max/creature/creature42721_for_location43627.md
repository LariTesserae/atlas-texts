# The Living and the Nearly

## First, the count is the wrong kind of count

Three hundred and eleven is a count of shapes. It is not a count of lives, and the tenders know this, and they take it anyway, once a generation, rim by rim, because it is the only number in the room that will hold still to be taken. Everything actually alive in there is either too small to count, too slow to count, or too few to need counting. The census is a handrail. It is not a description.

Sort them instead by what they do. One thing eats. Eleven things drink and cannot finish drinking. Some hundreds of things are intact and doing nothing at all, which is a kind of doing. One thing ends. Three hundred and eleven things are not waiting, whatever the visitors say when they come out shaking. And a handful of people bring in the only commodity the room cannot manufacture, and give it away, and go back down for more.

## The nap

The film is the animal of this place, if you want to be strict about it. Everything else is furniture or guests.

It is colorless and thinner than a breath and it covers every wet surface in the chamber — rims, buds, the undersides of the rims where no hand goes, the walls of the throat, the pale flowstone of the two soft lanes. Under the fingers it is the inside of a cheek. It eats the dissolved stone out of the rising water, and eating smells: the warm-bread smell that finds you on the snowfield before you find the hole is the nap breathing. The iron sweetness is the water itself. The third smell, the one like the inside of a mouth, is not a comparison. It is the same substance doing the same work.

It doubles in a season and is grazed back. The tenders graze it with the antler spoon, which does not wear. The tenant grazes it in the deep bowl. The root tips are wrapped in it and it takes their newest cells as fast as they are laid down, which is why eleven curtains have hung an inch above the floor since before the ice: not because they cannot grow, but because growth and grazing have cancelled, and the inch is a standing thing, like the water level, like the temperature. The tenders call that inch *the gap*, and they do not like to talk about it, because it is the one place where you can see the room's arithmetic being done in something that might, given a chance, be a tree.

The nap has one enemy and it is not light. It is stillness. A body held in one place long enough — a knee, a hip, a sleeping man's whole side — stops the arrival locally. Nothing wears; nothing is crushed; there is simply a shadow where the water no longer stammers, and the film in that shadow starves, and what is left is bare slick stone with no cheek to it. There is one such patch, at the shoulder-place, the size and rough shape of a man who lay down eleven generations ago and was carried out too late. It has not closed. It will close in about four hundred years. Nobody has knotted it. Everybody finds it with their hand and takes their hand away.

This is why the tenders never sleep in the room, and why the hut outside is the size of a curled body and not the size of two.

## The slow drinkers

Eleven root curtains, hair-fine, white, belonging to eleven crooked shrubs a thousand paces downslope. Those shrubs are the amphibians of this world. Above, they are finished things: wood, leaf, bud, fruit, wind, the whole business of getting older in the light. Below, through a crack they found and followed, they are unfinished things: fingers in the warm dark, drinking, an inch short. The tenders go down and put their palms on the bark of each one, and can tell you which curtain is which shrub, and this is the closest thing they have to a doctrine — that a single life can be lived in both rooms at once, and that the crack between them is not a wound.

Ten of the curtains are alive.

One is not. It has been dead for four generations, and it is still white, still wet, still an inch from the floor, still warm to the hand, because nothing here rots and nothing here dries and death, in the Almost, is not an exit. You cannot feel the difference. You can taste it. The tenders know which one, and they do not tell visitors, and when a candidate is being tested they are sometimes taken to it and given a fingertip to lick, and what happens on their face afterward is part of the test.

The liverwort at the throat is the oldest resident and the only one with a history. It is the size of a spread hand and it is not green, having never had a reason. It sits where a rumor of daylight used to come in before the snowbank shifted, and it has been moving toward that rumor ever since: a finger's width in ten thousand years, by the tenders' reckoning, which is a reckoning kept in a knot rather than in a book. The knot is not a knot. It is a length of cord about a forearm long, and each generation adds one hitch to it, and it is called the long knot, and it is the only record in the room of something having *gone somewhere*. It is a scandal that has agreed to be slow.

The cricket is the same scandal in a hurry. A cricket sings, and a song stops, and stopping is a thing that only happens where there is a before and an after. When one is reported, a tender goes in and sits with it until it quits, and then carries it out cupped in both hands and puts it into the shrubs, where it will die in a day or two of the cold. The new knot does not record the cricket. It records the hand that touched it. To handle a thing that can end is to become responsible for its ending, and the tenders write that down in the only alphabet they permit themselves.

## The uncounted

The mud in the entry lane is not from here. There is no silt in the Almost, no sand, no rounded anything; the room has never cut anything and has never been cut. The mud is blown in, carried in on hems, brought up deliberately in the folds of clothes by people who understand that dirt is the one gift the room cannot make for itself. It is the tithe. It arrives with seeds.

Hundreds of seeds, intact, unrotted, unsprouted, in a warm wet dark that ought to be the finest germinating bed in the world and is instead the finest keeping-place, because sprouting is a direction and there are no directions here. Some of them are from plants that no longer grow downslope. Some are from plants that no longer grow anywhere. The entry lane of the Almost is the last place in the world where a certain winged seed exists at all, and it exists there in the plural, and it is doing nothing, and it will do nothing for as long as the tenders keep the lamps in the hut.

Nobody counts them. Counting them would be a proposal.

The mud is also the material of everything soft in the room, and here is the economy that visitors never guess: the two lanes are kept passable by combing, finger by finger, and nothing is carried out, because carrying out is not permitted and would in any case be a loss. The combings are pushed to the rims. The rims are where the clay figures sit. The little boats and crossed-armed women and bowls within bowls are the sweepings of the floor, made into shapes by hands that had somewhere to put the mud and no permission to finish anything. Housekeeping and art are the same act, and have been for eleven generations, and the tenders find nothing remarkable in this.

## The tenant

One shrimp, translucent, the length of a fingernail, in the deep bowl where the water arrives.

It molts. It grows a little and then comes out of itself and eats the shed, and the size it returns to is the size it left, and so it is always the same size, and there is never a second one, because a second one would be a difference, and a difference across a room is a slope, and a slope is the beginning of going somewhere.

Look at the dark thread of its gut through its body. That is the only line in the chamber besides the cord, and it is the more important of the two. Food goes in at one end and out at the other. That is a before and an after. It is the room's single gradient, held inside a body the size of a fingernail clipping, in the deep bowl, in the dark, where nothing else has to have one. The tenant does the ending for everybody. It is why they call it *the one who is also waiting*, and it is why the older tenders, if you get them tired enough, will say something that contradicts the name: that the tenant is not waiting, that the tenant is the only one in here who is *busy*.

The test is not a test of gentleness. Anyone can hold a shrimp gently. The test is whether you can feel a death walking on your palm — six or eight legs, fainter than a pulse, slower than a pulse — and stay soft while it does. Most people cannot. Most people's hands go to stone with the effort of not trembling, and they are sent out, and they make a lamp.

## The crowd

I want to say they are patient, and that is a word from downhill.

Patience is a slope. It requires a later. There is no later in the Almost, and so what the three hundred and eleven are doing is not waiting to shrug. They are shrugging. The shoulder has been going up since before the ice, at the speed of a place with no gradient in it, and the face has been turning, and the ear has been folding, and none of them are held at the instant before, because *before* is a thing the visitors brought up in their hems along with the mud. What is missing from the room is not completion. What is missing is time, and time is exactly what a body imports, and the room accepts what you give and returns it to the mountain and remains exactly itself.

That is where your afternoon goes. Up through the pores of the ceiling with the vapor, at precisely the rate it arrives. The ledger balances. The melt-ring on the snowfield is four paces across because that is the width of the room's exhale, and the snow there is thin in a way that has nothing to do with heat.

The shapes are not all human, and this matters. There are hips and ears and shoulders, yes, and stair treads with the next tread beginning above them, and the keels of birds. The water is not trying to make people. It is trying every kind of finish there is, the way water tries every crack: an ear for a sound nobody has made yet, a stair to a place that does not exist, a bird of no species. Under the hand, four faces recur, in different basins, at different heights, over generations — and one of them is the woman with the narrow feet, whose prints are at the throat, and one of them has never been matched to anybody, and the tenders have a private suspicion about that one which they do not knot, because a face that resembles someone not yet born would be a slope, and they have enough slopes to worry about.

The tenders' craft, in the middle of all this, is refusal. They are midwives who attend and do not deliver. They keep their palms on a shoulder for three hours and help it in no way whatsoever. It is the hardest work anyone in that country does, and it looks like sitting still in the dark, and it is not sitting still, because you must never hold one place long enough to make a shadow.

On the rims at the height of a kneeling hand sits the other crowd, the soft one: the bestiary. Animals nobody has seen, maintained across generations by thumbs — the one with too many shoulders, the boat that is also a bird, the woman with her arms crossed, the bowl inside the bowl inside the bowl. They have no bottoms where they sit in the water. They slump a little and then stop. Some are less an object than a habit a family keeps, remade so often that the shape has drifted, a boat becoming a shoulder becoming a bowl, and the family does not consider this a change. In a room where the antler does not wear and the flint does not dull and the footprint does not fill, only the soft things take an impression. Only the soft remember. The clay is the archive, and the archive is remade every time it is read.

## The tenders

They are the only number in the world that is allowed to change.

They are born downslope and they die downslope, and between those two finished events they come up at night without lamps and sit three hours in the cold letting their eyes open all the way, and then go in and be, for a while, a thing with no gradient in it. Their names are unfinished: the last sound is left off and never said, so that a living tender is called something that stops just short of being a word. The whole name is spoken once, at the burial, downslope, and then not again. To name a thing you must have finished it, and they will not do that to each other while there is still breath.

They eat the nap. They drink from pools that do not fall. They comb the lanes and make the sweepings into boats. They count the basins once in a generation and find three hundred and eleven, and the finding is not a measurement, it is a reassurance, and every one of them knows it.

Outside, beside the hut, the stack of unfired lamps grows by one or two a generation. They are made by the ones who could not feel the tenant walking. That is the whole of the punishment and the whole of the ceremony: you press a lamp out of the entry mud, and you set it on the stack, and you go down, and you do not come back up. The stack has never held oil. In the melt-ring the old ones at the bottom have slumped into the shape of the ones above them, so that the failures are slowly becoming one another, and no one has ever fired one, and no one ever will, and it is the most finished-looking thing anywhere near the mountain.

## The lying

The only predator here does not live here. It lives in the visitor, behind the eyes, and it is the only inhabitant with an appetite.

When the opened eyes begin at last to show you something, that is your own nerve talking to itself in the absence of the world, and the tenders have a taxonomy of it, taught by hand-signs in the dark: the doorway. The stair going down. The mother. The water moving. The pale face turning. And the one they call the asker, which is not a shape at all but a pressure, and which wants a lamp.

That is the appetite. The lying wants to be confirmed. It will show you a face three-quarters out of the stone and it will suggest, reasonably, kindly, in your own voice, that a little light would settle the matter. It has no malice. It is the ordinary hunger of a nervous system for a world, and in most places it is a harmless thing to satisfy, and here it is the end of everything.

Because it has worked elsewhere. The tenders know of other chambers, other slots in other mountains, that were lit. They are caves now. They have stalactites that reach the floor and rims that split and streams that cut and sand, and bats, and names, and people go into them with lamps and admire them and come out and say how beautiful. The tenders think of those places as graves, and they are polite about it, and they do not say so, and once in a while an old one will ask a visitor which caves they have been to, and listen to the list with her hands in her lap.

## The cord, and the ring

The cord is the only finished thing in the room and therefore the only thing in it that can die. Grass fiber, knotted, peg to peg, the ninth basin, the shoulder-place, the throat, the deep bowl. It will rot. Not soon, in the warm wet, but it will, and the tenders know it, and this is the death they have permitted: not light, not the faces coming the rest of the way out, but the slow loss of the map, and after that a room full of nearlys that no hand can find in order.

They touch it the way you touch a sleeping child's chest, to check.

So: the film that eats, and the ten curtains that drink and the eleventh that does neither, and the liverwort creeping a finger's width toward a light that stopped, and the seeds that are the last of some things, and the shrimp with the only gut in the world, and three hundred and eleven shrugging shoulders, and the soft animals on the rims, and a few people with unfinished names who bring up dirt in their hems and hours in their bodies and give both away.

None of them are waiting for you. You are the only thing in there with anywhere to be, and the room takes that from you, gently, over about three hours, and breathes it out through the ceiling; and the snow above the slot settles a finger's width; and the count, when they take it, comes out three hundred and eleven.
