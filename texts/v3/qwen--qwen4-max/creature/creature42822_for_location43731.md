## The holders

The first one you meet is scraping.

She is at the drain lip with a blade of pressed husk, taking the film off the water in long economical strokes, and she does not look up, and she is not remarkable. Knee-high boots of dead leaf, actually dead, actually grey. A short pelt on her forearms and across her shoulders like moss on a north wall, groomed into a nap, dyed at the wrists with something rust-colored. A face you would forget. She is bored in a way that takes about forty years to earn.

They call themselves a word that means *holders*, and in the same breath means *pauses*. A person here is a place where phosphorus slows down enough to have an opinion.

---

Nobody has a skeleton in the sense you do. They have a deposit.

Bone is the savings account: laid phosphate in the long struts, drawn down in a bad quarter, redeposited in a good one. So wealth is posture. You can read a stranger's year from across a hundred meters of open water by the angle of their spine, and this is not considered rude, only weather. The rich stand like arguments. The indebted bend, gently, from the hips, and the bend is called *the softening*, and there are eleven polite words for it and none of them are polite.

At the market you can watch a man straighten. He has just sold well. Two centimeters of him arrives out of nowhere, his chin comes up, the pelt on his shoulders lifts like a cat's, and everyone within thirty meters sees it happen and prices him accordingly, and the whole thing takes four seconds and is over before the water settles. This is most of why the town is so loud. Everyone is legible. There is nothing to be discreet with.

Their teeth are the only hard part of them and they count them, constantly, with the tongue, the way you check a pocket.

---

A name is a scent-chord of four notes and the house you are subscribed to. Change houses and the second half of your name changes with it, so a person who moves often is hard to write to. Children are underwritten before they are conceived; the terms are read aloud at the naming; everybody claps. It takes a child about eleven years to understand that the applause was a credit decision, and there is a specific afternoon on which every person in this bowl learns it, and no one talks about that afternoon, and everyone remembers where they were standing.

To die is to stop paying upkeep on yourself. Nine hours, politely, from the feet up.

The family sits in the room. That is the funeral: you sit in a warm white chamber full of drowned daylight and you listen to the wall eat your mother, that slow celery sound coming through the ribs from everywhere at once, and at hour six somebody brings food, and at hour nine you sign. The signing *is* the grief. There is no separate ceremony for the grief. They have a word for people who try to hold one anyway, and the word is not unkind, and it is not kind either.

Because what you inherit is the phosphorus. What you are mourning is the collateral. These are the same object and the same event, and every person here has felt both at once and been ashamed, and the shame has its own name, and the name is very old, and it is one of the first words children learn to say properly.

---

Their minds are not entirely in their heads.

A thought is a wave in the water table; the head does the fast small work and the plaza does the rest. So there are things you cannot remember indoors. To recall your mother's face you must go out and stand in a particular plaza at a particular depth, and you will see people doing this at all hours — standing in two inches of blood-warm water with their eyes shut and their soles rooting, waiting for a face to come up through the Floor.

This is why the plazas are empty. Zoning here is neurology. A stall parked in the wrong braid is a small stroke. There are inspectors whose entire job is walking the open water at dawn looking for places where the town has accidentally stopped being able to think, and they carry a rod, and they are paid extremely well, and they are unpleasant at parties.

The worst thing you can do to a person is contradict them at minute seven. They cannot compose a reply until the water comes back. There is a whole art of the seven-minute insult, and masters of it, and feuds four generations deep that consist of nothing but timing.

And: to let someone stand upstream of you is the declaration. You are letting their water arrive. Since the braids reverse every few minutes, the courtesy alternates all day, and a marriage, in the local definition, is two people who have stopped keeping track of who is upstream. They can be located by it. A long marriage makes a small standing wave in a corner of a plaza, permanent, findable. When one of them dies the other's water goes strange for about a year, and the neighbors step around that corner, and nobody says anything, because it happens to everyone.

---

There are more minds in the pelt on your left shoulder than there are people in the bowl.

Ten thousand mites to the handful, and the handful is one of forty handfuls, and they are not idle. They breed in the nap of you. They clear their markets in the interval between a merchant opening her mouth and the price coming out. They do the sorting, the matching, the assay, the actual work of the world, at a tempo where a human sentence is a geological era.

The merchants know. Ask one who sets her prices and she will laugh and say *I don't set them, I announce them*, and then she will sell you something at a number that is correct to four decimal places and that she cannot explain. The pelt-groomers' guild is the nearest thing this town has to a central bank. They rake the nap, they thin the crowded districts, they seed a poor man's shoulders from a rich man's back at a rate posted publicly on a leaf, and the entire monetary policy of a hundred-million-year civilization is conducted with combs.

The disagreement about who is in charge here is polite, and it is the same disagreement the instruments have about the Floor's age, and it will not be resolved, and both parties are content.

---

The other citizens:

The bell-frogs are staff. The eight-minute frog has a name, a lineage, and a pension paid in midges, and one family has kept that line since before the exchange had a roof. There was a schism, once, over a frog that counted seven. It was not a theological dispute; it was a scheduling dispute; people were born on the wrong minute and have never quite caught up, and you can still meet them, and they are always slightly early for everything, and it has made them the way they are.

The midges take their tithe and carry the audit samples, and to be bitten is to be counted. Being counted is the whole of citizenship. So the frightening people are the unbitten. When the midges stop coming to a man, his neighbors begin, very gently, to use the past tense about him, and he can hear them doing it, because the water carries, and he stands in the plaza with his arms bare and his pelt groomed and nothing lands on him.

The ribbon eels go down the swallow holes. They are the only things that go into the karst and come back. They come back thin. There is a woman at the north lip whose profession is to assay what an eel brings up in its gut, and she has been doing it for thirty years, and the running total of foreign phosphorus recovered from eel-gut in her lifetime is a number she can say without pausing, and it is small, and it is not zero, and it is the entire eschatology of this place. Nobody has ever thanked her. Everybody knows her name.

And there are feral contracts. Clauses that escaped their tellers and went wild in the arcades, breeding: deeds with no parties, obligations looking for a mouth, a lease that will attach to your ankle if you stand still. Children catch them in leaf-glass jars and sell them to a guild that eats them. Once every few years one gets into the water table and a whole district finds itself briefly committed to something nobody signed, and the town has to stand in the plaza and think hard, together, until it comes loose.

---

The houses are people too, at the next tempo up.

They are kidneys and they are also fond. A house expecting you grows you a room overnight, and the room is warm and slightly wrong-shaped in a way that is specific to that house, and you will recognize the wrongness years later in another room and feel something you can't name. A house in debt digests its own east wing while its family sleeps in the west, and the family talks over the sound, and the talking-over is a skill taught to toddlers.

They are ninety meters of straining membrane and they have appetites and preferences and something that behaves exactly like patience. The folk are fond of their houses the way you are fond of an animal that is also an organ. They talk to them. The houses do not answer in any way that survives scrutiny. Everyone talks to them anyway.

---

Once a day the wader comes across, and the market stops to watch, and everyone assumes you understand why, and you don't.

It is the post.

There are other bowls. Not near — a thousand miles of spent limestone between them, and nothing on the plain to stop sound or carry scent, and no road, and no reason. But the waders walk between them, stilt-legged and terrible and slow, and in the mud on their dinner-plate feet are the scent-packets of other towns: prices, deaths, marriages, a joke that has been traveling for eleven years. Its footprints are the only holes ever made in the Floor, and there is a profession that does nothing but run to a footprint and read it before it closes.

They assay the mud too. Grain by grain. Somewhere out there another bowl is leaking, and a bird stepped in it, and one atom of new phosphorus has arrived in a place where nothing new has arrived since the mountains dissolved. That is the import sector. That is the entire credit side of the ledger of a hundred million years. One bird's foot, once a day, and the whole town stops shouting to watch it come.

The wader does not know. It is not a courier; it is a bird-thing with a route. Out on the plain there are wader-roads that nothing marks, and where a wader falls there is a green smear that lasts a decade and is called, locally, a rumor.

---
