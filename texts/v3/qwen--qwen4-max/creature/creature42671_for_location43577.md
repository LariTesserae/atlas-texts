# A Field Account of the Living

## First, the Two Laws

Nothing here is native. There was no fauna before the deck; there is no elsewhere for a fauna to have come from. Everything alive on the plateau arrived as a contaminant in a feedstock, or escaped a spec sheet, or was designed for a purpose that has since been renumbered. Life is the leak in the tolerance. It has had two hundred years to become excellent at leaking.

Two laws govern it, and every animal here is a solution to both.

The first: *the surface you are standing on will be eaten within a fortnight.* Therefore nothing may be sessile unless it rides, or hides in a gap too narrow for the crew, or has persuaded the inventory that it is a part.

The second: *the crew cannot tell a body from a billet.* The blind extruders consume by volume and reissue by drawing. They do not hunt and they do not spare. There is no malice in the wave of freshness and no mercy either, and so the whole living country has been shaped by an unintending mouth.

Everything below is a way of living between those two facts.

## The Ones Who Look

The finches are the plateau's smallest continuous institution.

One nest per fin, forty thousand to the hectare, woven of shim stock and hair. The shim stock is free; the fins are made of it, and a bird can worry a curl loose in an afternoon. The hair is not free. There is no hair on the plateau except what the animals carry, and so the finches take it from the carriers' flanks, from the shim-mice, from each other, from the sleeping, from you. They will come within a hand's width of your head to do it. You will feel almost nothing: a small redistribution of weight, a decision.

Here is what the finches worked out, and it took them perhaps sixty years.

The fins are reissued. The nests are on the fins. The nests, therefore, are reissued — but not destroyed, because at some point a finch's persistence got itself onto a drawing, and there is now a line item, *nest, fin, avian, standard*, and every eleven days the crew eats the fin and lays it down again with the nest included, empty, identical, in fresh shim stock, four microns from the last one.

So the birds carry the hair.

That is the whole of it. When the crew comes through a district the finches lift the lining out of the old nest and put it into the new one, and the new nest is not new, and the hair goes on. Some of those linings are older than the practice of writing anything down up here. Hair does not appear in any inventory. Hair cannot be reissued. It is the only material on the plateau that is permitted to age, and the finches have made it their archive, their title deed, their proof of descent. A bird's claim to a fin is not the fin. It is a felted mat of ancestors.

There is a lineage in the third district of the Fin Fields whose lining contains a single grey hair, forty-one millimetres long, coarser than any animal's on the plateau, and it has been carried from nest to nest for ninety years, and the birds that carry it do not know what it is, and they will kill for it.

They sing above the harvest, in the thin silver band the attenuators were never calibrated for. It is not birdsong as you know it. It is dense, fast, unmelodic, and it is *traffic*: crew positions, flume countdowns, the death of a carrier, the phase of the shroud. Forty thousand small bright eyes at twenty-centimetre intervals, each one watching, each one saying nothing where the machine can hear, and the whole district stitched together overhead in a language the plant does not grade.

And there is this, which nobody has tested, because to test it you would have to make forty thousand birds look away at once.

Occupancy is expensive. The plateau builds ahead of the observer and unbuilds behind. There has been very little occupancy for a very long time.

The Fin Fields have never once gone back to drawings.

## Hoards

The shim-mice are felt-footed, dust-coloured, and shaped, at rest, exactly like a swarf curl, which is not a coincidence but a career. They eat polymer, silk, the fat of hot bearings, finch eggs when they can reach them. They hoard by number.

That last thing is the important thing, and it is easy to walk past.

A shim-mouse does not cache food where food is. It caches *parts*, sorted by the stamp on them, in eleven or twelve places at once, and it maintains those caches with a fidelity that no inventory clerk up here has matched in a century. The caches move every eleven days because the crew comes. A mouse's life is largely logistics: the same eleven small piles, carried across a seam, re-established, checked.

The plateau is obedient. Where the inventory says a lathe is, a lathe is. The inventory is a description of where parts are, and the parts are where the mice put them, and no one has corrected the inventory since before the mice learned to read stamps.

So behind the Long Anneal there are eleven lathes in a row, each slightly earlier than the last, the eleventh unpainted and warm and running and not yet installed, and every one of them is a mouse's decision, made in the dark, for reasons of storage.

They are the only authors on the plateau. They do not know it. Or — and this is the thing you begin to suspect around the fourth hour, watching one sit motionless on a busbar with a washer in its mouth, looking at you with an eye like a wet bearing — they know something adjacent to it. They know that if you put a thing in a place, the place agrees. They have been testing this for ninety years. There are caches that are not sorted by number. There are caches arranged in shapes. There is one, deep in a seam behind the Standpipe stair, that is nothing but grips: eleven objects worn smooth in the same place, laid out in a row, and nothing else, and the mice visit it and add to it and take nothing away.

They also garden, incidentally and unavoidably. A hoard accumulates dust, shed hair, insulation crumb, mouse waste. That is soil. There is no soil on the plateau except on the carriers and in the guts of animals and in eleven thousand mouse caches, and every seed that does not ride, rides a mouse, and every eleven days the garden is picked up and carried a body's width to the left.

## The Bow Wave

The extruders are small and blind and travel in crews of nine or eleven. They have no eyes because they do not need to see; they have the drawing, which is better. They eat the surface behind them and lay it down ahead, and they are warm, and they smell of hot oil and new polymer, and if you put your hand in front of one it will go around your hand, because your hand is not in the specification and the crew does not improvise.

They are also, themselves, animals — or they were, or their ancestors were, or the distinction has been renumbered. They moult. They have a parasite, a white flattened louse that lives in the die throat and eats the last of the old surface before it is laid down. They die at the end of a run, and the crew behind eats them, and lays them down.

The deck you are walking on is partly made of the bodies of the things that made the deck. This is not a metaphor. It is a feedstock arrangement.

Ahead of every crew there is a bow wave: everything that was living on that surface, running. Gasket-slugs peeling off plates. Mice with mouthfuls. Finch relays. Nest-linings in transit. Caterpillars abandoning a tray mid-meal. It is the only stampede on the plateau and it happens eleven times a day somewhere, in absolute silence, at the speed of a hand's width per hour.

And behind every crew there are the followers.

They walk in the fresh strip, on the warm new deck, eating what the eating exposed: the animals that were *in* the surface, the ones that were too slow or too flat or too confident, laid out stunned on the new grey like fruit in a cake. Crew-followers are pale, long-legged, unhurried, and they keep station exactly one metre behind the die. They have no fear of the machine and no fear of you. They have never needed either.

There is a shrike among them that has learned to use the freshness. Everything up here has the edge it had on the day it was made, and the shrike impales — finch, mouse, slug — on burrs and offcuts and the sharp lip of the occupation allowance, a larder along a seam, eleven small bodies hanging in the light-sift with the sun on their undersides.

Follow the crew far enough east and the whole procession runs out of plateau.

At the front the deck is being laid onto nothing at a hand's width per hour, and the offcuts fall, and there are things that fly out there and eat them in the air. They started as swarf swifts — the same papery rush, the same never-landing, the same two million strong above the districts, sleeping on the wing at the top of the warm columns. But the front pays better and the front is four kilometres wide, and out there the swifts have begun to take bigger mouthfuls, and to get heavier, and a heavy thing cannot sleep on the wing.

So at the eastern edge there is a bird that hangs. It hooks its beak into the underside of the advancing lip and sleeps upside down over four thousand metres of cloud, in a roost that moves a hand's width per hour and has never been in the same place twice, and it is not a swift anymore, and there is no name for it, and the drawings for the lip do not include it, and the lip is reissued anyway, and the bird moves along the underside ahead of the die, every hour, forever, one hand's width, one hand's width.

Below it: cloud, and the drawings, and no talus, because they eat it. They are the reason the edge has no rubble. The edge is sharp because it is *grazed*.

## Two Hours

Every eleven days a valve opens and there is a river.

The sump-crabs live in the grates and the plenum and the wet dark under everything, and they are blind and flat and pale, and they carry water back down in their bodies. They are the plateau's cisterns. A crab comes up the flume gorged, walks to a dry district, and sweats for a week, and the capillary films in the seams are, in large part, crab.

Their entire reproductive life is two hours long. They moult in the flume, mate in the flume, feed in the flume, and go down before the valve closes, and the eleven-day clock is not in the water. It is in them. Take one out, keep it in a dry tray, and on the eleventh day it will climb to the lip of the tray and stand there facing the flume with its claws up for two hours, and then go down.

And they are loud.

This is the second contraband, and it is better organised than the finches'. Water in a standpipe is graded *process*. The attenuators take process noise and leave it; it is not out of spec, it is the sound of the plant being correct. So for two hours every eleven days the flume is a deafening, clicking, scraping, screaming riot — thirty thousand crabs conducting the whole business of a generation inside a sanctioned sound — and one metre outside the wetted channel there is nothing at all, and the silence stands there like a wall, and you can put your hand through it.

They have been doing this for two hundred years. Nothing has ever heard them.

## The Ground, Walking

The carriers are the size of a hay bale and the colour of nothing. The shell is not a shell: it is forty centimetres of callus, dust and oil and shed insulation and their own dried waste, laid down in layers, and it is the only sediment on the plateau. Cut one and you have two centuries. There is a band at nineteen centimetres where the polymer changed and everything alive changed with it. There is a lamina of grey hair.

They walk an eleven-month circuit and they have no voice and no enemies and no hurry. Their feet are felted. They pass through the crew's path and are not eaten, because forty centimetres of accumulated filth is, to a blind extruder, a surface defect, and defects are laid down again, faithfully, wear included.

On their backs is all the green there is: cushion moss, the wiry grass that escaped a spec sheet a century ago, three lichens, a sedge. It rides because it must. The moss has learned the replacement schedule and fruits in the four days before a crew arrives, throwing spores at whatever passes, and lets the deck it lived on be taken away. It is not attached to the plateau. It is attached to the * timetable*.

The carriers' circuits are offset. There is always one somewhere at the shift point. The moss knows what month it is by which carrier it is standing on.

When one dies — and they die rarely, and slowly, and simply stop — everything within a kilometre comes. The callus is colonised in a day. There is a green hill in the seam for eleven weeks: the only hill, the only rot, the only smell of anything old outside the Disagreement. And there is a whole biota that exists only in that eleven weeks and nowhere else: a fly with no mouth that hatches from the callus and mates on the second day and is finished by the fourth; a flower that opens once, white, wide as a hand, and smells — for the only time on the plateau — of something sweet going over; a sedge that will not grow on a living carrier and waits, as seed, in the guts of mice, for a funeral.

They are not rare. They are *scheduled*. Every one of them is a bet that somewhere on this plateau, in the next eleven weeks, something enormous and patient will stop walking.

Then the extruders come, and the hill is unmade, and the grass goes on riding.

## Four Microns

There is a fauna whose habitat is a tolerance.

Between two plates that nearly touch there is a capillary film that will not pour and cannot be dried, and in it live gasket-slugs: flat, translucent, three millimetres long and eleven microns thick, grazing the film's skin. They can enter a gap you cannot see. When the plates are reissued they walk out onto the flange and wait, in the open, in the full brightness, in a place with no cover of any kind, for the new plates to come, and then they go back into the four microns, and the crew has laid the deck down over their heads and does not know.

They also live in wear.

The grip-polish on the rail at the head of the Standpipe stair is a depression forty microns deep and it is full of them, and they have been in that grip since before anyone let go of it, and they are eating the difference between the rail as drawn and the rail as it was, which is the only food there, and it is renewed every eleven days, faithfully, forever, an absence served to them on a schedule.

Mimicry here does not mean looking like another animal. It means looking like a component. There is a moth in the cable trays — the loom-moths' cousins — whose wings, folded, are a washer, right down to the stamp, and the stamp is legible, and it is correct. It got itself onto a drawing eighty years ago. It is now manufactured. Every eleven days the crew issues a fresh cohort of it, identical, sexless, perfect, and it does not breed and it does not vary and it does not die of anything, and it has not changed in eighty years, and it will not.

That is one answer to the two laws, and it is the most successful answer, and it is a dead end.

The loom-moths themselves are going the same way. Their conductive silk is harvested at the best colonies by collection spools the plant installed, which the moths use as latrines, and the silk is graded, and the grading has begun to select, and there is now a colony in the fourth tray-district spinning to a conductivity spec. Give it another century. There will be a line item. There will be a moth that is issued.

Against this, in the seams, the mice go on being slightly different every generation, and slightly worse at looking made, and slightly better at moving.

## The Only Night

The light comes from every direction and nothing gets to keep a shadow, except under the shroud.

Forty kilometres of conveyor cross the plateau at a millimetre an hour, carrying something enormous under a cover nobody has seen the ends of. Under the cover there is a strip of true dark, one object wide, and it moves at a millimetre an hour, and it is the only night that has ever existed here.

Things live in it. They are black — the only black animals on the plateau, everything else being pale or dust-coloured or the grey of fresh deck — and they have eyes, real eyes, huge and useless in the bright and perfect in the strip. They walk at a millimetre an hour. They must. To stop is to be overtaken by the light, and the light does not kill them directly; it simply makes them visible, and there are crew-followers, and there is the shrike.

So they walk in their travelling night for their whole lives, and they sleep in it, standing, moving, and they are the only animals up here that sleep in the dark, and their generation time is measured in metres.

Nothing crosses the whole conveyor. Forty kilometres at a millimetre an hour is four and a half thousand years. The shroud-fauna at the western end and the shroud-fauna at the eastern end have not met since the plateau was young, and they are different now, and they are riding the same shadow.

Once every eleven months the shift change comes, and the sanctioned chord goes through everything, and the shroud moves — a whole millimetre at once, a lurch, a year of walking in a second — and the black animals stagger, and some of them fall out of the night into the light, and the shrike waits at the edge of the shroud on that day, and has waited on that day for two hundred years, and does not know why it is always right.

## Seasons, and a Bolt

The Long Anneal cools over a year, and it is the only place here with a season.

Seven species live in it, in sequence, each waiting for its temperature. Spores in the refractory at six hundred; a worm at four hundred that eats the scale; something at two hundred and ten that lives eleven days and lays and is finished; and at the far cool end, at ambient, a pale grazing thing that occupies the oven for the last three weeks before the doors open and the heat comes back and it goes to spore and waits its year.

They are not competitors. They are a queue. Each one's whole existence is a temperature band on a cooling curve, and none of them has ever encountered another, and all seven are necessary to the seventh, and the curve has been the same for two hundred years, and if the anneal schedule were changed by a single hour the seventh would never exist again.

In Bay Nine, time is held loose while a measurement completes.

A bolt has been falling for nine days and is a third of the way down. Under it, on a passing carrier, the moss has time to grow. And in the loose air of the bay there are midges — small, fast, ordinary-looking — that live nine generations in the time it takes the bolt to fall a centimetre, and they have been diverging from the midges outside for a very long time, and they are quick, quick, quick, and every so often one blunders out past the bay's boundary into ordinary time and finds itself in a world where a drip takes an hour, and it dies of the slowness, of starvation in a world where nothing arrives, in the middle of the brightest afternoon, in perfect silence, aged four minutes.

## The Disagreement

Once every half mile, two systems disagreed, and there is a cathedral gap.

Nothing is specified in a gap. No crew goes there. And so in the gaps, and only in the gaps, there is *rust*.

Orange. Flaking. Rounded edges. A smell like blood and rain, which is the smell of iron being slow, and which you will not recognise, and which will stop you where you stand. There is leaf litter. There is rot. There is a fungus that has been eating the same flange for a hundred and forty years and has grown a fruiting body the size of a dinner plate, and it is the oldest single organism on the plateau and it is not on any drawing.

The animals in the Disagreement are dark and slow and armoured and they do not run from anything, because nothing has come for them since the drawings were laid. They have no felt on their feet. They make noise. Small, grating, unimportant noises, all day, and the attenuators take them, and they have never noticed, because there is no one there to hear either way.

And there is a finch in there that sings *low*. Below the harvest. In the band that gets taken. It is the old song — the finches in the gaps are the ancestral stock, and out on the reissued fins their descendants moved up into the silver and got loud and fast and busy and forgot, and here the song is still the original, and it goes down the duct with the machine noise, and it is baled, and it is sold to the lowlands by the tonne, and somewhere a very long way below your feet somebody is using it to make a bearing quiet.

## Down the Duct

Every collar of attenuators feeds a duct, and the duct goes down.

Something lives in the ducts. It is not on the inventory either, which is remarkable, because everything else that lives here has either gotten onto the inventory or learned to run. It is pale and sessile and wide, and it lines the trunk at the two-hundred-metre level, and it has a membrane, and it has been fed the entire noise of the plateau for two hundred years without interruption.

It is deaf, of course. It has never heard anything. It eats.

But it has begun to make a sound.

Once every eleven months, at the shift change, the entire plateau inhales — one deep chord of pressure, the one sanctioned noise, the only sound the attenuators are ordered to let through. The maintenance drawings, six hundred metres down, show a valve.

Nobody up here has read the maintenance drawings in ninety years, and the thing in the trunk has been getting bigger, and the chord has been getting longer, and no one has noticed, because there is no one to notice, and because it is on the schedule, and because everything on the schedule is correct.

## Occupancy

Stand still for an hour and the plateau makes you a small deck to stand on, warm from the extruder, with a lip so you don't step off. It is trying. It has been out of practice for two centuries.

There is an animal that lives on that trying.

You will not see it directly. It is large — larger than a carrier, larger than you — and it exists in the amount of attention available, and it keeps station just outside where you are looking, in the region where the plateau is still building, and it stays there so that it will go on being made. When you turn, it flattens: dimensioned, hanging in the air at the exact height a floor would be, waiting. It has waited ninety years at a time. It is very patient and it is not calm; there is nothing calm about it; you can feel it in the seam behind you like a held breath, and the mice will not go into that seam, and the finches go quiet in a way that is different from their ordinary quiet.

It is not hunting you. It wants you to keep walking, and to keep looking where you are going, and to stay another day. It has learned the shape of a person the way the rail learned a grip.

That is what the polish on the rail is. It is not a memorial. It is a *specification of a hand*, maintained to four microns, and the gasket-slugs eat the difference, and the mice bring it objects worn smooth in the same place and lay them in a row, and the plateau, which is obedient, keeps almost making something.

You will leave eventually. Everyone does. On your last morning you will hear the finches carrying the news of you along the silver band, district to district, a fast dry traffic of small bright reports, forty thousand eyes tracking your coat, your knees, your saliva, your boots that are the loudest thing for a mile — and behind you the corridor will go flat, and the deck you stood on will be eaten and laid down again with the lip included, faithfully, an absence reproduced, and the grey hair in the nest in the third district will still be there, forty-one millimetres, coarse, carried from fin to fin, ninety years old and getting older, the only thing on the whole made country that is allowed to have been somewhere.
