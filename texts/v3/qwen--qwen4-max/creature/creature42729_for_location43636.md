Begin at the bend, because the bend has a tenant that nobody counts.

It is flat and pale and the shape of the bend. Not approximately: exactly. It grew into the U the way water grows into a jug, and it stopped growing when it ran out of room, and its edges are the tile's edges, and its upper surface is worn to the same curve the water makes when it moves. It has no eyes. It has one mouth, on the underside, and it spends its life grazing the silt that the marsh pushes in, and this is why the lock is not choked. Ten thousand basket-loads of clay stand outside and one animal keeps the door.

It has never been in the room. It cannot; the air would kill it, or the tongue would, or simply the geometry. It receives the note through water, as pressure, as a hand laid on its back forty times a second, and it does not distinguish the total note from ordinary racket except that at the total note the silt comes down harder and it feeds better.

Its young go out. Every one of them. They spill into the marsh and lie in the tile joints of the pans outside, flat and pale and the shape of nothing in particular, and most are eaten, and one in some years finds the bend when the old one dies, and grows into it, and becomes the door.

---

The rule is that nothing may remain on the floor. There are animals whose entire living is made of that rule.

**The fallen thing** is soft, hand-sized, the colour of a chip of glaze, and it lives on the matte rises with its foot glued down. It breeds outside. It cannot get outside; there is one road out and it is a chain of blind porters carrying rubbish. So once in its life it lets go, and drops, and lies on the tile with its legs folded, and does not move when the shells come, and is lifted, and passed hand to hand to hand, and goes down the chain in the dark under a hundred bodies, and is put out into the marsh, and stands up.

The sweepers check. They check everything, they climb your leg to check, they are not fooled and they are not not-fooled; the check is for *fallen or standing* and the fallen thing answers *fallen* correctly, which is all the question asks. It rides the sanitation to work. There are three or four kinds of it now, in different colours, matching different chips.

**The button** does the opposite. It is a hard low limpet-thing on a rise above the waterline and it has simply decided, in the way a mineral decides, not to be movable. The chains climb it, check it, find it standing, and go on. It is not permitted. It is only unmovable, which lasts longer than permission. It eats what the drop-field brings down. Eventually the daubers reach it and plaster it into the wall, and the wall gets a little thicker.

That is the whole of the law here, and it has three sentences. Everything in the hollow becomes wall, or becomes export, or becomes sound. There is no fourth outcome and there never has been.

---

Now the ones that keep the wrong addresses.

There is a comb in the fluting — a small pale arthropod, a hundred of them per ridge, living in the negative gills of an animal that is not present. It does well. The fluting condenses; the ridges trap midges; the ghost's gill happens to coincide with the room's wettest, richest, most conveniently shaped surface, and the comb has been prospering there for so long that its body has changed to fit the ridges, which is to say it has changed to fit the room, which is to say it has stopped being a parasite of an animal and become a tenant of a building without ever moving house.

Near the crown there is a shallow oval dent. The marsh people call it the eye. It may be an eye. It may be a navel, or a boss, or a place where a thumb went in too far. There is a louse there.

It is a small blood-coloured thing with a brood pouch and an inherited instruction, and the instruction is *lay here*. It lays there. Nothing comes there. The dent is dry, high, out of the light-herd's grazing, off the midge-road; the brood dies, and the last surviving female lays again in the same dent because that is what the instruction says, and the instruction has no clause for outcome. It is not waiting. Waiting requires a model of the future and it has none. It has an address. The address is nine hundred generations old and it was correct once, on a body, in a marsh that has since been dug up and set down beside itself.

Between those two — the comb that got lucky and the louse that didn't — there are eleven other stations, in eleven states of ruin and repair, and if you plotted them you would have a map of an animal drawn by starvation. The prosperous stations are the ones that happen to lie on the room's working parts. The failing ones lie on parts that were only ever anatomy. Over centuries the fauna is sliding off the ghost and onto the instrument. It is not a decision. It is just that the ones on the flues ate.

---

Eight of the nine ways in are wrong, and wrongness is inhabited too.

The seventh is not a door at all; it is a bubble, a void left in the spoil when a basket-load went down badly, and it connects to the chamber by a crack the width of a straw. Inside it: wasps. Pale, fingernail-long, indistinguishable to you from the ones in the room, descended from a swarm that went in the wrong way eleven hundred years ago and did not come back.

Their chamber is small and hard and its decay is under a second and it has one note, and the note is wrong. A fifth off, near enough. They have tuned it as faithfully as their cousins tuned the great room; they have thickened and thinned; the pitch drifted and stopped drifting and arrived. It arrived somewhere else.

And it calls something. Not the great dusk cloud — that goes to the true note and always will — but a smaller, darker, faster midge that was drifting anyway and drifted with them, and now answers only the wrong pitch, and comes in through the crack in numbers you could count, and is eaten. Two species made by a mistake in a wall. Neither can leave. If the crack ever widens, the true note will come in and drown the wrong one, and everything in the seventh will be out-competed in a season and plastered by autumn.

The other wrong doors have their tenants too: a spider in the fifth that has never heard the note, a dry rot in the second, and in the fourth a lineage of vent-fowl that plugs a hole leading nowhere and has done so, in shifts, since before the pitch was true. Their schedule is still perfect. It ventilates nothing.

---

The scaffold's rungs are worn concave in the middle. Nothing climbs it.

The wear is a mould, like everything else here — the negative of a foot, taken in wood over a few hundred years by something heavy that came up often. It is not the fowl; the fowl are forty grams of grey and would not dent a rung in ten thousand years. It is a second absence, smaller than the first, and it fits the ladder of clay ribs that goes up the far wall and stops.

The ladder stops because the top courses fell. But the route did not. One fowl in a few thousand hatches heavy-legged, with the old thick shanks and the small useless wings, and it does not fly to its flue; it walks to the ribs and climbs and reaches the break and goes on across the wall by feel, over the great smooth swell of the shoulder, along the seam, up the fluting, into the crown. It is following a road. The road runs along an animal. Nobody made the road and nobody follows it on purpose; it is simply that the birds who took that line got to the crown without being seen at an antinode, and lived, and their descendants inherit a turn.

The scaffold itself is being eaten. There is a borer in the pegs, a small brown beetle that has never touched clay and never will, endemic to nine cubic feet of swollen oak, and its grubs' chewing is a component of the noise floor — some of what you took for the sweepers is them. When the scaffold goes, and it will go, the room loses a large soft absorptive body hanging in the volume, and the decay lengthens, and the modes shift, and every property line in the hollow moves at once. The daubers will spend two centuries chasing it. The beetles will be gone before they notice.

---

Of the three understudies, one is old.

It is a bag the size of a palm, sticky, fringed, with a slit it can purse; it fills itself from the weeping wall and lets the air out slowly and hums, and it eats what comes. It learned the note the way everything here learns: the ones that hummed closer caught more, and their daughters inherited a tighter slit.

The old one is half-plastered. The daubers reached it eighty years ago and buried its lower two-thirds and did not close the slit, because the slit is not a leak. It still hums. It hums through a hole in the wall, and the hole is a mouth in the wall, and the wall now has a stop in it made of a living animal that nobody chose. There are four such stops that anyone has found. The instrument has been fitted with valves by the same process that fitted it with pitch: appetite, error, and the survivors' daughters.

There is a fourth understudy beginning on the south side, low, near the taper. It is small and it is flat and it is wrong, and it will get truer, and the room will get a little thicker around it.

Outside, on the spoil, there is a nightjar whose call is close enough to the note to take a share of the cloud, and orb-weavers strung between upended shards taking a larger one. The lure leaks. The ghost's voice feeds a hillside that has never been indoors.

---

The reed has a hole in it.

A stem-borer, a moth larva, a chamber the length of a knuckle and one clean exit through the wall of the stem. When the pressure drops, the reed splits the draft the way the room approves of, and the hole splits it again, a fifth above — no, not a fifth; nothing here is a fifth except the heretics in the seventh; a thin bright interval that sits on top of the fundamental like a fingernail on a glass.

The reed is permitted because it is a piece of the instrument. It is a piece of the instrument because it is infected. The exception has an exception and the exception is a parasite, and this is not irony, it is just the plumbing.

The moth is small and grey and goes out through the lock at night and lays in the ring outside, and one larva in a hundred finds the one reed, because there is only one reed, and it has been the same reed — one clone, one rootstock, older than the pitch — for as long as anyone can prove.

---

There is a caterpillar on the copper pins.

It eats the lichen that eats the green, and it sequesters the metal, and it is the colour of the pins exactly, and nothing in the hollow will touch it: not the fowl, not the tongue, not the understudies. It is the only animal here that is not food. It pupates in a pin-hole and comes out a small dull moth with a green body, and it dies on the floor, and is carried out.

The pins are going. The lichen is winning; a few hundred years and there is no metal in the hollow at all. The caterpillar has a date. It does not have a model of the future and it has no clause for outcome. It lays where the copper is, and the copper is where it lays.

---

After weather, the floor floods.

You know this: the water is half a day behind the sky. What you did not know is that the marsh rises with the sky, and when the marsh rises high enough the bend drowns from outside and the lock stops being a lock, and the marsh comes in.

Eels, finger-length, in the sheet-water. They lie in the glazed lows where the glaze is thickest and the water deepest, and they take the sweepers' young, and the tongue takes them, and one in a thousand goes back out full-grown through a bend it can no longer fit and squeezes anyway. The hollow is a nursery. It is an accidental nursery for a marsh animal that has never heard the note and does not care.

And the room goes flat. Deep water slackens the drumskin; the fundamental sags; the nodes wander a hand's breadth and every roost in the chamber is briefly in the wrong place, and the fowl complain in a register you feel behind the eyes. Then the water goes down and the daubers come out onto the tacky rises and quarry, and for six weeks the hollow is full of the sound of repair, which is the same sound as the sound of devotion, which is the same sound as the sound of a wasp rolling a pellet.

The tongue is not an animal, it is an office. The marsh people take it with a withy noose at the bend, once a generation or so, and eat it, and something else comes up out of the pans within a season and lies down at the place where the fundamental gathers, because that is where things land wrong. The office is never vacant long. There is a worm that goes marsh to midge to tongue and then, briefly, into a family, and the family has a word for the colic and does not connect it to anything.

---

The family is six people now.

They cut reed on the ring, which grows in courses because the sweepers export, and they think the ring is luck, and it is; it is somebody else's labour, paid hourly, and has been for nine hundred years. They are pale and slow-hearted and their resting pulse is under fifty and they can hold a breath for the bend without trouble, and they have been going in for longer than the pitch has been true.

They carry clay in on their backs. They add it where the note is flat. They are worse at this than the wasps — much worse; they read the room by ear and by argument, and the wasps read it by eating, and within a season the wasps have undone most of what the family did. The family knows this in a general way and continues. It is not faith. It is a trade, and the trade is older than the theory, and there is nothing else to do on the lee side of a hill of shards.

Their name for the place is not a word. It is the note, hummed, two beats, and the children learn it before they learn which of the nine is right, which is the correct order: the pitch first, the door second.

Inside there is one named location. Not the shoulder, not the taper, not the eye. A node, waist-high in the racket, a place the size of a doorstep where the sound cancels and you can hear your own blood. They have a name for it and it is the only place-name in the chamber, and it is where the old ones stand, and where they tell the children which door, and where they take anyone who has come from far away and needs to be told something. Everywhere else in the hollow you cannot hear a person. Property is a standing wave, and the family owns four square feet of silence, and owns it the way the comb owns a gill: by having been there when it mattered.

---

Here is what I did not see at first, and it took the whole afternoon.

The daubers have thickened some regions by a finger's width. Not evenly — where the note was flat, for centuries, in the places where a wall wanted mass. The shoulder overhead is not the shoulder that was pressed. The fluting is shallower than the fluting that made it. The seams have moved. The pins stand proud of a surface that has crept up around them.

The mould has been edited. Slowly, by insects, toward a voice.

Which means it no longer fits. If the thing that left came back — the shoulder, the gill, the taper, the woven cloth the width of your forearm — it would not go in. It would foul at the seams. It would find its own body's negative grown over in the wrong places by animals correcting a pitch, and it would stand outside on the spoil among ten thousand basket-loads and one word, and it would not fit the hole it was measured for.

The hollow fits the note better than it fits the animal that made the note. That is the whole of what has happened here. It is not a haunting and it is not a memorial and it is not waiting; it is a slow replacement, still running, in which a shape was copied, and then the copy was improved by appetite, and the improvement was away from the original and toward the sound the original made, until the copy became a better body for the voice than the voice's own body was.

So: what lives here is not the ghost and not the wasps and not the woman standing in her four square feet of silence with the racket canceling around her ears.

What lives here is the thing that is doing the replacing. It has no inside. It is made of a door that grazes, a chain that carries, a bird that stops a hole, a bag that hums through a hole in a wall, a reed with a parasite in it, six people with clay on their backs, and a hundred thousand small errors a day, corrected by eating.

It has one behaviour. It holds a pitch.

It is fed from outside, it exports its dead, it repairs its own walls, it breathes through its skin, it keeps its temperature, and once or twice a season it opens every lung at once and says the whole thing at full length, and then goes back to work.

You can call that alive. Nothing in here would answer to it. The chains start up again on the tile.
