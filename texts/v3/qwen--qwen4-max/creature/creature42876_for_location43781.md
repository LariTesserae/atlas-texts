## The gap

The first thing to know about the people is when they talk.

The Bell strokes, and the thud goes through the sinuses, and then the column comes up and the Kettle swallows, and for four of the eleven seconds nothing can be said by anyone. Then the swallow thins out into the return-falls' roar, and there is a trough — six seconds, near enough, before the next thud — and in that trough the town says everything it has to say.

So the dialect has six-second clauses. Verbs come early. Subordination is almost absent; you cannot embed a thought inside another thought if you have to land it before the water starts rising again. Long arguments are chains, and a speaker who is not finished holds a finger against the listener's wrist through the swallow, which means *wait, I am still saying it*, and the listener stands there in the roar with the finger on their wrist and does not answer. Testimony in the pump-courts is counted in strokes. A marriage is eleven clauses. A joke that needs two gaps is not a joke; it is a statement, and it is received as one.

Infants learn the trough before they learn words. A Toll baby cries in the gap. Visitors find this the most upsetting thing in the Kettle, more than the lamps or the smell or the birds turning aside at the lip, and they can rarely say why.

There are sixty hushes and there are a great many people, so speech is also a commons. Every hush has a roster cut into its jamb, re-cut each season, and the strokes are apportioned: so many to the household, so many to the guild, so many held back for births and deaths and the reading of the ledger. To be rich in Toll is to be able to say a thing at the moment you think of it. To be poor is to keep your whole life in your hands.

## Hands, and what is written on them

They are lamp-tanned orange, big-pupilled, slow-blinking, and they hear with their jaws. The fundamental is too low for the ear and arrives instead through the bones of the face; the sinus bone in a Toll skull is thick and porous and the physicians call it the brow, and they can age a body by it. Deafness is common by fifty and is not an event. Nobody makes a sign for it, because the sign for *deaf* is the sign for *ordinary*.

The back of the hand is the second ear. The palm alphabet is not a code for speech; it is older than the machinery and it does not map onto the spoken dialect at all. It has pressures and durations, a grammar of where on the hand and how long and with what part of the thumb, and it has tenses the spoken language never developed, because the spoken language never had time. In the loud galleries — a third of the town, where no word has ever been said — people carry on conversations of enormous subtlety while reaming a pipe, and the whole thing is invisible from four paces through the fog. There is poetry in it that cannot be spoken. There are households three generations deep in the antinodes whose members have never heard each other's voices and are not estranged.

The callus on the back of a Toll hand is read like a face. You can tell a liar by the hand. You can tell a stranger by the accent of the pressure, the way a Nine Throat reamer presses too hard and too long and thinks everyone else is being cold.

Their teeth are perfect. Their joints are not, and the reason is the same reason the walls are beautiful: agitated water gives up its lime, and a body is wet, and a body in a loud room is agitated. Sound grows stone. It grows it in the knee and the hip and the small bones of the wrist. The pump-crews go heavy at forty-five and are carried at sixty. The hush-rich bend at eighty and are hated for it, and you can hear a rich man coming along a gallery before you see his lamp, because his clogs fall evenly.

That is the whole of politics in Toll, and everything else — the deeds, the rosters, the retuning riots — is commentary.

## Names are water

A child is named for the drip above the bed it was born under. *Wick*. *Grudge*. *Second Mercy*. *Little Aunt*. *Hobble*. Forty of them learned before reading, eleven thousand in the full register, and the register is not a book of people but a book of places where water arrives.

Which means a name can stop.

A drip limes shut like anything else; a hair a year in the quiet, a finger in the loud, and a channel is only a millimetre of preference in a sheet of moving water. So there is a trade of namewrights, who go along the weeping faces with hooked wire and a lamp on a pole, keeping the named channels open, logging their visits, arguing about the ownership of a bifurcation. When a namewright lets a drip close, some hundreds of people wake up dry-named, and there is a fine, and the fine is never enough.

The dry names are the oldest families in the Kettle. They are named for water that stopped before the last retuning but one, and they will explain to you, in six-second clauses, over several hours, in a hush they have booked, why this makes them senior.

And this is why retuning the pumps is political violence and not engineering. When the chord changes, the standing wave moves; when the standing wave moves, the water finds new faces; and for a generation the children are named for drips that have no ancestors, while the old drips run dry in galleries nobody visits. There are people alive whose names are now three tiers above them and falling into a sump. They are called the unnamed by people who should know better. There were killings after the last retuning. The ledger records them under *maintenance*.

## The other mouths

There are eleven vat-mothers and they are older than the guilds that keep them. Each is a scum, a skin, a curdling intelligence of sulphur and heat, and each has a name and a lineage and a documented temperament, and the curd of the Fourth Mother is not the curd of the Ninth and a Toll tongue can tell them apart blind. The mothers were carried down the stair in jars at the founding. One was stolen in the second century and there is still a schism about it. A vat that goes sour is not a spoilage; it is a bereavement, and the vat-keeper wears the felt for it.

The mothers are in the people, too. Nobody in Toll digests curd without them; a Toll gut is a vat with a person around it, and a body brought down the stair sickens in a day partly because it arrives alone.

Under the tiers there is the mat, and the mat is one thing. It eats what dies. When a dog is carried down and lies down in the fourth switchback and goes to slime, the mat is what does the going. Its felt is sheared for clothing; its fruiting bodies come in eleven preparations; its runners are under the raceways and under the vats and under the sleeping-shelves of the poor, and it moves at a metre a year and has been moving since before the mould was brought down. There is no word in the dialect for a mushroom as an individual. There is a word for a mushroom as a *harvest*.

The algae in the raceways is one clone. The cable-fern is one clone. The liverwort is one clone. Count the living things in the Kettle by bodies and you get a number in the hundreds of millions; count them by individuals and you can do it on your hands, with the people left over.

And nothing rots. That is the part the visitors cannot hold. In a hush on the second tier, sealed in a jar that has never needed sealing well, there is honey, two hundred and eleven years old, from the last hive anyone tried to bring down. The bees died on the fourth switchback. The honey did not die, because there is nothing here that eats it. Once a year children are taken to taste a finger's worth and told to hold it on the tongue and not swallow and remember it, because that is what an animal tastes like, and there will not be another.

The stair has a sieve and the sieve has inspectors, and the priests say emptiness is a condition of the lease. The inspectors do not argue theology. They kill what comes down. It is the only killing in Toll and it is done on the third step, in the warm air, before the noise.

## The Solver

In a hush on the third tier, in a frame of damp baffles the size of a market square, there is a yellow slime that has been alive for four hundred years.

They call it the Solver, or more often just *it*, and the vein-wrights feed it whey and keep its temperature and cut it back at the edges, and it has never been divided in a way that made two of it. It advances a centimetre an hour. It finds the shortest path. It has preferences that the guild records and cannot explain: it goes to brass before it goes to stone, it will not cross a sulphur seam, it takes the outside of a bend. It solves in a week what would take a surveyor a season, and every pipe in the Kettle runs along a vein it made.

It is the only thing in the bowl that hunts, and what it hunts is shortness. Not food; food is everywhere and is handed to it. It wants the minimal route, and it wants it with its whole body, and watching it you understand that appetite does not require a face.

In the eleventh year it fruits. The fog goes yellow for a day and a half and everyone stays in their keeps with wet felt over the vents, and the children are told it is the Solver counting itself.

## Brass

The creepers were released nine generations ago to keep the rind off the pipe-runs and nobody has supervised them since. They charge from thermal gradients and trickle-cells, they graze the loud faces, they cluster and disperse, they fail and are taken apart by the others for parts. Their numbers boom and crash on the water's eleven-year cycle. Children keep them in jars and feed them chalk, and a jar-creeper outlives its owner and is inherited, and there are brass bodies in circulation with a serial number that has been read aloud at the scrap-house eleven times and has had nothing original in it for two centuries.

The numbers now run into series no living archive can name. The scrap-house reader says them anyway. That is the job: to say a number nobody can place, in the gap, to whoever is standing there.

And in the last two surveys of the Ninth Throat there is a fold in the fourth storey that the pumps do not account for.

The flutings are a diagram of a chord; everyone knows this; it is the first thing children are taught about stone. The diagram has matched for four hundred years. Now there is a rib in it that is not in the chord, and it is growing, and where it is growing the creepers graze in a pattern that is not dispersal and not clustering. Nobody can watch the loud faces for long. You get eleven minutes and then your sinuses bleed. The reamers' guild has a phrase for the fold, which is not a technical phrase: *the fifth voice*. It does not appear in the ledger. What appears in the ledger is the extra depth removed, in fingers, and the cost.

## The missing layer

Green follows feet.

There is no pollination and no dispersal, so every plant in the bowl travels as a fragment on a clog, in a felt hem, in the mud of a cart. Cable-fern greens a new gallery along the exact route of the first person to walk it, and then thickens along the routes of everyone after, and a map of the Kettle's green is a map of four centuries of walking, with the abandoned stairs still marked in fern by a traffic that stopped before the last retuning. The mat rides in the felt. The mothers ride in the guts. The creepers ride on the barrows. The Solver is carried to its mazes on a frame by two men who wash their hands afterward, ritually, because the guild is old.

The Kettle has no animals. The Kettle has an animal layer, and it is the pale people with their lamps and their six-second clauses, carrying the bowl's few individuals around on their bodies and in their bellies, doing everything a beetle would do except be eaten.

The priests say emptiness is a condition of the lease. There is a reading of the lease, held by a small and unfashionable sect on the seventh tier, in which the emptiness is not a prohibition but a vacancy, and the people are what was brought in to fill it, and the lease has a term.

They do not proselytise. They are subtractors, mostly. They are very good at their work.

## Setting, and going to the loud

By sixty there is chalk in the knee. By seventy it is in the spine, and the old go heavy and quiet and are said to be setting, and the word is the same word used for a lamp.

The dead are carried to the antinodes and laid against a growing face, and in a season the rind has them and in three years there is a rib with a shape in it. You visit by touch. In the Ninth Throat there is a column eleven paces high that is mostly great-grandmothers, and on the eleventh day the families come and put their palms flat on the warm gold and stand in the roar saying nothing, because nothing can be said there, and because the stone is speaking at a frequency they can feel in their teeth, and because their hands can read.

Some go before they die. That is *going to the loud*: an old woman walks to a growing face with a blanket and lies down with her hand in a drip, and the rind takes the hand in a week, and after that she is not movable and does not want to be. There is no law against it. There is a roster of who is expected to sit with them, in shifts, in the noise, holding the other hand.

And some are held. A family with money can keep a setting grandmother's room open around her — a void maintained inside an advancing solid, at four fingers a year, with a reamer on the wall every night of her life. It costs what a house costs. It is the single largest expenditure a Toll household can make and it is entered in the ledger as *maintenance*, and the neighbours read the ledger, and everyone knows exactly what is being paid for and how long it can last.

The arguments about this are the worst arguments in the town. They are conducted in fingers per year, in six-second clauses, in booked hushes, with a finger on the wrist through the swallow.

## What is actually alive here

The deed has two numbers: the hush, in bells, and the rind-rate, in fingers per year. It has no name. The name is not property. The name is what the neighbours call the hole.

Nine Widows, fifth tier, four bells quiet, half a finger a year, has been open since before the Solver came down the stair. Eleven households have kept it. There have been four fires, two retunings, one schism, and a period of ninety years in which nobody lived in it and a guild paid the reaming out of a bequest because the bequest said to. The people who kept it are in the wall of the Ninth Throat, most of them, rib by rib. The hole has not changed. It is the same void, in the same place, at the same note, and it is the oldest named individual in the Kettle after the mat, and it is not made of anything.

This is the thing that takes visitors longest to see, and some never do. A house in Toll is not a structure. It is an absence with a metabolism. It persists by being continuously opened, at a rate, by hands, against a solid that is advancing at a finger a year and is not hostile and is not indifferent either, and the hands are replaced every sixty years and the absence does not notice. The people are not the inhabitants of the rooms. The people are what the rooms do to stay alive.

Stand in Nine Widows in the trough of the eleventh second. The fog moves orange. The fern-halo glows around a lamp that will be a pebble in nine years and a stone in thirty. The gold wall is a hand's breadth from your shoulder and coming. Somewhere under the floor the mat is advancing a metre a year toward something it wants, and out in the loud a brass body with no original parts is grazing a fold that is not in the chord, and eleven vats are curdling, and a woman named for water that stopped three hundred years ago is putting her chisel into the wall to keep the hole open for one more season.

Ask her what lives here and she will not answer in the gap. She will take your hand and put it flat on the warm gold, and hold her finger on your wrist through the swallow, which means *wait, I am still saying it*.
