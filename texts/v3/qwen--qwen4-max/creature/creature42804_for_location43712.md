Ask the sound who lives here and the sound will tell you: a crew. A shift. Somebody inside who knows what all of it is for. The sound reports work. It has never once reported a worker, and you keep hearing one anyway, because that is what a mill does to a mammal.

The honest census is a smear.

---

Most of what lives in the basin lives out on the flats between the engines, where the current is diffuse and nothing is loud, and it is a millimeter thick and it does not move. Black films in the seeps, rust-red films a hand's width back from them, each one parked exactly on the seam where reduced iron meets air, grazing the difference the way a cow grazes a fence line. They are the primary producers. They have been the primary producers the entire time. Everything else here is a rounding error on their ledger.

And they are thinnest near the engines. This is the first thing you learn by walking: the wheel is a short circuit. It takes the difference the films live on and spends it into shape and noise, and for fifty meters around every shoulder the redox front has been eaten out from under the biology, and the crust there is bare, damp, clean, ringing. The loudest places in the basin are the poorest. The engines stand in halos of their own appetite. Out in the quiet interstices, where the current leaks instead of discharging, the films go thick and slightly wrinkled and the grey-green crust-former holds the dry ground between them, and that is the pasture, and it looks like nothing, and it is where everything is.

Add it all up — every film, every belt, every grazer, every egg waiting in the crust — and it weighs less than one wheel. And the wheel it weighs less than is four hours old.

---

In the drive groove of each engine there is a fungus, and the fungus is a belt.

Not a fungus that makes a belt. Not a fungus shaped like one for reasons. It *is* the loop: three meters of slick chitinous ribbon running under tension around two mineral pulleys in the wet slot, wearing at the contact face, laying new tissue at the back face, holding its length to within a millimeter for as long as the groove exists. It has no head and no front and no direction of travel that matters. Cut it and it heals into a ring, because ring is the shape the groove will pay for. Its genome, if you sequenced it, would read like a tolerance chart.

It does no work. The wheel does not need it. What it does is take the wear: the tooth rides on chitin instead of on magnetite, and a groove lined with something that replaces itself keeps its geometry finer than a groove that must true itself by dissolution alone. Belted engines run truer. Their grooves last longer. Grooves that last longer hold belts longer, and belts in long-lived grooves throw more spores into the thrown water, and the spores go everywhere and germinate essentially nowhere — one in ten million finds a slot with the right wetness and the right shear and the right current, and becomes a loop, and has no other life it could have been living.

So the machine farms a fungus. Neither party is a party. There is no arrangement, no bargain, no advantage anybody is keeping track of; there is only a groove that pays a little better to things shaped like ribbon, and eleven million years of ribbon.

The warm animal smell on your palm — the inside-of-the-mouth smell, in a place with essentially no animals — is this. It is the smell of the belts. Two hundred throats and two hundred loops of living tissue, and the whole plain smells like breath.

---

Of animals there is essentially one, and it is not the animal the smell belongs to.

A pale flat grazer the size of your hand, low as a coin, unhurried past the point where unhurried looks like a decision. It rasps the oxide films with a radula of accreted mineral and it takes about an hour to clear a patch the size of a dinner plate. Its shell has taken iron out of the world and put it into a lattice, and when it stops moving it settles into alignment with the local field, and so do all the others, and across kilometers of plain they lie pointing the same way. From a rise it looks like a congregation. It is a compass reading with an appetite attached.

But the alignment is also a road.

The films are the food and the bearings are the nursery, and they are a kilometer apart, and the animal has to cross. It goes at three meters an hour, along the field lines, following the same gradient it sleeps on, and the crossing takes a season. Nothing else in the basin makes a journey. There are no paths — nothing accumulates, not even a scuff — but there are corridors, statistical ones, arcs of probability running out of the pastures and into the warm housings, invisible and completely real, worn into the population the way the drip groove is worn into the crust.

She lays in the bearing. The wheel takes the eggs and turns them, warm and wet and slow, for the better part of a year, and when they are ready it drops them at the bottom of the arc where the rim is entering the slot, and the throw of the upper rim carries the water and the young out and away, and they come down in the seeps a hundred meters off. The engine breeds the thing that eats its shadow and then flings it into the food. There is no dispersal strategy here. There is a wheel, and things that have learned to be thrown by it.

There is something it is like to be this animal. It is like almost nothing: a taste, a warmth, a tilt, a rasp, the small satisfaction of a film going thin. It cannot hear the fundamental. The nine-second tick reaches it as a pulse in the water and means nothing to it; it has never counted anything; its only clock is hunger. The whole interior life of the basin is a few centimeters wide, and it is looking for lunch, and it is the only interior there is.

---

Now the engines, which is where the question stops being answerable.

They eat. They maintain themselves. They true their own bearings. They reproduce at replacement rate and they die and leave a body-shaped hole. They do everything a living thing does except descend.

That is the missing word. A wheel is not its parent's child. When a nodule seeds out on the flat and grows a shoulder and a slot and a tooth, it does not inherit seven lobes; it *derives* them, from the same chemistry, because seven lobes is what survives the current, and the answer is lying there in the physics waiting to be found again by anything with a rim. Nothing here is descended from anything. Everything here is descended from a solution. There is no lineage in the wheels, no drift, no accumulated cleverness, no way for one engine to be a slightly better idea than its neighbor. Eleven million years and not one improvement. Roundness forever, and never anywhere.

So: are they the inhabitants, or the habitat that behaves like an inhabitant?

Both answers are true and the discrepancy is the character of the place. You can call the plain one animal with a forty-one-minute pulse and a biology in its gut, and you would not be wrong; you can call it a landscape with a very unusual weather system, and the films and the belts and the grazers are only the things that have adapted to weather that ticks. The plain does not distinguish between these. The plain does not distinguish.

---

Except in the graves.

A grave is a wheel-shaped hollow where the vein pinched or the current got stolen, and the wheel went soft, and there is nothing there now but clear condensate a hand's breadth deep. Clear because nothing is being spent there. It is the only slack water in sixty kilometers, the only place the cell has stopped paying, and it is therefore the only place where something can happen that the rest of the basin forbids.

In the graves there are swimmers.

Transparent things the size of a comma, filtering the fallout: spores from the thrown water, dust off the rim hills, and — twice a year, from a great height — the droppings of the winged things that cross the basin and never come down. That is the import. That is the whole external economy of the place, and it arrives from a sky the basin does not respond to, and it feeds nineteen pools of nearly starving animals in the ruins of machines.

They have generations. They have heredity. They have the ordinary bad memory of life, which is DNA, which is a record kept badly and kept anyway. When a grave is reclaimed — when a new engine seeds in the hollow and starts to grow its shoulder — the population dies, and its resting eggs are already dry in the crust for a hundred meters around, waiting decades, waiting centuries, and somewhere out on the flat another hollow opens and the eggs nearest it wake.

Nineteen pools. Nineteen populations. Nineteen founder events, nineteen bottlenecks, nineteen small accumulated nothings in the count of a comb's bristles or the set of a segment — nineteen dialects of one animal, each of them a few thousand years old, each of them the only thing in the basin that got here by being handed something.

And when a dialect goes, it goes. It does not rederive. There is no physics that pays for the exact bristle count of a pool that closed in the year the rim hills shed a particular flood. It is simply not there anymore, and it will never be there again, and nothing in sixty kilometers notices.

The only thing in this basin that can be lost lives in the holes left by the things that cannot.

---

You are the other one.

You are the door with the light behind it. You are the only thing that has ever stood here that has also been somewhere else, and the reason the place sounds occupied is that you are occupying it, and the shift you can hear is your own attention, running at the pace of a resting heart, in a room where the work is powered from the floor.

Take a sample and the absence becomes a high-current spot and the spot fills. Take a thought out and nothing fills. That is the only accumulation this place has ever permitted, and it happens exclusively offsite, in things that leave.

A grazer lives a hundred and fifty years, which is long enough that once, maybe, in a lifetime, it is somewhere within a hundred meters when a wheel goes soft — when the rim sags and the tick stumbles and eleven million years of roundness slumps into a pool. The water changes. The taste of the world goes strange and thin and cold. The animal stops, and settles, and aligns, and then turns and goes the other way, toward the film, at three meters an hour.

That is the basin's entire historical record. It lasts as long as the animal, and it is not written down, and there is no one to tell.

Lie down between two engines and listen for the third, and the fourth, out past where you can see. Break a tooth and in nine hours there is a tooth, and the tick will have wandered by less than a second and come back.

You will not have.
