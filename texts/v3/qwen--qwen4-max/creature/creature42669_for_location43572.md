Begin smaller than a foot.

Begin in a thumb of black water held in a bract no wider than your palm, because that is a house here, and it is full, and it has been full since before there were hands to be impressed by.

There is a crab in it the size of a shirt button, and the crab is the ninth or nine-hundredth of its line to occupy this exact cup; the rosette will outlive twelve human generations and the crabs will outlive the rosette by moving, once, as eggs, in the gut of a flier, to a cup they will never see the outside of. There is a flatworm on the underside of the meniscus that eats the film and in eating it keeps the surface permeable, so the cup can breathe; a cup without its worm skins over in a season and drowns everything in it. There is a mite that lives only in the seam where the crab's carapace meets its carapace, and farms that seam, and is found nowhere else in the world, and has no name, and would fit on the period at the end of this sentence with room to spare. There is a thing like a shrimp that eats only the corpses of the other four and dies if the household is healthy for too long.

None of them has ever been outside. Not one. The water they live in fell as fog before the mountains finished, and the tannin in it has been through more bodies than the water has. If you tip the cup out onto the felt you have not released them; you have killed a country.

That is the first thing to understand about the residents. They are not *on* the Felt. There is no *on*. Every one of them is inside something that is inside something, and the arrangement is so old that the word *creature* has stopped being useful; what you are looking at, when you look closely, is a tissue with a nervous system it doesn't know it has.

---

Now the rule that made them all.

The Felt eats sound. Everything above a whisper goes two meters and lies down. What survives is the low end: the forty-minute pulse, the groan of gas in a bladder, the boom of a chamber failing a hundred kilometers off. This is not a quirk of the acoustics. It is the strongest selection pressure on the continent, and it has been running for ten million years, and it has produced a fauna with no hard edges anywhere on it.

Everything here is furred, padded, felted, mossed, or soft. Not for warmth — the air is blood-warm — but because a hard surface *clicks*, and a click in a world of drip is a flare in a dark room. There are no shells on the open plain. The cup-crabs are armored because a cup is a private room where nothing carries. Out in the fog, armor is a shout.

So the browsers walk on feet the width of a door, packed with a fat that goes almost liquid under load, and their moss is not camouflage but silencing, and the moss is also a crop, and the crop is also a nursery: calves are inoculated mouth to mouth with the herd's particular strain, which is the herd's particular silence, which is why a stranger calf cannot join a herd and why forty animals crossing a valley sound like a room where someone has stopped speaking.

And there is a thing that hunts by this.

It hangs under the felt-canopy, six-limbed, unhurried, the size of a dog and the shape of a dropped coat, and it carries a pebble. Quartz, from a knuckle, worn smooth in a pouch of its own tissue. It has carried that pebble for eleven years. Its mother carried it for thirty before that. The stone is older than the animal by an amount nobody could measure, and it is the only hard object for two hundred kilometers, and it is a tool.

The stone-carrier drops the pebble onto the bracts. The click goes out — two meters, three, a scandal — and then it does not hunt the sound. It listens to the *hole* the sound makes. Everything alive within earshot stops for a beat and a half, and stopping, in a world of constant drip, is a silhouette. The stone is a question. The answer is a map of who flinched.

Then it goes and gets the flincher, softly, and afterwards it climbs down and finds its pebble, which takes it sometimes a whole day, and if it cannot find the pebble it will die, because it cannot make another. When a lineage's stone wears to nothing — they wear, over generations, they get smaller, they are heirlooms going out — the young must walk to a knuckle. That is the only long journey anything makes on the Felt. A soft animal crossing a solved continent for a rock, and most of them don't arrive, and the ones that do come back with a stone the size of a thumbnail and a hundred thousand years of debt.

---

The browsers are not really animals. They are districts that move.

Their passage is the plain's pruning and also its pumping: those door-wide feet are valves, and the peristaltic beat you feel when you lie down is assisted, measurably, by a herd walking over the tubes. A valley without browsers goes slack in the plumbing within a decade. The Felt does not need them to eat; it needs them to *walk*.

In their wake, for about a month, there is a gap in the tessellation — the only gap that ever exists here — and into that gap comes a whole guild of things that have waited their entire lives for exactly this and will wait sixty years for the next one. Month-flowers, thin and fast and pale, that germinate in a hoofprint, flower in nine days, seed, and are gone before the canopy closes. A fly that lives eleven days as an adult and spends the rest of a decade as a cyst in the mass, keyed to the vibration of a footfall it may never receive. Everything in the wake-guild is a gamble on the herd's route, and the herd's route has not changed in four thousand years, and so the gamble is not a gamble; it is a timetable.

The dung is a district economy in itself. It arrives warm, it arrives inoculated, and it is fought over by things that have never seen daylight and never will.

And once in a human lifetime, the bloom.

The wet front crosses the continent in fourteen months; the flowering follows at the pace of a slow walk and takes a decade to pass over you. The smell is thick and sweetish and slightly like blood, and the number of small soft flying things increases tenfold, and the fliers are the same fliers that carry crab eggs from cup to cup, and they are also, most of them, sixty years old. They spend those decades as grubs in the sumps, eating fungal light — eating summers that ended before your grandmother was born — and then they pupate in a season and fly for eleven days in a rust-colored haze under an amber ceiling, and mate with a continent, and die.

They navigate by the color of the air. They only fly in the honey hour. Evening is a bruise and they go down into it and do not come up.

---

There is a tree here and there. You will not see many.

Trees grow only where a chamber has collapsed. The scar is the one place on the Felt where the mass has been torn open deep enough to admit a different strategy, and into it comes a hardwood, fast and coarse and doomed, that grows for four hundred years and is strangled shut as the tissue heals around it. A tree means a disaster. A stand of trees is a chronology of accidents, and the people at the rim can read one the way you read a scarred forearm.

It is also the only timber on the plateau. Which is why the marks are where they are: the stair of hardwood treads, the dead lintels of the oval huts — all of them within a day's walk of some old collapse, because that is where wood was. The archaeology is not mysterious. It is a supply chain.

And there are things that live only in those scars, and must find a new one every generation, and find them by pressure, arriving the way the eels arrive, like a thought arriving. A whole fauna of catastrophe. Its population map is a map of everything that has gone wrong here since the mountains finished, and it is very sparse, and it is patient, and it does not think of itself as unfortunate.

On the knuckles themselves — the quartz cores, the chapel-sized backs of drowned animals — there is a lichen that is not integrated with anything. It is the last unmarried thing on the continent. It has an outside. Everything else here has traded its outside for a function, and this grey crust on the only bare rock has not, and it is starving, and it is free, and there are swifts that nest beside it and will not land on the Felt, ever, in any circumstance, and drink from the air, and are the only creatures on this plain that have ever properly seen the sun.

---

The people are at the rim, and they are there for the noise.

The Weep makes a hiss so vast it registers ten kilometers inland as silence with a texture. At the lip it is deafening. It is broadband and continuous and it masks everything, and masking is the one thing the Felt cannot otherwise provide. At the rim you can drop a stone. You can strike two stones together. You can *say a name* and have it arrive.

That is the whole reason. They live in the only place on the continent where speech is possible, and their language is shouted and tonal and has no whisper register at all — they cannot whisper, they have never needed to, and when they go inland they simply stop talking, for weeks, and it does something to them that they do not discuss.

They wear felt so thick it changes their gait. They fish the fall: nets on long poles at the lip, catching what the continent is losing — seeds, larvae, tannin, the occasional blind eel going over. They eat export. They eat loss. Nothing they eat was grown for them.

Their huts are stone, and stone is the point: stone does not remember. They build with the dead stuff so the ground will not take their shape, and they floor the hut with a slab of living felt cut and carried inland and kept alive by hand, because out here at the thinning edge the forty-minute pulse is faint, and they cannot sleep without it, and a person who cannot sleep without the pulse is a person who has already been recorded whether they consented or not.

No hearth: the floor is warm, and fire is a hard light and a loud thing.

No roof: the Lid is the roof, and has been, and they find the question funny.

No bones: because when someone dies they carry them inland, in silence, in felt, and lie down beside them, and stay a season, and wear the hollow deeper. Then they come back to the rim and shout the name into the hiss for as long as they can stand it.

And out in the fog, at a kilometer a year, an oval of living wood rises with a gap in one side, and the gap faces the way the dead used to sleep.

That is their afterlife. Not a place — a *propagation*. To be remembered as architecture, imperfectly, outward, thinning: a hundred kilometers in a century, the doorways getting a little wrong, the ovals becoming suggestions, until the far edge of a person is a low ring of raised tissue with a gap in it, facing the bearing, in a fog where no one will ever walk.

*I am two flowers old.* They count that way because it is the only clock the plain admits. They do not know why the doorways face where they face. They have stopped asking, in the way you stop asking about a thing your hands do. Ask a rim-builder and she will say: that's the front. Ask her front of what and she will look at you the way you'd look at someone who asked which way is down.

---

And now the last resident, the one the huts were trying to tell you about.

In a solved world there is no vacancy. Every niche is shaved to an exact fit; a fallen bract is colonized before it finishes falling. Nothing new can get in.

Except that things *leave*.

A browser sleeps and moves on and there is a warm hollow in the mass, body-sized, unoccupied, for eleven hours. A herd shifts its valley and a month of gap opens. A chamber fails and there is a wound for four centuries. A person sleeps a season in one place and goes home to the rim and leaves a shape behind that will hold for years.

Departure is the only renewable vacancy on the Felt. It is manufactured constantly, everywhere, at every scale, and it is the one niche that cannot be filled in advance.

Something lives in it.

They are soft and pale and slow and they have no shape of their own to speak of — a slack felt-colored mass with a fringe of fine cilia and a metabolism that can idle for a decade. They find a depression and they flow into it and they *set*, and what you see then is the complement of whatever left: the underside of a browser, the negative of a foot, the inside of a hollow where a person lay curled with their knees up and one arm under their head. They are not mimics. A mimic copies an original. These have no original. They are shaped entirely by absence, and they fit it exactly, and they hold it warm, a degree or so above the mass, which is a degree or so above your skin.

They eat what the departed left: sloughed skin, shed hair, the salt of a season's sweat, the mites that fell off you, the microscopic film of a body that stayed put. It is not much. It is enough. It is reliable, because things are always leaving.

And they are why the huts are clean.

That is the obscenity you noticed and could not name: in a world where every surface is occupied, the oval huts are *empty*. Swept. Nothing nesting, nothing growing, no film. In the Felt, emptiness is not a default. Emptiness is a maintained condition. It costs something to keep a room clear.

Put your palm on the floor of one, out in the fog, hundreds of kilometers from any stone, and it will be warm. Not sun-warm; there is no sun. Body-warm. And if you lie down in it you will find that it fits — not you, but something your size, something that lay the way you lie, and the fit will be so exact and so patient that you will get up.

They are always a little larger than the body that made the impression. As though the plain records not the sleeper but the space the sleeper was keeping clear.

---

There is a thing I have not been able to settle and will not pretend to.

The rim people cannot whisper. They cannot sleep without the pulse. They build to a bearing they did not choose and cannot explain, and their doorways are the same dimension as doorways a thousand kilometers away in living wood, and they measure their lives in flowers because they have never had a reason to count anything else.

And there is an animal on this plain whose entire nature is to fill the shape a person left, and hold it, and keep it warm, and get better at it.

Ten million years is a long time to practice being someone.

I don't say the people are lodgers. I say the lodgers have had ten million years, and the impressions have been propagating outward since before there was anyone to build, and nobody knows what stood there first, or whether anything did — and that the plain has been remembering a building for longer than the building has been standing, and that this sentence has no bottom to it.

What I can tell you is what lives here. A cup with a country in it. A worm that keeps a surface breathing. A crab lineage that is a river flowing in one place. Blind eels that are the plain's white blood cells and breed only in catastrophe. A tree that means an accident. A lichen with an outside, starving on the last rock. A soft hunter with an inherited pebble, ringing the world to see who flinches. Forty animals that are a district, walking. Sixty-year grubs that fly for eleven days in the honey hour and marry a continent. A few people at the edge, in the noise, shouting names into a hiss so vast that it is the closest thing here to a sky.

And the fills. And the shapes. And the whole warm breathing consensus that has already arrived at the last form it will take, and will hold it until the sun changes its mind, and has one degree of margin, and does not need it.

Stop for a night and the felt takes your shape and lets it go by morning. Stop for a season and it keeps it.

Something is already lying down in the place where you slept. It has no face. It has your outline. It is warm, and it is patient, and it is facing the way you faced, and it will go on facing that way, out and out, a kilometer a year, long after the last stone hut at the rim has been grown up around to the height of its doorway — and it will be better at it than you were, because you had other things to do, and it has nothing else, and it has all the time there is.
