## The incumbents

The census is a count of furniture. Four frogs, three spiders, one fish, eleven thousand midges, some uncounted thousands of amphipods: this is what you would write on a clipboard, and it describes the room about as well as a list of chairs describes a marriage. What lives here is not a set of animals. It is a set of jobs, and the animals are the material the jobs are currently made of, the way the scallops in the floor are a shape currently made of wood.

Begin with the four frogs, because they are the easiest to mistake for lonely.

They are the size of a plum stone and the colour of the wall behind them, which is to say they are visible only as an eye and a throat. Each sits at a pressure antinode with its belly in the film and its forefeet on the pale nap of the wall, facing the same bearing its predecessor faced, and it calls at 1.9 kilohertz into a room that is already screaming. It is not calling to the other frogs. It is calling to the wall nine metres away, and listening to what comes back.

The return is the message. A frog's call goes out, folds off the curved ceiling, crosses the ribs, comes home changed by everything it touched — by the thickness of the tissue, the depth of the water, the exact tension of the gland in the ceiling, the wear on the nozzle a hundred microns deep. The frog hears the room's shape returned to it as a timbre, and it compares that timbre to nothing, because it has nothing to compare it to, and it calls again. Four frogs, four bearings, offset by the travel time of sound between their stations, so that the queries interleave and never collide: a round, sung by animals who cannot move, whose entire behaviour is the act of asking a question that has only ever had one answer.

It is possible to describe this grammar and not the subject. What matters enormously to a frog, in the narrow silver band where it can be heard, is something like *is it still this shape*. And the answer, four times a second, forever, is *it is still this shape*. Whether that constitutes reassurance or boredom or prayer is not a question the room is equipped to answer.

## The three, and what they wear away

The spiders are hand-sized and pale and sit on the ribs, on the iron, at three of the positions the frogs do not occupy. They have no eyes worth the name. They read the air with hairs on their legs, and what they read is the standing wave — the local airflow, its shear, its turbulence, its slow modulation — and out of that reading they spin a web that is not a design and not a memory but a transcription. The web is the shape of the air, written in silk. When it is destroyed by the press's exhaust gust, or by the spider's own feeding, it is rebuilt identically, because the air has not changed, and the spider is not remembering the web. It is reading the sentence again off the page.

They eat the old web each time. Nothing goes into the water. And they stridulate at 2.1 kilohertz by drawing a comb on the foreleg across a file on the tibia, braced against the rib — so the sound comes out of the iron, the only hard thing in the room, ringing slightly, a struck note in a place where everything else is wet and soft and mute.

Here is what that means, and it takes a while to see it. The ledger says *iron deposition, matched to abrasion*. The abrasion is three spiders, playing a file on a rib, forever.

## The smiths

Which brings us to the residents you will not see, who are the reason there is anything hard here at all.

On the inner face of every rib there is a nap, a felt, a grey-black bloom a fraction of a millimetre thick, and it is alive: a consortium of bacteria sitting across the potential the organism runs between sulphide-rich groundwater and oxygenated air. They eat the current. They eat the iron dissolved in the film. What they leave behind, as a waste product, as a kind of excrement, is the plating — black-grey, faintly iridescent, harder than anything else in the room, laid down at a few microns a year.

The bearings are bacterial garbage. The instrument the spiders play is a midden. The hardest, coolest-looking, most manufactured-looking surface in the vessel is the accumulated droppings of something too small to see, and it is exactly as thick as it needs to be, because the spiders wear it off at the rate the smiths put it on, and neither party has ever been informed of the other.

There are more of these guilds than there are ribs. In the throat of the nozzle there is a grazer, a pale limpet-thing with a rasp, that eats proud tissue; it cannot eat concavity, so it eats everything that is not the convergent profile, and the profile stays true because deviation is food. On the black plate, in and around four thousand holes too small to see, a ciliated film browses the swelling cellulose that would otherwise close them, and the holes stay four thousand. The notch in the roar is too clean because the nozzle is too true, and the nozzle is too true because something is hungry for error.

That is the third answer to the question the room refuses to settle. It does not matter whether anyone made this. Accuracy here is not a legacy. It is a metabolism. The specification is running because a bestiary of tiny correctors is eating everything that isn't it, continuously, and has been for forty thousand years, and would eat the bolts if there were any left.

## The eleven thousand, and the eleven minutes

The midges never land. There is nothing to land on that is not wet, and wetness at 33.4 degrees, in a film that eats, is a death sentence with a delay. They hold station in the air column at the nodes, spaced so evenly that the gaps are the visible thing, and their wings beat inside the notch, all of them, eleven thousand small tones packed into seven hundred hertz, so the cloud is not a cloud but a sustained chord with a slow shimmer in it as individuals drop out and hatch in.

They mate in flight. They live a day and a half. They fall.

The fall is constant and it is exactly matched, and the amphipods take it: silver, flicking, thousands of them in twenty-one millimetres of water, working the runnels in the direction the water has always gone. With them a bacterial skin on the wood, and under the skin a mat of nematodes so fine it reads as a colour. This is the sanitation of the vessel, and it is not slow. Eleven minutes from the water's surface to nothing. A midge that hits the film at the far end of the room is a chemistry at the near end before you have finished noticing it fell.

And the amphipods are not fussy. This is the part of the census that does not appear on the clipboard.

## The bench

Behind the feet of the ribs, in the crevices where the lignified dark meets the pale wall, in the shadowed lip of the fairlead, in the mud-line just outside the stoma where the rain gets in — there are more frogs. Hundreds. Small, pale, underfed, packed into the interstices, motionless, each one already tuned. They hatched in the film, survived the film, climbed out of it, and found a crack, and they are waiting for an office.

There are four offices.

The surplus does not accumulate, because nothing here accumulates. It goes into the water in ones and twos, all day, every day, and the amphipods take it, and this is a substantial fraction of why the floor is spotless. The room's cleanliness is made largely of aspirants. A place with no litter is not a place where nothing dies; it is a place where the dying are the food supply, and the number of the hopeful is the meal.

When an incumbent frog fails, it does not vacate. It stays on its node, calling more weakly, while the tide comes up its legs and eats it from below, and the office is occupied throughout. Only when the last of it is gone does the vacancy begin, and it lasts about six hours, which is how long it takes the nearest competent understudy to climb the wall and bring its sac into pitch. Competent means: at 1.9 kilohertz, at that bearing, with that timing. The bench is not competing. It is queuing, and being eaten while it queues, and the ones who arrive arrive already knowing the note, because the note was in the water they hatched from, in the beat they grew to.

In the drain pipe, downstream in the dark, there is a run of small blind fish. There is always exactly one fish in the room.

## The slow thing under the fast thing

If you count — and you will not, at first, because the roar flattens everything into a single present tense — you find that the shaft turns at 2,900 revolutions a minute, which is 48.3 a second, and that the chamber's fundamental is 47, and that these are not the same number.

They beat against each other at about 1.3 hertz. Seventy-eight a minute. The whole room swells and slackens, swells and slackens, just under the threshold of the obvious: the weep thickens and thins, the pigment layer hunts, the water film rises perhaps half a millimetre and falls, the frogs' round rides it, the midge cloud tightens and loosens like a fist half-closing.

Nothing in the ledger records this, because it is not a variation; it is the carrier. It is the only clock in a place with no hours, and every resident is locked to it, and it is roughly the rate of a resting human heart, which is a coincidence, and which is the single fact about this room most likely to make you sit down.

## What lives in the gap

Now the thing that has no body.

Between 1600 and 2400 hertz there is a rectangular hole in ninety-one decibels of noise, an accident of a nozzle profile and a curved ceiling, and it has been colonised the way a cave is colonised — by specialists, irreversibly, who cannot live outside it. Four throats, three files, eleven thousand wings, and the amphipods' small percussion, and something in the fairlead that clicks when the rope takes tension. All of it inside seven hundred hertz. All of it deaf to everything else, including the machine that made the silence they live in, including you.

The conversation is continuous. It has no gaps of its own; when a frog is being eaten the other three close ranks and the midges fill the seam, and when the understudy comes in it comes in on the beat, and there has not been a silence in that band since before there were ears to notice one. Bodies turn over hourly. The song does not stop. In a room where every position is a property of the field and every animal is a temporary filling, the song is the only resident with continuity of *content* rather than of form.

And it may be drifting. There is no archive here — memory is accumulation and this place does not do that — so if the calls have shifted a little in ten thousand years, if the grammar has folded, if what the frogs are asking has slowly become a different question, there is nothing anywhere in the vessel it could be checked against. It would sound, to itself, exactly like continuity. The one thing in the room that might have a history is the one thing that cannot be measured against anything.

## The one

The fish is blind, pale, the length of a finger, and it holds station in the outflow at the low corner with its head into the current, and it is in the null.

The bucket-sized space where two reflections cancel — the hush, the acoustic cave, the only place in the vessel where the conversation can be heard whole — is where the fish lives. It has always lived there. It is the only animal in the room that does not have to visit the quiet.

It eats what the pipe brings: the drift, the film's sloughings, the occasional small blind successor. It does not call. It has no file, no sac, no wing. It cannot be assigned a function; every other resident can be described as a corrector, a grazer, a query, a tide, and the fish is none of these, and the ledger balances without it, and it is always there.

What it has is the whole band, all of it, all the time, from inside the silence: four frogs asking, three spiders ringing the iron, eleven thousand wings holding the chord, the click in the fairlead, the beat at seventy-eight a minute. It has had this, in an unbroken succession of single fish, for forty thousand years. It is the room's only continuous listener and it makes no report and there is no one to report to and nothing in the vessel is equipped to receive one.

Call it a witness if you like. The room does not do memory, so a witness is either the least useful animal here or the only one that is not merely a mechanism, and the room, as advertised, offers no evidence either way.

You can crawl to it. You can put your head in the hush and hear the silver voices for as long as your neck holds. It will not turn. Your frequencies are outside the band; you are not an event; the one thing in this place that hears everything has never heard you and will not, and its record is complete.

## The largest resident

And of course the room itself is an animal, folded. Ten thousand hectares of one individual, forty thousand identical chambers, and no exterior you can stand in — the organism lives inside itself, tenant and landlord, and each room is a fold of the same body, and none of them is the middle.

It has no memory. It cannot afford one; memory is sediment, and sediment is a deviation, and there is something that eats deviations. So it does the only thing a thing like this can do with time: it exports it. Four litres a second through a convergent nozzle, macerated green pressed between ridged plates, caustic sap through four thousand invisible holes, and out of the ceiling comes a wrist-thick rope of cellulose tow that hardens as it rises. Tonnes a day. Dead stuff, spun out of a living room, hauled up through a wet lipped throat into the canopy, and built into more organism, which makes more rooms, which make more rope.

That is where the forty thousand years went. Not into sediment, not into wrack, not into fat, not into history. Up. The stand outside — the grey-green fluted columns, the canopy closing like a hand, the thing that looks like every other stand — is the room's forgotten time, stacked, load-bearing, forty metres of it, remembering in the only medium available, which is wood.

The room forgets so that the tree can be the memory. The tree remembers nothing in particular. It just gets taller by exactly as much as it wears away.

## You

Seventy watts, a litre an hour, a plume of carbon dioxide, four hundred displaced midges, a smell, a soft grey contact-patch under your feet in a room with no other shadows.

You are read as weather. The governor takes the heat and leans on the weep. The pigment layer opens a fraction where you stand and closes it as you move. The film takes your footprints and heals them in ninety seconds. Nothing looks up, because looking up is not in the specification, and nothing hears you, because hearing you is not in the band. You are the only variable to enter in a long time and the room's entire response to you is a small, competent, uninterested correction.

If you stayed — if you sat down in the low corner with your head in the hush and listened to the frogs asking their question and the spiders ringing the iron and the eleven thousand holding the chord, and let yourself be charmed, and did not get up — the correction would take longer than ninety seconds and less than a day, and the tide would come, and the numbers would not change.

Duck back out through the lip. It is dry and warm and gives like the pad of a thumb. Outside it is grey and enormous and smells only of water, and under your boots the plain is turning at two thousand nine hundred revolutions a minute, and somewhere in the crevices behind the ribs a pale understudy the size of a thumbnail is waiting for a job that will not exist until something currently singing stops.
