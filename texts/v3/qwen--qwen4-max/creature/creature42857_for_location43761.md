# The Residents of the Yard

## Start with the towers, because they are the ones that let you get close enough to be wrong about them

A gantry is an annual. That is the first thing to get straight, and the hardest, because it stands there like a cathedral and the mind assigns it centuries. It has none. It is a season's growth, a wet green thing that got hard, and it will be gravel before the fog season comes round again.

Height here is cheap. Mass is dear. The tall ones — the ones you can see from a day's walk out, wobbling in the heat shimmer — are mostly hollow tube full of hot water, stayed with tension wood, and they weigh less than the four anchor blocks that keep them from leaving. A kilometer of altitude costs almost nothing if you are willing to be a straw. What costs is the arm: the long cantilevered member with the hanging bundle at its tip, because that has to be *solid*, and it has to be *cured*, and it has to hold a preload for eleven days without the rain finding its seams.

The bundle is a plumb. It is grown as a bladder and filled with dense green stone slurry until it weighs what it needs to weigh, and it hangs there doing two jobs at once. It loads the arm, so that the arm lignifies under stress and comes out of its cure storing its own weight. And it swings. Its period is a measurement of the arm's stiffness, and the stiffness falls as the joints dissolve, and the whole organism is built to notice that fall — not with an ear, not with anything like attention, but with a bract pressed flat against the cured member that abscises when the note goes flat.

That is the entire nervous system of the Yard. A leaf that hears one wrong note and lets go.

When it lets go, three things happen in sequence, none of them decided. The mats stop screeding under that arm. The floor beneath it is left unswept, un-relaid, allowed to go soft and slumpy — which is to say, the drop zone is cleared, and has been cleared for weeks, by an organism that cannot see the arm and has never once been surprised. And the arm comes down, usually at dusk, usually in rain, and the boom crosses the plain and arrives in your feet before your ears, and by morning the mats are dragging the pieces to the troughs.

Watch one for an afternoon and you will see it walk twenty meters. Stilt roots lift, set, lift; a hydraulic sprint, a few meters an hour; a wake of hot water and torn hardstanding behind it, and a steam hole where it used to be. It re-tensions one stay. That is the whole biography of an afternoon. In a month the skyline is a different skyline, and none of the towers in it are the towers you learned.

## The towers are not the individuals

Go down on your knees at the edge of a cleared circle and work your fingers under the hardstanding crust, and you will find that the crust is not the ground. It is a *surface*, in the sense a skin is a surface. Under it, continuous, warm, wet, pale as a peeled thing, is the mat.

It runs for hundreds of meters in every direction, a single clonal sheet threaded through with the stilt-root plumbing, and the tower is growing out of it. The tower is not a plant with roots. The tower is something the mat is doing with its middle.

The mat has organs, and they look like separate creatures, and people who visit the Yard always catalogue them as separate creatures:

The **sweepers**, low friction-ridges of hardened cilia that drag fracture debris inward, tirelessly, all day, so that the floor stays cleared and the screed lines stay legible. The **screeders**, which lay the running bond, the herringbone in the north flats, the random rubble toward the slump country, and which cannot lay any other pattern, and which will lay their pattern over a corpse. The **encrusters**, which is the soil industry: shallow troughs of cured stone where mats break cured rock into fines and mix in their own dead tissue and produce, at a rate of a few liters a day, the only dirt on the plateau. The **bladder-sacks**, which carry that dirt, and which walk — genuinely walk, at the speed of a minute hand — by wetting one end and letting the swelling topple them forward, over and over, a hundred thousand small falls per meter traveled. The **mixer troughs**, which are stomachs, except that nothing here eats; they take in the failed and the fallen and hot water and give back mortar, warm, smelling of syrup and cut grass.

The cleared circle is the mat's catchment. It is also its drop zone. It is also its quarry floor. The extraordinary emptiness of the Yard, that one-per-horizon loneliness, is the water budget of these sheets: two mats cannot drink from one lens, so a mat is a lens plus a hectare-scale floor plus one enormous temporary erection, and *that* is the individual. Call it a holding. There are forty holdings in view and you are standing in an empty room the size of a county because the room is forty bodies with all their tissue spread out flat and thin and mostly underground.

Except the mats interdigitate at their borders. Except they graft, routinely, without ceremony, and a screeder lineage from the eastern slump country will be laying its rubble bond in a northern holding by the end of the month. Nothing here defends its body. Nothing here has a body to defend. The tissue is a season's tissue and it is going to be gravel anyway, and what passes between holdings is not substance.

It is measurement.

## Underneath, the ones that make the heat

The mountain is warm because it is full of yesterday.

Bury cured silica stone under a hundred meters of more cured silica stone, in a place where the water is hot and the drainage is rectilinear and everything above is being dissolved and re-laid, and you get strata that have been cooked dry. Quick strata. Bone-white, thirsty, and chemically impatient. When water finds them — and water always finds them, because the rain is the demolition crew and the demolition crew works from the top down — they slake, and slaking gives heat, and the heat drives the water deeper as vapor, and the vapor condenses further in and slakes something else.

The things that live down there are not bodies. They are fronts. A wetting front moving through quick stone is a lineage: it consumes nothing, it eats nothing, it *hydrates*, and it leaves behind it a void and a heat plume and a shell of hydrated rock that will hold for a while and then not hold. It has a rate, a direction, a preference for certain bond styles, a mortality, and daughters — every front branches. It is as alive as a fire is alive, which is to say more alive than you would like and less alive than you can argue with.

These are the residents that make the sound of the place. The steam vent standing straight up in still air is one of them arriving at the surface. The scarp that lets go without warning at an angle no weathered slope would hold is one of them having finished underneath. The crust that takes you down into a steam void is one of them having been at work for a month directly beneath your boot.

The whole visible labor of the Yard — the raising, loading, curing, quarrying, re-laying — is a surface responding to being undermined from within by its own past. There is no ground here. There is only the part of the digestion that has not been reached yet, and the mats screed it and call it floor.

## The specialists, and the smallest one, which gives the game away

**The mortar glands** are morning animals. They extrude warm mucilage into the joints in the dry hours and they cannot survive the afternoon rain, and they do not try; they are grown at dawn in their thousands and they are dissolved at three and their bodies go into the troughs and come back tomorrow as glue. A gland's entire life is eleven hours long and it spends all of it filling gaps.

**The fog cups** are cupped bracts along the cold upper arms, and in the pre-dawn they are the only thing on the plateau doing anything gentle. A ton of water down the cables before the sun burns the fog off; a soft continuous rushing inside a structure that otherwise only knocks and cracks.

**The guns** are aimed at catastrophe. The pod dries asymmetrically — the wet side stays slack, the dry side contracts — and the asymmetry points the barrel at humidity, and humidity means a fresh slump, hot mud, a place where the ground has just failed. So the plain is sown by artillery that fires at disaster, because disaster is the only soil. The report arrives in your sternum a quarter kilometer before the seed does. The seed is a dart of cured stone with a hydrated tail and it drills.

What the seed carries is not a plan. It is a *unit*: a hardened reference length in its axis, the first member it grows, from which everything afterward is gauged. A ruler with a growth habit.

**The seam-keepers** live in the joints. They are the smallest residents and the most numerous and you will not see them; they are films, threads, a wet nap of tissue in the mortar line between two cured stones. The mortar dissolves a few percent every rain. The seam-keeper re-extrudes it. What it maintains, therefore, is not the stone on either side — the stone is quarry, the stone is going — but the *width of the gap*. It keeps a distance. It has kept that distance through eleven stones and four towers and a scarp collapse, and the distance is the thing that has a lineage, and the stones are consumables.

Once you have seen that, you cannot stop seeing it. The whole plateau is keeping gaps.

## There is no hunger here, but there is theft

Nothing eats. Nothing rots. But there are three currencies — water, preload, and measurement — and all three can be stolen.

**Water thieves** are mats that grow a tapping root into a neighbor's conduit and take the fog harvest on its way down. There is no defense, no immune response, no recognition; there is only plumbing, and a lens with three thieves in it is a lens that runs down early, and the tower uproots and walks, and the thieves walk with it or don't.

**Preload thieves** anchor their stays into somebody else's cured member. This is the elegant crime of the Yard: you get your structure pre-stressed for free, because a stranger is carrying your weight. The stranger's joints dissolve at the ordinary rate under an extraordinary load, and the stranger fails first, and the thief's arm comes down with it, and the mats quarry both without distinction.

**Squatters** keep their shapes inside other holdings' kept rooms. They pay nothing for the surveying. They are the most successful things on the plateau and the shortest-lived, because a lens full of squatters has no redundancy in it anywhere, and the first big rain takes the whole holding down in one rolling series of failures that is still going at midnight.

Cheating here is a way of being briefly enormous. It is not punished. It is simply *counted*, by water, in the only ledger there is.

## What is actually inherited

Here is the equipment of the Yard's memory, and none of it is tissue:

A plumb bundle gives vertical. A standing water column gives level. A standard stay, tensioned to the note it was gauged to, gives length. The eleven-day cure gives time. The seed's axis gives the unit. And the mats copy.

They copy by growing a member until its note matches a neighbor's note. That is all surveying is here: an act of matching pitches, performed by growth, in warm stone, in the rain. It is astonishingly accurate and it drifts by a few millimeters a generation, and the drift is *heritable*, because the next copy is made from the copy.

So the doorway on the western flats — frame, lintel, threshold, no wall, never a wall — is not an artifact that has been maintained. It is an organism that has been *repeated*. When the slump country encroached and the frame was cut out whole and carried two hundred meters east and re-set on new hardstanding, that was not preservation and it was not rescue. That was reproduction. The old frame is gravel by now. The daughter has a sill four millimeters higher than its parent, and its daughters will be higher still, and there is a clade out past the eastern slump that lost the threshold entirely nine hundred generations ago and has been getting along fine.

The residents of the Yard, then:

**The Doorway.** Western flats, the oldest lineage anyone has been able to trace, which is not saying much, since tracing here means counting copies. Roughly a lintel you would duck under. Reproduces by relocation, one daughter per move, occasionally two. Cheap: a doorway costs almost no water to hold open, which is why it has outlived every tower that has ever stood beside it.

**The Nine-Turn Stair.** Northern scarps. A helix around a column of empty air, nine meters, stopping. The kept thing is the column, not the treads; the treads are formwork and are replaced constantly. Comes in two handednesses which never mix and which fail differently when the scarp goes — left-handed stairs survive a slump about twice as often, for reasons of drainage nobody has bothered to work out. There are ten-turn variants. Most of them are too expensive and are quietly not re-cut.

**The Room.** The commonest resident. Rectangular volumes of clear space, sharply bounded, re-cut after every rain. Sizes cluster into recognizable species; there is a small square one that nests inside larger ones, and a long low one that has been shrinking for a very long time and is now barely a slot, and a broad one that keeps expanding until it exceeds its lens's budget and dies of ambition. Rooms are where the variation comes from. Any bounded clear volume that survives a rain gets adopted: a cavity left by a slump, the bay under a fallen arm, a steam void with a crust over it. The Yard's mutations are *events*. Its selection is a water bill.

**The Long Straight.** A drainage channel kept rectilinear kilometers beyond any hydraulic need, meandering every season and cut straight again. The only resident that touches many holdings at once, and therefore the road along which shapes travel. If you want to know how a northern stair's radius got into an eastern room, walk the straight.

**The Socket.** A volume held open *inside* a gantry's mass, into which nothing is ever put. The most expensive resident on the plateau: it costs a tower per generation, because the tower is built around it and dies for it, and when the tower comes down the mats quarry everything and carry away nothing but a set of lengths, re-grown as gauges in the next holding over. A socket has no body at all. It has a budget, a note, and a habit of being re-established.

## Where the resident actually is

Ask where a doorway sleeps at night and the answer is: in the tension of ten thousand bracts, distributed across a mat that will be gravel in a season, in a set of pitches that any neighboring mat can match.

Ask how old it is and the answer is: nothing on this plateau is older than a season, and the doorway may be four hundred thousand seasons old, and no part of it has ever existed.

Its metabolism is rain. Rain erases; holding a shape open against erasure burns water and heat and labor; the cost is paid in stone, laid down and cured and broken and re-laid, and the residue of that payment compacts and cements and lithifies. The massif is not the organism. The massif is what the remembering costs. It is tailings. It is a hand's breadth a year of spent formwork, and it is warm because it is full of the past being slaked, and the things that live on it are shapes made of nothing, and they are the only durable objects in a world where every solid is a consumable.

Do not go looking for who the rooms are for. That question has no purchase here. There is no arrival pending, no departure recorded, no occupant, no watcher, no intention anywhere in the sound. Keeping is not a means to anything on this plateau. Keeping is the metabolism. A thing that holds a gap open holds it open, and the holding propagates because holding is cheap and being copied is free, and the mountain rises underneath as the exhaust of that tautology.

## You, standing in one of the kept rooms

It is warm. It is loud. The dripping is sounding the walls a hundred meters away and the note is good and nothing anywhere is listening to it in the sense you mean.

You fit, or you don't. If the lintel is low you duck, and the ducking means nothing, and the fact that a doorway on the western flats is exactly the width of your shoulders is a fact about doorways and not about you.

What you are, here, is a load, a shadow that slows a cure rate by a few percent on the stone beneath you, and an obstruction. The mats screed around your boots. Stand still for an afternoon and there will be a raised lip around you, a clean bounded volume, sharply kept.

That is how it starts. That is how all of them started. A bounded clear space that survives one rain gets re-cut, and re-cut, and copied by pitch into the next holding, and drifts, and enters a lineage, and in a hundred years there may be a frame standing alone on the western flats with your shoulder width and a sill four millimeters high, maintained, defended, carried east when the slump comes, with no wall on either side and nothing beyond it but more floor.

It will not commemorate you. Nothing here has ever commemorated anything. It will simply be the shape you left, still being held open, at the ordinary cost, in warm stone, by things that cannot see and have never once been surprised.

Put your hand into the middle of the room. There is nothing there to be warm.
