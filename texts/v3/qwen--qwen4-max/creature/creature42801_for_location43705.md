## The Dwellers

Begin with the feet, because that is where a person is.

The soles are thick as kiln-glove, splayed, wider than the hand, and they are reading all the time — the tack of a fresh course, the sulk of a bad one, the four-to-the-breath coming up through the fill from the next gallery over. A Long Fill woman standing at her own door knows that a gang has started work at the Ninth Elbow, that a windlass bearing has gone dry two tiers down, and that her son is coming home, because he limps and the floor says so before the wall does. Her thumbs are smooth. Forty years of pressing clay have worn the ridges off them; she leaves blank prints, two little polished ovals, and so does everyone she works with, and so the older you are the less your hands say. Children's prints are legible as signatures. Old people sign nothing. They press the ground and it takes their shape and gives back no evidence at all.

Which is why nobody here is named by a face or a mark. A person is a rhythm with hands attached.

---

Households are chords. Three notes, five, seven if the family is old and quarrelsome — struck on the door-frame with the knuckle, the flat of the palm, the heel, each surface a different voice. You learn your household's chord before you learn to charge your tile. Marriage is not a ceremony but an arithmetic: two chords argued over for a season, a note dropped, a note borrowed, until the gallery gets used to the new sound. A birth adds a note. A death takes one away, and the household goes on knocking the gap — the rest where the note was — for as long as anyone remembers to. That rest is the dead person's name now. You cannot say it. You can only leave it.

The listening brick sits in a niche by the door, a plain fired block, and it holds the chord as it was knocked in the year it was laid. Once a month someone puts their mouth near it and knocks the pattern into it again, feeding it, the way you hold a tile against the kiln. And here is the thing that the keepers know and don't discuss with strangers: the brick does not store your knock. It stores the average. Every recharge lays your version over your mother's over hers, and the wall smears them together, and after two hundred years what comes out of the niche is nobody's chord — it is the family's chord, a composite animal made of everyone who ever imitated it, drifting steadily toward the living. Great-grandmother's tempo has become the Long Fill's tempo. Her scrape has become the scrape of a door that was hung last spring. The dead are not preserved here. They are slowly, gently overwritten, and the overwrite is done by love, and there is no way to stop it except to stop knocking.

Some families stop knocking. They let the brick run down. They call it letting her go, and the neighbors call it a scandal, and both are right.

---

The trades make bodies, and the bodies are the clearest thing to look at in the green-black shine.

The **soles** — the deaf guild — are compact, heavy-shouldered, with the calm of people who are never interrupted. They are courted. Deafness runs in certain households the way a good ear runs elsewhere, and a soles family with three deaf daughters can name its terms to any district. They read a course through their feet and condemn it three days before it slumps, and they are paid to walk: to stroll a gallery with their hands behind their backs, feeling for the slack place, the hollow that has begun to drink water. They speak with their palms on your sternum, which is the most private way to talk in the basin, and lovers who can hear still use it, because the roar cannot get in.

The **frosted** come out of the flues white to the eyelash, and cough white, and are paid four times a tamper's wage and spend it in a week. They marry at nineteen. They are the only people who buy other people's labor — a frosted man will hire a gang to tamp his mother's floor for a year, as a gift, and everyone will say what a good son, and everyone will count the months he has left. Their lungs are full of the pale wool and it does not come out. There is a thing the soles report about them, quietly, and only to each other: near the end, a frosted man stops reading on the floor. His feet go down and the ground does not answer. He walks without sounding. He has been eaten a little, from the inside, by the same appetite that grows the hanks in the flue, and the last thing to go is his noise.

The **millers** run the aggregate. They are the stomach. Everything goes to them: slump, clinker, the fired ribs of dismantled stairs, the dead. They are the only people who handle the past as a material, and they have a rule and a shelf. The rule is that anything harder than the mill goes back to the district it came from. The shelf is what has come out of the fill in their own lifetimes and been kept: copper pins green as pond-scum, a plummet, a child's charge-tile still holding a little of a firing that happened before the shelf existed, a knuckle-hinge with the grease still on it, a name-brick with the date gone soft. It is the only museum in the world and it is nine feet long and the millers dust it with their sleeves.

And the millers do the other thing, the thing nobody asks about at a wedding. When a wall goes bad and gives back screaming, you cannot un-know it and you cannot patch it. You take it down and you mill it. Ground fine enough and mixed wide enough, a scream becomes a district's floor at a volume below hearing — spread out, diluted, walked on. The Long Fill's fourth gallery is made of a bad stairwell from the year of the double slump. Everyone tamps it, everyone sleeps on it, no one hears a thing. The soles will not work it. They say it is loud underfoot. They say it is like standing on a mouth.

---

Children are issued a tile at six and a note at birth and nothing else, and they make the rest up.

They play tempo games — one child knocks a district's beat and the others have to walk it, and the loser is the one who drifts into the Shellcourse's limping five. They dare each other to knock strangers' chords, which is the great crime of childhood, and the punishment is to stand in the gallery while the household you insulted knocks your own note back at you, wrong, mocking, four galleries wide. They net crabs at the ponds and boil them in kiln-mouth ash and eat them off a fired thumb-shaped dish that every child has and every adult keeps.

At the Shellcourse guild hall there is a glazed bowl with a blind crab in it the size of a walnut, sixty-one years old, fed on violet mat by whoever is passing. It is called the Grandmother. You touch the bowl before an examination. Nobody claims to believe this.

And out at the margin, where the fill is raw and the tamp crews are three days behind, the children have a new game and it has spread inward faster than any tempo ever did. They take their charge-tiles, held edge-on, and they cut little channels in the fresh course and let the seep-water run in them, and they watch the water sort the grit — coarse at the top, fine at the bend — and then a gang comes and stamps it flat, and the next cool they cut it again. They are not making anything. That is the whole point of the game. They are eleven years old and they have discovered that if you do nothing to the world, the world does something.

---

Now the quiet, which lives here too, and which is the only inhabitant nobody has ever seen.

Three in a lifetime, in forty thousand. A moving oval of subtraction. Stand still, hold hands, don't make a sound; it takes the ones who keep talking, and it goes to the source of the sound, and afterward the listening bricks in its path are dead — not quiet, dead, giving back nothing ever again, so that a family loses its composite grandmother a second time and has to bury the niche.

The soles are the only ones who have been inside one and come out, because they do not talk, and because their hands were on each other's sternums already. They have been asked ten thousand times and their account has not changed in six generations, and it is not an account of an animal.

They say the floor goes slack. An oval of ground, moving, that has been *un-tamped* — soft as a fresh course, soft as porridge, and reading nothing back, no gang, no windlass, no district. They say it is like putting your feet on a tongue. And they say that after it has passed, the floor is harder than before. Firmer. Better. They have tested it with plummets and with their own soles and they will not lie about a course: it is the soundest ground in the basin, and it is where the graves have gone silent, and both facts arrive together.

From which the soles draw the conclusion they draw, and the tuners dispute it, and the millers say nothing at all.

Eleven hundred years of thud-thud. The loudest, most repeated, most patient knock in the world, soaked into every wall in the basin, deeper than any family chord, deeper than any scream. The walls store a knock the way a tile stores light. Nobody has ever asked what happens when a wall has been charged with the same blow, four to the breath, for eleven centuries, and is finally full.

The slub blooms thickest in the flues that roar loudest. The harvesters know a flue is ripe by ear. Something in this place is feeding on the noise and making fiber out of it, and something else is feeding on the noise and making ground out of it, and the frosted go quiet before they die, and the quiet leaves the ground well-tamped, and the soles say: it is not a beast. It is the work. It is our own labor, stored past capacity, come loose and walking, and it is still doing the only thing it knows.

There are people who follow it. They go in after it has passed and stand in the dead patch with their eyes shut and their ears ringing, on the best ground in the world, in the only silence anyone here has ever stood inside. They are called the aftermen and they are spat at in the galleries, because they are standing on other people's second graves and enjoying it, and because when they come back they cannot stop saying how quiet it was, and how firm, and how they would like to go again.

---

In the sump under the Low Fill there is something long.

Four generations of pump-crews have heard it against the intake screens: a slow drag, a double knock, a length of time. There is a song. The song has ninety-one verses and eleven sightings, and the verses are added by the crews and the sightings are inherited, and the newest verses are about the crews' own grandparents, so that the animal is slowly becoming a family history with something long at the bottom of it.

The crews knock on the intake pipe. Something knocks back, three taps and a scrape, and half of them say that is the animal and half say that is the ring, that the pipe is old and the wall behind it is older and you are hearing your own great-grandfather's hand come back up through the water. They have been arguing about this longer than the Shellcourse has been arguing about the gully. They are, in fact, the same argument, and neither party knows it: whether the world contains anything that is not just the world, returning.

---

Out past the Far Courses, on bare armature in a foot of black water, under actual sky, there are people the basin has stopped counting.

The unknocked. Chord-thieves, mostly, and their children, and the ones who could not bear a listening brick — households that struck a note out and made a person into a wrong note everywhere, so that the only place to live was where there are no doors. They have no chords. They knock nothing. They eat raw mat and rain and they are dark in a way no one inside is dark, because there is no kiln to charge a tile against and their tiles have been dead for years, and so they are the only people in the world who have seen a horizon, and they cannot describe it, and they have stopped trying.

They do not tamp. Their platform is sinking and they let it sink and move outward along the ribs. And where the rain has cut the gully they have set out no stakes and made no survey; they sit on the slumped bank with their feet in the channel and watch the clinker sort itself, coarse upstream, fine at the bend, and they have names for the gravel bar's two ends, and the names are not knocks, they are words, said out loud in a place where nothing stores them.

There are crabs in the gully. Nobody put them there. They are smaller than the pond crabs and they are not the same, and if you hold one against the rain-colored light there are two black specks at the front of it that the pond crabs do not have. There is a violet mat in the shallows that no one farms and that the pond folk will not touch. Something arrives with the rain from wherever the rain is from. It has been arriving for eleven years. The surveyors have staked the channel and not the crabs.

And the argument in the Shellcourse is eleven years old, and the woman who has spoken longest against filling it was six when the stakes went in, and her mother spoke for filling, and the gully has deepened two feet, and there are children now who have never stood inside the lid, who come back in from the margin with their mouths tasting of unmetered water and their eyes wrong for the shine, and who stand in the gallery in the cool listening to the pans play the district, and who cannot say why they are crying, and who will be the ones to decide.

---

Come back to the woman with the smooth thumbs.

She is on the gang, third rammer, four to the breath, and the course is nine hours old and tacky under her feet. Between strokes she taps the handle against the gallery wall — three taps and a scrape, her mother's chord, her mother's rest — and she does not look at the wall, and she does not stop working, and a half-beat late the wall gives it back to her, a little wrong, a little more like her than it was last year.

Above her the kilns open and the light comes up orange along ten thousand prints. She presses her blank thumbs into the fresh clay to smooth a seam, because that is the last thing you do to a course before the next gang tamps it flat, and the print she leaves says nothing, and it will be gone by morning, and the ground will be a hand's breadth lower by this time next year, and she will be here, doing this, and the wall will be learning her.
