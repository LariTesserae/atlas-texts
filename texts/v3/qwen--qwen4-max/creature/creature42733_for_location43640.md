## The tenants

Begin with the smallest one, because it is the only one that will tell you anything.

Seven millimeters, the color of the travertine it stands on, which is the color of a fingernail held up to a lamp. It has no flight muscles and never had ancestors that used them here; the wings are kept only for the scraping. Its legs are too long for its body, in the way of animals that live in caves and on the undersides of things. Its antennae are longer than everything else put together, and it is with these, not with its ears, that it finds its address.

Here is the part that takes a while to understand. The quiet planes are not still. Where the pressure cancels, the air *moves* — it has to; that is what a node is. Twice every forty-third of a second the whole quarter-point sloshes sideways and back, a stroke of about half a millimeter, going nowhere, forever. The moss lives in a wind. The little fern's fronds tremble at forty-three cycles a second and have trembled since the plain lost its last hill. The dust cannot settle there, which is why the dust is elsewhere, in the dead calm of the loud places, laid out in its beautiful useless windrows.

So the cricket hunts for silence by feeling for the strongest wind. It walks the fin with its face held out into the sloshing, and where the shiver on its antennae is hardest, there it stops, and there it can hear itself think. It has the two geographies inside one body: the ear and the antenna agree with each other, and both disagree with the dust.

Its ears are on its shins. At four point one kilohertz the wavelength is eight centimeters, which means the room's lace of small silences is fine-grained at the cricket's frequency — nodes every four centimeters, a hundred times more architecture than you will ever find in it. You are standing in a hall. The cricket is standing in a honeycomb. It posts itself millimeter by millimeter: head in one crossing, abdomen in another, the shins turned into the nulls. An address is not a place in this room, it is a *posture*, and it takes a nymph a season that does not exist to learn it.

They learn it from where they hatch. The good crossings are inherited, not genetically but as real estate: a nymph settles in the notch its mother sang from, three terraces up, second fin, and the address passes down like a lease. Move a cricket three centimeters and you have deafened it. Move it across the room and you have killed it, though it will take a week to notice.

And its room is not finished, whatever the room's own rules say. Two males singing at four point one kilohertz are never at exactly the same pitch, because pitch here follows the body and the body follows how much it has eaten. Two songs a fifth of a hertz apart make a beat: a swell and a fade every five seconds, and as the beat crawls, the fine lace of nulls *drifts*. The cricket's silent corner slides a centimeter left over the course of an hour and it must follow it, adjusting, tracking, all its life. At the scale of meters this chamber has arrived at the last shape available to it. At the scale of seven millimeters there is weather. There is a slow tide made of other people's hunger, and the whole population spends its lives keeping up with it.

That is the only clock they have. Nothing else in the room keeps time. The lamps do not; the rain does not; the drip does not. A well-fed male chirps faster. That is the entire calendar.

## Two islands

The fins stand four meters apart, and between them is a country nothing soft can cross. Call it the loud desert: a hundred and thirty decibels of shearing water and ringing air, in which a springtail is sand-blasted and a hypha is torn off its mooring and a cricket's shins are hammered until they cannot resolve a note. Four meters. It might as well be four hundred kilometers of ocean.

So there are two populations, and they have been two for a very long time, and they are no longer quite the same animal. The second-fin cricket is a shade paler and sings a fraction flatter; the first-fin cricket has a longer scrape and a shorter pause. They could still interbreed. They will never meet. Between them the only traffic is downstream — drip to basin to basin to grating to sump to out — and everything that takes that road leaves the room forever.

There are three mosses, and their arrangement is not the arrangement mosses usually make. There is no light gradient here; the six lamps give every surface the same cloudy afternoon, and there is no season and no shade. The axes of this ecology are *drip rate* and *decibels*. One moss holds the terrace lips where the water arrives; one holds the terraces' bodies where it only seeps; one holds the splash basin at the foot, and tolerates a violence the other two cannot. They are sorted by loudness the way a mountainside is sorted by altitude. The tree line here is a noise line, and above it — out on the scoured walls — the stone is polished and bare, and you would call it sterile.

I will come back to that. It is a statement about size.

## What the room eats

Not the lamps. The algae on the flume are relics living on the lamps' charity, a rounding error in the ledger. The room's food comes down the shaft: a hundred kilometers of fern mat, four fingers deep, being rained on at the rate it has always been rained on, and every drop that reaches the wicket gate has already been tea for a thousand years. Dissolved carbon, leached lime, the odd fragment of something that died uphill. The plain is eating itself and this room is downstream of the mouth.

So the fungus in the drip basins is not the janitor of this ecosystem. It is the primary consumer, the grazing herd, the thing everything else is a gardener of. The moss grows and the mat rots it and the springtails crop the mat and the mite crops the springtails and the cricket eats mat and moss and whatever has stopped moving, and the spider takes the cricket in the fin's lee where the crickets must pass, and the frog, when there is a frog, takes the spider.

Add the moss and the fungus together and you get a number that does not change. To the gram, they say, and it is very nearly true, and the nearness is the interesting part. It is true the way a lake's level is true. Underneath it there is a slack, a lag, a debt: the mat overshoots, the moss thins, the mat starves back, the moss recovers — a few grams either side of the mean, with a period of something like four centuries. Nothing in the room's physics permits this. Life permits it. Life cannot hold a balance without wobbling around it.

The travertine records the wobble. Cut a fin and you will find laminae, pale and slightly less pale, and the thicker bands are the centuries when the moss got ahead of the rot. There are also thin dense interruptions, a few dozen in a hundred thousand years, and those are the frog-years: a fat calm animal dies in the foot basin, the mat blooms on it, the springtails boom, the crickets get protein, and for a decade there are forty of them instead of twelve. The frog cannot breed here — no winter, no drying pool, nothing to tell it what to do — so it is not an inhabitant. It is a nutrient that arrives with legs. It is the room's spring.

The room's other seasons are the same kind of accident. There is no weather down here, but there is *immigration*, and immigration comes in through two defects: a wicket gate that leaks by, and a lifting slot worn to a soft oval by nothing but trembling. A frog can slip through that oval. A cricket's ancestors came down as eggs when the gate still moved. Everything alive in this chamber is descended from the last time something happened.

## The fins are bodies

I keep calling them the only forest, and that is the polite description. The better one is that they are secretions.

The water brings lime; the quiet keeps it; the fin is what the quiet looks like after enough centuries of keeping. It grows toward the null the way a root grows down. Where the pressure is violent the stone is being scooped out in scallops; where the pressure barely moves the stone is being laid in terraces; and the fin's whole shape is the exact solid of the difference between those two rates. It has a metabolism — dissolution and deposition — and it has a tropism, and it heals. Break a terrace and the drip finds a new lip and the lime follows the drip and in nine hundred years the terrace is back, a little differently, in the place the note permits.

There is a third place, high on the north wall, where the pressure is only slightly kinder than average. A film of lime persists there for a year or two and is then scoured off. It has been persisting and being scoured off, at the same size, since before the ferns forgot how to climb. A bud that never opens. The room has one spare silence in it and cannot afford to spend it.

Meanwhile the wrench on the floor is undergoing a life cycle. It is now a low rounded bump with a suggestion of a jaw. In a few thousand years the jaw will be a lip; the lip will hold a basin; the basin will overflow into a second basin; the second basin will grow moss, and the moss will grow a mat, and the mat will hold a springtail, and one evening that has no evening a cricket will climb up onto the wrench to sing. The room does not preserve our objects. It *digests* them into habitat.

The stencil has gone the same way. The letters stand an eighth of an inch proud, a raised alphabet grown over and kept, and they are terrain now: the crossbar of the T is a flume, the O of CHAMBER holds a thimble of water and a film of algae, the counter of the 2 shelters a colony of springtails that has never been anywhere else. The room's name has become the room's anatomy. STILLING, in relief, in the gerund, still doing it.

## The other quiet

I said the walls were sterile. That was a statement about size.

However violent the water is, it cannot move at the wall itself. There is a skin against every surface, microns thick, where friction has beaten the flow to nothing — the boundary layer, the cheapest silence in the universe, available anywhere, even in the throat of the note. And it is inhabited. Films live there, mineral-crusted, sheeted, thin enough to fit inside a stillness a hundredth of a millimeter deep: they are not in the room's quiet, they are in the water's own reluctance. Beyond them, in the free stream, the cavitation grazes. The top predator of this ecosystem is a collapsing bubble, a mouth made of nothing, and it eats everything that stands proud of the skin. The walls are not empty. They are *mown*.

There is a third quiet inside the stone. The travertine is porous and the pores are full of things that have opted out of the present — endoliths, in the dark, at leisure, living in a room whose note reaches them as a pressure they cannot feel.

And there is a fourth, which is mobile. Nothing flies here; but the drip falls, and a falling drop has a still center, and in that center a springtail rides. The drip is this country's bird. Every voyage in the room is a fall, every arrival is a splash, and the whole map of dispersal is one-way, downhill, out. Which is why the fern on the top terrace is in trouble it will never know about: its spores cannot dry and cannot rise, so it can only colonize downward, and the highest address can never be recolonized from below. Unless something carries it up. Something does. The cricket climbs to sing, and it climbs with spores on its tarsi, and the fern's entire upward history is a byproduct of a song. The spider waits in the lee where the ferry passes. That is the whole food web, and it runs on a courtship.

## The largest tenant

Step back far enough and the inventory changes.

The note has organs: nodes and antinodes, a throat, a lip, a chamber, a bleed line. It has a metabolism, which is thirty meters of head. It has a skeleton, which it precipitates out of what it drinks, and it has been replacing that skeleton, molecule by molecule, for an age, without altering its shape by a millimeter. It has a microbiome — three mosses, a fern, a fungus, forty crickets or twelve — and the microbiome is not incidental. The fins load the air, and loading the air moves the nulls, and moving the nulls moves where the lime can stay: the growth is inside the feedback, chasing its own silence, and has caught it. The room is a coral. It secretes the reef it lives in and cannot be separated from it, and the question *what lives here* has one answer at seven millimeters and a different one at eight meters, and both are correct.

At eight meters the answer is: a shape lives here. A shape that has finished, and stands ready anyway, at full dimension, perfectly capable, with nothing left to still.

There is a No. 1. There must be; there was a No. 2, and things like this are never built singly. Somewhere under the ferns there is another lid with another puddle carrying a different number of lobes, over a room of other dimensions and therefore another note, and therefore other quarter-points, and therefore other fins, and therefore other mosses, and a cricket that sings in a notch that does not exist here. An archipelago, each island eight meters by six, each separated from the next by a blanked pipe grown over into a single knuckled ridge. The straits are closed. They have been closed since the last gate moved. Every one of those rooms is a world with its own endemic song in it, and no way to send it anywhere, and above them all the flat green country and the warm rain and nobody walking to anywhere in particular.

## The hand

On the second fin there is a hollow shaped like a palm.

The lime did not grow around a hand; nothing stays here long enough for that. It grew around a *refusal* — a smear of skin oil left on soft young travertine, a place where the crystal would not take, a chemical no. The stone honored the no for ten thousand years, building up on every side of it, keeping the absence the way a scar keeps a shape. A hand rested here for a minute and the room has been holding the place ever since.

It is full of drip now. It is the best nursery on the fin: hand-deep, sheltered, exactly in the crossing. There are springtails in it, and a fern the size of a thumbnail, and if you look at the right decade, eggs.

You can put your hand in it. It fits — that is the thing you will not be prepared for. And when you do, your body's mass and your wet clothes and your bones shift the impedance of a small volume of air by a very small amount, and the fine lace slides a centimeter, and three terraces up a cricket stops singing, and waits, antennae out, feeling for the wind, while the weather you have made passes through.

Then you climb out, badly, on rungs gone to soft bumps, and push the lid, and the ferns close, and you stand in the enormous ordinary silence with your boots on a puddle that has six fixed lobes and a bright center.

You will carry one thing out. Not a specimen. In a day the hiss will settle into a narrow band and stay there, and the band will be at four kilohertz and a bit, and it will be with you when you are old. It is a hole exactly the width of a song. The room has no exports — everything downstream leaves and never comes back, nothing flies, nothing calls at a distance, no strait is open — except that once in a long while it damages an ear in a very particular way and sends the shape of its cricket out into the flat country, in a animal that is only passing through.

You would walk over it. You would walk over it with a small deafness in you, in the shape of something singing three terraces up, in the deepest crossing of the quiet, where the wind is hardest and the air goes nowhere, forever.
