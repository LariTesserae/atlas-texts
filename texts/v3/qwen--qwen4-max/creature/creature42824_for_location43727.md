# Who Lives in the Lay

## I. Bodies

You can identify a Lay person upriver in three seconds, and it is never by their clothes.

It is the feet. They walk with a rolling, splayed, deliberate gait, weight going down through the whole sole at once, and they cannot stop doing it on pavement. They stand in doorways with their soles flat and their eyes half-closed, reading a floor that finished curing before their grandmother was born and has nothing to tell them. The boots they wear at home have soles of thin pressed felt, three millimeters, replaced every nine days; the wear pattern is a diagnostic document, and a layer's discarded insoles are worth stealing, because they say where he has been working and how good the ground was.

It is the hands. Even in sleep the fingers move a little. A Lay person listening to you is not looking at your face. They are looking at the space between your collarbone and your waist, and if your hands are in your pockets they will wait, patiently, for you to become intelligible.

It is the throat. They speak in a breath-register pitched just under the audible, and their vocal folds have adapted to it the way a swimmer's shoulders adapt. Upriver, ordering a meal, they are inaudible and know it, and they compensate by leaning in and over-articulating, which reads as either intimacy or menace. When they try to shout they produce a cracked, embarrassing noise, like a hinge. They are ashamed of it.

And they laugh without sound: mouth wide, head back, shoulders going, nothing coming out. Visitors find this the single most frightening thing in the Lay, more than the fog, more than the ground. The Lay finds the visitors' laughter frightening in turn — that bark, that sudden percussion of a body — and has to be told, again and again, that upriver it does not open a hole under anyone.

One more thing. Outdoors, on foot, a Lay person never stands still. They shift, they rock heel to toe, they take two steps and come back. Standing motionless on the flats means one of two things: you are listening to the stone, or you are about to scream. Both are private. Children are corrected for it before they are corrected for lying.

## II. Two names

Everyone has a hand-name and a sound-name.

The hand-name is the real one. It is a movement, usually two-part, made at the sternum, and it is given by whoever raises you. It cannot be said, only performed, and the address-clerks write it into the ledgers as a small drawing of a hand — a thumb hooked at the collarbone, two fingers walking down a wrist — so the registries of the Lay are books of little pictures that no upriver office has ever successfully indexed. In the ledgers such a woman is written *Hook*, which is not her name, and which she answers to, the way you answer to a number.

The sound-name is given at birth and is almost never used. It exists to be shouted, and there is nowhere in the Lay to shout it. So it lives in two places: in the floor, and out at sea.

In the floor, because the midwife murmurs it into the birth-tray while the grain is still taking, and eleven years later the slab is set into the threshold of the child's room, and on certain cold mornings it says the name, faintly, perfectly, in the mother's voice, forever.

Out at sea, because once a year — the ferrymen's great trade, the naming trips, cheap in autumn — you go out to the cured islands, and stand at the seaward lip past the vent towers where the gulls are, and the ferryman opens his name-book, and shouts your name at the water for a coin. Some people go every year. Some go once at nineteen and never again and say it was stupid. Some have never been and do not know what they are called out loud, and are careful never to be asked.

The ferrymen keep the books because the ferrymen are the only people who go both places. They are half-exiled, hoarse, and rich.

## III. The children of the fog

The inversion puts the fog line at about the chest of an adult. This is the most important fact about growing up here and no adult ever mentions it, because they have forgotten.

Adults live in sunlight. Children live in fog. A child of five walks all day in a warm grey medium smelling of chalk and moss, seeing knees, seeing the dripping undersides of pipe bridges, seeing hands. Faces are a thing that happens above the weather, occasionally, when someone bends down. Children read adults by their hands and their boots and the way their weight goes down, and they are unnervingly good at it, and they think a face is a kind of hat.

Their games are all silence games. Still-tag, where the loser is whoever makes the ground hear them. Breath-holding in the warm seams, sitting cross-legged in a horsetail stand with your mouth open, tasting the mineral. A game with no name that consists of standing on one foot on fresh lift for as long as you dare, which is the childhood rehearsal for the crime, and which every adult pretends not to have played.

They learn their coordinates the way other children learn a surname: *Ninth of Frost, east-northeast, four fingers a day*, chanted, and the rate is a class marker they understand by six. Four fingers is warm. Four fingers is a young quarter, a hot floor, a fog you can lean on, a family that has not been here long. One finger is cold and rime and piped heat and moss on every wall and a floor that talks. The warm poor live at the growing edge, where the ground is cheapest because it does not exist yet, and where the pipes are hottest, and where you cook dinner by burying it. The rich live in the finished core, and buy their warmth by the therm from the people who make it.

Birth happens offshore, in the crying houses: wooden, cheap, loud, gulls on the roof, hinges that squeak on purpose. Women go out for a month. The islands are cured stone and keep nothing, so a baby may scream there as much as it likes, and does, and the sound goes out over the water and is gone forever. Then the hush-nurses do their work — the moss swaddle, the breast at the first indrawn breath, the palm flat on the sternum — and the baby comes home quiet, and stays quiet, and the whole city is built on that training and nobody has ever found a way to say so out loud.

The ones it doesn't take stay out there. Island children. They come back at seven or ten or never, and they talk at full volume in the street, and everyone flinches, and they can see everyone flinch, and it does not stop them, and this is the loneliest thing in the Lay.

At home, the first cry is taken with a crying cone: a moss-packed horn, wide end over the mouth, narrow end at the tray, so the room is safe and the grain gets a whisper of it. So your first cry exists in exactly two forms — a murmur in a threshold slab, and a memory in a woman who is not a reliable medium and who will die.

The rich have solo trays. The poor have the common trays at the lying-house, forty births to a slab, and at eleven you kneel on the floor with your ear to the stone and hear a nursery of strangers, and your mother says *that one, that's you*, and she may be right.

## IV. The hands

The sign is not a translation of speech. It is the language; speech is the export version, used offshore and upriver and to dogs.

It has dialects by quarter and accents by trade. Layers sign low and compact, below the waist, because their hands are filthy and their shoulders are tired; vat people sign huge and slow, out of the habit of talking across a church-sized room of grain. There is a whole grammatical apparatus for rate and bearing that has no spoken equivalent at all — a classifier system for how fast a thing is becoming ground — and a Lay person can tell you, in one movement, that a channel is migrating east-northeast at a decelerating three fingers and that they think this is a lie told by the laying house. Upriver scholars have been trying to write it down for sixty years and have produced four incompatible dictionaries and one very good poem.

The deaf houses are the old families and the engineering guilds. Not by policy. By physics and by history: they were reading the thixotropy through their palms while the hearing were still shouting at it and dying, they built the sign, they built the jacks, they can work the noise mains and the vent towers without going strange, and they can put a hand flat on a voiced slab and read a woman laughing through the bones of the wrist. The best sound-surveyors in the Lay are deaf and always have been. A hearing surveyor uses a stethoscope and a chart and gets the patch boundaries wrong by a meter.

There are hearing people who learn sign at thirty because they married in, and they are fluent and clumsy forever, and their accent is described, kindly, as *loud*.

## V. Work, and being paid in acres

The gangs. Layers in splayed felt shoes, walking the fresh lifts in echelon, treading the lift to knit it, reading the cure through their soles; the treaders who diagnose; the jacks who lift a house a finger at a time on screw-piles and hydraulic feet, and who say *hold it, hold it, hold it* more often than any other phrase in the language, which will matter later. Laggers who wrap pipe against heat loss and who are covered to the elbow in fungal shelving by the end of a shift. Comb-keepers who climb the dew towers into the clean sun above the fog and come down wet. Mossers, who farm the sound-crop in deep felt beds and cut it in slabs and hang it on everything, and who are the second most powerful guild in the Lay, because they own silence.

And the vat-tenders, of whom more below.

Wages are paid in ground. Not in money that buys ground: in ground, fractional, future, at a bearing and a cure-date. A layer's wage is eleven hundredths of an acre that does not exist, delivered warm in nineteen years, if the channel behaves. So every working person in the Lay is a speculator in the thing they make with their hands, and every working person keeps a bearing-book, and every quarter has its acre-clubs — friendly societies pooling fractions, insuring each other against drift, meeting in moss-hung rooms to argue about the sea. The laying houses sell the same acres twice and have done for a century. There is a whole profession of people who work out which of two men owns a place that is currently water.

Food is cooked in the floor. Moss-wrapped, buried in a hot seam at the start of shift, dug at the end: elk, horsetail hearts, mudskipper paste, glasswort, the purple-stemmed thing that grows a hand a week and tastes of iron and pepper and is in everything, always, in every meal, forever, and which everyone is sick of and everyone eats. Seam-cooks are specialists in the cure-calendar; a four-finger ground steams differently from a one-finger ground, and an old cold quarter cannot cook at all and must buy heat. There is grit in the food. There is grit in the beds and the eyes and the teeth. The teeth matter: the bitten-brass halls, the bone-conduction music, six hundred people clamped to rods in absolute quiet, weeping in unison at a chord nobody hears. Professional listeners have worn molars. A worn bite-rod is an heirloom and a diagnosis.

## VI. The murmurers, the listeners, the attenders

Because the ground takes small steady sound and keeps it, there are trades.

**Murmurers** are hired to talk into fresh lift. Contracts, wills, boundaries, testimony. They work in pairs on rotation over a tray or a pad, reading aloud in a flat, unaccented, plosive-free register for six hours at a stretch, and they are paid by the fidelity of the cure. Their throats go. They have a guild and a hospital and a sign for *I have nothing left to say* which is also the sign for *retired*.

**The slow post.** You murmur a letter into a tray; the tray cures; in eleven years the slab is cut and set into somebody's floor and they hear you. Lovers do this. So do creditors. There are commissioning houses that will lay a message for a child's twenty-first year, and there are warehouses on the cold cured edge full of undeliverable slabs — recipients dead, addresses drifted, blocks slid — and in a north wind those warehouses mumble. Porters will not work them alone. It is not superstition; it is that you cannot tell how many voices there are.

**The deferred assize.** Serious cases are continued eleven years and heard on the paving. Perjury is nearly impossible and memory is nearly useless, and the entire legal profession has reorganized itself around the difference. A good lawyer does not argue; she lays.

**Listeners** patrol the fresh flats in the fog, and what they watch for is a person standing still. That is the whole training. A working body moves; a body that has stopped is either listening to the stone or has decided. Listeners carry no whistle and make no sound. They walk up and put a hand on the arm. Most of the time it is someone grieving, and they walk them out to the ferry. The Lay's suicide rate is not high. Its interception rate is enormous, and it is achieved entirely by people noticing that somebody has stopped shifting their weight.

**Attenders** are the ones who go afterward. When someone screams on fresh lift, the ground takes them, and there is no digging — digging is percussion — and eleven years later the street says it. The surveyors sell the hour. The family hires an attender, or attends itself, and someone stands on that slab in the cold at that hour so that the scream is heard by a person and not by a gutter.

Ondry has an appointment she inherited from her mother: a slab in the Ninth of Frost, laid the year of the Bell Riot, 6:40 on the first hard frost, a man she never met. She has stood it thirty-one times. It has spoken nine. The rule of the trade is that you do not answer, and the reason for the rule is not manners. Cured stone gives and cannot take. Fresh takes and cannot give. The medium runs one way. Everything the ground says to you, it says without any possibility of reply, and the attenders' whole discipline is standing in front of that with your hands at your sides.

The kept holes are related. Where somebody went down, you do not dig, and you do not build. There are hundreds of them, small, mossed over, marked by nothing but the fact that the moss is different. Children know every one.

## VII. The loud islands

Out past the last gantry, on cured stone that finished long ago, the vent towers stand and mutter the city's unavoidable clamor seaward. Gulls nest so thick the rock is white and moving. Around them: the crying houses, the bell towers (bells are illegal in the Lay and there are eleven of them out here), the howl wharves, the wooden towns with dogs that bark.

The howl ferries run at dawn. For a coin you stand at the seaward lip in a row of forty people and scream at the sea for as long as you can, and the gulls go up, and it is the most ordinary thing in the world. Bereaved people go out for a week. Women go out after a stillbirth. Children are taken out on their birthdays to be shouted at. There is a bench where you sit afterward, and a woman who sells hot purple-stem soup, and nobody speaks to you unless you speak first.

The permanent islanders are vent-wardens, gull-keepers, bell-ringers, main-cleaners, ferrymen, and the island children who never came back. They are loud, wooden, poor, and considered savages by both the Lay and the upriver towns, which is the only opinion the Lay and the upriver towns share. Their sign is enormous and coarse and full of jokes that cannot be made quietly. Many of them are deaf by trade and by choice, and a hearing islander who has worked the mains for twenty years has ears like a fired brick and a reputation for fearlessness that is really just an inability to hear the thing that would frighten him.

Here is the joke that the Lay tells itself, in hands, at arm's length, in the fog:

The loud places keep nothing. The silent places keep everything. You can howl out there until your throat bleeds and the stone will not remember one second of it. You can whisper *hold it* over a tray in a shed and the street will still be saying it when your grandchildren are cold.

## VIII. The neighbors

The **silt-crabs** carry grain-cement on their claws and lay ground, badly, for free, at a rate of about an acre a year across the whole plain. *Cobb v. the Laying Houses* has been running for sixty-one years: are they machinery, livestock, or members? The laying houses say machinery. The layers' guild says members, and has admitted four hundred thousand of them, and cannot explain how the dues would be collected. In the meantime the crabs are *kept*: cement ration, shed, and a shed-shaped cast when they die, and the stone of the Lay is full of crab-shaped voids that surveyors read like tree rings.

The **mudskippers** till. Millions on the fresh lifts, aerating, and no lift treads properly without them, and the layers walk around them.

The **felt-moths** are farmed on the shouters' return pipes, where the heat leaks and the noise is bearable, for their wing-scales, which absorb sound. Moth cloth is the second upholstery of the Lay, under the moss. The farmers clip and release; the moths live nine days after and cluster on the warm walls in drifts a hand deep, and the children stroke them, and the drifts sigh when they move, which is the loudest thing an animal is permitted to do here.

The **owls** are the survey instrument. Grey, huge-eyed, voiceless, on every ridge, so many at dusk the rooflines look crenellated. They hunt by ear in the fog, and they also stand on paving with their heads tilted at nothing, listening to stone that is speaking below human threshold. Every sound-surveyor in the Lay follows owls. So does every illegal cutter. An owl staring at the ground in a north wind is worth money, and there are men who walk the cold quarters at dawn with a bird on their wrist and a felt saw in a bag.

The **stilt-elk** are herded by people who have gone half-feral at the edge: splayed shoes, false ears on a headband, throat painted in the bare-skin colors the elk use at rut. A herder stands in the fog in a fog of her own breath and conducts an argument in pigment and ear-angle, and she must not laugh, and she cannot laugh, and she has not made a sound at work in nineteen years. Herders go out onto the flocculating blooms where nobody else will walk. They come back with things to report and no reliable way to report them.

Also: the **hush-dogs**, bred over eight generations for a growl below the danger register, who speak in tail and lean and eyebrow, and who are the only animals in the Lay that look people in the face. When one dies, the family cuts the print of it out of the pad — they always sleep in the same place, the pad always takes them — and keeps it on a shelf, a perfect grey cast of a sleeping dog, bolt-holes and spilled oil and all.

And the **quiet midges**, which have solved the same problem as everything else and are therefore unhuntable by owls, and which bite, in silence, in the warm seams, all year, without season. Everyone's ankles are scarred. It is the one thing about the Lay that nobody has made beautiful.

## IX. The seed, and the moss

Now the actual resident.

The grain is a diatom-thing, cultured in vats the size of churches, and it precipitates a pale cement out of the water and glues particle to particle, and everything else here — the elk, the gangs, the ferrymen, the attenders — is traffic in its metabolism. It has no nervous system. It has a growth habit: it builds its frustule around whatever pressure it is under while it builds. That is all the memory is. The stone's voice is an accident of architecture, a shell that grew around a pressure pattern and, a decade later, when the cure completes deep down and the last bound water goes, gives the pattern back. The city's entire relationship with its dead is a side effect of how a microbe makes its house.

The vat-tenders know it as an animal with moods. Strains go slow, go loud, go bitter, sulk in a cold spring, take a fever and lay a whole quarter that will never speak clearly. There are lineages with names and pedigrees and a liturgy of transfer — the Ninth Vat has been decanted continuously for a hundred and forty years, and the woman who tends it is called the grain-mother, and she is not being poetic; she signs *mother* and means it in the ordinary way, and she has a shift schedule and a bad hip.

The laying houses breed for fidelity. This is not in the brochures either. High-fidelity strains go to the core, to the rime houses, to the floors that will say a mother's voice cleanly on a cold morning for two hundred years. Cheap strains mumble. The warm poor at the growing edge are standing on ground that will spend the next century saying something almost, but not quite, like the people who made it.

And the moss eats the stone. Slowly, by acid, at a rate of about a fingernail's depth a decade — the sound-crop, the upholstery, the thing that makes silence possible, is digesting the thing that makes ground. So every mossed wall in the Lay must be re-laid eventually, and the two great guilds hate each other with a hatred that has no outlet, and the whole plain is a slow war between a diatom and a moss, and the human beings are what the war is fought with.

Out at the seaward edge, past the last gantry, the seed has escaped. Feral strains, flocculating in the grey blooms, laying ground nobody ordered at the pace of a stain on cloth. It incorporates whatever is there: the bore, the gulls, the tide. Unauthorized acres saying the sea.

There are people who walk out and stand on it. Wild listeners. They believe the feral grain takes and gives at once, that it is not one-way out there, that you can answer. This is a heresy, and it is spreading, quietly, in the fog, among the warm poor and the island children and the herders, and there is no way to argue with it, because the evidence is eleven years away and out past the last gantry where the ground is not yet a fact.

## X. 6:40, first hard frost

Ordinary daylight, coming. The sun a plain cold coin over the sea, the fog chest-high and warm, the dew-combs ticking their kilometer of falling water, ten thousand roofs edge to edge, the owls going home along the ridges with their heads full of other people's mornings.

Ondry is on her slab with her hands at her sides and her weight going heel to toe, heel to toe, because she is a Lay person and cannot help it. Above her the sun is on her face. Her hands are in the fog.

The paving says a cart. It says three notes of a bitten-brass tune, badly played, somebody's shift-break forty years ago. It says a dog. It says, from four directions at once, in four voices, the most common sentence in the country: *hold it, hold it, hold it.*

Then it says the man.

She does not answer. There is no answering. At 6:52 the gang comes down the street in their splayed shoes, moths in the fog around their knees, and the jacks begin to tick, and the street rises a finger, and she steps off onto the moss and walks home over her mother's threshold, which says her name.
