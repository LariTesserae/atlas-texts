You will take them for a crew. Nine terraces, and on every terrace somebody is at work: a figure on a reed platform with a hod at their knee, a figure crouched at a sluice lip dressing a joint that is already dressed, a figure standing in the blue-dark of a fig court doing nothing at all with great concentration. Bone goggles, slit-cut. Cord wound round the hands. Lime to the elbow, and the lime is warm, and so are they.

They are one person. Not one kind of person — one person, laid repeatedly, the way the heron is laid. The thumb is the same thumb. The stance is the same stance, the small habitual turn of the left foot on a stair tread, the way of holding a pebble-burnisher between the second and third finger.

What differs is the firing. One came out with a hairline split from lip to collarbone. One has a double whorl on the right thumb, a doubling like a stutter in the clay. One's ear is short, as though the mould was not quite filled. These are their names. They answer to them, they are fond of them, they will show you: *look, this is mine.* A flaw is the only thing anybody here owns.

---

Nothing they do leaves a mark. Watch a whole shift and you will see a course laid, and at the end of the shift the course will be where it has always been, and the mason will not be able to tell you what they did. There is no memory of accomplishment in the basin because accomplishment does not occur. The stepped rag at the day's end is not a record of progress. It is a calendar with one date on it.

And still they work, in shifts, seriously, with the cord half-tied and the loop open at the height of a hand.

Because unfinishedness here is not a condition. It is an activity. It has to be done. A thing left alone does not stay new; a thing left alone gets closed. They learned this on the fifth terrace, where there is a bay of wall whose chisel-dressing is faint — the fan-marks blurred, the arris gone slightly kind. It is the only soft place in the world. They call it the Rest. Somebody sat down there and stopped, for an hour, for a morning, and the joint began to heal like a wound, and a heron somewhere lost its chip and got it back differently, and the total came right in a way nobody was shown.

They take visitors to see the Rest. It is the closest thing here to a ruin, and the closest thing to a warning, and they cannot tell you which.

---

The cord on their hands is not protection. Their hands do not need protecting; nothing here can cut them, though everything here looks as though it could. The cord is to keep their skin off the work. To touch an edge bare-handed is to take some of its edge. Wear is not forbidden in this basin. It is redirected.

So they handle the world through hemp and matting and fired bone, and they are careful, and the carefulness is a whole etiquette. You do not lean on a pier with your palm. You do not put your cheek against a coping stone however much the glare makes you want to. Lovers walk in each other's shadows, which cool nothing, which do nothing, which is the point — a gift with no use in it, the only kind that cannot be spent.

And when they mean it, they take the gloves off.

A caress is a transfer. That is what the gesture means here, and everyone knows what it means, and it is not done lightly or often: *I will take your sharpness. Give me your arris. I will be the one who goes soft.* Then two people stand in a green room with the frogs' throats working and hold each other's faces in their bare hands, and something goes out of the world's edges and into them, and the walls stay crisp, and the moss keeps its seams, and the two of them are very slightly rounder than they were.

---

The ones who have done this most are called the Smooth, and they are the only inhabitants you can tell apart at a distance.

Their whorls are gone. Their faces have lost the fine grain of the firing and gone to a polish, like a step worn by feet, like the hollow in a trowel grip. One has no fingerprint at all and no lines in the palm and a face you cannot hold in your mind for more than a moment, and this blankness is not vacancy. It is a history. It is eleven figs eaten in a bad year, and a cord cut, and thirty years of holding somebody's hand. You read a Smooth the way you read a tally: by what is missing. They are the basin's ledger, walking around in reed sandals, and they are the only ones who can be missed, because they are the only ones who are anybody in particular.

They are also the only ones whose speech fades. Everyone else's ripple stands forever, combed and fixed. A Smooth lays a disturbance on the film and it spreads and thins and dies away, and that dying is the most valuable thing in the valley. People come from the ninth terrace to sit at a tank margin and watch a worn woman say something that ends.

They are not finished enough to die. That is the trouble. They go on smoothing, and there is no bottom to it, and no rest at the end.

---

The rest of the population is instruments, and they are content, or they are not anything.

Frogs in their thousands at the tank margins, leather-skinned against the lime, throats swelling and going slack, saying the one thing. Fish crossing the open plazas in the inch of film, brick-coloured, heavy with mucus, carrying it — this is why the pattern on the ninth terrace is the pattern on the first. The fish are the copying. The frogs are the choir. The whole water surface of the world is a mouth that has not closed on a word, and the word is not in any language they have, and every one of them has heard it all their life.

Snails carry the specification in their guts: lime, crushed pot, the ratio, the exact stiffness. Turn one over and you can read the repair. Masons keep them in a hip pouch and consult them the way you would consult a code, and the snails are holy and slow and eaten by nothing.

Wasps build the basin in miniature — nine shelves, courts, a sump — and leave one cell uncapped, and are orthodox, and are furious, and cannot sting a mark into anyone.

And the heron, in every tank, at the same offset from the inflow, is one attention distributed. They keep a vigil on it. Not on the bird: on the split from beak to shoulder, the firing flaw that ought to whistle when it breathes and does not. Someone is always listening for that whistle. It is the certificate. Flaw is not death here, the crack is not fatal, the world is still being made — and if the whistle ever comes, the joint has closed. The vigil has no liturgy. It is people taking turns to attend to a silence, and it is the nearest thing in the basin to prayer.

---

There are two opinions.

The figs are on one side. Eleven in the eastern court, always eleven, ripe and staying ripe, and ripeness that does not end is not a gift, it is a held note. They turn their leaves edge-on. They want to be taken. They want the fall and the wasp and the black smear on the brick and the smell of it. They are patient and they are eleven and they have been recruiting for as long as there has been anyone to recruit, and every eater they win is a Smooth in the making, and the Smooth want what the figs want: an ending, a softness, a joint that closes, a morning in which something has happened.

The others want the arris. They want the fan-marks in the spillway dressing and the dust of the tool's own making lying in the groove where the water has not taken it. They want the heron alive with its crack. They will tell you, reasonably, in ripple, that the price of a world where things can end is that everything ends, and that the fig is asking for a mercy it does not have to pay for, and that they are the ones who pay.

Both parties are right. Both parties wear cord gloves. They work the same shifts and show each other their flaws and take the gloves off for each other, and the argument has no forum because nothing here will carry a loud thing.

---

Down the sump, past where the light will follow, is the finished part.

They do not talk about it much. It is where wear is allowed: rounded arrises, talus at the wall feet, silt in the tanks, green and stain and slumping, echo, and the dead. Things down there can be heard. That is what the masons at the sump-head believe, and they believe it the way you believe in a country you have not visited.

The Smooth go down when they are nearly blank. Nobody stops them; nobody escorts them; there is a moment at the lip where the cord gloves come off and are left folded on the corbel, and then the square throat takes them. And the clay comes up already wedged, warm, the right stiffness, in blocks the size a person can carry.

At certain hours — they will not say which, and it may not be hours — a smell comes up out of the throat. Sweet, thick, vegetable, wrong. It is the only smell in the basin that has a direction. They stand in it with their goggles pushed up on their foreheads and their eyes streaming in the glare, and they do not speak, and afterwards they go back to work.

The trowel in the roll on the top platform came up that way, with thirty years already in the grip. The open loop of cord on the third scaffold is held at the height of a hand that went down there. There is one absence in this valley and it appears in several places.

---

Out past the far rim, in the grid of stacks, are the Unlaid.

Bricks in courses on the flat, drying, each with the thumb in it, and there are exactly as many as there are laid bricks in the basin, and every mason has one out there. They know which. They can feel it drying. It is not a soul and not a twin and not a promise; it is the other half of a thing whose two halves are the same half, and it faces inward, and it is warm, and it waits with the enormous patience of material.

You cannot walk out to yours. The horizon keeps its distance from wherever you stand on the rim, and this is not a trick of light. If you reached the stacks you would have to lay them.

Something lives in the gap at the head of the last stair, too. It is the size and shape of a brick and it is not a brick. The bed is buttered for it, the line is stretched to it, the mortar has been waiting for it with its heat and its slickness and its beads of sweat. It is the hand not closing. It has been there since the first day, which is the only day, and it is tired in a way that has no word in ripple.

---

None of them were born here. Nothing is born here; the only new thing that ever arrives is a crack.

Every one of them came down over the laid brick rim, one at a time, from somewhere they can no longer describe, and walked the hot shining floor between the green rooms, and learned to read a little of the water and then stopped wanting to, and was shown the Rest, and was taken to the top of the highest wall and handed the trowel whose hollow fit their hand exactly.

And they put it down.

That is the population. That is the whole census: the ones who put it down. The refusal is not a law imposed on them and not a heroism they perform. It is what they are instead of a biography. They keep the scaffolds up by working, and the lime slaking by not finishing, and the edges sharp by wearing in the world's place, and they do it forever, and their palms are polished, and the figs go on being ripe at them.

So they will not stop you. That is the last courtesy they have and they spend it on every arrival: they stand at their offsets in their slit-cut bone with their corded hands and let you decide, and the heron does not look up, and the frogs' throats swell and go slack, and the whole floor of the world holds the sky steady so you can see your own face while you do it.

Half of them want you to lay it. Half of them will never forgive you. It is the same half.

The brick is warm at your feet, and the thumb in it is yours, and when you pick it up you will feel, unmistakably, how much it wants to be laid — and that is the thing nobody warns you about, because it is not the world that is asking.
