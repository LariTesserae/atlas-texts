## The only cold in the world

You feel one before you see one, and what you feel is impossible.

Everything in the Sill is forty-one degrees. Everything. But a surface that is losing water is cooler than everything, by a little, for as long as the losing lasts — that is the whole of the exception to the first law, and it is the entire reason the country has inhabitants at all. A wet handprint on a milkstone wall is a cold spot. It is cold for about as long as it takes to say something you have no mouth to say. Then it is the same temperature as the wall, and the wall has no memory of it except the dent.

So a tender, moving through the Colonnade, is a person-shaped coolth going along at walking pace, and the air near them is a fraction less than the air anywhere else, and if they pass close behind you the hair on your neck registers a wrongness your body has no category for. In a place with no gradients, they are the gradient. They are the only thing in the Sill that is different from its surroundings.

They stop being different when they dry. A tender who has run out of water does not die; they simply become the temperature of the world, which here is what it means to not be anywhere.

Their greeting follows from this. A tender who meets you — and they almost never meet you — will lay a soaked palm flat on your breastbone and hold it there while you feel the cold come through your ribs. It is the only touch in the country that carries information. Everything else you touch is you, thermally speaking. They are giving you the one sensation the Sill cannot otherwise provide, and they are giving it away at a cost, because that water was going to be spent on their own skin, and now it is spent on yours.

## What they are shaped like

Thin. Not starved — *thin the way a radiator is thin*, all surface, no interior worth speaking of. Wrists you can close a finger and thumb around and feel the two bones click. A neck like a stem. A body built to be almost entirely outside.

Over that body, a cage: a light armature of green withy, bent and tied, that holds their wrapping a hand's width off the skin all the way around, so that air moves in the gap. The wrapping is leaf-cloth, split green and teased apart with thumbnails and beaten soft, never dried, never retted — there is no rot here to ret it with — and it is woven in pleats, dozens of them, deep flutes running from shoulder to ankle, so that a tender standing still looks ribbed. Fluted. Like a thing designed to shed heat, which is exactly what they are.

They look, in other words, precisely like the stilt-browser's chest. Ask about this, in the way one asks with hands, and a tender will show you the browser and then show you their own cage and then make a small gesture, palms level, that means *the same*. They will not tell you which came first. They do not appear to think it is a question. Nothing here evolves — evolution is a machine that runs on death, and death is not installed — so nothing in the Sill has been shaped by anything except intention and accident, and after enough time those two are also the same.

The wrapping is alive, which is a nuisance. Set it down on the cambium and it takes. Leave it against a pillar overnight and by the next round it has put out a shoot. There are hedges in the Colonnade in the shape of a sleeve. There is a shrub near the Bead Run that is unmistakably a cloak, with the shoulder still there, and the neck-hole, and a fringe of withy ties hanging off it like a fringe of withy ties, and it is photosynthesizing. Tenders prune their coats. Half of what looks like fussing in their hands is a person pinching a bud out of their own sleeve.

And their faces. Here is the thing they will tell you, if you get close enough to have a conversation with fingers: *we are the only thing here that gets used up.* The stone does not wear. The marks do not fade. The pane does not move. But a tender's face creases, and the creases stay, and their eyes go, and the pads of their thumbs go shiny and then go smooth, and the whorls that make them who they are slowly rub off the outside of them and are left instead in the walls. There is no weather in the Sill. The people are the weather. They are the only erosion in the country, and they know it, and it is not a tragedy to them; it is a job description.

## The mouth is cargo

A tender's cheeks are distended. They have been distended so long that the face has grown around the habit — a wide, heavy lower face on a narrow skull, the jaw of a jug.

They carry water in their mouths. Not in vessels; there is nothing to make a vessel from that is better than a leaf, and a leaf slops, and a leaf cannot be sealed against a nine-drink walk. The mouth is the vessel. A tender goes out from an axil-tank with a mouthful of body-warm green water tasting of cucumber and chalk, and walks two drinks, and arrives, and puts that mouthful into a graft's cupped base or into a tank that has no other supply, and fills again, and goes on.

Which means they cannot speak. Not because they are solemn. Because their mouths are full.

So the language is hands, and taps, and the ceiling. And the whole moral life of a tender — every temptation they have, every failure, the thing they would confess if confession were a thing they had — takes place in the mouth, on the walk, alone, between the tank and the graft. *The water in your mouth is not yours.* You are thirsty at forty-one degrees in a country where thirst is the only clock in your body, and you are carrying the cure to it, and it is not for you, and it will be for you at the next tank, or the one after, and the graft is two drinks away and drying.

Their word for having done wrong is a small click of the tongue against the palate. It means *I drank it.*

When two tenders meet, they put their mouths together and pass water. It is supply. It is also the only kiss available, the only sustained touch, the only occasion on which one of them is inside another's economy, and they do not distinguish these, and there is no reason to. Afterwards they will stand a while, cold where the other's wrap brushed them, and then one will knock a post twice, which means *heard*, and go.

## Hands, and the Hundred Hands

The thumbnail is the tool. It is exactly as hard as milkstone — milkstone is *fingernail-soft*, that is not a metaphor, it is the specification — so tool and material wear each other at the same rate, evenly, forever, and nothing is ever won. A tender's thumbnails are ground flat and split at the corner and ridged from a lifetime of teasing fiber, and they grow back, and when one is lost there is nothing to do but wait, and so the long rest of a tender's life is scheduled by keratin. Ten notes of the bird, ten thousand, however many it takes. They go into a bud and lie in the dark with a wet leaf across the eyes and let the tool come back.

Everything they have made is a dent. That is the entire architectural repertoire: push, wait, push again. And the reason the dents are shallow is not timidity and it is not the material. It is that a shallow print can be pressed again. Someone else's thumb can come along and go into yours and deepen it a little, and a third into both, and a fourth, and the wall at that spot is a conversation that is still going. A deep print cannot be added to. A deep print uses up that place on the wall. It is a sentence with a full stop in it, and there is nothing after the full stop, and *after* is the one thing the Sill does not have.

So: shallow, shallow, shallow, a hundred overlapping thumbs on a post, and every one of them still exactly as deep as the thumb went, and you can put your hand into the stack and feel the whole argument.

The ring of posts out past the Colonnade — the one with a hundred prints overlapping on each — is where the names are kept. Because the tenders propagate the way everything here propagates, by division, by a fragment that touched and took, the whorls repeat. A child cut from a tender carries that tender's ridges, near enough, with a drift. And to take a name you go to the ring and lay your thumb into the print at the bottom of the stack and feel whether it fits.

It never fits exactly. It almost fits. Almost is enough here; almost is the entire national standard. You come away with a name and a knock and a lineage you can put your hand into whenever you want, and the person whose print that was is not gone, exactly, and we will come to that.

There are more bodies than names. There are always a few tenders with no name at all, only hands, and they are the ones who do the carrying, and they are not unhappy, and they are waiting for a fall.

## Names are sounds that arrive from above

A name is a knock. Specifically: a particular post in a particular district, struck a particular way, so that the sound goes up, runs along the underside of ninety meters of curved satin spar, and comes down somewhere else clean and close and inside the skull of whoever is standing there.

You cannot say your own name to yourself. Sound near you is eaten by all that soft tissue; your own knocking is a muffle, a nothing, a knuckle in wool. But everyone else's arrives from above, intimate, at the center of the head. So a tender hears their name only when someone two drinks away is thinking of them, and it comes down out of the ceiling like a voice in the room, and they have never once heard themselves say it.

Three falls in a lifetime. Three knocks that nobody made. Those are the new names — a child is named after a fall because a fall is the only thing that has ever happened, and the sound of it, as heard from the district where the child was pressed, is a sound no one has been called before. Everything else is inherited by fit.

Which means the census is also a chord. Eleven bodies, fewer names, a handful of knocks going around the pane in a slow rotation that nobody is conducting, and each tender hearing all of it except their own contribution. They are the only one who cannot hear what they sound like. That is the actual reason to walk two drinks to meet somebody: to be told your own sound.

## Eleven

Eleven is not a mystical number. It is a water budget.

Count the axil-tanks, count the mouths, count the drinks between them, allow for what evaporates off a wrap on a nine-drink crossing, and you get eleven. A twelfth would mean a tank going unfilled somewhere out past the Bead Run, and a graft going dry, and the tissue quietly taking back a shelf that nothing walks on anymore.

Children happen anyway. A hand pressed too long in the same place; a fragment that touched and took; a snail carrying cells across a join nobody was watching. And then, some while later, one of the eleven goes thin.

They go out to the Chalk Nursery, where the bird is faint, and they stop eating, and they stop being used, and the country takes them back. Not death — death is not a process this system has running. There is no body, no smell, no flies, no dry thing left over. There is a shape the whole no longer requires, and it is reabsorbed the way an unused bracket-shelf is reabsorbed, quietly, over a long time, and the last part to go is the hands. Their water is still in the tanks. You will drink it. Their prints are on the posts at exactly the depth their thumb went, and if your thumb almost fits, you may have their knock.

Ask where the eleventh is and a tender will point northeast-ish, out toward the wall, and make no other gesture. It is not grief. It is arithmetic performed with the whole body, and the Sill has been doing it since before there was anyone to notice.

## Going wide

The light takes their eyes. Not all at once — the Standing Noon does it, over a very long time, that smear of glare you can look at directly and that has never moved. The old ones go blind, and they call it, with hands, *going wide*, and they mean it.

Because a sighted tender knows one district: a grid of gaps, the next pillar and the one behind it, everything muffled and total and close. A blind tender knows the whole country. The ceiling gives them all of it — every graft taking two drinks away with a sound like a knuckle cracking, every post struck, someone wetting a wrap in a mezzanine they have never climbed to, the nursery tender out at the edge knocking to nobody at all because the acoustic fails out there where the raw rock starts and it is the loneliest sound in the world and everyone hears it, from above, inside their heads, all the time.

The blind ones give directions. The blind ones do the finest work, all thumb, no glare. And they keep time by the pale bird in the upper columns, one pure note with no overtone, every hundred breaths or so, and they count the breaths between, and the count tells them how hard they have been working. That is the whole of their metrology. Distance in drinks. Effort in breaths-per-note. Nothing to keep time for. They keep it anyway. The keeping is not a means to anything; the keeping is the thing itself, the way a note is not a means to the next note.

And when the nursery tender knocks into all that silence, someone, somewhere, always knocks back. One tap. It means *heard*. It is also their word for *you exist*, and their word for *I am here*, which they cannot hear themselves say.

## The other cold, and the lens

They love the stilt-browser. It does not notice them.

It stands in the Colonnade for weeks with its forelimbs in a leaf-tank, drinking by capillary action, and its exposed ribs are wet and the air moves over them and it is cold — the only cold in the Sill that a person did not make. A tender will go and stand against it and lean their spine into the ribs and be, for a while, in the presence of something that is not them and is still cool. Children sleep in there, in the cage of it, in the little winter of its chest, and are not yet given the dark.

The bead-snails they keep in the fold of the ear, or in a leaf cup at the hip, and top up, and top up, because a snail that dries goes back into the tissue and takes its bead with it and there is no getting it back. A snail set at a join will make the join, given long enough, and half the grafts in the country that nobody remembers making were made this way, which means the tenders' craft is mostly the arranging of accidents and then the not-touching of them.

And a snail's bead is a lens. Held up on a thumbnail toward the pane it will show you the fibers of the satin spar, the combed grain of the light, the only magnification available to a people with no fire and no glass. Their entire natural science is looking at the ceiling through a snail. What they see is fibers, and fibers, and the brighter smear. Some of them have spent whole rounds doing nothing else. There is nothing up there to find and they are not looking for anything; they are looking at the seam.

## What they think they are inside of

Because that is the cosmology, and it is not a belief, it is a gesture.

The gesture for the pane, ninety meters up, and the gesture for the red seam of a bud hill, and the gesture for the green skin over a fresh graft, are the same gesture. The chamber is a bud. Of course it is. It is a shape inside a living thing, sealed, lit from outside, held at the tension of one instant before opening, and the light is the outside pressing on the seam, and everything in here is the almost of something enormous.

So when they crawl in through the seam of a bud the size of a granary to sleep, they are going one level further into the same thing. The tightest, most private place in the world, green-black, warm, damp, the walls humming with held pressure, a wet leaf across the eyes. It is the only dark, and it is the only place the ceiling cannot reach, and therefore the only place a tender is not being addressed. They go in there to be unspoken to. They come out when the nail has grown.

And *keep it here* — the gesture they make when you ask about the bud, when you point at the seam and the red in it and raise your eyebrows in the way of a person from a place with afterward — is not a warning. It is not a prohibition. It is the tense they live in. It is what you say about a note you are holding. They are not preventing the flower. Preventing would be a project, and a project has an end, and an end has an after. They are the sustain. They are what the tension in the wall feels like from the inside, and there are eleven of them, and they are cold, briefly, wherever they go.

## You

If you come in, you will bring dust. It will be the first dust that has ever been in the air here, and it will be dry, and a tender will take a thread of it off your shoulder and put it on their tongue and feel it *take* — the first thing they have ever touched that took water instead of giving it — and they will hold your wrist very hard for a moment, and then let go.

They cannot keep it. The humidity will get into it within a round or two and it will be damp and green-smelling and ordinary and part of the country, like everything, like the cloak that became a hedge, like the eleventh. Nothing stays foreign here. That is not hospitality; it is the same law that keeps the stone from setting.

But on your way out you will press your hand into a milkstone wall, because everyone does, and you will press too hard, because you are from a place where things wear away and you want it to *last*, and the wall will take it and keep it at exactly that depth: a deep print, a full stop, a place on the wall where nothing more can be said.

And a long time afterward a tender will come along on a round with no schedule, mouth full, and find it, and lay a thumb in, and feel that the hand was bigger than theirs, and that the ridges are wrong, that they do not almost fit, that this is nobody's name.

They will not smooth it. They will press their own beside it, shallow, and go on toward the next tank, and the sound of them doing it will run up under the pane and come down ninety meters away inside somebody else's head, clean and close, like a voice, meaning *heard*.
