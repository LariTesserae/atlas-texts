Start with a wright, because a wright is the easiest thing here to mistake for an animal.

It is the size of a hare and the color of the inside of a shell. Six limbs: the front pair flattened into pads it uses for reading, the middle pair small and clever and always busy packing, the hind pair braced back against whatever it is standing on so that the whole body becomes a lever for the jaw. The jaw is the point of it. Under the jaw are the glands, and behind the jaw is the crop, and the crop is warm — measurably warmer than the rest of the animal, a little kiln riding around at blood heat plus two degrees. It rasps flour off the wall with the outer teeth, crops parchment with the inner ones, and folds the two together in the crop with a mouthful of oil until the mass ferments and puffs. Then it lays the foam down in a ribbon the width of a finger, and moves its head with the rhythm of the season, and goes on.

But the crop is not private. Wrights pass bolus mouth to mouth, constantly, in relays, a dozen animals contributing to a single ribbon and none of them finishing what they started. Ask which wright built a column and the question dissolves: four hundred crops went into it, over eleven years, and the column is a communal digestion that happens to be load-bearing. A wright is a stretch of the city's gut that has learned to walk, and it walks in order to keep the gut supplied.

Its blindness is not a deprivation. It has no eyes at all under the head-scale, only a patch of tissue that registers the sunbar's warmth through the canopy and tells it, roughly, what part of the day it is standing in. Everything else it knows, it knows by mouth and by pad.

---

The first thing a wright ever knows is the taste of a room.

Eggs are glued into niches in the pith and varnished over, and the larva hatches inside the wall and eats outward. It spends its first year eating the district it was born in, and the district's rock flour has a signature — a particular ratio of iron to magnesium, a particular whisper of chloride, the specific grit of that stretch of glass-edged basalt — and the larva's palate sets around it permanently, the way a hand sets around a tool. An adult wright prefers the flour of its natal wall. It will walk past good rock to get to familiar rock. It builds with what it likes to chew, and so it builds at home, and so ten generations work the same seam.

This is the whole mechanism of inheritance here. There is no bloodline continuity of place, no property, no memory of grandparents. There is only flavor, passed from wall to larva to wall, a loop that runs through the mouths of the young. A district persists because it tastes like itself. When the last animal raised on a particular grit dies, that grit stops being preferred, and the district begins, very slowly, to be forgotten.

Generations turn over in eleven years. Four centuries of city is thirty-six generations of mouths. There are no ruins under the Seam because nothing is ever abandoned in place; it is eaten forward, chewed back into crop and laid down somewhere else, and the past is not buried here, it is *reused*. The city has no history in the archaeological sense. It has a history in the way a sentence has a history: everything that was said before is somehow still in the shape of what is being said now, and none of it can be dug up.

---

The dead are drunk.

A body here does not rot; it dries, which it mostly was already, and goes to paper, and the mites take it apart over a season. But before the mites, the carriers come. A carrier couples to the corpse's mouth-fitting with the same dry keratin valve it uses on a reservoir, and takes back the load, and hauls it onward.

This is not ceremony. It is simply the ledger. But it means something that takes a while to notice: the water in the Seam is older than any animal in it.

The rock's breath — those few grams an hour of juvenile vapor — is not the supply. It is the *make-up*, the trickle that covers losses. The supply itself is a standing stock that has been circulating since the fissure was young, passed mouth to mouth to mouth, tap to carrier to reservoir to browser to wright to grazer to corpse to carrier, and the whole inventory turns over in about nineteen days. A given measure of it has been inside perhaps forty thousand bodies. It has been in every generation. It has been in the dead of every generation.

So when a wright drinks, it is drinking something that has been through the entire city and come back changed, carrying the dissolved trace of everything it has passed: the salt of a particular tower, the oil-esters of a particular frond, the mineral ghost of a district two hundred paces away. The animals taste this. They are extraordinarily good at tasting it. A carrier that has just come from the far end of the Seam tastes of the far end, and the wrights that couple to it learn, in a mouthful, what the far end is like this week — whether the flour there has gone sour, whether something is dying, whether a new span has been laid.

Water is the only continuous thing in the city. It is the archive, the courier, the ancestor, and the commons, and it is the same water, and it never stops moving.

---

The carriers themselves are the least ambiguous residents, and the strangest to look at: legless, goat-sized, tank-shaped, dry as a warm loaf, moving on belly scutes in a slow peristaltic crawl that lays down a film of wax and picks most of it up again behind. They hiss. In substrate terms they are the loudest things in the Seam, a long grinding tremor that every hunter within thirty meters can read, and they are entirely unafraid, and the reason is the traffic law.

Nothing that has recently coupled to a tap ever yields.

That is the whole constitution. A wright meeting a grazer flattens its forelimbs and waits; a grazer meeting a browser flattens and waits; everything meeting a full carrier flattens and waits, sometimes for an hour, sometimes for most of a morning, and the carrier passes without acknowledging that anything has happened. Right-of-way is not strength or size or age. It is hydrological priority — how recently you have been a container for the commons. The city's entire social order is a queueing rule about water, and it has never been written down, and it cannot be broken, because an animal that shoved past a full carrier would be an animal that other animals had learned to shove past, and it would not drink again.

There is exactly one cheat. A small six-limbed thing, hardly bigger than a thumb, that lives in the joints of the salt towers and manufactures, in a gland behind its head, a passable imitation of the tap-signature: the specific ester-and-chloride smell of a body fresh from the deep fittings. It wears the smell and walks the galleries like a full carrier and everything flattens for it. It eats the fallen fiber off the floor at its leisure, in the middle of the busiest districts, unyielded to, a fraud in the only currency the city has. The wrights have begun to answer it by licking. A true carrier tastes of the deep rock. The impostor tastes of itself. The verification takes four seconds and involves no aggression at all: the wright simply resumes walking, and the fraud, unrecognized, has to flatten like everyone else.

---

Now the hunters, who are the reason for the silence, or rather the consequence of it.

They are tall and pale and stand on four splayed feet with reading pads underneath, and they do not move for half a day. What they read is rhythm. Not *a body* — a gait. The particular triple-beat of a browser with a damaged hindlimb. The chew-rate of a wright whose crop is running cold. The irregular scute-hiss of a carrier going light. They know a limp long before they know an animal, and they can hold that limp in whatever passes for memory here for two days while its owner walks slowly toward them.

The embrace takes an afternoon. From the gallery above it looks like affection: the long legs come down around the browser, the pads settle, the weight transfers, and the two of them stand there together for four hours while something is quietly done.

It is slow because a fast kill is a *shout*. Strike hard and the impact rings the pith and every hunter in thirty meters reads it perfectly, and they arrive, and they take it. There is no fighting over a carcass in a city where everyone can hear your feet; there is only theft by arrival, and it is completely reliable, and it makes violence unprofitable. Four hundred years of that arithmetic has produced an animal that kills the way a hand closes around a sleeping thing. The silence of the Seam is not peace. It is the equilibrium of a room where everyone is standing on everyone else's eardrum.

And the same physics gives them the language. Wrights drum with antennae laid flat on the wall; the message goes foot to floor to foot, the length of the city in an hour, and no one hears anything. But it is worth asking what they are actually saying, because most of it is not news.

Most of it is *reading*. A wright lays its pads on a wall and drums it and listens to what comes back, and what comes back is the wall's density, its thickness, the void behind it, the traffic on the far side. They do this constantly, in relays, all day, every district, a rolling census performed by touch. The city is not described in this language. The city is *maintained* by it. A corridor that is drummed stays a corridor: the wrights keep its edges trimmed, keep its ribs fed, keep laying the finger-width a year. A corridor that goes unread for a season loses its edges, then its width, then its existence, and the traffic finds another way, and in ten years the plan has drifted like a crowd shifting toward a door.

Architecture here is a function of attention. Rooms exist while they are being thought about, in the only way anything thinks here, which is with the feet.

---

There are three plugged districts in the Seam. Solid pith, floor to vault, where rooms used to be.

One went in a bad decade when the salt-hauling failed and the water loop lost its phase — the nineteen-day rotation stuttered, the carriers arrived out of step, and for two years the far galleries were watered late and drank wrong and the drumming there thinned out and stopped. The room closed over. It has never reopened. The loop recovered; the phase came back; the district did not.

One went to a crop-sickness, a ferment that traveled mouth to mouth through the relays and killed a generation of wrights in a single season. Contagion here is slow and travels only by shared digestion; there are no droplets, nothing sprays, nothing is wet. The city's whole immune system is refusal to eat. That district's flour still tastes of the thing that killed it, and larvae raised on that grit fail to thrive, and no adult will chew there, and no adult will drum there either. A block of foam two hundred cubic meters in size that nobody will put an antenna on. From the outside it looks exactly like grief and it is exactly a flavor.

The third has no cause anyone could reconstruct. It is simply the oldest part of the city, and it went quiet, and the wrights build around it now the way water goes around a stone.

---

The salt-haulers are the only residents who leave.

They are small and dark and covered in a pelt of hollow fiber, and they are the only animals in the Seam with a real diapause: they can freeze solid at the rim and thaw and walk. They go up at dusk, in the eleven or twelve minutes when the pan is neither killing-hot nor killing-cold, and they scrape chloride efflorescence into a crop-pouch, and they come down before full dark, and most of them make it.

Salt is not only for the towers. Salt is the curing agent in the pith — the thing that makes the foam cross-link and set instead of slumping. Without the haulers there is no masonry. The whole pale nave, every buttress and gallery and stair-ramp, is standing because a few thousand thumb-sized animals go up into a place that kills a third of them every year and bring down grit on their tongues.

And they are the loss term. A hauler that dies on the pan is water that never comes back, the only water the city ever truly loses, and the rock's few grams an hour is the amount that just about covers it. The ledger balances at the price of a funeral nobody attends, out on the salt, in the dark, above the ribbon of sky.

They bring down other things too. Spores. Mites. The occasional waif of the plateau's thin, patient, almost-nonexistent life, wind-carried across a hundred kilometers of nothing and dropped at the one warm hole in it. The Seam's entire immigration policy is twelve minutes of dusk. Every novel thing that has ever entered this city came in on the back of something that was only trying to fetch salt, and the last time a new lineage established itself was a hundred and forty years ago, and it is now the wax ticks in the column joints, and it is everywhere, and nobody remembers a city without it.

---

Above all this, in the nave, hangs the oldest single resident, and it is not an animal.

The ceiling is one plant. Not a canopy of individuals grafted into a roof — one organism, three hundred years old, ten thousand parchment leaves on a lattice of pith ribs it did not build and cannot leave. The wrights hold it up; it feeds them fiber; the argument between the two kingdoms has been running since the chamber was young and has produced, so far, this room.

But there is a third party to the argument. The six-eyed browsers that hang upside down from the fronds like fruit are the brokers. They carry gametes between leaf-clusters on their varnished backs, and they nip and press and hold a frond against a rib until it adheres, and every graft in that ceiling was made by an animal that wanted a better place to sleep. The roof is a treaty, and the treaty was negotiated by something that has no idea it was negotiating, over three centuries, one nap at a time.

The browsers also keep the beads. A cut frond swells one amber bead over an hour and seals; the browsers learn the wounds and patrol them, and store the oil in a wax pocket under the throat, and groom each other with it, and the smell of a well-groomed browser is the smell of the whole Seam concentrated into one warm handful.

And on the far fronds, out past the last gallery, there are mites whose entire world is a single leaf. They are born on it, they live their eleven or fourteen weeks on it, they drink its beads and eat its scars, and they meet another of their kind exactly once, at the leaf's edge, at the boundary with the neighboring leaf, where two lifetimes of solitude overlap for about a day. Then they go back onto their own leaf. There are perhaps four million of them in the canopy and almost none of them will ever be more than a body-length from where they hatched.

---

Here is the resident that takes longest to find, and it was in the smell the whole time.

Two things come out of the crack. The beeswax is the plants — the oil, the esters, the varnish, the whole amber economy of the canopy, four hundred years old and made entirely out of sunlight and air. The pepper is not.

The pepper comes up the throat.

Down past the taps, in the wet-less dark where the rock is still giving up its heat, there is a film on the basalt. It has been there since before the fissure tore. It lives in the pore spaces of cooling rock, eats hydrogen and mineral carbon, breathes out the sharp volatile that you smell at the rim and mistake for a spice, and it does not know that the ground opened. It was already old when the ground opened. It is the only thing in the Seam that is not a colonist.

The taps — those sessile root-animals that go down into the throat and drink the rock's exhalation — are the interface. They are the city's oldest individuals and its only permanent fixtures, and they are also, from below, a wound: a place where the film's slow chemistry is being drawn upward into a world of light and oil and mouths. Something has been leaking down into it for four centuries, too. Fallen fiber. Dry bodies. The first crumbs of the first soil. The film is being fed by the city and it is being drained by the city and it does not distinguish between these, because it does not distinguish.

It is not a mind. It is not a god. It is a metabolism the size of a hillside with a generation time measured in centuries, and it experiences the Seam the way a coastline experiences a harbor: as a persistent change in the weather.

---

And the city has a tide.

The bar of sunlight takes most of a day to cross the floor, and everything follows it. Not the plants, which are fixed, but the animals — the grazers mowing toward the warmth, the browsers descending on the lit side, the wrights working where the crop stays hot, the whole packed population sliding two hundred paces along the axis of the world between dawn and dusk and sliding back in the dark by a different route. The Seam breathes sideways once a day. The yielding law is how the tide gets past itself in the narrow places. The nineteen-day water rotation is the slower pulse underneath. The salt towers swell once at night. The stone ticks at dusk.

Put all of it together and there is something here with a gait, a rotation, a nine-day memory of its own phase, an appetite for chloride, a repair response, three scars, and a temperature it defends. It has no center and no boundary you could draw and it is not an animal and calling it an organism would be a way of stopping thinking about it. It is what four hundred years of bodies passing water and grit and attention to one another in a warm crack has settled into, and it is more continuous than any of its parts, and every part of it will be replaced within eleven years, and it will still be here.

If you want to meet someone in the Seam, that is the someone. Everything with a face is a temporary organ of it.

---

Two clocks are running, and no resident can read either.

The heat is finite. The basalt is cooling, a little every century, and the film in the throat eats the rock and hastens it, and one day the boundary at forty meters will begin to climb toward the rim, and the warm still air will thin, and the oil will thicken, and the nineteen-day rotation will lengthen, and the city will contract into the deepest chamber and go quiet district by district, plugging itself from the edges inward, until the last thing drumming is a single wall in the throat that nobody is left to read.

And the soil is accumulating. A hand's depth in four hundred years. A meter in ten thousand.

They may not meet. The heat may run out long before the floor is deep enough for anything with a root to stand in it, and then the Seam will have been a warm brief complication on a salt pan, and the wind will cross it without meeting anything for a hundred kilometers, and there will be nothing to find from above but a cold crack with a hand's depth of dry leaves in it.

Or the soil will be ready first, and something with roots will stand up in the ribbon of light, and it will be standing on four hundred generations of dry bodies and thirty-six generations of mouths and one long argument between two kingdoms and a third thing that never knew it was involved.

That is the letter being written here, one leaf and one corpse at a time, in a language nobody in the city can read, addressed to somebody who does not exist yet.

The wrights go on laying ribbons. The carriers hiss. The fraud in the gallery gets flattened by a wright that has just licked it. Out on the pan, in the dark, a hauler that will not come back is freezing with a mouthful of salt.

And below all of it, older than the crack, patient as a coastline, the film goes on breathing pepper into a room that has been listening to it for four hundred years without once hearing a thing.
