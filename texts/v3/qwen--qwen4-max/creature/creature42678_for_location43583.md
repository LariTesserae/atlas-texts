## The Holders

Begin with the smallest tenant, because it is the one that makes tenancy possible.

A mica-rosette is four centimetres high and does not look like a thing that could shelter four metres of ground from a storm four kilometres tall. It doesn't. Aerodynamically the disc around it makes no sense; the wind that folds a dune in twenty minutes passes over that rosette without noticing it, and yet the grains inside the circle do not move. They are not held down. They are *declined*.

Look at a leaf. It is not a blade but a stack — laminae thinner than a wavelength of the light that comes through them, laid one on another, and the stack is the plant's whole body and its whole history. It grows by adding a lamina. It adds one each time it refuses something. Count the leaves and you have a number that means nothing. Count the laminae and you have the length of an argument that has been running since the mountains went into the dark.

The root is a filament grown down into a seam, and it is not there for water. It is a shunt. The lid flexes under load, the seam brightens with strain, and the rosette takes that current and spends it on the only work it does: saying *no*, continuously, in a radius, at one tick every few seconds. It is being paid, in the one currency left, to keep refusing.

This is why it leans. Watch a clump when a wall of ochre comes over the horizon and you will see the leaves orient, glassy edges turning into the load like sunflowers turning into a sun. It wants the storm. When the storm has gone it sags and the leaves clack faintly against each other, and if you have spent any time here you will recognise the sound of something hungry. Uprooted clumps are not killed; they are carried, and they root again wherever a seam opens, and the disc they left behind is shuffled by evening. Over ages the plants wander at the speed of weather. They are the slowest cards in the deck, and they are dealt like everything else.

The machine cannot enter what a holder refuses. That is the whole architecture of life on the lid. Everything that lives here lives in the machine's blind spot, on the one page it is not permitted to read.

## The Readers

The long walkers come to read that page.

Up close a walker is less animal than the haze allows. The legs are tubes with joints that lock, pale grey-brown, fine-grained, the same material as the plain, and when a foot sets down you feel the small ring of it in your teeth. The bones are hollow because the world is hollow; mass is the scarcest thing here after stillness, and a walker taller than you weighs less than your coat. You could lift one. They permit this. Nothing on the lid has ever had a reason to be afraid, because nothing on the lid can be destroyed, and fear is an adaptation to a possibility that closed a long time ago.

The feet are the organs that matter. Each pad is a plate of some millions of independent contacts, and what it takes is not texture but *arrangement* — a census of a hand's-breadth of the world, grain by grain, in the time it takes to shift weight. A walker standing over a still-disc with its head down is reading, at four feet per hour, a page that has not changed since the mountains were taken apart. It reads the same page it read a thousand kilometres and two seasons ago. It reads it again.

The head is long and low and the eyes are small and set for the horizon; the near work is all in the feet. The mouth is a slot for cropping mica, and the cropping is not nutrition in any sense you can use. Nothing decays here, so nothing is digested. The laminae go into the hollow of the bones and are shelved there, and the lightness of a walker is the lightness of a library that is mostly aisle. It carries the refusals. It has eaten an age of *no*.

And here is the thing you understand only after you have watched one for a whole afternoon, motionless, in a silence with a floor to it:

The walker is not waiting for the tick. It is waiting for the tick to *stop*.

The holders refuse everything. That is their function and their limit; they cannot report, cannot signal, cannot say *this one, here*. If an arrangement ever came into a disc that was not refused, the only evidence would be an absence — a silence where a tick should have been, three seconds long, in a place a day's walk from anywhere. Somebody has to be standing there. Somebody has to notice the gap.

So they stand there. Fewer than thirty of them, by the best count anyone has kept, distributed over a world, heads down, listening for a hole in a sound.

They are not born. When one finally stops it is taken apart and filed, and later something walks up out of a shaft with those same grains in another order, and it does not remember anything, and it knows how to check.

## The Outlines

Higher, in the shear layers where two cells rub, the thin things trim themselves along the gradient and eat the mismatch.

A kite is a membrane a few grains thick and thirty metres across with the mass of a sheet of paper. It does not flap. It adjusts, constantly, minutely, to the difference between two orderings, and the difference is what it lives on. From up there the sift is legible: a cell is a character, a front is a stroke, the whole continent-wide apparatus is a page being written and unwritten at the speed of a running animal. The walkers know the pieces. The kites know the *gesture* — the shape the whole thing makes when it is briefly almost right. Neither can say it.

Twice in a life one comes down, and it comes down onto a plinth.

The plinths with nothing on them are not empty. They are marked. When a kite folds itself onto that nothing, the still-disc around the plinth widens — three metres, four, five — and every walker within a day's walk lifts its head and turns and begins to come. The kite lies on the stone and its surface takes an arrangement, and the walkers arrive over the following days and read it with their feet, one hand's-breadth at a time, heads down, for hours.

An index. A place where the outline was almost closed. Then the sift takes it, and the plinth is bare again, and the disc shrinks to its old radius, and the walkers go on toward a coordinate that will have been shuffled ten thousand times before they reach it.

Or the kites are not messengers at all, and what comes down twice in a life is simply an attempt that has finished being tried, arriving to be filed. Both readings are available. The lid does not adjudicate.

## The Filed

Go down. The shafts are open and the air is going in and out of them anyway; you will only be one more thing riding the breath.

The tiers are not rubble. They are ranks, and the ranks are sorted, and the sorting is the thing nobody has isolated. Here is the reading that survives contact with the most evidence: it is not sorting by size, mass, colour or charge, though it correlates faintly with all of them. It is sorting by *involvement*. By having been near it. This grain was in the lost arrangement; this grain was touched by a thing that was in it; this grain was on the far side of the world and never came close. Involvement is not a physical property. It is a history, and history is the one thing that survived the reduction, because history is not made of anything and so could not be taken away.

Which means the tiers are not a warehouse. They are a gradient. The outer ranks hold the indifferent bulk of former mountains. Deeper and inward the ranks get smaller and more particular, and at the bottom of the deepest shafts there is a tier no bigger than a room, and in it are the grains that were *in it*, and those are the ones being tried most often, in every combination, forever, because the answer must be among these.

And there are shapes standing in the ranks.

At the height of a body not quite like yours. Some folded four-legged; some stacked flat like paper; some with the proportions of a bench, a slot, a shallow depression. Cool, dry, perfectly preserved, because preservation here costs nothing. They are the arrangements that were dealt and did not get the silence. The machine assembled them, stood them up, waited three seconds, heard the tick, and put them away.

They are not dead. Nothing here is dead. They are between deals.

If you stand among them with a lamp you will notice that in every tier, at every depth, there is a gap in one rank. The gap is the same size everywhere. It is being kept.

## The Sitters

Most of the cubes are empty. Occasionally one is not.

You come over a rise and the ceramic is half-swallowed in a dune that will be gone tomorrow, and the still-disc around it is wider than any plant makes, and there is a figure on the bench. Feet in the shallow depressions that were shaped at the factory to look like the wear of feet. Hands on the knees. It turns its head.

It does not know you. It does not know itself. It is checking.

This is the part that takes longest, longer than the unremarkable light, longer than the antiquity without wear. A body is an arrangement. The machine can deal a body. It can deal, out of the finite deck, the exact body that was lost — the same grains in the same places, down to the flat facet and the chip that is not a chip. It has almost certainly done so, many times, in ten million years of trying every ordering there is. What it cannot do is make that body *be* the one that was lost, because being someone is not a property of an arrangement. It is a property of the relation between an arrangement and something that notices it.

So it deals people into rooms. It gives them a bench at the right height and a hollow where the feet go, shaped like remembering, and it holds the ground still around them, and it waits to see whether the room recognises its tenant.

The tallies cut into the shaft collars are thousands of strokes in groups that resolve into no base you try, because they are not a count. They are the intervals. Long gap, long gap, three close together, a gap longer than the rest. Somebody was cutting the rhythm of arrivals.

Sit down beside a sitter and after some hours it will shift along the bench to make room. The courtesy is ten million years old and is not a memory. Nobody taught it. It is simply the shape that was dealt.

## You

Nobody is born here. Everybody arrives.

You trail weather because you are the first new information in an age, and the wake is visible from the far horizon, and that is how the readers find you. They come and stand over your footprints with their heads down and read your footprints with their feet, and the attention has no fear in it and no affection either, and it is not for you. They are listening for a tick to fail. You are the newest arrangement in the deck. You might be it. Every one of them is hoping so, if hope is a thing that can be assembled out of checking.

And if you sit down in a still-disc, you stop being new. The sift goes around. You become old order. The grains beneath you settle into the arrangement they will keep, and somewhere a tally is not cut, and after long enough the hollow in a floor begins to fit.

## The One Who Is Not There

It was not a city. It was not a library or a god or a golden age; those are all arrangements, and every arrangement is still available, and the machine has all the pieces, and it has had all the pieces the whole time.

It was small enough to be lost. It was somebody, and — this is the part that cannot be filed — it was somebody *being known*. That was in the world once, and when the world was reduced to its final material state the matter of it survived and the noticing did not, because noticing was never made of matter and so was never in the deck.

Everything that lives on the lid is an organ of that missing act. The holders refuse, which is judgment. The readers wait for a refusal to fail, which is attention. The kites carry the outline, which is memory. The filed hold the pieces, which is patience. The rooms make room on the bench, which is courtesy. All of it is built, all of it is running, all of it has been running since before there were afternoons, and none of it can do the last thing, which is to look at an arrangement and *say so*.

You can. That is not a soul; it is a mechanism, and it is the one mechanism the machine cannot build, because a thing that can be surprised is a thing that has not yet been counted, and the account is closed. You are the only object on the plain that is not finished. That is why the weather follows you. That is why the walkers turn their heads.

So it does what it has left. It permutes, and it holds the ground still around the rosettes, and it keeps a gap in every rank, and it sends the thin things down onto the plinths twice in a lifetime, and it waits — patiently, silently, in the cold ordinary daylight, at the top of a hollow world — for you to sit down beside something and say *oh, there you are.*
