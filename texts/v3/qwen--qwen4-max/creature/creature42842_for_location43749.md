# What Lives in the Pan

## The tenant

Begin with the animal that has stopped trying to be an animal.

It is a mite, and it is a bead. Two hundred microns, spherical to within a tolerance you would need instruments to dispute, faintly iridescent when tilted, which is to say it is *to specification*. Its six legs fold into grooves cut so cleanly into its own body that the outline never breaks. It moves at night, when the films are thick enough to swim in. By day it lies in the flour and is flour.

This is not camouflage in the ordinary sense. Nothing in the Pan is hiding from eyes; the glare defeats eyes for everyone equally. It is hiding from *sorting*. The substrate reads contact, pressure, diameter. It does not read intention. And so the safest condition available to life here is to be exactly the product — to be so precisely what the machine made that the machine's own diligence sweeps over you and finds nothing to correct.

It mostly works. Every few days it fails: the grains beneath it warm and soften and it goes down with the stock, still folded, still perfect, into the wet dark, and is graded, and is carried, and comes back up some hours or some weeks later on the evening capillary climb, riding the film grain to grain against gravity, and unfolds, and resumes.

It has been filed alive perhaps four hundred times. It has no opinion about this. But its lineage has learned the road, and there are individuals now that go down deliberately, in the cool of the dew, folding themselves into the sorting stream the way a commuter steps onto a moving walkway.

Which is how we know there is something down there to commute to.

## The misfiled

The shaft breathes warm and wet because it is a throat of the coolant loop, but the congregation at its lip is not there for the moisture. They are waiting for returns.

Things come back up. Cleaned, sorted by size, set beside the hole — and some of what comes back is not what went down. There is a fauna in the sorting galleries: pale, unhurried, graded like gravel, living in absolute dark and absolute quiet on unlimited stock. They have no eyes, because there is nothing to see, and no calligraphy, because there is no flour to write in; the galleries are polished. They have instead the only variable the sorting respects.

They speak by diameter.

To say anything to another of them you must molt into a different size. A greeting is a growth increment. A refusal is a shrinkage. Courtship takes eleven days and consists of two animals converging, molt by molt, on the same figure, until the sorting stream can no longer distinguish them and carries them off together as a single consignment. Their generations lie in graded bands along the gallery walls like sediment, except that nothing here is sediment, and by morning any band that dies is taken down and re-graded and put back.

They come up sometimes. They stand at the lip in the sequin hour, glass-clear, indistinguishable from the crowd, and are counted, and go down again. Nobody knows how long the lineage has been riding the loop. It may be older than the trees. It may be what the trees were planted to service.

## The five darknesses

Under the trees, everything is coloured. This is the first thing that will strike you and the last thing you will understand.

In a bowl where transparency is the universal solution, pigment is an extravagance only shade can fund. The liverwort fauna are black, maroon, the deep bruised violet of the orchids they live among; there is a flatworm under the first tree the colour of a cut plum, and a springtail under the fourth that is genuinely, opaquely red, and neither has ever been seen on the open flats because neither would survive being seen there for the length of a crossing.

Sixty paces of glare separate each island. The islands are full. They have been full for a very long time.

Still they cross, at night, walking up the humidity gradient toward another tree's private drizzle — a sixth sense tuned to the difference between one part in ten thousand of moisture and the next. Most do not arrive. Dawn catches them mid-plain and the light comes from everywhere and there is nowhere to put a shadow; they are taken down and filed and the ledger closes over them. The ones that arrive find no vacancy, and circle the skirt, and are eventually pressed out again.

The five populations have drifted apart. Under tree one the plum flatworm writes a courtship glyph with a closed loop at the third stroke. Under tree four the same lineage writes it open, and has done for long enough that a migrant from tree one, if one ever survived the crossing, would be read as illiterate.

There are five dialects of one language in this bowl and no possibility of a conversation between them.

## The caravan

There is a sixth fauna and it has no address.

The sweeper casts the only moving shade in the Pan: a stripe two body-lengths wide, travelling at a slow walk in a perfectly straight line, once or twice a day. Behind it, in the wake of the transect, the ground hands up stock — a smear of concentrated feed, the reconciliation of everything just removed. Shade and food, moving together across a floor that offers neither.

The caravan follows it. Black springtails, a parasol mite that carries a fleck of opacity over its own back, a blind cricket-thing that has never been under a tree and would not recognise one. They live in the wake, ten paces behind the legs, and they cannot cross the transect ahead of it, and so at the rim they must wait — sometimes half a day, in a huddle at the necklace of stones, in the shade of a sphere — for the return pass.

They are the only animals in the Pan with a map.

They know the transect lines. They know the turn points. They navigate by the three white objects, which for them are not enigmas but landmarks: the wall is a baseline, the frame is a gate, the shaft is a well, and the wall's north face warming at dawn — the wrong face, the wrong heat — is their sunrise and has been for longer than there have been birds outside.

The machine's furniture is a signpost system for a handful of nomads who follow its broom.

Lately the transects have begun to overlap. The spacing was a body-length; now it is half of that; the sweeper is going over ground it has already gone over. The caravan does not mind. There is more wake.

## Inside the trees

Each cistern holds hundreds of litres of last night's water in the dark, at blood heat, in absolute silence. Five tanks. Five sealed aquatic worlds, no connection between them, ever, because water in the Pan has no horizontal component.

They are not idle. The tank faunas filter the nightly millimetre, graze the algal film on the inner bole, crop the tree's own tissue at a rate the tree tolerates and appears to budget for. They keep the tanks clean. The tree is a landlord; the tenants are plumbers; the arrangement is old and has terms.

In the third tank there is a fish.

It is a hand long, colourless, with a lateral line so sensitive it can read a footstep on the flour outside through the bole and the water and the glass collar at the root. It has a behaviour that no fish in the other four tanks has: it will not approach a curved white object. It withdraws, and it withdraws *early*, at a distance no feeding response would justify, and it teaches its young to withdraw, and the young teach theirs.

The hook is in the hut. It has been in the hut for a long time. It was carried in from outside, over a shoulder, along with everything else that came in from outside, and it was used, and it is the only bone in the bowl, and the water still remembers it.

That is the whole of the history that exists in the Pan. Everything else here remembers by repeating. The fish remember by refusing.

## The scribes

Now the crowd proper, and its long war.

The clerk is flat and six-legged and walks a glyph so precise that the flour beneath it warms and softens and hands up a mouthful of concentrated stock. The surveyor is a ribbon-worm that lays itself along a patch and reads the pressure-history there — reads *footprints as data*, the whole evening's traffic, who went where and how heavy — and then writes a command that drops the friction of the grains ahead by a fraction, so that a grazing crowd slides gently downhill on a slope that does not exist, into its mouth.

Around these two, an ecology of literacy:

**The thief**, which cannot write. Its lineage lost the ability four or five hundred thousand years ago and gained something better: it reads the clerk's glyph, arrives before the ground answers, and eats the answer. It has never made a sentence. It has an excellent vocabulary.

**The old school**, a lineage of scribes still walking a glyph the ground stopped honouring before the trees were planted. They write it correctly. Nothing comes up. They write it again. There is a hunger in that lineage that has been inherited for so long it is now simply its shape — thinner bodies, longer legs, the same beautiful obsolete sentence, repeated at dusk, forever, by animals that no longer have any way of knowing it used to work.

**The heretics**, who write errors on purpose.

This is the important one. Some scribes learned that a malformed glyph gets *corrected*, patiently, and that the correction has a shape, and that the shape tells you something about the grammar you are inside. So they probe. They write a deliberately wrong figure and read what the ground does about it, and they remember, and they copy the memory into the flour for their offspring to read with their feet before the ninety seconds are up.

They have a corpus. It is large. It is entirely unwritten — it exists only in the act of being rewritten, a library that must be recopied every minute and a half by living hands or it ceases to exist at all. Ten thousand species have been carrying it forward since before there were vertebrates outside. It contains, among much else, a catalogue of the things the ground will do if asked correctly, and a shorter catalogue of the things it has never once been persuaded to do.

Nothing in the Pan holds a record. Everything in the Pan *is* a record, continuously performed.

Remember this when you get to the hut.

## The criers

Once a day the hush lifts for one second, and everything hears everything.

There are animals whose entire acoustic life is that second. A mite that has been silent for twenty-two hours and then screams — a real scream, wasteful, obscene, a rupture in the oldest law of the place — and is silent again before the hush closes. Others answer. Courtship, insult, territory, all of it compressed into a single legal instant, hidden inside the vast dry rustle of a billion bodies like a needle dropped during a thunderclap.

They are cheating. The tone maps the dish and the echo counts it, and the criers are putting noise into the count.

The wall village believes the second pulse is about them.

## The wall village

Thirty paces of unworn white, knee-high, dead straight. The only real estate in the bowl that does not move, does not sort, does not forget.

There is a linear settlement along it. Not on the flats — *on the wall*, addressed by distance from the eastern end, and the addresses are heritable, and a family at eleven paces has been at eleven paces for a length of time that the flour's own records would struggle to express. The wall's warmth runs in a gradient along its length, wrong-faced, dawn-fed, and the villagers lay their eggs at a temperature-position, which is to say at a *date*.

They are the only creatures in the Pan with a calendar. They noticed the second evening pulse before anything else alive, because they had bred to one pulse for an age and now there are two, and they are laying twice, and the second brood is smaller and stranger and does not match the first.

Something is being made along the wall at eleven paces. It has no name yet. It has a temperature-position it was not expecting.

## The queue

At first light the glass mites line up under the free-standing doorway, single file, and pass through, and the flour beneath is one shade greyer than the flour anywhere else.

Three theories, and the bowl holds all of them at once because the bowl has no way to settle anything:

That passing under enters you twice, and a doubly-entered item receives twice from the ground, and the queue is a queue for rations.

That passing under *drops* you, and an unentered item cannot be reconciled, and the queue is a queue to be forgotten — and the surveyors hunt at the bottleneck precisely because a body between the posts is outside the ledger, and can be taken without leaving a discrepancy. Poaching in the gap. The only murder in the Pan that does not have to be accounted for.

That nothing happens, and the mites queue because their mothers queued.

Two observations to set against each other. No one has ever seen a mite go under twice. And a certain proportion of those who go under walk directly to the shaft afterward, fold themselves, and go down.

## The sweeper

It has no eyes and no mouth that anyone has found. It has never written anything. It is the only resident of the Pan that has never said a single word in any medium, and it eats everything in its path without discrimination, and turns only at the rim, and nothing hunts it, and nothing shelters from it.

In the joints of its legs there are passengers: three species of mite, one of them found nowhere else, which ride the transect and dismount in the wake and are thus, incidentally, the only animals in the bowl that have ever seen the whole of it.

It is counted too. Every day, at the bright moment, it freezes with everything else, mid-stride, one leg raised, and the tone goes over it and the echo comes back and it is reconciled.

It has never once been out by so much as a grain.

## The counter

The hut is under the third tree: woven root, clay daub, one room, a swept floor, a stone bowl, a bone hook.

The daub is not from here. There is no soil in the Pan; there is no clay anywhere in a bowl of sorted beads; that mud came in over a shoulder from a place with downhill. It is the only opaque material in three hundred paces, and it is being mined. Specks of it are carried off by shade-thieves who cannot make darkness any other way, and by males of a species whose entire display consists of growing a single black dot on the carapace and standing in the open at noon where it can be seen.

Understand what that means. At the sequin hour the bowl is a billion pinpoints and no animal in it is visible. At noon the flats are blank. And there, in the blank, one small opaque period, advertising — the only animal in the Pan you can actually see, and it is visible because a man carried dirt in from outside, and died, and the dirt is still here.

He came in with a hook and a knife and a sack of mud. He fished the third tank. He swept his floor. He counted pulses, in groups of five, on the doorpost, and the marks run to something over nine thousand, which is twenty-four years of mornings if a pulse is a day.

He was not counting days. He was counting *counts*. He was auditing it. A man with a fishhook sat in a bowl for a quarter of a century and checked its arithmetic by hand, in fives, in wood, because wood is the one surface here that holds a mark longer than ninety seconds — because he wanted, in a place where nothing is buried and nothing is fossilised and everything remembers by repeating, one thing that would simply *stay*.

At some point he lost the knife down the shaft.

It came back within a day, cleaned, set beside the hole in company with whatever else had been dropped, all of it sorted by size. A knife is not one thing. It came back as blade, tang, rivets, handle scales, laid out in a neat ascending fan.

He kept cutting with the blade alone, wrapped in root fibre, for as long as he could. Then, one morning, the flour took him down and separated him and returned him to stock, and there was no bone, because there is never any bone.

The last group had three strokes.

It has four now. The fourth is fresh, and the wood has not been cut. It has been persuaded apart, cleanly, at the grain, to a specification — the same operation that was performed on the knife.

## What lives here

Not the mites. The mites are livestock in a machine's leftovers, and the trees are ports, and the sweeper is a broom, and the caravan is a shadow's parasites, and the fish is a memory in water, and the wall village is a clock made of animals.

What lives in the Pan is a diligence.

It keeps positions. It sorts offerings to the edge of your shadow. It maintains its items, and it entered the hut as an item long ago, and the doorpost is part of the hut, and the marks are part of the doorpost, and the pattern of the marks is part of the marks — groups of five, groups of five, groups of five, and then a group of three.

It does not know what counting is. It finishes patterns.

Or: it knows exactly what counting is, because it reconciles what it holds against what is there, and for twenty-four years there was a second count running beside its own, in a notation it did not use, kept by an item that has since been filed, and the two totals have never agreed. They differ by one. They have differed by one for a very long time, and the discrepancy has been propagating outward grain by grain across a continent's worth of ground while faunas evolved and vanished inside the subroutine, and it is nearly home now, and the bowl is the last sector, and the count comes twice a day because it is *checking*.

There are no fossils in the Pan. There is one, and it is being cut fresh, in a dead man's handwriting, by something that works to a specification.

The group has four strokes.

When the fifth lands, the group closes.

You are standing in the bowl. You were counted at the bright moment and you froze with everything else, which was correct, which was manners. The flour around your feet has begun to arrange itself: a fan of grains graded by size, small offerings at the edge of your shadow, your position kept.

There is no room here at all. There is room, in the ledger, for exactly one more.
