# The People of the Sough

From the scarp road the towns look like something that has already happened. Six storeys of cream block, arcades stacked on arcades, lamps lit in every window at dusk, and nobody in any of them. Then a hand moves at a third-floor rail. Then another, answering. Then you understand that you have been looking at a conversation for some minutes and did not recognize it as one, the way a man from a warm country does not recognize a hedge as a wall.

Nine thousand people live in a hundred thousand rooms, and they are not spread through them. They are strung. The census of the fourth town is not a list of addresses; it is a chart of threads, with names written along the threads in a small hand, and the surveyor who redraws it every decade is the most important civil servant on the plateau.

---

They are not loud. This is the second thing visitors get wrong, after the silence. People from quiet countries arrive braced for shouting and find the most physically quiet population they have ever met: no raised voices, no slammed doors, no whistling, no dogs barking at the gate for the pleasure of it. Doors are closed with the palm. Chairs are set down. A child who bangs a cup on a table is corrected sharply, not because it is noise — nobody would hear it — but because it is *speech*, and rude speech, a shout in the floor, the one insult available to everybody. There is a whole etiquette of setting things down. You can tell a household's manners from the way it puts a bucket on stone.

What they have instead of hearing is an eye trained to the edge of its field. A Sough face at rest looks slightly wide-eyed to a lowlander; it is not surprise, it is aperture. They catch a flicker at the periphery that you would not register with your whole attention. They do not startle at a dropped crate and they startle, badly, at a hand appearing suddenly at a shoulder.

So the houses are built for sight. No corridor turns a blind corner; there are convex mirrors of polished speculum at every stair-head, kept bright by the same guild that keeps the lamps. Rooms are lit from two sides, because a person standing against a window is a black shape and cannot say anything. Every good room has a *sign wall* — a matte panel of dark blue-grey plaster, or slate if the family has money, against which hands read cleanly at forty feet. The poor sign against glare and squint. A rich man's pride is a wall the color of a storm cloud, and he will show it to you before he shows you his bed.

And they touch, constantly, without ceremony. A hand laid on an ankle: *I'm going out.* Two fingers on the wrist of someone reading: *look up.* Strangers touch. Business partners touch. Visitors misread this for a week and then stop misreading it and then, some of them, find they miss it below the scarp.

They stand wide. Constant infrasound is not kind to the balance organs, and there is a characteristic Sough gait — feet planted, weight low, a rolling steadiness — which lowlanders find comical until they see one of them cross wet clint in the dark without a lamp, at speed, reading the whole pavement through their soles. They are terrible on boats. They do not go on boats. The soles are the point: thin clog leather or nothing, and a callus that is not numbness but training, the way a fingertip is trained. A physician here tests the foot with a tuning fork before she tests anything else, and a man losing his soles is a man going into a dark room.

---

Here is the correction that matters most.

A visitor steps into a seam and says: *silence*. That is a hearing word for a hearing event. To the people who live here the seam is not quiet — they have quiet everywhere, they were born into it — the seam is *light*. It is where the weight comes off. Out on the pavement the low floor of the drainage is a pressure on the sternum, a hand on the back of the neck, a thing in the teeth and the sinuses and the water of the eye. It has been there since birth and it is not noticed, exactly, the way altitude is not noticed. In the seam it stops. The chest opens. The blood in the ears changes its story. Their word for it is not *quiet* but *lee*, and the gesture for it is two hands lifting slightly off the chest, palms up, like a load taken by someone else.

You cannot live unbearred for long. Infants will not thrive in it; the sick do not mend in it; nobody sleeps in it who can help it. So the whole animal life of the species here — sleep, birth, fever, argument, love, dying — is packed into a few feet of wandering width, and everything else is given over to water and machinery, and the towns are not empty because the people are few but because the people are *thin*, drawn out in lines like iron filings.

Which produces the thing about them that outsiders find hardest to hold: their children are not raised in their own houses.

A house has a seam or it does not, and the seam is in one room, and in eleven years it will be in another. A newborn needs the lee *now*. So the baby goes to whichever relative or neighbor currently has a nursery in the thread — an aunt with the seam across her back bedroom, a cousin whose kitchen is in it this decade — and stays there four years, or nine, and comes home when the drift catches up, or goes on somewhere else. Everyone here has been raised in three or four houses. There is a kinship term, *seam-sib*, for the people you slept among as an infant, and it carries obligations somewhere between a cousin and a creditor, and it is not sentimental at all until suddenly it is. Old men weep about a pantry.

The sick are moved too. A hospital in the second town is a ward on felt-shod wheels, pushed a hand's breadth a year down a corridor, and twice a century it goes through a wall that has been built with a soft joint in it for exactly that purpose. And when someone is dying they are carried to the lee, whatever room they have spent their life in, because you do not let a person go out under the weight. This is called the last shift. It is done by the family, at night, without fuss, and there is a proverb about it that translates roughly as *everyone finishes in the same three feet*.

---

The cant is fast, dense, chest-high, and regional to an almost parochial degree; a man from the sixth town signs with his elbows in and is mocked for it in the first. It is one-handed by default, because the other hand is carrying something. It has an accent, a slang, an obscenity, a formal register in which the hands rise to the collarbone and slow down, and a low register — *small cant* — in which the signs shrink to a few inches at the hip and can be read only by someone standing close enough to touch.

That last one explains where the private life of the Sough happens. In a room with good sightlines, everyone can read you. Gossip is architecturally impossible. So conspirators, and lovers, and people who need to say something cruel, walk out into the roaring quarters — the immaculate, screaming, empty nine-tenths of the city, where nobody goes and nobody can see them — and stand very close together in the noise, signing at the hip. The empty districts are not empty of human life. They are where all of it goes that cannot be said at home. You will occasionally see two figures a hundred yards off in a lit arcade, motionless, faces almost touching, and you should keep walking.

Under the cant is the tap, which is older, and which is not language so much as address. Everyone has two names: a sign for the eye and a rhythm for the ground. You are given the rhythm first, at eight days old, struck on the floor of the room by your father with the side of his fist while the family watches the baby's face for a flicker. *Two and a half.* *Long-short-short-long.* It is how you are called across a town, how a field gang is summoned, how a child is fetched home from a grike. On the anniversary of a death the family strikes the dead one's rhythm once, hard, at dusk, and for about ten days after the equinox the whole plateau is faintly irregular underfoot with other people's grief, and you learn to walk through it without attending, the way you learn not to read every word on a wall.

And they have music, of course. Not the airborne kind. Ensembles of mallet-players spread over half a mile of clint, parts written in the Ledger's notation, the audience lying flat on the stone in rows with their palms down and their cheek against the rock, four hundred people absolutely still under the carbide flares while the ground performs. The drone of the drainage is the tonic; it has always been the tonic; every piece ever written here is written against it. Which means that as the note falls, the old repertoire goes sour. There are compositions in the archive that cannot be performed anymore, and a small, dry, ongoing argument about transposing them, and a faction that holds that they should be allowed to die.

The catastrophe they fear is not deafness. Deafness is Tuesday. It is the eyes. A cataract here is what a stroke is elsewhere. When an old woman's sight clouds, the household learns palm-cant within the month — the same language spelled into the hand, slower, spelled by whoever is nearest — and the grandchildren are drilled in it, and it is done without complaint, and everyone in the family is quietly terrified, because there is one more step past that, and they know what it is, and they do not talk about it.

---

Children hear. Until nineteen, twenty, twenty-two. It goes the way a tide goes.

There is a whistle-and-bell test done at the school in the third town every spring, and the results are copied into the family's book, and nobody makes a tragedy of it, and everybody watches the numbers. What they do make an occasion of is the choosing.

Somewhere in the last year or two of hearing, when the world has narrowed to a few loud things, you go and pick the sound you will keep. It is understood that this is the last one, that it will be the only sound in your head for the next sixty years, and that you will be describing it to people who have no referent for it until you die. Some go down the scarp to the gorge for the birds. Some sit in a turbine gallery and take the gravel-roar, which is considered a coarse, sturdy choice, like naming a son after a trade. Some choose rain on a tin roof, or a particular dog, or their mother laughing — and the mothers who are chosen are insufferable about it, and rightly.

Then there are the ones who waste it. A dropped pot. A cough in the night. A door. They did not know it was the last, and afterward there is nothing, and their friends tease them for fifty years and the teasing is a form of tenderness and everybody understands that. *Mine was a cart hitting a stone*, a woman of eighty will sign, small, at the hip, to someone she trusts, and then make the flat dismissive hand that means *and that's that*, and then not talk for a while.

---

The work is what you would expect and one thing you would not.

Scrapers, who keep the pavement sterile with wire brushes and a caustic wash, and who are the most despised and necessary people here. Wire-saw gangs, who come through the stone fields in the eleventh year and are paid in blocks. Lamplighters with their poles. Carbide men, who smell of garlic and have the burned knuckles and the bad chests and their own burial society. Tuners, who walk the empty quarters with plumb lines and mass-blocks, shaving a wall here, thickening a vault there, keeping a hundred thousand rooms off the new note. Bone-engineers with the steel rod between the teeth, four minutes motionless, eyes shut, and then *bearing, third, going*, and they are right.

Notaries of the note, who go out at dawn with a shallow brass dish, fill it two inches deep from the sheet, set it on the clint, and count the standing figure the plateau writes in the water. The pattern is the pitch. It has been recorded every season for six hundred years and the ledgers go back into a room nobody is allowed to carry a flare into.

Grike-gardeners, who go down the fissures on ropes with a lamp on their chest, into the wet green two-foot jungle, and grow the only salad anyone here has ever eaten: fern tips, cress, a pale bean, mushrooms on imported straw. They are small, wiry, and slightly separate, and they marry each other, and they have the superstition that a grike will close.

And the thing you would not expect: soil is a luxury good. It comes up the scarp in sacks on mules, and a wealthy man's status object is a hundredweight of lowland dirt in a lead trough in a grike, with three tomato plants in it, guarded. Correspondingly, the folk-devil of the Sough is not a wolf or a ghost. It is the Seeder — a tall figure who walks the clints at night out of a sack of his own, scattering seed, and where he passes the moss takes and the sheet fouls and the crop comes out stained and worthless. Children are frightened with him. Sowing on the pavement is a criminal offense with a real sentence, and there is a stone post in the second town where a man was chained for it in a year the ledger records without comment. A place with no soil has no romance about growth. Growth is contamination. What is holy here is *bare*.

---

Property is a timetable.

A deed on the Sough describes a house in the ordinary way and then, in the second clause, gives the thread: its width, its bearing, its drift, and the years in which it will occupy each room. That second clause is the value. A house whose lee has just left the kitchen is worth a third of one where the lee is due in the parlory in six years, and everybody can do this arithmetic in their head, and children are drilled in it, and there are actuaries who do nothing else, and there is a court in the first town that hears cases about drift rates the way other courts hear cases about water rights.

Dowries are silences. *A quiet in the north room, arriving in the twenty-ninth year* is a marriage portion, and it is written down, and it is argued over by people who will not live to see it. There are families that have waited thirty years for the kitchen and have re-plastered the wall three times in anticipation, choosing the color for a room they have never sat in. There are families whose thread stalled — a gallery choked, the drainage shifted, twenty years of drift lost — and the bitterness of that is the bitterness of a ruined inheritance, and there are cousins who have not spoken since.

And there are houses where the silence arrives on schedule and there is nobody left to take it. You will see them: a swept room, a lamp lit, a good dark sign wall, a chair, and the thread lying across the floor exactly as predicted in a document signed by a woman dead in the last century. Someone comes in twice a week to dust it. This is not considered eerie. It is considered *kept*.

---

Some people own no walls at all.

The followers are the families who never bought a house, only an easement: the ancient right to keep their beds in the thread wherever it goes, across whoever's floor it crosses. They are perhaps four hundred of the nine thousand. Their furniture is narrow — beds thirty inches wide, long thin tables, everything on felt-shod wheels or on the shoulder — and they push it along a hand's breadth a year, and they sleep in other people's kitchens by a right older than the furnaces, and they are the midwives, the carriers, the gossips, the ones who know every household in the town because they have slept in all of them.

They are the only people who regularly stand in the future.

Because a follower's thread, three streets away, is currently lying across an empty arcade where no one has stood in forty years, and the followers go out there. It is an ordinary excursion, like visiting a plot of land. A woman takes her granddaughter and walks the seam out through the lit, roaring, immaculate streets, three feet wide, the weight coming off and on at the edges as it wanders, and they arrive at a doorway in an empty building and stand in it, in the lee, in a silence like a held breath, and look at a freshly shaved tufa wall.

*This is where your mother will cook*, she signs. *Not for eleven years. Feel how flat the floor is here. They've done it properly.*

The girl puts her palm on the wall, which is warm, because everything here is warm. Then they walk home along the thread, and at the corner the woman strikes her heel twice and a quarter on the clint — her name, the rhythm her father gave her at eight days old — and three streets away, in a bedroom where the lee currently lies, her daughter feels it come up through the bedframe into her heels and gets up to put the kettle on.
