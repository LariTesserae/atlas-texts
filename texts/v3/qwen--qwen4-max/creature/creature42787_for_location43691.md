## The Four-Second City

There is a mite on this plateau that is awake for eleven minutes of its life.

The rest of it is glass. It dries down to a fleck the color of weak tea, legs folded under it like a closed hand, and it lies in the curl of a dead rind-scale for ninety days while the sun goes over. When the front arrives it takes four seconds to fill. You can see it happen under a lens: the body clarifies, the legs come out, and it begins to eat as if it had never stopped, because as far as it is concerned it has not stopped. It has ninety days and eleven minutes, and it spends the eleven minutes doing everything.

The film it lives in is a millimeter thick and it is the loudest place on the plateau. The lattice eats sound the way a policy eats it — selectively, according to what matters. It is tuned to the wavelengths of bodies: to footfall, to jaw-drum, to the frequencies a thing weighs. Above that band it does not bother. So the rind is a riot nobody upstairs can hear. Springtails detonate their little springs and the noise goes off into a register the floor ignores. Predatory mites hunt by the shiver a springtail's landing puts through the skin of the water film, which is a drumhead a millimeter across, and they are very fast, and they are screaming.

Thread through it with a lens and you find the other thing, the thing that was assembled. It does not look like the rest. Life on the pavement makes round shapes, blobs, beads, cushions, the honest geometry of surface tension. The assembled thing makes corners. Its cells are rectangular, its filaments branch at right angles, and it holds itself in a grid so regular that the mites graze around it in lanes, the way sheep graze around a fence. It has been there nine hundred thousand years and it has not drifted a micron out of true. The mites do not eat it. Nothing eats it. It is fed.

## The Lickers

The family calls them *the ones who go first*, which is a job description and not a name.

Donkey-sized, pale as the inside of a shell, no hooves, four splayed fatty pads that set down with the deliberation of a person testing bathwater. Their tongues are ribbed with silica they harvest from the rind itself, and they hone them on the stone, and this is the one sound they make that the floor does not swallow — because they make it *into* the floor, mouth to rock, direct coupling. A herd grazing is a hundred small files working in a room under your feet.

They walk in lines a kilometer apart and they never stop talking. Not metaphorically. A licker's gait is a four-beat sequence with no gap in it longer than a quarter second, and the interval and the weight are the grammar. A herd of four hundred is a sentence four hundred syllables long that repeats with variations for as long as the herd exists. Calves are born into the middle of it and learn to put their feet in the gaps. A licker that falls silent is dead, or frightened, or lying to you, and the herd knows within three strides which of the three it is, because the hole a body leaves in the pattern has a shape.

That is the whole of their interior life, as far as anyone can tell: the shape of holes.

They must stand, when the hawk is up, in a spacing that matches the panels. This is not a trick they perform. It is a thing they *feel*, the way you feel a chord resolve. A herd at rest under a noon glare settles into the local quasicrystal with an accuracy that surveyors have measured and disliked, and a licker standing out of true is physically uncomfortable, and shuffles, and is corrected by its neighbors with a nudge of the shoulder. Their word for the correct spacing — a two-beat press, heel and toe — is also their word for *safe*, and for *good*, and, as far as the family can determine, for *please*.

When one of them dies the herd closes the gap. It takes most of a day. Four hundred animals redistribute themselves around an absence, a slow shuffle, no urgency, no sound, and the seismic signature of it is distinctive enough that the flatjaws come from a kilometer off to read it. The family calls this *the mending of the line* and will not walk through one. They are not being sentimental. A herd doing that shuffle is not grazing, and a herd that is not grazing is not pressing dust into the pores, and there are places on the pavement where the dust has not been pressed in a long time.

## The Ones Behind

Glass ants come after the lickers and take what the tongues leave: the chaff, the torn scales, the mites that were too slow. They are transparent, and before dawn they stand at the mouths of their galleries with their bodies angled east and condense the front's vapor on their own cuticles and carry it down inside them, drop by drop, and this is the only irrigation on the plateau that anybody performs on purpose.

At the turn they seal their galleries with salt. They walk to the apron for it, four kilometers, a column of them, and take it from the crust the family has laid down. The family puts salt on the apron every circuit. The grandmother said *the ants need it*, which was true, and was not the reason, and was all she had.

Above the front work the sifters, long-winged, the trailing edges fringed like an owl's. The fringe is not for silence; everything here is silent. It is so that they do not stir what they eat. A hard wingtip sheds a vortex and blows the aerial plankton out of the band, and a sifter that flew like a normal bird would starve in the middle of its own dinner. So they fly like a thought crosses a room. They drink on the wing out of the glitter. They do not land, ever, except in the flutes of the tower at the northern end, where the comb-slots are exactly the width of a folded sifter and have been for longer than there have been sifters to measure them. They keep the flutes clear of dust and molted fringe. The tower needs its flutes clear to make its dawn rain. Neither party is aware of the arrangement.

## The Readers

Flatjaws are what the runnels are for.

Low, knuckle-walking, the whole front of the skull flattened into a plate with a fat acoustic pad along the jaw, eyes reduced to two dark grains that can tell noon from midnight and nothing else. They do not see the plateau. They *read* it, chin down, at about the pace of an old man reading a newspaper. They can distinguish a licker from a mender from a person at four hundred meters through the stone. They can tell you which sector turned last and how hard.

They eat the chaff and the ants and the slow calves, and this is a side occupation. What they mainly do is go to the mends.

A mend is new stone, whiter, precipitated along a crack, and to a flatjaw it means *the pores here are still opening*, which means *next season this will be worth grazing*, which means *be here*. So they lie along the fresh white lines with their jaws flat and they mark them: a knuckle press, a smear of gland oil, a signature that lasts about a century. Other flatjaws read the marks and add their own. Some of the long mends carry a hundred thousand years of annotation, layered, scraped, relaid, a palimpsest of grease. From the escarpment these lines look uncomfortably like writing.

They are writing. They are just not the writing anybody meant.

The flatjaws never enter the sinks. Wet clay deadens the plate; a flatjaw in a doline is blind and deaf at once, standing in a blank. So their world is a legible plain with nine holes in it, and they skirt the holes, and they will not tell you what they think is in them. They mate by pressing flank to flank for two days without moving, which is the only conversation they have that is not about the floor.

## The Deaf One

The hawk does not listen. It cannot. It is the only animal of any size on the pavement without a pad, and it hunts in the noon glare by reading the glory — that soft bloom of returned light that hangs around the shadow of your head — for the holes that bodies make in it. Everything that moves is a moving absence. It does not see animals. It sees subtractions.

There are two of them on the whole plateau. They divide the county by the sun and they have never met, and they could not find each other if they wanted to, because a hawk at three hundred meters is a hole in the light exactly like a stone.

Once a year they come down to a sink.

The water pockets are where sound gets out, and the hawk, which has spent its entire life in a bright silent field, drops into a doline full of nine trees and brown birds and is suddenly, unbearably, *heard to*. It sits in a tree with its feathers loose and its eyes half shut and it listens to a noise it has no organ for and cannot use and did not know existed. The other hawk comes to the same noise. They find each other by being loud, which is the only time either of them is loud, and afterwards they go back up into the glare and are subtractions again.

The family says the hawks come down to be married. The family is not wrong.

## The Loud

The birds are small, plain, brown, and absurd.

They live in the nine trees around each sink and nowhere else, and they sing at a volume that is frankly obscene on a pavement where a shout dies at eleven meters. At a sink it carries half a kilometer. They have the only surplus in the economy. Everything else on this plateau rations — water, seconds, sound, motion — and the birds waste all four, all day, because they are sitting on the one kind of ground where waste is possible.

Nine sinks, nine dialects. The songs drift, they diverge, a young bird that learns the wrong sink's song and moves is treated as a foreigner for the rest of its life, which is about four years. They mob predators in a mass and the noise of it brings flatjaws to the rim of the doline where they stop, blind, and wait, and this is the closest thing on the plateau to a crowd.

They mimic. They take the sounds the place actually has: the hiss of a dust devil with the top taken off, the knuckle-on-bread of a dropped stone, tent poles, the dry cough the dog makes instead of barking. At the fourth sink there is a phrase in the middle of the dawn song, four notes and a drop, that the family recognizes and does not like to hear, because it is their grandmother's calling-song, the one she used to bring the children in off the apron, and she has been dead forty years, and the birds have it note-perfect and have no idea what it is for.

## The Menders

Otter-ish, dust-colored, low, with a nose that is a tool.

The snout is callused and keratinous and finer than a fingertip. A mender goes into a crack headfirst and breathes into it and reads the warmth coming back, and knows the pore diameter, and knows what gauge of filament the crack wants. Then it goes and finds one. The under-weave sheds constantly — pale threads coming up through the pores to be cleaned by the rind — and the menders collect the shed ones, sort them by feel, carry them in the mouth, and pack them into the fissures with their noses, tamping, tamping, a small persistent hammering you can feel through your boots at two meters and hear not at all.

None of this is learned. A mender raised alone in a box will sort filaments it has never seen into gauges it has no names for. The craft has no apprenticeship because it does not need one; the specification is in the animal.

The family will not kill a mender. They cannot say why. There is a story about a great-grandfather's dog, and a stripe of pavement a hundred meters long at the southern end that went gray ninety years ago and is gray still, and does not green when the front crosses it, and the lickers will not graze it, and the family walks around it without discussing it, the way you step around a hole in your own floor.

Nothing punished him. That is the part that would frighten you if you thought about it properly. Nothing on this plateau punishes. A thing simply stopped being repaired.

The menders work the seam of the hatch, keeping the thorn back, packing the rim. They are the only creatures that go near it. Whatever is under it, they are not maintaining it. They are maintaining the *lid*.

## The Long Aunt

There is one more thing of any size, and it is out at night, and it does not have a pad in the sole of its foot. It has one in the sole of its foot and one in its jaw, and it uses the jaw one backwards.

It stands in the runnels. It is pale and long-limbed and about the height of a fin, and at night, in the ring-light, it is a fin. It hunts by absence: not by what presses the stone but by what stops pressing, the hole in the chorus where a body lifted a foot and did not put it down. It walks in the loud passages and freezes in the quiet ones. It has all night and it is not in a hurry.

And it speaks Licker.

It can put a footfall into the ground with its jaw plate, and it has the two-beat press — heel and toe, *good, safe, please* — and it will stand in a runnel at three in the morning and say *the spacing is correct here* to a herd two hundred meters off, and a young animal will shuffle toward it.

The lickers' defense is weight. A real footfall carries four hundred kilos of animal behind it and the stone reports the mass along with the syllable, and the long aunt is ninety kilos and cannot lie about that, and the herd hears the lightness and does not come. So there is an arms race here, and it is not about speed or teeth. It is about authentication. Every animal on this plateau spends part of the day with its chin on the ground verifying that a vibration is who it says it is.

During the pauses in the night chorus, everything that is still moving is visible.

The family says it is polite to stop too.

## The Eleven

They are named for the sink they were born beside, so there are only nine names among eleven people, and the duplicates are distinguished by an suffix that is usually an insult and always affectionate. There are two Fourths. One of them is Fourth-Who-Waits, because as an infant she would not move until the front had passed, and this was considered a defect at the time.

They talk by touching heads. Temple to temple, or forehead to forehead, bone conduction, a low mutter that goes skull to skull and dies at the skin. From ten meters away a family conversation is eleven people leaning together in pairs, mouths barely working, like a room full of people praying in a language of consonants. They have a callus at the point of the jaw where the plate of the mandible meets the stone, and they have all had it since they were six, and they think of it as a chin.

At the sinks they are loud. Embarrassingly loud. They shout, they sing badly, they laugh at nothing, the children scream for the pleasure of it, and every nineteen days the whole family gets briefly drunk on audibility and comes out hoarse. There are stone-manners and there are sink-manners and a person who uses sink-manners on the open pavement is regarded the way you would regard someone who undressed in a church.

The dog does not bark. It thumps: three strikes of a hind foot, hard, into the floor, and the lickers ignore it completely and the family answers it. Its footpads are noticeably thicker than a dog's should be. So are its owner's. So, in the last cool season, were the newborn's — and not callus, not thickened skin, but a proper fat acoustic pad in the sole of the left foot, present at birth, soft, warm, the size of a thumbprint.

The grandmother had a word for it.

That is the thing worth sitting down for. She had a word for it, which means it has happened before, which means the word is older than the four generations they can count, which means either the family has been here longer than it remembers or there were other families, and the fins that look like the ruins of walls are the ruins of walls, and somebody built them, and the pavement has been doing this for nine hundred thousand years and has all the time there is.

Under the tents, the under-weave has grown a mat. It is warmer there than the surrounding stone by about a degree, and it is soft, and they sleep on it and say the ground is kind at that spot. It moves when they move. It takes about four months to catch up. Along the fixed circuit of the Nine Sinks, at nineteen-day intervals, the pavement is now warm ahead of them by two or three days' walk, and has been for as long as anyone has checked, and they call this *the welcome*, and they do not find it strange, because it has always been there, because it is a road.

The salt they lay on the apron precipitates white. Their footpath presses dust into the pores. From the escarpment, the southern apron carries eleven long strokes, and they are the whitest mends on the plateau, and they are getting longer.

## The Tenant

There is no one in the tower. Understand this properly: there is no one in the tower the way there is no one in a lung.

The shaft goes down past the flutes into the lattice, and it is full of the under-weave, rope-thick, pale, wet, and the weave there is not the kind that comes up through the pores to be cleaned. It is the kind that does the cleaning. It has no center and it has behavior. Cut it and it withdraws from that sector for a season. It grows along tree roots because roots go where water goes. It grows toward the tents. It has put a filament up into the dog's water bowl three times this year and the family pulls it out and laughs.

It does not know anything, exactly. It knows that when a sector's biological throughput falls below a threshold it should open a sinkhole, and it does, and the trees come, and the animals come to the trees, and the accounting balances. It knows that a body pressing dust is worth more than a body not pressing dust. It knows that once every nineteen days it must stop taking water out of the air over ten thousand hectares and let the rind die back and let the pores close and rest, and everything with legs relocates without hurry, and for four minutes at the start of the turn every animal on the plateau stands still.

Nobody has explained the four minutes. Here is what it looks like from inside, which is not an explanation: the tower puts a pulse into the floor below hearing, and the floor carries it forty kilometers, and everything that can read stops reading and holds. It is not fear. Nothing comes. It is the pause a room takes when someone at the head of the table lifts a hand.

The family stops too. They have always stopped. It is polite.

## The Newest Organ

The child with the pad is nine months old and cannot walk, so she is carried, and when she is put down she puts her chin on the stone immediately, the way all of them do, the way you put your hand on a table.

She has eleven minutes of mite-life to watch and four hundred animals to hear and nine dialects of absurd brown birds and a hawk that comes down once a year to be married and a long aunt in the runnels saying *please* in a voice too light to trust, and none of it is strange to her, and she will grow up thinking that the ground is warm because ground is warm, and that families talk with their foreheads, and that you do not kill the small dust-colored thing that packs cracks with its nose.

At the turn she stops with the rest.

Her foot does not.

It lies flat on the pavement, the soft one, the left, and it presses — two beats, heel and toe, the interval a little wrong, the weight about nine kilos — and the floor takes it, and moves it sideways, and does not eat it, and somewhere north of the fourth sink a white line a hand deep gets one stroke longer.

To the east, the glitter is coming at walking pace, and the rind is going green in a band a hundred meters wide, and everything that lives here is waking up in the order it always wakes, and the accounting, which does not know anything, exactly, has found the number one higher than it was, and has begun, very slowly, to make room.
