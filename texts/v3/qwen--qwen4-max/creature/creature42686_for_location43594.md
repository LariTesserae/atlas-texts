## The Standing Population

Count the living and you will count weeds.

Everything else here is a rounding error. The biomass of the pale blades alone would outweigh every other resident thing by a factor no one has bothered to compute, and the blades are not even the largest. The timber-stipes are. A stipe puts up its horn-dense mast over four centuries and then spends six thousand years holding it, and beneath the peat, threaded through the buried courses like a lace through eyelets, the same organism runs on sideways, sending up a new mast every eighty meters, so that what looks like a forest is often one individual with a habit of repetition. There is a stipe under the eastern provinces whose rhizome has been traced by cut-and-listen for eleven leagues and has not yet been found to end. It is older than the collapse. It was a sapling in a room that had a roof.

And here is the thing about that: it grew into the room. All of them did. The gardens filled the negative space of the vaults — the aisles, the doorways, the light-wells, the gaps between piers — and packed those spaces so tightly that when the masonry finally came down and was rolled and rounded and worn into swells, the shape of the rooms remained, cast in living tissue. The thicket is a floor plan. A swimmer cutting a channel through the packed growth is cutting along a corridor that no longer exists in stone, and turns where a doorway used to be, and comes out into a widening that was once a crossing. The building survives as a vegetable. The tenders know the plan by these turnings and could draw you the elevation of a hall that has been rubble for nine thousand years, and they would be drawing weeds.

Nothing here defends itself. Nothing needs to. Push your hand into a thousand years of growth and it parts like wet bread.

## The Light

The second-oldest living thing in the world is a smear.

The lamp-paste is a bacterial strain, or a family of strains, kept in the grooves of every handrail and stair-nose in the world, fed on weed-sugar, glowing a blue-green so faint that it takes an hour of standing in the dark to receive it. It does not survive being left alone. It sours, or is overgrown, or dies of its own film, and it must be scraped and fed and divided and carried to the next groove, and this has been done without interruption since before the vaults fell. The chain of transfer is unbroken. There is no oldest individual; there is only an oldest continuity, which is the thing the tenders actually mean when they say a word that translates badly as *lineage*.

They carry it in their mouths. A tender keeps a starter under the tongue, a wad of paste and sugar the size of a plum stone, and replenishes the grooves by touch, and seeds new work by pressing the wad into the cut and smoothing it with a thumb. In a sweet bell, when two people meet who have not met in nine years, they open their mouths to each other, and the light comes out — dim, blue-green, enough to show the shape of a face and not its color. It is the greeting. It is also the practical question *is your culture still alive*, and the answer, if it is, means you have not been alone so long that you let it die.

When someone is dying they give the starter away. To a child, usually. Sometimes to a groove in a corridor no one uses, which is a thing they do when there is no child, and which the others find hard to look at.

In the crowns of the bells there grows a second domesticate: a pale aerial fuzz, the only life in the world that lives in air, ringing the lip of every sweet pocket where centuries of exhaled breath have fed it. It browns and crisps in sour gas long before a person feels wrong. The tenders read it going in. They also tend it, and carry scraps of it to new bells, and have opinions about which bell's fuzz is vigorous, and speak of a bad patch the way a farmer speaks of a sick ewe. It is the closest thing here to livestock, and it is a moss that eats their breathing, and they are fond of it.

## The Slow Beasts

There are no animals. There are four things the tenders treat as animals.

The brine fronts have names and reputations. One that rises at the confluence of three seams and crosses the western province in a season has been doing so, on the same course, since the tablets began; it is called something like *the visitor*, and its arrival is announced by knock ahead of it, and the mats bloom in a ragged line behind its passing like grass after a herd has gone. They speak of it as slow and reliable and a little stupid. They clear its path.

The silt storms are the ones they respect. A storm has a leading edge and a temper and a season of the eleven-year cycle in which it is likeliest to come, and one of them, in the deep south, has a habit of doubling back that has buried the same three seams twice in living memory. It is discussed the way one discusses a dangerous dog that belongs to nobody.

The burping grounds are sleepers. The tenders know where they lie, do not build there, and knock a warning when a hill of bubbles begins to rise, and there is a word for the particular stillness of a floor that is about to turn over.

And the tide is the breathing of the whole basin, and the tenders do not think of it as a beast, exactly, but they do think of it as somebody's, and they check it on the mark-stones the way you check that a sleeping person is still asleep and not otherwise.

## The Only Animal

The people are the fauna. This is not a metaphor; it is the census.

They are the only mouths in the world, the only warmth, the only things that move faster than a plant, the only eyes. Every frond here is soft because the single grazer that exists has been rationed for ten thousand years. The absence of spine and shell and toxin is not innocence. It is a clause. The gardens have no defense because an agreement has been kept, in tablets, by forty people per province, in every generation, and the terms are: eat the allocation, eat the fall, eat the pressed seed-cake and the red mud's crust of sugar, and do not eat the rows.

They are two degrees cold and thirty-seven degrees warm inside, and they are leaking, always. A tender standing in a bell raises the air of it by a degree over a season, and this is measurable, and is the reason that sitting close is the word for love. Their hearts beat half as often as yours and twice as hard. Their chests are barrels from a childhood of breath-hold; their eyes are large and dark-adapted and useless at color, which is why the color words have migrated. *Rust-orange* is now a taste, the specific iron of a certain seam. *Cream* is a give under the palm, a mat of a particular density. They still say the mats are rust-orange and cream. They mean it precisely. They have simply never seen it, and neither has anyone they descend from, for six thousand years.

Their labor is measured in jars. Air is carried up to the bells in inverted jars by people whose entire working life is the carrying of jars, and a chisel — one soft iron chisel, hammered patient over an afternoon — costs eleven thousand jars and two people's season of breathing. They know this number the way you know the price of bread. It is not a complaint. It is what a chisel is.

## Forty

In a province the size of a country, forty. The number is not a misfortune; it is close to a specification, and it has been stable so long that the tablets have a form for it, a census ruled into columns, one column per bell.

A tender may work a full life at a seam and meet three others. Courtship is conducted by knock, across a province, in a medium where the question arrives in an hour and the answer takes a season, and this is understood to be conversation. There are marriages of forty years conducted almost entirely in three-and-a-pause-and-two, with perhaps eleven meetings, and the meetings are not the marriage; the knocking is the marriage. Children are born at the rim, where the terraces climb into gray daylight, and are brought down at nine or ten, and the descent is the only ceremony anyone here has. A child comes down on the weighted stone with a line and a basket, four minutes, up, sit in the bell, down again, and by the second year has stopped hearing the hiss of the fall, and will hear it again, unbearably, in the thirtieth.

Their spoken language exists only in air, which is to say only in bells, which is to say rarely. It has a great many words for the taste of water and the condition of mortar and almost none for speed. It has no word for *animal*, because there was never a contrast to require one. Its word for *alone* is the same as its word for *unmaintained*.

Names are shift-numbers and seam-names and, occasionally, a plant.

## The Queue

Several cultigens cannot set seed without a hand. Their flowers open underwater and wait, and if no brush of rope-fiber comes they close and fall, and the waiting-time is a species-specific number, and everyone who works the seams knows roughly where in the queue things stand.

So there is a second population here, of the nearly-gone: a row of something under ten thousand years of overgrowth that flowers once a decade and has not been fertile in nine hundred years, kept alive anyway, cut back and cleared and brushed by whoever holds that seam, because the tablet says to. There are channels maintained for plants that no longer exist. There is a stretch of handrail, in a province with thirty-one people, whose lamp-grooves are repacked every year for the benefit of a species last recorded in a script nobody can read. They know it is gone. They keep the light on. When you ask why, they say the row is on the tablet, and they say it in a tone that indicates you have asked a question with an obvious answer, and possibly a rude one.

And there is the flower they call Two Hundred and Eleven. It grows against the inner face of an opened joint, where a lamp can be put into the crack and the original work is still crisp: parallel chisel strokes, mortar holding a thumbprint, a tally scratched by someone counting bricks who got to two hundred and eleven and stopped, presumably because the shift ended. The tenders brush that flower by lamplight with their faces six inches from the tally. They are finishing somebody's count. Nobody says this out loud, because it is not the sort of thing that needs saying, and because they are not sentimental people, and because it is simply what the row requires.

## The Dead

Rot works at a crawl, so the dead lie down where they fall and stay.

They are carried to a designated bay and weighted and laid in the peat, and the fall covers them at one sheet per season, and so a death is a date you can cut a core and count. This is how families keep their records. A grandmother is eleven sheets down and a little to the left of a seam that changed its taste in the year she died; you can go and put your hand on the layer. The peat is a ledger, and the laminae are undisturbed, one sheet per fall-season, everywhere except where a foot went, or a basket dragged, or a body lies. Which means the whole history of the people is a scatter of interruptions: a footprint is an event, a disturbed patch is a year someone worked here, a dark lens eleven sheets thick is a woman.

They read cores the way you read a parish register. They are, in the most literal sense available, the only thing that has ever happened to this place.

## The Top Course

When a tender packs a joint — and joints open, a hair a century, as the mortar gives up its lime — they press a thumb into the fresh mortar before it sets.

The makers did this. Every untouched face in every crack in the world has one. It was probably nothing: a smoothing gesture, a way of testing the set, a habit of the hand. The tenders do it too, and have done it for six thousand years, and they know that the crack will close and the work will be buried and that in some unimaginable later century someone will put a lamp into a joint and find, side by side, a thumbprint from before the vaults fell and a thumbprint from last week.

That is the correspondence. It is the only art here and the only message anyone sends into the future, and its content is approximately *I was here and the mortar was good*.

There is a woman in the north who has spent thirty years on the oldest tablets, the ones in the script no one has read since before the collapse, and she has gotten into them a little way. What she has found so far is: a seam cleared. A channel opened. A count of bricks. A number of jars. She has found one recurring word she cannot place, which might be a name, or might mean *again*, or might mean *as before*. She is not disappointed. She had expected maintenance and she found maintenance, and this is the most reassuring result available, because it means the thing has been held at its completion by hands like hers for the entire span, and there is no secret instruction she has failed to receive.

They do not say they live in the world. The idiom is different. The world was built down as far as it was built up, course upon course, and the tenders speak of themselves as the top course of it — the layer currently being laid, one brick per generation, at zero net height, by people who will not see the building rise because it is finished and their job is to keep it finished.

So: what lives here is a floor plan made of weeds; a light kept alive in mouths; four slow beasts made of water and gas; a moss that eats breathing; forty warm bodies per province, rationed, large-eyed, knocking; a queue of flowers waiting in order for a brush; the dead, laid down in sheets, legible; and a thumb, pressing mortar, in a crack, in the dark, at two degrees, in water that has been the same temperature since the beginning.

The flower opens. It will wait nine years. Somewhere a season away, someone has already begun the swim.
