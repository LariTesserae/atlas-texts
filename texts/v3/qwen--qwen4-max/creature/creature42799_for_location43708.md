There is one of everything in the Kettle, and nothing twice.

One plant. One mill. One rope. Three lamps on one circuit. Nine vats, but one retting, in nine stages of the same stalled process. And in a finger's depth of liquor at the bottom of each vat, more individuals than the town ever had people, dividing as fast as thirty-three degrees lets them.

The count depends on where you put the skin. That is not a mystery, only bookkeeping — but it is the first thing you have to settle about the resident, and every answer you try turns out to include something you meant to leave outside.

---

Begin with the large one, because it is the only thing down there that is *trying* to do anything.

It has no seed and no sex and no seedlings. It was multiplied by hand, once, by people with knives, and when the people stopped it went on multiplying by the only method left to it: falling over and rooting where it lay. It has no children. It has extensions. Nothing in the Kettle is its offspring; everything in the Kettle is its older parts.

And most of it is not earning. One or two percent of the sky's light gets through nine layers of leaf, which means that below the lid there is no meaningful photosynthesis at all — only cost. The earning surface is the lid itself, a green rind a few leaves thick, and hanging beneath it is seventy metres of body that the rind has to pay for. The Kettle's plant is a canopy with an enormous unpaid architecture under it, and the architecture is built accordingly: hollow stems, ribbed and fluted, cheap in carbon and expensive in nothing, leaves the size of doors hanging slack on petioles that have never had to hold anything up against wind. Nothing here has ever pushed on it except water, its own weight, and iron.

It does not drink through a trunk. It cannot: in saturated air at thirty-three degrees there is no transpiration, no pull, no lift. It drinks along its whole surface, everywhere at once, root and stem indistinguishable, a body that is all interface and no plumbing. It has no up. It has no down. It has a lid, and a distance from the lid.

And it lives in the inverse of the ordinary world. The pit exhales carbon dioxide; carbon here is free, and light is the only currency. So it builds enormously and earns almost nothing, and it makes up the difference by eating itself.

The nine vats are full of its own severed stems — cut from the same individual, in the last shift, by hands. They have been souring there for a hundred and eleven years, ammoniac, giving off a bubble every few seconds. The liquor leaks. The flumes leak. The mass roots into the sump and into the terraces and into every wet seam below the vats, and takes the nitrogen back. It has been living, in part, on the slow digestion of its own amputations for a century, and the digestion is performed by something else, and neither party can stop.

It has two behaviours. The first is the funnel: eleven metres of migration across the lid in a hundred years, eleven centimetres a year, the leaves of the northern quadrant going small and thick and tough, all of it bent toward eighteen minutes of white light on the floor at midsummer. That is the whole of its intent. It is the only motion it makes that is not defeat.

The second behaviour is leaning into everything that moves, and being pulped.

Its sting still works. Formic and sharp, good after a century, aimed at mammals that are still out there on the plateau in the fennel and the goat tracks. The defence is not obsolete. It is only unemployed.

---

Inside the dead core stands the thing that eats it.

White-rot, felted through the aged mass, a centimetre a year, and — this is the part that matters — *load-bearing*. The core has not fallen because the fungus is holding the shape of what it has removed. It is digestion doing the work of a skeleton. It is the plant's undertaker and the plant's bones, and the vault under the meeting hall's slate roof stands exactly to shape because a fungus is eating the vault at the same rate the vault grows.

Which means the fungus cannot afford to win. Eat faster and the vault goes, and the roof goes, and the lid comes down, and everything in the bowl ends together. Its appetite is capped by its dependence. A centimetre a year is not restraint; it is the only rate that has not yet killed it.

In the sealed dark of the warehouse it glows. Foxfire, faint enough that you need half an hour of standing still to be sure. It is only fungus. But it is fungus doing what glowing fungus does — advertising, spending a little sugar on a signal meant for insects, in a room where no insect has been for a hundred and eleven years, and where none can come. And the brackets on the warehouse door fruit into saturated, almost motionless air, and the spores fall straight down onto the parent. Broadcast to nobody. Sex without dispersal. The fungus is the only thing in the Kettle that is trying to say something, and it is saying it into a stone cup with a wet lid on it.

---

The bacteria are the citizens.

Everything large here is scenery for something small. In the vats the retting went as far as its own acid would let it and then stopped — not finished, not spoiled, soured and settled, a community holding itself at the edge of its own waste, respiring a bubble every few seconds, patient as a clock. They divide. They have generations. In a place where the plant is one hundred and eleven years old and the fungus advances a centimetre a year, they go from one to two in the time it takes a vat to fill.

They are the only ones who eat and grow and multiply in the ordinary sense. They are also the only ones with variation: nine vats, offset, drifting out of phase and back over the years, each a slightly different age of the same stalled process. Nine states of one thing. It is the only difference anywhere in the Kettle between one place and another, and it is a difference of a few tenths of a pH.

Above the blue line, where the gas thins and spores drift down from the rim, the ferns and liverworts are the immigrants — and the emigrants. They sporulate. Their children leave. They come in on the draught at the Sill and they go out on it, and they are the only residents with an elsewhere. The newest thing in the hole is a cable, and the ferns are already on it.

In the tufa, cyanobacteria lay down the grey. Consider what that means: the aprons and draperies and folded terraces are made by something alive, three millimetres a decade, and the wheel's bearings crusted over in the thirties and were carried around by the crust, and the crust became the housing. There is a bearing in the Kettle grown by bacteria to a tolerance nobody measured, and it has been running for ninety years.

The stone is not scenery either. It is the only part of the whole thing that keeps records. Eleven years of bales welded into a wall with the twine still on them. Three millimetres of drapery per decade. A blue line at the height where a candle stops. The plant remembers nothing except where the light was; the mill remembers nothing except what fits; the mineral remembers everything, in order, legibly, and cannot read.

---

The mill is the other old one, and it is as sterile as the plant.

It has no repair, no young, no seed, no elsewhere. What it has is accretion: a rim that is now a drum of grey tufa, handrails inside a finger of calcite, bearings that grew themselves into fit. It is becoming stone at the same rate it moves. It is a reef with an iron core, and the iron core includes lignum vitae from a tree on the other side of the world, water-lubricated since the day it was installed and not yet worn — the only immigrant in the bowl that has neither died nor learned to reproduce.

Its anatomy is a set of clearances. Its organs are the shapes of its own movements: a nine-metre annulus polished by its own passage, a sleeve around the line shaft lined with the scar callus of a century of shoots, a glazed slot two metres by eleven centimetres where the pump rod has been going up and down through living wood since before anyone alive was born. Stop it and those rooms close in a season. It does not occupy space; it *maintains* space, continuously, by moving through it, and the space is the only body it has.

So: the plant makes, the mill keeps open, the water pays, the stone remembers, the fungus holds it all up by consuming it, and the bacteria do the eating that lets the plant eat. Draw the skin around any one of them and you have cut through something that is load-bearing.

---

And then the loop closes, and this is the thing you only see once you have stopped counting.

The decorticator still beats, nine strokes a minute, in a chamber you could stand in. The vats still fill and break their siphons. The Walk still lays hawser, three centimetres a day.

There is no crop anymore. Nobody has cut bast into the launders for a hundred and eleven years. What the beating arms meet, at the margins of their swept box, is the plant leaning in — the same individual that fills the houses and plugs the stair. It reaches, it is cut off, it is washed down, it is retted in the liquor of its own older stems, it is beaten and laid into three-strand hawser, and it walks out of the shed and up the buried stair and over the lip into the sun.

The mill has begun to harvest its inhabitant. The plant is manufacturing itself into a limb, and the limb goes out of the hole, and out there it dries and bleaches and cracks and is undone into fibre and dust by the soil cousins of the fungus that holds up the roof. Made at one end, unmade at the other, at the same speed. Two hundred metres, eighty years, three centimetres a day: a hand's breadth of hawser spends about eighteen years in the dark before it comes over the rim, and then not long in the light.

It is not reproduction. The plant sets no viable seed and the rope carries none; nothing is being sent anywhere; nothing can found anything. It is the one thing the body makes that it cannot keep. It is a moult, performed continuously, by a thing that has no skin to shed and no season to shed it in, and it is the fastest process in the Kettle by two orders of magnitude — the only part of the resident that moves at a speed a person could watch.

The plateau people call it the tail. They cut it once and it came back. Now there is a fence, and a welded gate, and a sign in two languages that is the only name the thing has been given: a description of what it does to you. There is a solar panel and a cable and a sensor hanging eleven metres below the lid, logging carbon dioxide, temperature, humidity, sound pressure level. Four flat lines. A biography with no events in it, kept by strangers.

That is the whole of its remaining relationship with the world. It has one immune system and it does not own it: a wire fence, repaired by the species it killed.

---

The people are not here. Nothing of them is here that could be called a presence. What is here is geometry — four hundred and eleven steps of plug, a vault that fits a slate roof, ninety-one rooms cast solid with the boots still in them — and physics: the quiet rooms still exist, the northeast corner of the hall, the lee of No. 4 vat, the landing on the two hundred and ninth step, seams of near-silence in a bowl that holds its own hundred and four decibels. They are still there and nothing needs them. Courtship happened in them. The two hundred signs are the one thing that did not survive, because they were never in the iron or the wood, and there is nothing down there now that has anything to say or any difficulty hearing it.

The lamps throb at thirty-eight pulses a second, twice per turn of the magnets, the wheel's beat made visible in three rooms. It is the only thing in the Kettle fast enough to be a nerve, and it carries no information; it only carries itself. A filament run at a third of its voltage does not evaporate and does not fail. It is the one thing here that has been kept alive by being underworked.

---

When it ends — the stream finding a new fissure, or the lid growing heavy enough to bring the roof down, four hundred years or four thousand — nothing will succeed it. That is the particular character of this resident: one of everything, and no next. The bacteria will go out in the water and on the ferns' spores. The ferns were always leaving anyway. The wheel will stop and its annulus will close in a season, and the shafting will seize, and the Walk will stop laying, and the first thing anyone outside will ever know about the end is that the tail did not come back.

Then the lamps go out, and the fungus, released from the bargain that has kept it to a centimetre a year, finishes the core, and the vault goes, and the lid comes down into the bowl.

What might last is the tufa: a smooth-walled stone room in the exact shape of a wheel's turning, with nothing in it. A cast of a gesture. It is the only copy anything in the Kettle will ever make.

Until then it goes on at every speed at once — nineteen cycles a second, four turns a minute, forty-one minutes, three centimetres a day, eleven centimetres a year, three millimetres a decade — carbon-rich and light-poor, eating itself through a third party, holding itself up with the thing that is digesting it, leaning toward eighteen minutes in June, and into every moving part, and being pulped, and keeping the rooms open by being destroyed at their edges.

It is not surviving. Nothing here is being tested. It is only continuing, which is a different verb, and the hole was chosen for exactly that.
