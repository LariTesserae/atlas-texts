# What Lives on the Tableland

Nothing lives in the rooms.

This is the first correction you have to make, standing in a cell the size of a coffin with your shoulders turned and your palm on the weeping wall. The chambers are not dwellings. They were never dwellings. They are the shaped interior of a water-system, the way a pipe's inside is a pipe's inside, and no animal on the plateau has ever treated one as a home. There is no dry floor to lie on, no still air, no dark that stays dark. The rooms belong to the fog and to the film.

Everything alive here lives in the joints. In the seams where a block sits in its settling-dish. In the lip no thicker than a fingernail where one basin hands to the next. In the thumb-sized weep-holes, the wicks, the hairline gaps between courses, the leeward corners where dust collects. Life on the tableland occupies the tolerances — the small unfinished spaces between the enormous finished ones. The architecture is empty. The plumbing is inhabited.

---

## The film

The most numerous resident is also the one you will not see until you lie down on the wet flags and put your eye a hand's breadth from the stone.

The water film that walks down every fluted face is between a tenth and a third of a millimeter thick, and it is unbroken, and because it never falls and never breaks into turbulence it is *continuous* — one connected skin of moving water from the windward combs to the last sump, ten thousand square kilometers of surface with no edge in it anywhere. This is a habitat. It has been a habitat for a very long time. It has an fauna.

Diatoms in it, in drifting rafts. Bacteria in a mat so thin it is only a slickness. Rotifers in the basins, which dry out in the retreat and revive in the return, and which have not had sex since before the limestone was lime mud; a basin's population is one animal, repeated, and the repetition has been going on longer than there have been vertebrates.

And a springtail. Pale, slow, about a third of a millimeter, and it cannot jump. Its ancestors could — the springing organ is still there, folded, useless, a relic under the belly like a sewn-up pocket. In a laminar film, jumping is death: you leave the water, you land on dry stone, or you go over a lip into a groove whose film is thinner than you are. Everything that jumped was lost. What is left is a lineage that walks, on the surface tension, upside down along the undersides of aprons, across the ceilings of stacked chambers, following the caustics it cannot see.

Its whole world has two dimensions and a slope. It grazes the mat and is eaten by a predatory mite of the same size and the same flatness. Over generations the populations drift downhill, because walking downstream is easier than walking up, and the uphill districts would go sterile if they were not reseeded — and they are reseeded, constantly, on the feet of birds and in the dried mucus of snails. The film's road network is not the film. It is the animals that cross the dry gaps.

Everything here is a surface-dweller. Nothing on the tableland has volume to spare. The vegetation is two-dimensional, the water is two-dimensional, and the animals have followed them into flatness.

## The graziers

The pale snail is four millimeters across and its shell is thin enough to read the lichen through. On a good weeping face in the wet season there are several thousand to the square meter. There are more snails on one windward comb than there are people in most nations, and the comb is one wall among ten million walls.

They eat the crust. This is the whole of their life's work and it is more consequential than it looks, because the crust is the map. The black lichen grows only in the drainage grooves; from the air the hydrology of the plateau is written in fine dark lines; and the snails graze those lines preferentially, because the groove lichen is softer and wetter than the territorial crusts on the open faces. Where they graze, the groove stays open. Where they cannot reach — the deep narrow cuts, the sub-lips — the crust thickens and the flow slows and the moss above it swells and throttles further.

So the snails are the drain-cleaners, and they are also, without knowing it, the editors of the only legible plan of the city.

When the wet line retreats they seal themselves into the seams with a disk of dried mucus, a little lens, and they wait. When it returns they wake in sequence. The waking moves across the plateau at a few meters a day — the same pace as the retreating water, the same pace as the eight-day crucifer, so that the flowering is not an event but a *line*, a white front traveling south across a city the size of a country for most of a season. From above, in the clear hour, you would see a thin bright edge crawling over the scale-mail of mirrors, and behind it a wave of animals unsealing, and above the wave the harriers, shifting.

That is the only migration on the tableland. It is made of things that individually move about two meters in a lifetime.

And they die in the seams. Their shells go on accumulating there, packed in the settling-dishes under the moss, dry-season layers alternating with wet, a stratigraphy a few centimeters deep and forty thousand years long. The moss roots in it. The shell-bed holds water better than stone does; it buffers the seams; it keeps the soft tissue alive through the retreat. The snails build the sponge that regulates the water that grows the food that makes the snails. Nobody designed this. It is what happens when you leave a closed loop alone for long enough.

The shell-beds are also the only archive. If you want to know what the weather here did in a year when there were no words anywhere, you dig a seam with a fingernail and count.

## The governor

The moss is the closest thing on the plateau to an intention.

It swells wet and shrinks dry and thereby throttles every handover in the system, and it does this at ten million lips at once, and the aggregate of those ten million small stupid decisions is the water distribution of a landform. There is no other regulator. There was never a valve.

But moss does not respond to this morning. It responds to the last few seasons, because the throttle is set by the bulk of the cushion and the bulk of the cushion is last year's growth carried on the dead stems beneath it. The memory is in the peat. Which means the control loop runs with a lag, and a control loop with a lag does not settle — it oscillates.

The city breathes. Wetness piles up in the windward districts until the moss there is at full swell and passing everything through, and the surplus goes leeward and wakes the moss there, and the wave reflects off the far terraces and comes back. The period is somewhere around sixty years; nobody has ever measured it, because measuring it would require one observer for three generations, and the fog makes it impossible to see anyway. There are districts that are wet out of turn. There are quarters that have been quietly out of phase with their neighbors since before the escarpment forest had its present species. The crucifer flowers there in the wrong month and no one notices.

Nothing that lives here has ever felt a whole breath. The newts should — they live long enough — but the cisterns fill from the bottom and buffer everything, so the newts live in the one part of the city that has no weather.

## Forty names

At dusk the midges come off the basins in columns, and the dark swift-like birds take them over the fog floor, and this is the loudest life gets: a thin ticking call, close, with nothing behind it, eaten by the moss before it reaches the next wall.

The birds navigate by the drips. There are about forty. Each one is a flaw — an unfinished ceiling, a bad seat, a course that never got its final cut — the only places in ten thousand square kilometers where water is permitted to fall, and therefore the only places that make a sound with an *irregularity* in it. In a city of perfect laminar silence, a defect is the only thing that can carry information. Forty defects. Forty hearths.

The map is not a picture. It is an itinerary: a fixed sequence of syllables, each one a drip, each leg between them a number of wingbeats and a bearing held against the hiss of the combs. No bird knows all forty. Lineages hold segments, overlapping, and the young learn by being flown the route behind an old bird until the route is in their body. The complete map exists — but only in the aggregate, distributed across a population that is constantly being eaten.

Names go out. A flaw heals with calcite, or widens until the drip becomes a runnel and stops being irregular, and the chamber goes quiet. First the old birds still detour and wait, circling a silence. Then the young ones turn but do not wait. Then they glance. Then it is only a chamber. A word dying here takes about four generations and looks, from outside, like nothing at all.

The harriers keep the dead names longest.

There are about a hundred of them, one per hundred square kilometers, pale, long-lived, silent, wings in a shallow V at wall-top height, quartering fog. They hunt the drip: they hold a handful of the named places and wait near them, because that is where the swifts will be. A young harrier inherits a territory and learns its ambush sites by watching where the birds it kills have come from. It is a conservative animal, territorially and temperamentally, and it will go on sitting beside a chamber that stopped dripping two centuries before it hatched, because that is where the old bird sat, and the sitting is the inheritance.

So the oldest entries in the city's only self-representation survive in its predators. The map is held in common between the thing that names and the thing that eats the naming, and neither has the whole of it, and neither knows there is a whole.

If you are looking for a person here, the harrier is the nearest candidate: an individual you could recognize, with a notched primary and a favorite apron and a grudge, attending. It is the only resident that listens rather than merely receives. It is listening for supper. That is what attention is for.

## The negative city

The volume of the emptiness equals the volume of the standing. There is a second city on the tableland, made of the holes, and it is full of cold water and no light, and the newts live in that one.

Pink-gilled, slow, never metamorphosed, some of them older than any bird on the plateau. They feed on insect fall and, in the dry years, on each other, and the cannibal years are written in the size classes if you can catch enough to measure. Each cistern holds a lineage; the cisterns connect only through submerged inlets; a newt that swims from one to the next is the only thing on the tableland that has ever crossed a threshold. They move a few meters a year through absolute dark, through the slots, into the next chamber, and the gene-flow of the whole plateau therefore traces the plumbing exactly. The relatedness of newts is a diagram of the drainage. You could reconstruct a district's water-system from the family trees of its animals, and it would be more accurate than anything you could survey on foot in a month.

They have never seen the sun. They live in the part the builders took away.

## The slowest, and the ones who left

The lichen crusts are individuals, some of them, and their borders have moved a hand's breadth since the ice. A single grey-green territory on a leeward face may have been one organism for forty thousand years, eating the city at a millimeter per millennium, abutting a neighbor it has never crossed. The black one in the grooves may be doing something else — there is a suspicion, unproven and unprovable at these rates, that it precipitates calcite as it grows, that it is not eating the grooves but re-cutting them, laying down stone at very nearly the rate the frost takes it. If that is true then the deepest cuts in the city are not merely the last to go. They are being maintained.

The builders are not here and I do not think they are hidden anywhere. There is no tool mark on the plateau, no lever purchase, no spoil, no scaffolding hole, and no wood and no metal to make a tool from. But there is frost, and there is slope, and there is water that will walk into any line you cut. Cut a shallow groove; let the film find it; wait for winter; the block comes free. Cut a shallow ramp; let it freeze; the block slides, a hand's breadth a night, downhill on ice, and you guide it with your hands and your patience and nothing else. That is a technology. It is the same technology the frost is using now, in reverse. It builds at the speed it erodes. A district would take longer than a language.

Whoever did that did not need to be clever, or many, or in a hurry. They needed to be the kind of thing that could hand a groove to its grandchildren. And then they went down the escarpment, into the wet forest, into the rain the city exhales, and they are there now, drinking it, with a word for the cloud that does not move and a reason not to climb it. Their descendants are made of this water. That is the only continuity available and it is not nothing.

## The second building

Here is what the residents are doing, all of them, together.

The wet faces are being dissolved and the lee corners are being filled. Shell in the seams, peat under the moss, dust and lichen fragment and midge-fall in the leeward angles, a few centimeters of soil where there was no soil. The mass of the city is being taken off its windward surfaces and laid down in its joints and its corners, a hand's breadth at a time, by animals that have no idea there is a city. It is the same operation the builders performed. It has the same tools — water, frost, gravity, patience — and it runs at the same speed, and it has been running without interruption since the last hand let go.

The place is not abandoned. It is under construction by things that will never know it.

And there is one more resident, not yet arrived. The unfinished chamber with the flaw in its ceiling: the frost has been working that flaw since before the birds had a name for it, and it is wider now than the groove that made it, and one afternoon — nine hundred years, three thousand, it does not matter — a slab the size of a door will finish its settling in a quarter of a second.

It will be the first thing to fall here. It will make a splash. Nothing on the tableland has ears for a splash; there has never been one; there is no response in any lineage to the sound of water falling. The swifts will go up in a sheet and the harrier a district away will lift its head and the snails will not do anything at all, and then the fog will close, and the moss will begin, at its own speed, to grow over the break.

The name will go out. The map will heal around the gap the way moss heals over a stone. And in the seams beneath, the graziers will go on laying down shell, filling the hole, as they have filled every hole, building the city back into a different order without ever once looking up.
