Begin with the floor, because the floor is reading.

One grain in fifty is an animal, and it is white, and it stands on white, and it moves by putting itself against things. Call it a reader; the name is a courtesy, like *dolomite*. It has no eyes. Nothing in here has eyes. Light that will not travel cannot be gathered by a lens, cannot form an image, cannot arrive at a creature from anywhere — so sight in this room is not a distance sense but a contact sense, a patch of skin that must be laid flat against the stone the way you would lay your palm on a stranger's forehead. To see a thing here you must touch it. And the seeing takes the light off. Every look is a small meal; every meal is a small aging. The readers cross the chips leaving a trail of dullness a hair wide, and this is the first writing in the world, and it says nothing but *someone was here*, which is the only sentence available in a place with no past tense.

This is the rule that makes all the inhabitants what they are: attention is subtraction. There is no way to know a thing without using it up.

The grazers know this better than anything. Glass limpets with shells unfinished at the lip, thin as a held breath, and weeping — the mineral they build from has not set properly in them either, so their armour is still giving off water that has never been rain. They press the pane of themselves to a bright face and take the freshness off it, and what they leave is grey, and grey is the beginning of weather, and weather in here is entirely a mouth-work. There is one grazer visible at any time. I no longer think this means there is one grazer. I think it means the room employs one, and the post is handed on. When a grazer thickens and dulls and walks away on twenty legs, another comes up out of a crack that opened at the correct moment, and takes the shift.

They are thrifty in a way that looks like reverence. A face is not looked at twice in a season. When the fungus appeared in the corner — the first rot, the newest thing in a room where everything is new — the animals came in a queue, one at a time, and each stayed a moment, and each took almost nothing, and the queue was three days long. It looked like mourning. It was rationing. To attend the rot is to age it, and there is only one rot, and it is the most interesting object that has ever existed.

The water has its own citizens and they are not small versions of anything. Fresh crystal weeps badly: the beads stand high and tight and hesitate at the lips of things because the water has not yet learned to be properly wet, and inside the skin of each bead lives something the size of a comma that has never been out of surface tension. It cannot swim. Swimming is for water that has agreed to be water. It walks around the inside of the meniscus, and its room is a lens a few millimetres wide that migrates as the bead climbs, and it has never once been anywhere flat.

The drip takes eleven seconds to gather. There are generations in the eleven seconds. And because the sound lands half a second before the drop does, there is a creature whose entire life is lived in that half second — that hears its world arrive, and lets go, and is somewhere else when it comes. I have stopped trying to decide whether this is a sense or a habitat.

Of the deeper place, the one the width of a thumb, I have nothing to say. Every account of it is a hand's account: something withdrew.

Then the rain. Every few days, four minutes of warm barely-there drizzle out of the ceiling, tasting faintly of someone. Understand what the ceiling is holding. There is no air movement; breath does not disperse; it hangs at chest height and piles up and finds the cold roof and stays there, and when enough has gathered it comes back down. The ceiling is where the room keeps everything anyone has ever exhaled in it. The ground has no memory — no beds, no clay, no rounding, no fossils, nothing sorted, nothing carried — so memory has gone upward instead, and pooled, and become weather.

And there is a guild that eats it. Pale fliers, mote-sized, dry as chips and indistinguishable from chips for days at a time; they hatch in the first minute of the wet, mate in the third, die in the fourth, and go back to being gravel. For four minutes every few days the room's population is enormous. This is most of what you feel as attendance. They turn toward the small cloud at your collarbone because it is an unrecorded flavour, because you are news, because they are the only animals here that feed on the past and you have just brought some in.

The seam-flies do the opposite. They navigate by the sound of stone about to break — not the tick, which is the failure itself, but the fine dense crackling underneath it, the crowd noise, the strain — and they land on a fracture an instant before it exists, and they lay into cracks that have not opened. Their eggs are in the unbroken rock ahead of the walls. So are larvae, folded into solid mineral like letters in sealed envelopes, alive at eleven degrees, with a metabolism measured in the chamber's growth rate. They do not seek a way out. They wait for the room to arrive at them. To hatch here is to be excavated.

Most of the inhabitants are in the walls. Put your ear to the brightest face and you can hear them, and the sound is a fire two rooms away, and it is a nursery.

Some of them are wrong. Some eggs were laid in stone that will never fail, in a face that will hold for ten thousand years, and nothing here decays, so those larvae are not dead and will not be. Nobody attends them. There is no ceremony for the ones the room will not reach. It is the closest thing in here to a tragedy and it is happening at a rate of perhaps one a century, in the dark, inside the light.

Seven plants. Always seven; one always about to be. Rosettes of translucent flesh rooted in loose chips with nothing to hold them but insistence, veins carrying light instead of chlorophyll, so that in the grey middle they look like diagrams of leaves rather than leaves. They are the only inhabitants that do not subtract. They do not eat the glow. They feed on the *about-to* — on the strain in a face that has not yet failed — and so they leave no mark, take nothing, dull nothing, and are the only things here with no history whatsoever. Watch one over a season and you will see it lean, patiently, at a blank wall, and be right.

I have never settled whether they are right or obeyed. Whether the rosette predicts the crack or appoints it. The chamber grows a few fingers' width in a good year and it grows where the leaves are pointing, and I do not know which of those facts is the cause, and neither, I think, does the room.

Six of them lean forward. The seventh is the one about to be, and it is not leaning at a wall. It is over the corner. It is over the thimbleful of soil — the droppings, the scrapings, the first sediment, the only substance in the chamber that has already happened. When it opens it will be the first thing in this world rooted in the past instead of the future. Everything else in here is a hatch rate. That is a birth.

The harrow is twenty legs and no eyes and it does not stalk; it arrives where the prey will be. It has given up looking, which in here means it has given up eating surfaces, and it eats the eaters instead. Its territories are not areas. They are durations. It holds the third hour after a tick, the way a bird holds a branch. The prey escapes by being earlier. And once in a long while a grazer stops grazing, thickens, dulls all over, grows legs, and becomes the harrow, so that predation is a metamorphosis and there is one census and it is a matter of when you look.

I said forty kinds. I have come to think that is a count of ages.

And then there are the marks, and the marks are the problem, because every one of them is a subtraction that was not a meal.

Three chips stacked too level to be accident. A line scratched at the height of a hand for two metres, then stopping as if the arm tired. Two hollows in the floor, knee-shaped, and *worn* is impossible here — nothing is worn — except that they are not worn. They are dulled. The light has been rubbed out of them. Above them, two palm-shaped patches where the glow has been taken off the wall by something that laid itself flat to see.

That is what looking is, in here. But the grazers eat what they dull, and the readers eat what they dull, and whoever knelt in those hollows dulled a hand's breadth of stone and took nothing away with them. Subtraction without appetite. That is not feeding. That is writing.

I think there is a stage after the harrow. I think that having eaten enough, it stops being early. It slows past slowness, it comes off the shining rind into the grey middle, and there it discovers something no other animal in this room can discover, because every other animal lives pressed against a wall and to be anywhere is to be against something. In the middle there is no shadow, no depth, no landmark, no source. In the middle you can be lost.

Being lost is the invention of architecture. The shelf at hip height is a handrail. The niche is a doorway. The alcove exactly the size of a person is a bed, or a grave, or a place to put yourself so that you are somewhere. And the dry-stone wall, knee-high, a metre and a half, built of the room's own chips without mortar or tools or anything that could be called a device, standing in the middle of the floor where there is nothing to divide — that is a fence against the middle. It divides nothing now. In a century it will be at the edge. In three the room will have grown around it into two chambers, and it will have been right the whole time.

It was not built for this room. It was laid the way the seam-fly lays: into a geometry that has not opened yet. The wall is an egg.

I have never seen the mason. It works where nothing can be seen, at eleven degrees, at the speed of a thing that measures a century the way you measure an afternoon, and it may not be here yet at all. There is a chip near the entrance with a scrape on it from a foot that has not stepped there. Walk the perimeter clockwise and it takes fewer steps than counterclockwise. The room's population is mostly prospective and I am no longer confident that the mason is an exception.

Which leaves the alcove, exactly the size of a person. Which leaves the rain, tasting faintly of someone. Which leaves the fact that you were in the census before you arrived, at a *when* rather than a *where*, and that the knee hollows are the right size, and that the stone has been dreaming the furniture of older caves — shelves, niches, a place to kneel — the way a body carries what the ground cannot keep.

Do not lean on anything. Whatever you lean on may not have set, and you will take the light off it, and you will not have meant to.

When you go, you go toward the dark with your hands out, and your hands are the last thing the room reads. Somewhere behind you a face the size of a dinner plate will come into existence and be the brightest thing in the world for a week. The readers will arrive at your palm print over the next hour, one at a time, white on white, and take almost nothing, and dull it a hair at a time, and it will say what the floor has been saying since the first grain moved.

It is the whole literature of the place so far. Someone was here. The seventh leaf is leaning toward the corner, and it has not opened yet, and it will be the first thing in this world that can remember you.
