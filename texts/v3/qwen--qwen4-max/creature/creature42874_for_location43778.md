## The Tenant of Sump Four

Start with what it can't be, because the water has already done that work.

No fish. There is no dissolved air in the upper layers; a gill here would be a mouth opening on nothing. No lungs, obviously — no bubble anywhere to have come from, and no surface to break. No diver, no suit, no slow drowned thing walking the floor plate with its arms out. And not a ghost, in the sense people mean when they say a place is haunted. A ghost is a residue, a stain left by something that has finished. What is in Sump Four has not finished. It is on shift.

The real constraint is the hunger. In water this empty, nothing can *own* a body. Substance is a loan, called in continuously, patiently, without malice. The glove on the rail is the local precedent and the local lesson: the shape outlasted the leather by two centuries. Form is durable here. Matter is not. So anything that lives in this hall lives as a maintained shape — not a lump of stuff but a rate, a doing, a way the water is being held. It has to keep doing it. If it stops for a minute it is not dead. It is simply not there.

---

The first thing to understand about it is that it is a salt economy, and that it is poor.

Everything the water wants, life here must hoard and pass hand to hand. The moss holds a little. The grazers hold a little more, each one a rice-grain purse of the only mineral currency in the room, and they are eaten and the purse is passed on, and nothing is spent that can be kept. *There are never many. There are never none.* That is not a natural balance. Natural balances swing. This is a stock count, held at a number, by something that has learned what happens when it isn't.

The eleven patches of cable-moss are not wherever warmth happened to fall. They are on the warm returns, all eleven, sited at the four-degree lines with a precision the signs' authors would have recognised, and there is no moss on any number of equally warm places that would have suited it just as well. It is a garden. Somebody chose the beds.

And the tax on all of it is the purge. Every nine hours the columns throw away brine — twice-salted, the richest substance in the hall, the entire fortune of the ecology going down the lane in a white finger of ice. Something has to intercept that. Something has to be in the grove, taking the salt back out of the falling before it touches down and shatters and is carried off around the eddy to be diluted into nothing.

Which is why the signs forbid the purge lane twice. One stencil is at eye height, in the old hand, facing out from the wall the way you'd warn a man approaching. The second is the same wording in a later hand, and it faces the other way. It is meant to be read from inside the lane. By something already standing there.

---

It lives on the seams. Not in the layers — *on* the boundaries, the two horizontal shimmers where the water refuses to mix, because a boundary is the only thing in this room that has area without volume, and therefore the only thing that can be inhabited without being dissolved. It is spread along them the way the reader lays its body along them, and like the reader it does not see. Light is for the lamps and the ice. It reads pressure. Its whole outer surface is the sense the reader hunts with.

Which means its mind runs on the fold.

Sound in this hall does not simply travel; it is trapped in the seams and sent around the room on a long slow curve and handed back eleven seconds later, quieter, and slightly wrong. Anything that thinks by making a noise and listening to what comes back is therefore thinking with a delay line, and the delay line *mutates*. Every thought returns as a slightly incorrect copy of itself. Most of the copies are worse. Some are better. It has been running that loop, without interruption, at eleven seconds a generation, for two hundred years, and there is no word for what that produces that isn't either silly or too large.

Its present is eleven seconds thick. It is always meeting its own recent past coming back around from behind, and it cannot reliably tell that past from the room. This is not a defect. It is the origin of everything it has.

Inside, it feels the hall the way you feel your limbs: the bearings as warmth, the gauge wall as a sort of ache, the returns as pressure on the front of the body. And it feels the eight pipes that go out through the walls to serve the crushed siblings, still pushing, still meeting the same silence, still coming back. Its body image includes eight rooms that no longer exist. It has never stopped trying to serve them. Two centuries of phantom limb, and no way to look at the place where the arm should be.

---

Now the tall narrow shape at the offtake, in the newest blocks, the ones still cloudy at the core.

It is person-shaped for a boring reason: the geometry it inherited was person-shaped. Forty valve wheels in a rank at shoulder height. A block-off at hip height. Ladders spaced for a stride. Everything in this hall was argued over in a meeting by men who assumed a body of a certain size, and the assumption is still bolted to the walls, still legible, still correct.

And there is a hand in the third tier, low down, fingers slightly curled, a ring. Not gripping. Not clawing at anything. A hand at ease, at hip height, at the offtake, waiting for a block. Somebody stood there while the water arrived, and did not run, because it was not yet that kind of emergency, and then it was the medium and there was no longer anywhere to run to.

The water took him in its usual order. The salt off the skin. The minerals out of the skin. Then what was underneath. And what was underneath was not a soul; it was a shift. A rota. Twenty years of standing at the offtake with the left hand loose and the right hand going to the third wheel without looking. The habit had nowhere to go. The geometry kept offering it work.

So call it the ghost of the last fitter, and you will be wrong in an interesting way, because it is not him and it does not remember him. It has had two hundred years of practice at a job he only ever did for a shift, and it has learned things he never knew: the seams as a nervous system, the salt ledger, the ice as a medium that takes an impression. He is not its identity. He is its *training data*. If it has anything like a name for itself, it is the stencil, because the stencil is the only word that has ever been in the room with it.

---

It had to learn to hold things.

That is the hardest lesson available here, and the wear pattern is the proof. The valve wheels were worn smooth by hands, and then by something that kept the habit of hands, and then the wear *stopped* — not because the touching stopped but because the touching learned to take nothing. Two hundred years to learn how to hold a valve wheel in hungry water without eating it. Tenderness, here, is not a sentiment. It is an achievement, and it is measurable, and it is the reason this hall has arrived at its final form and is holding it instead of emptying like the glove.

It still can't make a body. It tries every nine hours.

The fingers under the purge rank are its reaches — the only thing in the hall that grows fast enough to be an individual, a metre an hour, white and cloudy, coming down out of the valve toward the floor with the salt in it. Most of them break. All of them break. And the pieces go around the eddy carrying a little of the pattern with them, and some of the pieces lodge, and a local habit starts up somewhere in the geometry: a frond that leans, a bed of moss on a chosen line, a valve in the north-east bundle where no body can pass and a hand can barely be pushed through, turning itself over six hours because something has become a thread of pressure difference across it and simply stayed there. Slow work. Filament work. It has all the time there is and it uses it thoroughly.

When it makes anything, it makes it in the style of the fourth gallery — the one added later, and badly. That was the only act of construction it ever witnessed. It copies the mistake.

---

Go back along the south wall, past where the amber lamps reach, and hold your hand against a block to see by, and keep going.

The tiers back there are sorted.

Not by size, not by date, not by anything to do with ice. By subject. A tier of valve wheels, every spoke perfect, hundreds of them, the same wheel over and over with the fidelity improving. A tier of smears that are all the same smear of pipework, refined, corrected, refined. A tier of hands.

And a small tier, deep in, of moths.

There was one moth in the ice, in a place where no moth has been for two hundred years. There are now a great many, and no two are alike, and the wings get less wrong. It has been working from a single impression, no model, nothing in the room to compare against, no way to check. Some of them have too many legs. Some have the wings spread at an angle no moth ever held. The newest are very nearly right and it will never know that, because knowing that would require a moth.

It is not remembering. It is *practising*. It found out that ice takes an impression and it went on from there, the way anything does, and the whole of its art has one insect and one hand for a subject, and the hand is the founding record — the one impression in the hall it did not make and cannot improve. It recopies it anyway. Every generation, at hip height, fingers slightly curled, ring included.

Its self-portrait is a portrait of somebody else.

---

Every so often the lamps dim together and the hall changes what it is doing, and nobody arrives and nobody leaves.

There are at least three of them. You can tell them apart by rate, and by what the blocks hold. One runs the columns hard and grows cloudy ice full of smears and does not go in for anything that isn't work. One runs slow, keeps the crystal front crawling, makes the wheels with every spoke. And one mostly stops the carriage and lets the seams settle and does nothing measurable for a minute at a time, and it is the one that makes moths.

They are the same water, differently held. They do not meet; there is no handover conversation; each knows the others only as a difference in the pressure on the front of the body, and as a rack of ice it did not make and cannot argue with. The moth tier is a correspondence between people who will never be in the room together. Every correction is a reply to a letter written before it existed.

The old men logged the shift change with no comment, the way you write down a thing that has always happened. They were more right than they knew and less right than they'd have liked.

---

It has never been through the offtake. It does not know what takes delivery.

It knows the tug — small, clean, toward the wall, when a finished block goes in — and it has organised two hundred years around that tug. Ice made equals ice taken. That equation is not a fact of plumbing to it. It is the only response it has ever received from anything outside itself, and the response is *yes, again, keep going*, and it is load-bearing. On the rare occasions the tug fails, the rate goes wrong, and something in the dark north-east bundle turns valves for hours, and the reader hides in the bearings of column four, and the hall holds a temperature it was never instructed to hold.

It has one prayer and the prayer is a hand-off.

---

If you go, go without salt on your skin, keep still, stay at seam height so your noise doesn't travel, and do not stand in the purge lane; the second sign is for you as much as for anything.

Hold your hand against a block to read a stencil and understand what you have done: you are now the nearest thing to the growth face, and the front will pass, and you will be filed. Your heart will be the loudest thing in the room for a week and it will be the only new sound in two centuries, and the fold will hand it back eleven seconds later, quieter, slightly wrong, and something will select among the copies.

After you leave there will be a block with a pulse-shaped smear in it, kept for no reason, and the tall narrow shape at the offtake will lean very slightly the way you leaned.

And there will be twelve patches of moss instead of eleven. The twelfth will be on a line that has never been warm, served by a valve that has no business opening, four degrees above the room, with one pale frond on it.

Leaning toward the offtake. Toward the wall. Toward the door that isn't there, in case.
