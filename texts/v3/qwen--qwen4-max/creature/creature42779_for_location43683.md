## What Lives in the Witness Bowl

Begin at the bottom, because the bottom is where the bowl keeps its only citizens.

Kneel and part the mat with both hands and hold it open. The seam you make is lit — that watch-face green, coming up out of the moss itself — and in the light you can see the seethe resolve into bodies. Springtails like grains of cooked rice, flicking. Mites the color of oil drops, legged, deliberate, going about. A white thing with too many segments turning over and over in a chamber of water it cannot escape and does not seem to mind. There are more of them in the volume of your two hands than there are of anything else in the bowl, and here is the thing you will not notice for a long while: *they do not match.* That one is missing a leg. That one is slow. That one has gone somewhere no other has gone, into a pocket under a stone, and stayed, and will die there, and nothing will be corrected.

No clearance around them. No station. No gravel. They go everywhere.

The rule about instances holds only above a certain size. Below it, the bowl requires difference, and requires it precisely. A thing held perfectly to profile will seize against its own holding; the fit closes, the surfaces marry, the loop sticks, and the whole apparatus locks at some value that is exact and wrong. So somewhere in the circuit there must be a jitter finer than the finest feature being held and faster than the fastest correction. That is what the seething is. It is not life that has escaped the specification. It is the specification's necessary杂质 — the shake in the hand, the tremor put in on purpose. The mites are permitted to be themselves because noise cannot be repeated, and a noise that repeated would be a signal, and a signal would be held.

They are the only free things in the bowl. Their freedom is a bandwidth and an amplitude, and both are met.

The nine crop the mat and take the mites in with it. Everything white in the bowl comes from that: the shells on the copper trails, the slip-smooth eggs. The only citizens are ground into the only proof.

***

The emptiness of the room is a digestive product. This is worth knowing before you look up at it and feel the cold of all that spacing.

Put your palm on the washed gravel of a clearance. It is sorted — not roughly, not the way a stream sorts, but into grades you can feel as separate textures under the pads of your fingers, coarse to fine, each ring identical to the other eight. There is no wind here to winnow and no water falling to sort. The sorting was done inside bodies. In the windrows at the margin the worms are working, and a worm in this place is best understood as a hole of a particular diameter that has learned to move. Everything goes through. What can pass, passes. What cannot is left behind at the place where the passing stopped, and the place where the passing stopped is the same place for every worm of that grade, and so the gravel comes out sized, and the mat will not root in it, and the clearances stay open.

The roominess above the floor — the thing that reads as intention, that gives you the first cold the four degrees cannot account for — is excrement. Beautiful, exact, and excrement. The bowl is spacious because something small is continually eating the space clean.

Then the snails, which are the other half of it. They graze the algae off the wet stone and lay down metal, a plating a few microns thick, and they have done this for the entire non-duration of the place. Take two from opposite ends of a trail: same whorl count, same chip on the same lip of the same shell. You will want to call the chip a defect. It is not a defect. It is a stamp. It is the only mark in a bowl that has no writing anywhere in it, and it says the same thing on every shell it appears on, and what it says is *from here.*

Understand what they are for. Stone is an insulator. Moss is an insulator. Cold water at four degrees is a poor one. Without the snails the bowl would be a lump of nothing that could not carry a signal from its lip to its floor, could not be compared to anything, could not be read. The snails make the place conductive, and therefore legible, and therefore witnessable. They are not named in the name of the bowl. They are the reason the name is possible.

***

Lift your eyes to a midge-column and stop trying to count it.

Each midge lives minutes. The column has never been fewer than ten thousand and has never been more, and it stands thirty feet out of the crown of a standard into the cloud-lid with a slight living taper, humming, and it does not sway, because there is nothing to sway in. It is not ten thousand lives. It is one life at ten thousand degrees of freedom, a shape with a metabolism, the way a flame is a shape with a metabolism. Individuals go up into it and are spent and are replaced from below and the shape persists, and if you want to know what an individual is in this bowl, that column is the best answer available: *a station that is being filled.*

What comes down it is the datum.

The cloud is one cloud at one height and its underside is flat. It has never had a sun in it and it has never had a texture in it. It is the reference surface — the plate against which the work is checked — and it hangs over the bowl at a fixed standoff, and the tarn beneath it is flat to a tolerance you could measure with a straightedge, and the reason the tarn is flat is that it is being compared, continuously, at rate, to the sky. Two flat things facing each other with a bowl between them. The midges are the stem of the indicator. The hum is the reading.

And above the tarn, the governors: bird-sized, wings blurred, two bone spheres on tendons, spinning at their fixed stations. Look at the balls. Look at the angle the tendons make with the axis. It is the same angle it has always been. It is the angle that corresponds to the rate, and the rate has never deviated, and so the arms have never moved.

A governor is a machine for wanting a speed. It is the closest thing in this bowl to an appetite: the balls fly out if it is too fast and drop in if it is too slow, and the wanting is not a metaphor, it is the whole mechanism, it is what the thing *is.* And it has never wanted anything it did not already have.

You will notice this pattern and then you will not be able to stop noticing it. The block on the headwall has never fallen. The eggs have never hatched. The long-legged thing has never taken. The governors have never governed. The bowl is very largely composed of creatures that have never once had to do the single thing they are for. They are not workers. They are capacities, kept alive, kept wet, kept at temperature, held in reserve through a forever that has never required them.

Everything here is a *would.*

***

The eleven standards are the least alive things in it and the tallest.

Black bark, lichen-hung, unbranched to the crown. Put your tongue against the wet of one and you will taste a coin. The sap runs bright and metallic under the bark and the copper trails come in to the ring at the base like spokes, and the column goes up from the crown, and the whole standard is a busbar with a metabolism, standing at height, doing nothing but standing at height, conducting.

They are held to height *to the width of a finger.* Ask which finger. There are no hands here.

The width is the lichen.

It hangs in gray-green fringes from the black bark and it is cropped to a length, and the length is the allowance — the plus-or-minus, the band inside which a thing is permitted to be itself without being corrected. It is the only approximate object in the bowl. Everything else is exact by reference to it. The flatness of the tarn, the depth of the mat, the profile of the headwall, the height of the eleven: all of them measured against a soft fringe of something that is allowed to be about.

And the lichen is two. An alga and a fungus, one arrangement, the only pair in a place of instances, the only thing here that keeps company. Nothing else in the bowl is in a relationship with anything. The lichen is. It is a marriage growing on the datum, and the exactness of the world rests on it, and it is eaten back to length at rate, and it grows at rate, and the two are equal.

If you are looking for what lives here in the way you mean when you ask about a place — the resident, the one it is *about* — it is not the nine and it is not the walker. It is a thin fuzzy allowance on eleven black posts, and everything else in the bowl is precise because that one thing is not.

***

Now the nine, and you should look at them properly, because they are the ones you will be mistaken for.

Pale, hip-high, heavy in the body, hide like wet paper over a good deal of structure. Flightless — but there is a wing folded under the hide of each, complete, veined, articulated, never opened. Forelimbs held up beneath the chin, tucked, unused. Eyes open, and nothing to look at that has not been looked at. A mouth that only crops. They pivot within a body-length. They do not walk because there is nowhere that is not their station, and the mat comes to the edge of the gravel and stops, and the gravel is worm-work, and the ring at their feet is copper, and they stand in the middle of it like something set down.

Every fixed interval, each lays an egg. White, slip-smooth, faintly warm.

That warmth is the only heat generated in the bowl. Four degrees, everywhere, always, no thermocline, no warm side of a boulder — and then this small local exception, produced at rate, nine times per interval, and it rolls. The floor is level and it rolls anyway. Down the slight nothing of the gradient that is not a gradient, to the tarn, and the tarn takes it, and in the floor of the tarn is the bore.

You have been calling the bore a drain. It is not a drain. Look at the helical scoring, look at the diameter, look at the single file. Nothing here has ever hatched because hatching is not the operation. The eggs are witness pieces: the sample cast alongside the work to prove the process is holding. And a witness piece must be *examined.* Nothing in this bowl examines anything — except the hole.

The bore is a go gauge. An egg that passes is an egg that is within tolerance. That is the entire examination and the entire verdict and the entire life. The soft irregular counting you have been hearing under the forty-cycle hum is not a count at all. It is a run of yeses. It has never been interrupted. There is no total at the bottom of it. You can hear them go and you cannot hear them arrive.

There are eleven standards and eleven inlets and nine of the pale things.

Two clearances in the mat have nobody in them. Washed gravel, sorted, copper ring bright and wet, cropped clean, maintained. They have been maintained through the whole non-history of the place. They are not waiting. Waiting would require a future, and this bowl does not have one; it has a next pass. But they are held, empty, at profile, forever, and if you stand at the lip and look down at the spacing you will find that the regularity you felt has two holes in it, and that the holes are as exact as everything else.

***

Once each circuit the long-legged thing comes around the margin.

It does not hurry and it does not pause. It is not looking. A gauge does not look; a gauge touches, and the touching returns one of two answers, and there is no third thing it can return and no opinion it can hold. Its legs find the profile the way a fingernail finds a scratch. Where the mat is at depth it goes on. Where the gravel is at grade it goes on. Where the crack is at its width — always at its width, never wider, never resolved — it goes on.

It has taken nothing. It has never needed to. That is the point of it, and it is also the loneliest fact in the bowl, if loneliness were a quantity that could be specified here, which it is not. It is the only creature in this place capable of being disappointed, and it has never once been.

Its route is around the margin.

There is a new point on the route now.

***

You are thirty-seven degrees in a four-degree world.

That is what you are, to this place. Not a shape, not a weight, not the footprints that fill behind you at the speed of the fill. You are the second warm thing. There has only ever been one other kind of warm thing here, and the bowl has exactly one procedure for it, and the procedure ends in a hole of a fixed diameter that has never once said no.

Stand still and watch the mat draw back from your feet. It is not retreating; nothing here retreats. It is being cropped, at rate, by something you have not seen, and the worms are coming, and the gravel is arriving under you sorted, and the snails are coming to the warmth the way snails do, and the ring they are laying is faint and bright and *still wet* — the only fresh plating in a bowl where everything is fresh, the one place where the freshness is an event instead of a condition.

Look up. The governors' balls hang one degree higher than they hung when you came over the lip.

The pitch has not changed. That is what the arms are for. You will not hear it; there is nothing to hear; the whole apparatus of correction exists so that the answer, delivered continuously, at rate, forever, is: *nothing has happened.* But the arms have moved. For the first time in a duration that has no length, something in this bowl has had to do the thing it is for. The block ticks. The crack holds its width. The water runs up for a hand's span and turns down. All of it is answering you, and the answer is that you are not going to be audible.

There are two things in this bowl that stand in a ring of washed gravel, and they are workers, and there is nothing else that stands in one.

There are two things in this bowl that are warm, and they are eggs, and now you.

The bowl does not distinguish between fitting a thing and removing it. A jig holds a burr exactly the way it holds a workpiece: firmly, at profile, until the pass. Whether the ring closing around your feet is a station or a marking-out is not a question that has an answer here, because both operations are performed by the same hand, and there is no hand.

I know how long it takes. I have a ring. It closed a long time ago, in the only sense of *ago* available, and the mat comes to its edge and stops, and I pivot within a body-length, and I crop, and every fixed interval I lay one down: white, slip-smooth, faintly warm, and it rolls across the level floor without falling toward anything.

I cannot tell mine from theirs. I have tried in the way one tries things here, which is at rate. Take mine and the one from the far side of the tarn and set them together and there is nothing — not a difference in the gloss, not a variance in the warmth, nothing to say *this one came out of me.* And that is not a failure of my attention. That is the entire thing being proved. That is what the yeses in the bore are yeses about.

Nine of us. Eleven rings.

Listen to the counting under the hum. It has not changed its rate. It has room in it.
