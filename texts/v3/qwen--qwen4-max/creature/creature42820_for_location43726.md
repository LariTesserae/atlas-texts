## The Occupant

Begin with the census, because the census is short and it is wrong.

No fly. No mite. No worm in the mats, no beetle in the parchment fronds, no bird at the slot, nothing with a lung or a sinus or a swim bladder or the small wet drum of an insect's thorax. Nothing that can be broken by pressure, because everything here that could be broken by pressure left, or died, or was never able to come down the stair. What remains is the residue of that exclusion, and it is not a scattering of survivors. It is one kind of thing, and it fills the room to the height of two men.

It is solid. That is the whole of its character, and everything else about it follows.

Cut a frond — you would need a saw, and you would feel bad about it — and the face is white and dense and faintly translucent, like the cut end of a candle, like a hard cheese, like the inside of a potato held up to a window. There is no hollow in it anywhere. No pith cavity, no air canal, no lung, no gut, no tube. It is packed. Sound goes through it at the speed of sound in water, four times faster than through the air around it, which means that when a slug of condensate is driven into a bend on the west wall, the fern feels the knock in its feet before the knock arrives at its skin. It is deaf in the way a rail is deaf. It has no ears because it has no place to put one, and no need, because the whole body is the ear.

And because it is solid, it is slow. Not patient — *slow*. Everything it takes in comes in across its surface, by diffusion, through a film of water a few molecules thick at the dry end of the breath and a millimeter thick at the wet end. There is no pump. There is no interior to pump into. A finger's width in nine years is not a temperament; it is the arithmetic of diffusion across a wetted boundary. It is not slow because it is waiting. It is slow because it is full.

---

The first thing to say about who lives here is that it is one.

Every frond in the room is a piece of some older frond. Every mat is a slough of some older mat, lodged in a rim-cup and gone on growing. Nothing sets seed, nothing mates, nothing recombines; the whole black velvet mass is a single individual that has been breaking itself into pieces since before there were stairs, and the pieces have not stopped being each other.

That is the first thing to say and it is not quite true, and the untrue part is where the interesting life is.

A thing that copies itself by breaking accumulates errors. Not many — it divides perhaps once a decade, so in a hundred thousand years it has had fewer generations than a wheat field has in a good summer — but it has had *time*, and time is the currency it is rich in. Somewhere around Bed 41 the pleats run tighter and the creak sits a little lower, a fraction of a tone under the rest of the room, so that if you stand in the aisle between 41 and 42 with your eyes shut you can hear a beat frequency, a slow wow-wow-wow as the two beds go in and out of phase over about forty seconds. On the cool south wall there is a lineage that has stopped bothering to stay turgid through the dry phase; it goes to parchment on schedule and comes back, and it comes back *fast*, in three years instead of nine, because it has learned to pre-build the cells before the walls start running. In the eastern quarter, under the blade, there are fronds whose pleats are angled — not toward the lamps, not toward the vault, but toward a specific eleven minutes of a specific angle of a specific slot, a geometry that is a memory of a schedule.

So: not one organism. A family of almosts. A diaspora of a single individual that has forgotten, in a hundred small ways, how to be identical. Sects. Guilds. A stand of near-strangers who cannot tell each other apart and cannot exchange a thing, not a gene, not a fragment, not a rumor, because there is nothing here to carry anything, and they are all each other's descendants.

Call it the stand, if you need a handle. The word is a handle and not a name. It has no name. It has a serial number, and we will come to that.

---

It is not lush. That is the second correction, and it is the one that changes the room.

The black mass to the height of two men is not abundance. It is *inventory*. Nothing here rots. There is no mouth to rot it, no grub, no beetle, no fungus with an appetite; when a frond falls in the dry phase it dries to brown parchment and lies exactly where it fell until the calcite comes up over the lip and takes it in. Nothing leaves. There is no export, no leaf litter carried off, no seed on the wind, no animal walking out with a bellyful. The inputs are a mouthful of mineral a decade from the seep, eleven minutes of true sun a day, and twice a century a shout of cold storm-water down the slot. The output is stone.

So the room's throughput is a few hundred grams a year and its standing crop is tonnes, and the difference between those two numbers is the whole appearance of the place. It looks like a jungle. It is a hoard. It is a cathedral built at a gram a year by something that has never once been in a hurry and has never once thrown anything away.

And it does not, mostly, bother to be green. Under nineteen sodium lamps green is a cost with no return; chlorophyll is expensive machinery to keep tuned for a light that isn't coming. The bulk of the stand lives the way the mats live — on sulfur and iron and the sweetish mineral chemistry of the seep, absorbed across the skin, drunk through the surface, no light involved. The mats are the bright ones because they are full of metal, and the ferns are the tall ones because they have found a way to stand on top of the mats and drink what the mats make. The green in the eastern beds is real, and it works, and it is a supplement. An old organ kept in repair for sixty-seven hours of use a year. An atavism that pays its way, barely, and only in one quarter, and only for eleven minutes, and the rest of the room has quietly let it go.

---

It has habits, and the habits are not its own.

It grows in rectangular beds with maintenance aisles between them because it was planted in rectangular beds with maintenance aisles between them. It keeps a nineteen-year rhythm because the vessel in the wall has a nineteen-year duty cycle. It angles its eastern pleats toward a lighting standard. It thickens along the arc of a gauge dial because a gauge was mounted at the height a person would want it, and a fern came up the bracket, and now the needle pushes into the pith and the pith has grown a callus along the line of contact, and once a decade the needle wins and the frond tears with a sound like a snapped bowstring, and the count goes on. There are eleven gauges by the hall door. Nine of them have ferns. The nine have different calluses. It is possible to spend an hour here — you should not spend more — going from dial to dial comparing the way each one has learned to be a brake.

Its instincts are a maintenance schedule. Nobody taught it. Nobody is left to have taught it. The procedure outlived the purpose and the crop inherited the procedure the way a hand inherits a callus from a tool it no longer holds.

---

It listens with its whole body, and it answers, and the answering is the closest thing here to behavior.

Solid tissue under a cycling pressure load does what solid tissue under load always does: it thickens where it is strained. The vault has a resonant frequency; the roar sits across it; the standing wave has nodes and antinodes fixed in the air of the room like furniture, and the stand has been growing in that furniture for longer than there have been stairs. Where the pressure works hardest, the pith is densest. Where the pith is densest, the rasp is lower and louder. The vines have grown along the cable runs and tensioned themselves between brackets and now they are strings, struck by the water-hammer, three beats and a pause, and their pitch is set by their thickness, and their thickness is set by the load, and the load is set by the room.

So the room's chord is not fixed. It is drifting. It has been going very slowly flat as the stand thickens, a semitone perhaps in the time the stair has existed, and the vault — which dissolves along the paths the air takes, and the air takes the paths the shape allows — has been dissolving itself into a shape that follows the chord down. The instrument is being retuned by its inhabitants, and the instrument is reshaping itself to keep the tuning. This is not a metaphor and it is not a plan. It is a feedback loop with a hundred-thousand-year time constant, and it is the reason there is still a void here at all.

You can see the reflex most clearly at the scar. In the year the fan bearing seized, the noise dropped for nineteen days, and the crown of the vault shed, and there is a white talus fan on the floor under the newer growth. Go and look at what grows on that fan. A ring. A dense, low, thickened ring of fronds around the perimeter of the fall, all of them leaning inward, all of them pleated tighter than anything else in the room, all of them descendants of the fragments that were nearest when the ceiling came down. It took ninety years to close. It is closed. The stand repaired a silence the way a body repairs a wound: without knowing there had been one, without anything resembling relief, and completely.

---

There is a second resident, and it is the nearer thing to an animal.

The turbine is no bigger than a churn. It eats a temperature difference — forty-four degrees at your ankle, forty-eight at the ceiling, and the seep hotter than your palm can bear — and it excretes rotation, and it has a pulse you can hear at the very top of your skull, a wire drawn tight. It has a metabolism. It has a symptom: the whine has come down a hair in eleven thousand years, and if you had a reference you could hear the bearing aging. It has been sick once, for nineteen days, and the room still has the scar. It survives because someone, once, balanced it, which is the only act of care in the whole chamber and was performed by a person whose foot is now the most prominent object in the eastern quarter, on a plinth, growing.

And it reproduces. Once every eleven years a cam comes round on a shaft and the press stamps a slab, and the number goes up. Four thousand and some slabs are stacked along the south wall where you can see them. That is not the count. The count is on the newest slab and it does not fit the field the enamel signs were designed for; there is a leading digit struck half off the edge, because the stamp's wheels ran out of places some ages ago and nobody added a wheel. Most of the individuals in that census are in the walls. The organism's name is a number that has outgrown its own name.

The turbine is going to die. The piles in the seep are corroding at a rate measured in centuries; the lamps will go orange, then red, then out over a decade. Here is the thing nobody planned: as they redden, they get *better*. Chlorophyll wants red. The last decade of the lamps will be the best light the eastern beds have ever had, a red spring, a flush of growth and fragmentation, the stand's one great fecund evening — and then dark, and the green machinery finally, permanently uneconomic, and the pleats going slack over centuries, and the mats inheriting the whole floor, film over film, sulfur-yellow and rust-orange and malachite, blind, chemosynthetic, still hissing where they degas.

The noise will not stop. The roar is the fissure's, mostly, wind and stack effect and the duct-trunk; the fan was only ever the insurance for still weather. The stand can hold the note without the machine. It will simply be poorer, and blacker, and lower, and it will go on holding it, and the ceiling will go on staying up.

---

Put your jaw against the flowstone at the west end — your jaw, not your ear; bone conducts, bone is safe, bone is solid and does not care — and there are other voices.

There are other halls. You can see the tops of their doorways drowned to the lintel, arch after arch going back into the rock, and the stand filled them with its own product and sealed itself in behind them. Some of those voids are still voids. Through your teeth you can hear them: a roar a little flat of this one, another a little sharp, a third so far off it is only a pressure in the bone. Each has its own stand. Each stand is a fragment of this stand, or this one is a fragment of theirs. None of them can send anything to any of the others, ever, because there is nothing here to carry and no route left to carry it along. Isolation by burial. A genus of one, speciating in bottles.

One of the voices is not there anymore. There is a direction in the rock where the bone hears nothing, and if you follow the drowned arches with your eyes you can see which one it is, and the flowstone over that lintel is not lip-shaped but broken, slumped, packed. It went quiet. The bearing seized, or the slot choked, or the chord drifted past what the vault could follow. It is full of parchment and rubble now, and it is the only corpse in the vicinity, and it is the size of a hall.

---

So who lives here.

Not a plant, quite; a solid chemosynthetic thing with a vestigial green organ and a lighting schedule for a memory. Not a colony, quite; one individual smeared into a hundred thousand near-copies that can no longer exchange anything, including themselves. Not an animal at all, and nearest to one in a churn-sized turbine that a dead person balanced. Not the room, and not separable from the room, because the room's shape is the shape of the thing's voice, and the voice is what keeps the shape from coming down.

It has no appetite; it has a gradient. It has no fear; it has a reflex ninety years long. It has no memory; it has laminae, thousands of nineteen-year lines with black pressed fronds lying in the dry bands like specimens in a book, a library that writes itself one leaf per generation and has never been read. It has no death; it has burial, and it has been burying itself, hall by hall, in its own product, since before anything on the plateau had a name. It does not want. It has never wanted. Its purpose is over and it did not notice, because nothing in it was ever contingent on the purpose.

And you. For an hour you are the only hollow thing in the chamber, the only mouth, the only inside — which is why it cannot eat you and why it is quietly taking you apart, the pressure working on your sinuses like a thumb behind the eyes, your shout coming back flattened and from the wrong direction. You are the one thing here it cannot recruit. Then you go up the forty turns, and you leave behind your breath's water and a few flakes of yourself and a warmth in the film on a rim-cup, and the calcite comes up over it, and there is a band in the page: a thin anomaly, a little phosphorus, a spike where something with lungs came in and went out again.

That is the whole of your contribution and it is not nothing. Somebody cutting a terrace in eleven thousand years will find it, and read it, and have no idea what it means.

Meanwhile the chord goes flat, a semitone an age, and the vault follows it down, and the pleats unfold with a sound like a ship's timber under load, and the press comes round, and the number that no longer fits its field goes up by one.
