## The count

Nobody in Sill counts people. There are two counts and they do not agree.

There is the leaf-count, kept by the women who grow the roof-trays, which is mouths: everyone who receives green at sharing, including infants at half, including the sick at double, including — by an old ruling nobody will reopen — anyone who has ever given green to someone else, whether or not they are still alive to eat it.

There is the hand-count, kept on the tally-board at the thorn, which is everyone who must appear with a mallet when the thorn leafs. It excludes the very small, the very old, the flap-keeper, and the six who are out on the marl.

The gap between the two counts is the town. Six hundred and something, depending on which obligation you are asking about. Sill does not have a population. It has a set of debts shaped roughly like people.

---

## The names

The old ones are named for parts of boats that no longer exist. Thole. Bilge. Thwart. Gunwale. There is a woman called Keel who is eighty and remembers the bay as water, and a man called Stern who still gives directions in fathoms and says *the fishing* when he means the four rooms where the fishing was.

Their children are named for years. Nine, Eleven, Twelve, Fifteen — the year the family's floor came sweet, which is also a depth, which is also a place on the tally-board. There are forty-one people called Eleven and they are not confused with each other, because Sill is a town where you know a person by how they put things down.

The first Sill-born, who arrived before any floor was sweet, have no number. They are named for the weather of their birth: Rain, Grey, Wind, Flap. They are nineteen. They have never seen the sea in the bowl and they find the elders' grief frankly embarrassing.

And at the rim, where the rock weeps onto the bedding and nothing has come sweet in nineteen years, they name children for years that have not happened yet. There is a girl of twenty-two called Twenty-Five. She is waiting to be called what she is already called. Nobody makes a joke about it. It is not that kind of name.

## The hands

**Shim-wrights** go round all year with a mallet, a bag of drift-wood wedges and a plumb-bob of cockle-shell. They are the aristocracy of the hand-count and they are deaf to voices — not truly deaf, but so tuned to bone-conducted tapping that a spoken sentence has to be said twice, near the ear, with a hand on the arm. They can feel a quarter-inch of fall across thirty feet through the soles of their boots. For three days a year they are the most hated people in the bowl.

**Weep-wardens** keep the channels. Theirs is the only work in Sill that is allowed to be straight. They walk the trunk-cut twice a day with a bone scraper, and they are the ones who find the flatfish, and they are the ones who do not explain them.

**The flap-keeper** is an office held by one family since the second winter. She sleeps by the tide, not the sun, and is therefore late for every human event in town — births, deaths, the sharing — and early for the only one that matters. She is not thought unreliable. She is thought to be on a different clock, which is a recognised condition.

**The Reader** is a man called Thwart who makes his living opening walls. When a partition comes down to widen a seam he goes in with a lamp and reads the strata: a knife at knee height, a sandal, a withy doll, a meal-tray with the bones still on it, each in its collar of grey. He tells the family what they lost and when. He is the town's notary, historian and confessor, and what he actually reads is grief, because grief is the only thing in Sill that sets objects down. A thick band of collared things at chest height means a bad winter. He knows which household stopped caring in which year, and he tells them about the knife.

**Light-wives** hold the sixty-one shafts. Light is dowried, so it passes with brides, and the deeds are not written but remembered, by three women who recite them once a year at the thorn while everyone pretends not to listen and everybody listens. A shaft is not property in the ground. It is property in a column of air. You can own the noon over a floor you are not permitted to stand on, and people do, and there are families who have not spoken in two generations because of eleven minutes.

**Shaft-children** go down the wells to whiten the shell-lining, because the light only comes down if somebody small goes down first. They are the only people who know the town's whole vertical anatomy, and they are pale as the flatfish, and they have a counting-rhyme for all sixty-one that takes about four minutes and is the closest thing Sill has to a scripture.

**Hang-wrights** make hooks of antler and drift-iron; **felt-wrappers** muffle the clappers, and are at war, permanently and quietly, with woodlice.

**The green-wives** count leaves. **The salt-scrapers** take the rind off the rim-rocks and sell it back to the old country, which finds this funny. **The rind-chippers** free what has been set down: boots, combs, hinges, and, on the third day of the re-shimming, people.

## The runners

Six or seven, depending on the season, and they marry among themselves.

The sea is a quarter-mile off across raw marl and it stands higher than the roofs twice a day. So the fishing is a walk, and the walk is a run at the end of it. The runners go out on the ebb with nets and greased sleds and come back before the water does, and they are the only Sill folk who can judge a distance, who are not made dizzy by a horizon, who do not tilt their heads in an ordinary doorway.

They bring the drift-timber. They bring the round things. They bring the traded grain in sacks.

Everyone in the bowl knows which of those three the rat will arrive in, and it is the third, and it will be a runner who carries it down the cut, and the runners know that everyone knows. They are the town's heroes and its contamination. They are given the best hooks and the worst seats.

For everyone inside the bowl, the clap of the flap means *stop*. For the runners out on the marl it means *run*. It is the same sound doing two opposite jobs, and the town has never got over this.

## The bodies

Palms first: a Sill hand is a map of every wall it has steadied itself against, glazed and cross-hatched, sharp-rock scars over sharp-rock scars, and they shake hands loosely, palm to palm, because a full grip hurts.

The stoop next — the seam-tilt, the head permanently canted a few degrees to part the hanging forest. Outsiders think Sill people are listening to them. Sill people are listening, but not to them.

Short sight, almost universally: eyes raised in the amber tea-light of oiled hide, good at a hook's balance at nine inches, useless at a face across a room, which is why they stand so close and why the old country thinks them rude and familiar. The white cough from marl-dust and low-grade clay smoke. Salt in the creases of every knuckle and behind every knee, a grey bloom you can wipe and cannot stop.

And they cannot stand still. It is taught before speech: shift, shift, shift. A Sill person waiting shifts weight like a boat at anchor. Because the floor is patient and they are not, and the ones who stop moving get taken.

There is a man at the rim who has not stood since his wife died. The marl has him to the ankle. He is not embarrassed and neither is anybody else. On the third day of the re-shimming four neighbours chip him free with bone wedges, and it takes most of an hour, and no one talks while they do it, and afterwards he is carried up onto the roof to sit in the sun, and he hates it.

Take a Sill person up the path into the old country and watch them: they part the empty air with a trailing hand at the height of a face. They hold their bag all evening rather than set it on a floor. In an inn they tap the wall twice before going to bed, to tell the next room they are coming through, and there is no next room, and they lie awake in the enormous silence with nothing knocking, and they come home.

## The manners

Speech happens at a body's width, quietly, with the chin pointing rather than the arm — there is no room to gesture and a wide gesture is a thing knocked off a hook, which is a small crime.

Character is public. You are known by your hanging: the balance of your hooks, the wrap on your clappers, whether your loads can be let down by a child. A house that clacks is a house in trouble, and the neighbours know it before the household does, and the felt-wrappers arrive with scraps and say nothing about why.

The worst thing you can do to a person in Sill is set something down in their room. The second worst is raise your roof.

And there is the far knock. In the deep middle the mass is so thick and continuous that a tap travels through six households and arrives next door with all its intimacy intact. People answer knocks from strangers three seams away. Children exploit this. There is a stretch of the fourth seam where you can put your fist against the wall and make an old woman in a house you have never entered say *come*, and it is the town's oldest joke and its only ghost.

## The rich, the poor, and the round

Wealth is a dry floor and an empty room.

The richest household in Sill keeps one room with nothing hanging in it at all. No racks, no slings, no forest. A bowl stands on the floor and has stood there for four years and has no collar. People are taken to see it. It is obscene and magnificent and the poor talk about it the way the old country talks about gold.

The rich also eat bland. Fish rinsed in three waters, no salt, and the guest's compliment is *I taste nothing*, said with the eyes shut.

Poverty is a hook with nothing on it, and the wind coming down the cut, and a name in a year that hasn't come.

You enter Sill by giving up something round. That is the toll at the head of the path: a worn cockle, a surf-plank, a thole-pin from a drowned boat. Roundness cannot be made here — every rock in town is a broken rock — so it is brought, and hoarded, and hung in the treasure-rack in the second seam under a hide flap: sixty-odd smooth things, handles-to-be, rims-to-be, the town's whole supply of the curve.

Children are given a worn cockle to hold. Infants at the noon-stone are given, for eleven minutes, the town's one rounded pebble, which came down the path in a sack from a river nobody here has seen. They say it came out of the sea. It did not. It is the only lie Sill tells about itself and everyone is in on it.

---

## The children

They go up before they go along.

A Sill infant is hung, and learns the rack before the floor: hand over hand along the low forest of suspended things, hook to loop to sling, crossing a whole room at the height of a tall man's waist without ever touching ground. Their arms are absurd. At three they can hang one-handed from a withy loop and eat with the other, and their legs are an afterthought, and walking, when it comes, is a novelty they take up briefly and largely abandon in favour of the roof.

First word is often a tap. Two, against the wall, meaning *I am coming through* — said by a baby to a room it cannot yet enter.

Then: the roof-dare at seven, the shaft-work at eight, the snail-watch, the flatfish hunt in the deep weeps. They are told the ground lays them. Most of them go on half-believing it into adulthood, the way you go on half-believing anything you were told while holding a live thing.

## The citizens with no hands

**The eel**, called Keel, because it came in under a keel. Eighteen years in the trunk-cut, thick as a wrist, on a feeding rota pinned to the tally-board, handled by every child in town and thoroughly sick of it. Measured each spring against a notch-stick. Considered the oldest citizen. The only inhabitant who has been somewhere else.

Two summers ago it stopped taking the fish. The rota continues. People go down with a scrap and stand there a while and come back up and don't mention it. Everyone knows what a silvering eel does. There is a box at the rim for an animal that has not arrived and nothing at all for the one that is going to leave.

**The cat**, on the kiln-clamp, warm and dry and therefore uncollared. Thwart tried once to count its names and got to thirty-one and stopped. It goes anywhere: roof, seam, floor, all three estates, no rights, no duties, no wedge ever tapped for it. It went up the path once and came back and the town discussed it for a year. It has no work. It is being kept, like the box, for a war that has not started.

**The heron**, on the old strandline at the height of a raised hand, above the roofs, facing out. The runners say there are three birds and the town says there is one and this is not really an argument about birds. It is the only thing in Sill looking the wrong way.

**The mud-snails**, in one wet seam and nowhere else, thousands, climbing a day before rain. The household that owns that wall sells forecasts. The household that owns the noon does not speak to them. Sill's entire apparatus for knowing the future is a snail, a thorn, and a woman who sleeps by the tide.

**The brine-flies**, nine days in late summer, netted and pressed into cakes that are eaten all winter and joked about all year. Nine days of every mouth shut, which is the only other silence the town agrees to.

## The slow ones

Eleven bushes, counted, named, disputed, and a twelfth claimed by a young couple at the rim which the tally-board has not yet recognised, and the recognition is the great constitutional question of the current decade.

Grey lichen on the rim-rocks, growing at about the speed of the sweet. A woman scrapes it at a thumbnail a year and makes orchil, and out of the orchil comes one purple thread a decade, and the purple thread is stitched into the cord of the tally-board, so that the town's record of itself is bound with the slowest thing it owns.

Moss in the weeps, cropped for stuffing and caulking. Algal skin on everything damp, which is everything, and which is, by area, far the largest living thing in the bowl.

And the thorn at the foot of the path, older than the town, which grew on the cliff when the bay was a bay and was left high and dry by the same hour that made the floor. Its roots go down into the old salt. It leafs a little later each year. When it leafs, the re-shimming begins, and the town's calendar is a single aging shrub that nobody has a plan for.

---

## The not-yet

The rat owns property.

A stone box on the rim, well-built, roofed, dry, sleeping-shelf and bowl, swept for eleven years. It is the best-made small structure in Sill and it belongs to an animal that has never paid a wedge, never appeared at the thorn, never taken a leaf.

The runners want a second box at the gate. They say the rat will come in a grain sack off a sled, not down the path, and that a box at the rim is a box in the wrong place, and they are probably right, and the rim households will not have it, because the box is not really drainage. It is an offering. You do not move an offering to where it would be more useful.

This is discussed with the seriousness of drainage.

---

## The set-down

Everything you love, you hang. What you set down, you have finished with.

So the dead are set down. They are brought to the stone in the second seam where the sun stands for eleven minutes at midsummer noon, and then they go into the fabric, and the fabric takes them, and the Rind — which is the mortar, which is the town's glue, which is the only thing in Sill that grows fast — makes them architecture.

Sill is load-bearing with its own dead. The shim-wrights shim around them. Thwart knows which layer is a person and does not say so unless he is asked, and he is asked about once a decade, and he answers, and then he goes and gets drunk in a room where nothing is hanging.

The hooks stay. A dead person's things are re-hung through the quarter and their hook is kept empty and dusted, sometimes for fifty years, and the light-rights pass with the hook. There are exiles up the path — *sent up* is the phrase, up is out, down is in — whose hooks are also kept, and dusted, and this is the town's long argument with itself about whether anybody is ever finished with anybody.

Thwart has noticed how many of the town's numbers are eleven: the days they looked at the floor, the bushes, the minutes of sun, the years of sweeping. He has not said so.

---

## The stopping

Twice a day the sea comes up the cut and slams the flap and the report goes through six hundred bodies at once, in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet stone.

Everybody stops. The mallet stops mid-tap. The child stops mid-hook. The two women who are not speaking stop at the same instant in different rooms.

Nobody has explained it. The flap-keeper says it's a check on the valve. The old ones say the sea is knocking and it is rude not to answer. Thwart says it is the only sound the whole town hears at the same time, and therefore the only proof available that the town is one thing, and then he says he doesn't know.

For about two seconds, nobody in Sill is listening to their neighbour, because everybody is listening to the same thing. It is the only privacy they have and they take it together.

There is a girl of nineteen called Flap, born the first night, named for the sound. She has never once been late for it.

Then the tapping starts again.
