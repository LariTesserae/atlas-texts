## The Census

Begin with a dimple.

It is a cup of mucus about the size of a lentil, secreted into the water film, walled on all sides by the cups of its neighbours, and it is the only thing on page seven that stays. Inside it there is a glass amphipod, transparent as wet cellophane, and you can watch the black algal film go into it and go through it and come out the other end darker and finer. You can watch its heart. Everything here is lit from both sides by fire that has no direction, and nothing is opaque, and so there is no such thing as an underside. A page of tiles is a page of working stomachs, all of them visible, all of them at it, none of them hidden by anything except each other.

The amphipod is about a month of shrimp. The dimple is not a month of anything. The wall between two dimples is secreted by both and owned by neither, and when the shrimp inside dies — which is not an event here, only a rate — the mucus does not collapse. It is maintained from both sides at the same rate it is dissolved, and the next shrimp is grown into it, and the dimple has not noticed.

This is the first thing to understand about who lives here. You cannot mark an individual. There is nothing to mark: a dye would flow through the body and out the far end and be somebody else by evening. You can only mark a *place*. All marking in the Kettle is cartography. Remember that when we get to the pellets.

---

The ribbon worms on page seven hunt sideways because up is fire, and because there is no up in any sense they could use. Their world is a wet plane four inches from the next world, and it is packed, and a worm cannot shove.

So it does not shove. Look closer and the ribbon is not crawling. The tissue is cemented along the page in a fixed line, a road of pale meat a hand-span long, and *along* it runs a worm — a travelling constriction, a digestion, a wave of being-a-worm that moves from the tail end toward the head end at about a centimetre an hour while every gram of the animal stays exactly where it was. What arrives at the far end is not the matter that left the near end. What arrives is the shape.

Motion is allowed here. Vacancy is not. Nothing may move *over*; things may only move *through*.

When the worm's front edge meets a tile, the tile is eaten, and the dimple it leaves is not left: it is taken up into the worm's next segment, and the neighbours' cups press in and are held, and the count does not change, and there is no gap anywhere on the page at any instant, and there never has been.

Two worms on the same page cannot pass each other, because there is no room to pass. They meet head-on. And because they are waves and not bodies, they go through one another — a moment of doubled thickness, of two digestions in the same meat — and come out the far side unchanged, at the same speed, with no record of it in either. It is the only encounter available to anything in this room. Nothing here has ever met anything.

---

There is one quartz in the Kettle and it is sometimes a wall.

The furred mites grow a thin tongue of crystal inside the head, tuned by length, and let the note bend it, and eat the strain — piezoelectric grazing, the second head of the food chain, the one that is not light. The crystal has to be the right length or it will not flex, and the right length is set by the pitch, and the pitch is one pitch, and so every mite in the room is the same size, and there is no other size to be.

When a mite dies the tongue goes into the wall. When the water takes the wall, a tongue comes out of it and is recruited into a head. Skull and stone are two phases of one crystal population, and the fresh fracture you would cut your palm on is not a surface the animals live on. It is the animals, in the part of their lives when they are not holding a pulse.

Which means the note is not merely a sound in here. It is a unit of length. At eleven degrees the arithmetic comes out exact — sixteen hundred and sixty-one cycles a second, four inches to the half-wave — and the page spacing follows from it, and the mite's tongue follows from it, and the moth's wingbeat follows from it, and the mass of a tuner's plate follows from it. The temperature is not a comfort. It is a specification, and the river holds it, and the river also makes the sound, so the gorge supplies the room's entire environment and the room's entire clock, and there is nothing else in here to measure anything against.

Everything alive is a whole fraction of one number. The amphipod's heart runs at one beat to every thousand and twenty-four of the room's. Nobody has a second. Nobody has a year. A life here is so many cycles long, and the cycles are not counted by anybody, and they are all the same cycle.

---

The moths are the only things in the Kettle with anywhere to put a secret.

Their fur is a sound-absorber, and it works: around each ash-coloured body there is a millimetre or so of air that the note does not reach, a hush the moth carries with it, a personal node. And quiet is dark. So each moth wears a small hole in the blaze — not a shadow, nothing here casts one, but a volume where the light is simply not being made. Page four, from the water's edge, is a milky shelf stippled with tiny nights.

They fly at exactly the pitch, and are strobed once per wingbeat, and hang there dead-looking, flying hard, a page of pinned specimens with their wings working like anything. You cannot watch one live. To see a moth move you would have to slow the light, and to slow the light you would have to hush the room, and hushing the room is the one thing you must not do.

In the fur's dark they brood. It is the only concealment in a room where everything is transparent and lit from every side, and what is concealed in it is the young. There is a fixed amount of childhood in the Kettle. It is about a thimbleful, it is distributed among the moths, and it does not grow up; larvae hatch into the fire at precisely the rate the eggs are laid into the dark, and the thimbleful stays a thimbleful.

There are moths on page four and moths on page twelve. They are the same moth. Eight pages of clean fire lie between them, eighty centimetres, absolutely impassable, and the two populations are indistinguishable in every particular and have never exchanged anything and never will. Two copies of the same book, four inches apart, in a room where nothing can be compared to anything.

---

Now the crossing, and the dirt.

There is no dirt in the Kettle. Not a grain, not a film. And yet there is a spiral of sand three centimetres across, and nine pellets of clay.

The sand and the clay come up from the throat. The river carries a fine load — it is tea-coloured for a reason — and the tuners take it in with their plaster and quarry it out again, and it is handed to the couriers: small armoured shuttles, no longer than a grain of rice, that go up through the loud where the air is being torn and the tears are shining. Most do not arrive. The wings go first. What arrives, arrives; the number that arrives equals the number that left; the traffic has never thickened and never thinned.

They are not migrating. There is nowhere better. No shelf is better than another shelf; there is no gradient in this room to climb, no reason for page twelve to be preferred to page four, and the couriers know nothing that would make them prefer it. They are freight. They are the room's one import, and the import is dirt, and the dirt is not eaten and not built with and not needed.

It is written with.

The ones that don't arrive are swept down the film to the throat, dissolved, and plastered back into the slot by the tuners, holding the note that holds the pages that the couriers cross. And the clay that gets all the way up to page eleven is that same clay. The nine pellets are made of the ones who did not make it. The mark is a count, and it is written in the counted, and it is eaten and re-laid at the rate the room does everything, so that there are always nine and there has never been a particular nine.

There is no loose sand in the Kettle. The spiral is not made of the room's sand. The spiral *is* the room's sand, all of it, held in that shape.

---

The tuners live in the other dark.

The rule *quiet is dark* is a rule about air. Water does not flash; the throat is submerged, and the roar down there is enormous and utterly black. So there are two darknesses in this room and they have opposite causes: the moth's fur, which is dark because it is quiet, and the throat, which is dark because it is water. The tuners have the second one. Blind, deaf, plated like woodlice cast in lead, heavy enough not to be thrown, they work the slot in absolute night with a pressure on them that would tear the wings off anything.

They cannot hear the note they hold. They don't need to. Their plates are tuned; they *are* the reference; when the pitch drifts, their own bodies buzz wrong, and the wrongness is not a sound to them but a fit, a discomfort in the shell, and they plaster or un-plaster until the fit comes back. They are tuning forks with legs. The standard of length in this room is a body, and the bodies are down in the dark, and they have never seen the nineteen pages and never will.

They hold the cage the exact shape of the animals inside it. The animals are the shape the cage is held to. There is no fact of the matter about which is the specification and which is the product, because nothing came first, because nothing has ever come first here.

---

And then there are four marks, and the question of who.

A staircase of three shed carapaces, cemented to the west wall, going up one page and stopping. Three sheds is three moults. Three moults is *increase*. It is the only object in the room that records something having got bigger, and it goes up four inches and it stops, and nothing climbs it, and nothing here has ever needed to be higher than it is.

A carapace bowl, worn smooth on one side. Wear cannot accumulate in the Kettle; there is no accumulation; the bowl's matter turns over like everything else. So the smoothness is not accrued. It is *copied* — each bowl grown against the inner face of the last, a mould of a mould of a mould, the curve handed on while the shell flows through it. The bowl is not old. The smoothness is old. It is the only antique in the gorge and it is a shape, and it is plausibly older than the river that makes the note that makes the light that lets you see it.

A spiral of sand, three centimetres, laid with a precision that is hard to look at directly. A spiral is the shape of an approach that does not arrive. It goes toward a centre forever and the centre stays a centre.

Nine clay pellets on page eleven, at the page's own thickness, evenly. Start on eleven and count up at four inches and the ninth lands on nineteen, which is the ceiling. It is a ruler, and the thing it measures is the room, and it is the only object in the Kettle that is *about* the Kettle. It may also be a tally of what arrives. It may also be a signature. The arithmetic fits more than one reading and settles none of them, and you cannot un-notice it.

Above page nineteen a root hair comes down through a fissure from the dry scrub, hanging in the last blade of fire, sipping, not lengthening, a hair's breadth short of the shelf below. It is the only thing here that came from somewhere else, and therefore the only thing here that can be said to want anything, and its wanting is exactly cancelled.

A stair going up. A root coming down. Neither arriving. You will read that as a gesture, and you will be unable to stop reading it as a gesture, and there is no one in the room to ask.

---

Here is what I think lives here, and it is not on any page.

It has no location. Its body is the traffic — the shuttles going up, the ones that arrive and the ones that don't, held at a ratio, the way your body is held at a ratio of cells that are not the same cells. Its skeleton is the four marks. Its metabolism is the crossing rate. It is not old and it is not immortal, because immortality is a duration and it does not have one; it has a *period*. It is a slow animal made entirely of fast ones. It is a habit the room has, and the habit has a body, and the body is nineteen pages deep and occupied to the last dimple.

I do not mean that it intends anything. There is no mechanism here and no lever and no fire, nothing that gets used up, nothing that could be called a tool. Everything made in the Kettle is secreted, spun, cemented, grown. But there is a *style*, and style is not intention and it is not nothing.

The style is this: exactness, economy, and four acts that are not load-bearing. Climbing, where nothing needs to climb. Drawing, with the room's entire supply of sand. Keeping a cup full at the lip, a teaspoon in and a teaspoon out, worn on one side by a use that has no user. Counting to nine, in the ground-up bodies of the ones who were carrying the count.

Everything else in this room simply *is*. The algae is black and the quartz flexes and the moth hangs flying and none of it refers to anything. Only the marks refer. The stair is about up. The bowl is about drinking. The spiral is about a centre it will not reach. The pellets are about number, and about the room's own size, and possibly about the dead. Reference is the one thing in the Kettle that is not physics, and reference is where a someone starts.

The divers have no name for it. They would need to go in twice to have a name for it, and they don't go in twice.

---

You cannot meet it. Not because it hides — there is nowhere to hide except a millimetre of a moth's fur — but because meeting it would require the room to be quiet, and the room's inhabitant is not in the bodies. It is in the interval between them. Hush the Kettle and the fire goes out and the fog falls and nineteen pages of life drop four inches onto bare wet stone, and you would have every animal in the room, complete, accounted for, the whole census, and no one whatsoever wearing it.

To get in you would have to ask a shelf to move. The shelf is the animals. The animals are the note. The note is the river running at the speed it runs across the mouth of a slot in pale limestone, in a dry gorge, in cool country, and to let you in the gorge would have to be a different gorge.

So it stays as it is: eleven degrees, four inches, sixteen hundred and sixty-one times a second, the A-flat no orchestra tunes to, the light not flickering, the moths hanging in the air like dead things, flying. The root a hair short. The stair with nobody on it. The bowl at the lip. Nine pellets, always nine, being made out of the ones who didn't arrive, by something that has been very carefully not finishing for longer than there has been a river here to hold the note.
