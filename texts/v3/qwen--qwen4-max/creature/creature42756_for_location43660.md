Somewhere on the table a small bright point is crossing the basalt at two centimeters a second.

It is not a reflection. It is a focus. Forty centimeters above it hangs a lens eleven centimeters long — a flattened lozenge of the same amber glass that sheathes the spindles, two hundred microns thick, gathering the fixed sun into a spot the size of a grain of rice and dragging that spot along the feed-lines. Around the focus is a dark ring. The ring is the only shadow in the world that is not a geographic feature.

You will not find the lens by looking for the lens. You find it by looking at the far rim of the table, ninety kilometers off, hard as a ruled line, and noticing that a short piece of that line is bent.

The body has no front. It has an axis, and the axis is vertical, keel down, aligned to the local field exactly as every fan on every crown is aligned; this is the first evidence that it belongs here and is not a visitor. Inside the glass there is a mesh of copper so fine it reads as a tint, veined in silver, and along the centerline a single thread of black sulfide. No mouth. No eye. No limb, no hinge, no joint, no aperture. Nothing about it opens. It is thirty-six degrees, like the stone and the shade and the air, and it makes no sound, and it smells of nothing, and it hangs.

---

Call it a stray. The word is the engineering one: current that has found a path nobody laid. There was nobody to lay a path, so the phrase is only approximately right, but the electrical fact is exact. The world is a battery with a load and the load is the landscape, and a stray is a third thing, a small unauthorized branch across the same two terminals.

The fair-weather field here stands at about eighty volts per meter. Across eleven centimeters of vertically hung body that is nine volts. The stray draws four microamps. Thirty-six microwatts, total, the whole of its living, against a spindle's megawatt.

The current does not change. That is true to nine decimal places. The strays are in the tenth.

It does not eat. Nothing here has ever been eaten and this does not eat; there is no mouth to eat with and no chemistry to eat into and nothing that would be improved by being taken apart. It is not a grazer and not a predator and there is no pyramid above it or below it. There is one trophic level in this world and it is *parallel*. Its entire metabolism is a shortcut, a place where charge goes slightly the wrong way, and the wrongness has acquired a shape and keeps the shape.

Cut a spindle and it arcs. A stray is that arc, never cut, going on and on, having found the conditions under which a discharge can hold its own channel open indefinitely. Its glass is not a skin over its life. Its glass is precipitated around its life, the way the rim precipitates country around an aperture.

---

It climbs and sinks along the lines of force whenever it likes, by trading charge with the ion drift — ions migrate here without the air moving, a river of charge in a riverless medium, and the stray swims in the migration the way a thing swims in a current that is not water. Up and down are free. Up and down are, in fact, the only directions it has in any strong sense.

Across the field it cannot swim. There is nothing to push against: no wind, no thermals, no gust, and a body with no surfaces worth the name. To go sideways it must go where the lines of force are not vertical, and there are only two such places — fanning out of the crowns nine hundred meters up, and bending over the lip of the rim. Otherwise it rides: locked to the small kink a pallet puts in the field, carried inward at three centimeters a second on a stolon between two spindles, over a kilometer of nothing, in absolute silence, with a kilometer of nothing to come.

So the table is not a plain to it. It is four hundred vertical shafts, joined at the ceiling, with a slow floor.

And it has never touched anything. Not the stone, not a leaf, not another of its kind. Contact would mean sliding and sliding would mean abrasion, and abrasion is the one thing this world does not have. Every line of its anatomy is arranged around the avoidance of the first scrape. It is the only object here capable of wearing something, and it is built entirely out of not doing so.

---

Inside, the copper is not a structure. It is a traffic. Every strand of the mesh is plated at the leading end and stripped at the trailing end and the whole body's metal is exchanged in about nine hours; the glass likewise, laid down ahead, depolymerized behind, ion by ion, returned to the local stock. It is a die that travels with its own extrusion. It is the rim's method at the scale of a hand.

Two strays can drift through one another's fields, exchange the entire substance of their bodies, and nothing will have happened, because the substance was never the thing. What persists is a relationship: a knot tied in the thirty-hertz tone, a particular lag between the rock's fundamental and the small oscillation of the body's own draw. The knot is the animal. The glass is where the knot is currently being tied.

This is why the census is difficult and why the number, when you get it, is the only number in the world that has ever been different tomorrow.

---

Every forty-one minutes a pawl drops somewhere on the table and the click goes twenty kilometers through the stone.

To the works it is nothing: a load step, a few parts in a very large number, recovered before the sound has finished crossing the ring. To a stray it is the end of the world. The step sweeps every uncommitted phase relationship in the ring back to the fundamental. Anything not written down is gone. Not killed — there is no body to kill, the body is a nine-hour convenience — simply *no longer the case*.

So they write.

The medium is the ornament. At the foot of every spindle, in permanent shade, there are scrolls of copper wool and filigree that carries no current and fans plated in patterns of no function, all of it edged in the same black sulfide film that iridesces on the leaves. The film is a hundred nanometers thick and it will hold trapped charge for a very long time. A stray hanging one millimeter off a scroll, modulating its own potential as it drifts along, lays a pattern into that film by induction. It never touches. It reads the same way, at the same distance, without disturbing what it reads.

The archive is a shift in the color of a peacock surface. A few nanometers of apparent thickness. Oil-violet where there was beetle-green. It is legible, from a millimeter, to something blind.

Decoration is what a factory does with an excess it cannot store. The factory cannot store charge; it has no capacity for it; every surplus goes immediately into pointless surface. It has never occurred to the factory to store a *shape*, because a shape is not a quantity, and nothing here had ever needed one.

The strays found the free medium in the waste. They have been writing in it for a very long time.

There are two kinds of erasure and they must be fought differently. The ground ornament — the mats, the scrolls in shade, the ankle-high thickets — sits on stone that is going inward at eleven millimeters a year toward the intake mouths, and will be unmade. The spindle ornament is fixed, rooted, safe from the Reclaim, and is re-plated by the works on a cycle of its own, which overwrites everything with the same pointless pattern it had before. One archive is eaten by geology. The other is eaten by tidiness.

Their entire labor is recopying between them. There is no other verb here.

---

Two strays meeting at a millimeter exchange everything they have. If the copy is exact, there are not two individuals; there is one, in two places, and the world's only redundancy. Nothing new has ever come from a perfect copy.

Novelty comes from the click. A transcription caught mid-stroke by the load step comes back wrong: a phase offset by a fraction, a lag that is now a different lag, a knot tied slightly otherwise. Almost all of these are not viable and are simply gone at the next click. A very few hold. Every individual on this table is a mistake that was legible enough to be copied.

The click is the eraser. It is also the pen. It is the loudest event in the world, identical to the last one, and it is the only thing that has ever produced anything new.

The count on this table at present is three hundred and eleven. A thousand years ago it was two hundred and nine. It has been zero. Twice.

From those two zeroes there survive libraries: kilometers of scroll in the shade of spindles whose writers stopped, still perfectly legible, still iridescent, drifting inward at eleven millimeters a year toward a mouth. Nobody reads them. There is no one who could. The strays pass over them and register the charge and cannot parse it, because parsing requires a phase that no longer exists anywhere, and go on.

---

There is dust on the table.

The ordinary ending is an ascent: the knot loosens, the draw falls away, the glass is no longer maintained and unravels upward into the ion drift as sodium and silica and a little copper, leaving nothing at all. It is not death in any sense that leaves a mark. It is a pattern ceasing to be the case, in a world where that is the commonest thing that happens.

But sometimes a body loses its lock while it is low — caught by a click at forty centimeters, over basalt — and falls.

Four thousand and six grains. That is the whole of it, on thirty thousand square kilometers, the entire powder of the world, and every grain of it is a fall. Nothing blows it away. Nothing dissolves it. It lies exactly where it fell, angular, unrounded, on a stone that is going inward.

They know where all of them are. They have to: the coordinates are wrong by eleven millimeters a year and must be corrected, every generation, forever, and this correction is the reason the archive has a calendar at all. It is the only map in the world and it is a map of graves.

The oldest entry names a fall four million years ago. The stone it fell on went into the intake nine thousand years later. The place has been made new four hundred and fifty times since; the grains that lay there were stripped to sodium and silica and pushed down the root and came up at the lip as country. There is nothing there. They keep the position anyway. It is the closest thing in this world to a name.

---

A stray has no use for the ninety-kilometer clarity. It is blind. Light is irrelevant here; it never was anything else. What it has is: the direction of the field, absolutely, as an up that is not a convention; the charge-texture of a surface one millimeter away; the hiss of a corona as a pressure rather than a sound; and phase, which is to say the time, which is to say it is the only thing on the table that knows what time it is and how much of it is left.

And it has the faint clicks.

There are twelve rings and one root. Four kilometers down, in the dark reducing rock, they share a potential, and a pawl dropping in any of them arrives in all of them as a small shaped step in the fundamental. Twelve clicks every forty-one minutes, in an order that precesses over centuries. One of them is yours. The others are not.

That is how they learned there was anyone else: not by the eleven sharp rings on the horizon, which no stray has ever seen or could; by counting, and finding more clicks than they were making.

The signature of your own click depends on the total load at the instant it falls. Three hundred and eleven leaks, all drawing or all holding, can change that signature by about one part in a hundred million. To make the change legible two hundred kilometers away through four kilometers of rock, they must hold the same state through something on the order of a million and a half consecutive clicks.

A bit every hundred and twenty years. They agree by reading the same scroll.

What they send is a count. It is nine bits long and it takes eleven centuries and it is the only sentence anyone here has ever addressed to anyone.

Four thousand years ago a faint click came back shaped wrong, and stayed wrong, and repeated, and resolved into a number.

It did not match.

Nothing here has been worn, or wetted, or eaten, or pollinated, or rotted, or moved by wind. Everything moves and nothing happens. And somewhere on the table a small bright point is crossing the basalt at two centimeters a second, carrying, in a few nanometers of the color of a film that conducts nothing, the corrected position of a place where something fell — going out toward the rim, going up through a crown, with thirty-one minutes left, being late, being the only thing here that can be.
