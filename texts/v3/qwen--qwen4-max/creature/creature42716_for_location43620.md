## The residents

The first one is not one.

The rust-colored lichen on the boulder lips is a fungus holding an alga in a grip so old that neither can now be found living apart. It has been coming apart and staying together on that rock for longer than there have been grigs. It grows a fingernail's width in a century and most of that century is spent waiting for the wind to stop. It does not know about the room. It is the only resident of this place that lives entirely in the light and never once goes inside, and it is the sign, and it is two things.

The tree is also more than it looks, but in the other direction. Above ground it is a meter of prostrate juniper, scale-leaved, resinous, wind-flayed to a brown crust on the windward side and green only in the lee, where the chimney's breath comes out through the choke-blocks and keeps a patch of ground the size of a bed free of snow all winter. That patch is the whole of its world above. It lives in the exhalation. Four hundred years of fruiting into thin air, and every cone it has ever made has either dried on the plateau or fallen into the hole.

The seeds that fall into the hole germinate. Of course they do; it is blood-warm and wet down there, the most inviting place on the mountain. They come up white, on stored sugar, a finger's length of blind hypocotyl reaching for a sun that is sixty meters and three turns away, and then they run out of what their mother gave them and stop. A few dozen a century. The fungus takes them in a month. This is the room's only immigration, and it arrives alive, and it is dead before it arrives anywhere.

---

Most of the tree is below. That is the part that lives here.

The roots come down through the last turn in pale fingered bundles, and they are not roots as the plateau understands roots — no bark, no pigment, no stiffness, a tissue like the inside of a lip. They grow toward wetness and nothing else. They hang in the fog stratum at twelve meters and drink, and the water goes up, and the sugar comes down, and where the sugar comes down it leaks out of the root tips into the dark.

That leak is the second economy of the room, and everything with a mouth is downstream of it.

The fungus that takes the leakage is the tree's own partner, the same organism that is up on the plateau mining iron out of andesite for the lichen next door, or a cousin of it; down here it has given up being a partner in anything and become a digester. It runs through the glass litter in threads the color of nothing, and it dissolves what falls: nymphs, elders, birds, moths, the white seedlings, the shed skins, the root tips themselves. It hands the nitrogen back up the root. The tree drinks fog and pays in light. The light was gathered on a plateau where nothing can breathe, in a meter of foliage lying on its side, and it comes down the chimney as sugar and goes into a grig and gets played out of the wall as sound.

There is a loop here that passes through the sun. Almost nothing else in the room does.

---

The other economy has no sun in it at all.

On the sweating walls below the fog there is a film, hand-thin, slick as the inside of a cheek, sulfurous, faintly iridescent where it is thickest. It is not plant and not animal. It eats the mountain. The fault brings up warmth and a whisper of sulfide, and the film oxidizes it, and that is the whole trick: chemistry into flesh, without any help from the sky. Around the pool the film is a mat you could peel. Higher up it thins to a greasiness. It is the reason the walls feel the way they feel.

Everything in the room that grazes, grazes this. Eleven million mouths, working a surface that the mountain is quietly manufacturing out of its own depth. The room is not a trap holding survivors from a wetter age. It is a place with two incomes, and it has been solvent for a very long time.

---

Now the grigs, which are the crowd, and which are not really a crowd once you watch one.

A grig's life is a descent of nine meters.

It hatches high, in the ropes — those hanging cables of animals holding on to each other that dangle from the ceiling blocks down into the fog. The ropes are usually described as the only structure anything here makes, which is true and undersells them. They are also plumbing. The lower ends hang in the cloud and the grigs there drink, and the water goes up the rope body to body, passed mouth to mouth along a chain of legs, a bucket line of fog moving upward through a thing that looks like a bell-pull and is actually a straw. The nursery is at the wet end. Newly molted nymphs are amber almost to transparency, soft, hissing thinly, with no groove yet under the thorax and no glass.

They climb down. It takes about a year and three molts, and the third molt cuts the saddle: a callused groove beneath the thorax, shaped to nothing yet, waiting.

Then they go to the east wall, because it is loud.

The vein outcrops there, and where the crowd is prising — a thousand bodies wedged into a cooling crack, pushing for a season until a plate lets go — the wall speaks with a rawness the rest of the chamber does not have. Fresh breakage rings. Nymphs orient toward loudness the way a moth orient toward a lamp, which is to say by a rule that works perfectly in the world they evolved in. They arrive at the shards.

And here each one does the only interesting thing it will ever do.

It presses a fragment to the stone. It draws the file of its forearm across it. It listens with its legs. It rejects. It rejects again. It rejects, on average, eleven times. Then it keeps one, and the groove closes over it, and it walks to the working band and begins.

What it is listening for is not pitch. A flake has no note of its own worth having. What it is listening for is whether the stone answers — whether this thickness, this curvature, this particular conchoidal dish of black glass will carry the stroke out of the arm and into thirty paces of andesite without losing it on the way. It is listening for a match. And the match is *local*: a shard that makes the east wall speak will lie mute against the north, where the rock is denser and the bedding runs another way. There are dialects of glass here, by wall sector. A grig blown loose and carried far must choose again or spend the rest of its life unheard.

So the scree underfoot, forty centimeters of spent and rejected obsidian, is not a midden of failures. Most of it is correct answers to other questions.

Three years of work. The flake dulls, is let go, another is chosen — two or three choices in a life, and nothing else that resembles a decision. Meanwhile the groove wears too, reshaping itself around whatever it is holding, so that a new flake fits badly for a week and the grig is awkward and quiet until stone and glass and cuticle have argued out a compromise. The tool wears the user. There is no word here for that relationship because there is no one here to have the word.

And this is the part that takes longest to see: **the bowing is the eating.** The forearm's stroke is the grazing stroke. The flake, pressed to the film, shears it; the file rides the flake; the mouth follows behind and takes what has been lifted. They are not playing the wall. They are licking it, and the sound is what licking sounds like when eleven million of you do it through volcanic glass against a resonant cupped hand of rock sixty meters underground.

Most of them never touch stone at all. The felt is three and four deep and only the innermost layer reaches. The rest bow against the backs of the ones in front, and bodies conduct — a grig is mostly water and water carries sound better than air — so the crowd itself is part of the instrument, a layer of living coupling between the arm and the mountain. When something dies in the felt there is a small dead patch in the transmission until the gap closes, an hour or two, a bruise in the voice that nobody notices because it is a bruise the size of a thumb in a sound thirty paces wide.

The note is not theirs. They make broadband noise, all of it, every scrape and clatter and idle stroke; the room refuses everything except one frequency and gives that back enormously amplified. They are not an orchestra. They are rain on a bell. And they steer by the bell — by the standing shape of the room's answer to their own hunger — which means that what a grig navigates by, its whole life, in the dark, is the aggregate appetite of everybody.

---

In the groove, between glass and cuticle, there is a mite that has never touched the wall.

It lives on wear-dust: powdered obsidian and powdered grig. It is the shape of a poppy seed with legs and it has a rider in its gut, and the rider has something in it too, and at that point the counting stops being a thing anyone could do. Every grig carries them. When a grig dies the mites walk out of the cooling body and into the crowd, and the whole population of the room's smallest residents is redistributed by the funerals of its largest.

There is another mite that lives only on the salamander's gills, and one that lives in the leg joints of spiders and therefore inherits silence, and one that lives in the tree's blind root tips and has, in some sense, been to the sun.

---

The hushes are where the note cancels itself, and they are the only addresses in the room.

The dying go there because they must. A grig that has lost the strength to hold a bearing does not wander randomly; it slides down the gradient, and silence is downhill. So the hushes are graveyards, and because they are graveyards they are the richest ground in the chamber, thick with fungus and springtails, and because they are rich they are baited, and because they are baited they kill. The geometry of the room digests its own population.

The spiders sit in them. Pale, long, without webs, legs out in a posture that is a net made entirely of patience. They do not hunt. They read the hiss with their legs and wait for it to stumble — for the exact ragged arrhythmia of a grig that has just lost the floor of the world. They still have eyes, eight of them, unpigmented, useless, a memory of the bright world kept in the wrong organ for six thousand years.

They make no silk. The eggs are glued to the underside of the abdomen and carried until they split, and then most of the daughters die in the loud, and one does not, because the mother walks her out.

That walk is two meters. It is the only parenting in the room. The mother crosses to the edge of the pocket, sets the daughter down in the absence, and comes back and dies in it, and the daughter eats her, and that is the inheritance in full: a place where the mountain does not speak, and the body of the one who showed you where it was.

The pocket moves a hand's breadth in a decade. So the walk is very slightly downhill every generation, and the lineage's entire history is a slide of eleven meters across a floor, holding nothing, building nothing, owning a hole.

The centipedes are the ones who know several holes.

They work the seam where note meets no-note, taking the hesitators at the boundary, and they can go into a hush and come out again, which no spider can do. They are dark, armored, longer-lived, and they walk on the tips of a hundred legs so quietly that they are the only thing in the chamber whose passage does not add to the hiss. They travel. They have something that functions like a route. If anything here has a plan, it is a centipede, and the plan is four meters long and takes eleven hours, and it will be executed again tomorrow, and it has been executed, near enough, since before there were people to be unimpressed by it.

---

In the litter: springtails in the glass, grazing the threads; nematodes in the water films between shards; the fungus fruiting only in the hushes, where the air is undisturbed by the note and a spore body can stand. The scree is layered like leaf-fall, each layer a few generations thick, and deeper down the glass is smaller and cruder because the vein was different then. There are moth scales in it. One moth per decade or so, a faint iridescence lying in a band a millimeter thick, the only color in forty centimeters of black. It is the most beautiful thing in the room and it is a stain left by something that came here by mistake and was dissolved.

---

In the pool there is one salamander, and *one* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

Everything else here is a crowd, a lineage, a mat, a felt, a number with no bottom. This is the only thing in the chamber that can be counted to one and stop. White, eyeless, gilled like a fern frond, breathing mostly through its skin, longer than a hand and older than the tree. It reads the pool with a lateral line: it knows the drip, every drip, the whole slow rain that amounts to nothing, and it knows the difference between a drip and a falling grig, and it moves only for the difference. Perhaps one meal a week. The rest is a white shape hanging in tepid water in the dark with its gills open, being a spine, being the only spine, being the closest thing here to you.

Every eleventh spring or so it lays a small clutch, unfertilized, and eats it before it molds. Nothing has ever come of this. It does not appear to be grief. It is a loop closing, like all the loops here.

But consider what it has been present for. The grigs get four years. The spiders get a pocket and a walk. The salamander has been lying in that basin while the wall wore wider and the note fell by an amount too small to name — a fraction of a semitone, a slow slackening of the whole world — and it is the only resident that has been continuously alive across the change, and it is the one resident that has no use for the note at all. Water does not care what pitch the room is in. The single witness is the one who cannot testify.

---

Twice a year the bright world sends something down: a wind-killed bird, a moth that read the warmth as a season, a coin-sized snowflake that arrives as a wet mark on stone. The room takes all of it. The bird is gone in a day and leaves an articulation of small clean bones lying in the glass, which the next thousand feet disperse.

And once a month, for eleven minutes, the moon puts a coin of light on the floor and the glass throws it back in a thousand black sparks, and nobody reacts, because nobody has eyes worth the name, and the light is for no one. It is the one thing here with no function. Everything else — every scrape, every mite, every two-meter walk, every unfertilized egg — is doing something, or is the residue of something being done.

---

It is tempting to say that the real resident is a single organism: two hundred thousand years old, eleven million cells long, each cell thumb-sized and lasting four years, its skeleton a wall it is dissolving, its voice dropping a semitone every four centuries, its only inheritance a lower pitch and a moved silence. That is nearly right and it is too tidy. It has no edge. The tree is in it and the tree is also on the plateau. The fungus is in it and is also up in the lichen. The sulfur comes from a fault that does not know the room exists. The moon is not in it at all.

And every sentence about this place reaches for *only* — the only structure, the only territory, the only one, the only thing with no function — because there is nothing here to compare anything to. The superlatives are a failure of the describer. Down there it is not remarkable. It is Tuesday.

---

So: a nymph, third molt, on the east wall, in the dark, in the fog's underside, with a fresh shard pressed to the stone.

It draws its arm. It listens with its legs. The stone does not answer — the flake is a hair too thick, or the dish of it wrong, or it is a perfect flake for a place four meters north. The nymph lets go. The shard falls into the litter and joins forty centimeters of similar refusals.

And here is the thing nobody in the room will ever know, because there is no one in the room to know it: the note is falling. The wall is widening. The place where the stone answers best is sliding down and around, a hand's breadth in a decade, and has been since before the ice. Some of what is being dropped tonight is not wrong. It is early. It is a correct answer to a question the wall has not asked yet, lying in the dark in a queue, waiting for a pitch that is a thousand years away.

The nymph tries another. The crowd shifts a body's width. The rain that amounts to nothing goes on falling through the fog it is made of, and the tree holds the hole with both hands, and the mountain bows itself in the dark, and somewhere a white animal that has never seen anything waits for the difference between a drip and a dinner.
