## The Wrights

The first mistake is to count them.

You come down the throat and you see the columns standing in their terraces, three spans, six spans, nine, each rooted in its tapped vent, each wearing its ruff of black blades and its hanging curtain of ten thousand working tendrils, and you think: *there is one, and there is another.* They do not think this. They have never thought this. A wright is not the column. The column is the part of a wright that happens to be thick.

What is yours is what your sap reaches without anyone else's help. That is the whole definition, and it is a definition of plumbing, not of skin. Your trunk is yours. So are the lamp-cups in your niches, which you feed and breed and which are, in every sense that matters here, part of your face. So is the cilia-carpet at your threshold, which is also the street. So is the graft you grew forty years ago with the neighbor across the gulf — that pipe is a treaty and it is also a limb, and it is not clear whose, and the question has been litigated for six hundred years without resolution. So is the runner you have laid along the wall toward the cool side, and the pale bud at the far end of it that is not yet anyone, and the empty trunk you left behind on the hot terrace, which is furnished, and let, and has a stranger living in it who is legally, in some districts, still you.

The census is therefore not a count. It is an assay. Ring-readers go out with assay-hooks and taste the boundaries and argue, and their findings are knocked into the switchboard slabs and disputed for a generation, and a wright of consequence may be two persons in a bad season and one again when the pressure recovers. *The middle is not ours,* they knock, meaning the gulf, meaning the air, meaning the vast hot nothing that nine-tenths of their world is made of. It is the only doctrine all three sects agree on. A person here is a rind too.

---

Understand the division inside them, because everything they are comes out of it.

The body is slow. The fastest thing a wright's body does is a tendril closing on a salt blade, and you would need to watch for an hour to be certain it had happened. Their faces — the fans, the ten thousand hooks and pads, which is where expression lives — move at the speed of carpentry. To read a face you must hold it: greeting is the interlocking of two fans, tendril into tendril, hook finding hook, and it takes most of an afternoon, and it is a handshake and an embrace and a long look all at once, and by the end of it each has learned the other's whole arrangement, the private way this one holds its third rank slightly folded, the worn pad where forty years of the same lamina-work has dulled a hook. Friends know each other's fans the way you know a voice. Lovers can reproduce each other's fan from memory, in salt, blind, and do.

And then there is the root-plate, and the root-plate is *fast*.

Speech is the only quick thing in their bodies. A knock crosses the Bell in eleven seconds; a thought can be sharp, can be sudden, can interrupt, can be witty. So a wright has two faces at two speeds, and the entire moral life of the species lives in the gap between them. You can be furious in the stone and serene in the wood for a decade. You can be dying of a neighbor's sour exudate, blades going pale, sap thinning, the fan drooping by a finger's width a year, and keep your knocking courteous, level, perfectly timed, for eighty years. This is their virtue and their art form and their vice. The highest compliment is *he kept the interval* — meaning: his blades were dying and he did not let it into his voice. The vulgar are the ones who let the fan speak, who go pale openly, who make their bodies say it. There are quarters where that is considered honest and quarters where it is considered a smell.

Lying, accordingly, is easy and impossible. In the stone you can say anything; the stone carries a false knock as cleanly as a true one, and they have no lie-detector and never invented one. But you cannot fake a lamina. You cannot counterfeit forty years of grown conduit. *A thing said is a thing guessed,* they knock. *A thing grown is a thing known.* Their courts do not take testimony. They take cores.

---

They have no eyes. They have, instead, ten thousand faces.

The black blades of the ruff drink the dimness and the sour gas together, and they also *report* it: a blade knows the direction of a glow the way your cheek knows the direction of a fire across a room. No image forms. Nothing here has ever had an image. What a wright has is a field of bearings — that way warmer-bright, that way three cups and a doorway, that way the cold nothing of the gulf. This is why the city is drawn in outlines and not in masses: it is not decoration, it is legibility. A green thread along a stair nosing is a sentence a body can read from forty paces. The middle of a wall says nothing to anyone. They have built themselves a diagram because a diagram is the only thing they can perceive.

It follows that they have no word for *point of view*. Their word for an opinion is a word for a pitch — the note you are standing on, the vent you are tapped into, the place from which your intervals are measured. To change your mind is to be re-tapped. It is a physical procedure and it takes a season.

The fan tastes. Air moving out of a conduit carries the whole biography of the district it came from: whose sap is thin, whose graft is leaking, where the filaments have been let loose, which quarter has begun the pale conversion that means a bloom is coming. They read this the way you read a newspaper, and with the same mixture of appetite and dread, and the fan is always reading it, and cannot be closed.

And the root-plate is the ear, and here is the thing that makes them unlike anything you have imagined: *conversation is illumination.* Every knock that goes out into the lattice comes back off the cubes and the octahedra and the far wall, and the returning pattern tells you what is there — who is home, which slab has thinned, whether the quarter across the gulf still has its buttresses or has begun to shed. To speak is to light the room. To be answered is to be shown where you are.

So silence is not peace here. Silence is blindness. A district that stops knocking has gone dark in the only sense they can be dark, and the great cruelty, the one their word for *torture* is built from, is to be spoken to and not replied to: to go on sending your light out and have nothing come back, and slowly lose the shape of the world.

Which is why there is a rota.

It is not a priesthood, though it behaves like one, and it has no doctrine. It is a list. In the closing walls, in the slots narrowed to the width of a hand, there are wrights who have been getting thinner for eight thousand years, and the lattice around them has folded until their knocks come out faint and strange, bent through ribbons of salt. Somebody answers them. On the hour, every hour, in every quarter, some wright stops its fan and lays its plate flat and replies to the far ends — about the weather, about the chord, about a stair that was finished last century. It is considered the smallest possible duty and the one that cannot be given up. *Answer the far ends.* Children are taught it before they are taught to knock in time. There is no belief behind it. When you ask why, they say: *because otherwise they cannot see.*

---

A name is an address and a rhythm.

The address is the pitch of your tapped vent — a bore, a whistle, a note in the floor's choir. The rhythm is your idiom, the particular way your plate strikes, unforgeable, the grain of your quickness. Together they are what you are called, and both halves are unstable, and this is the great comedy and the great grief of their society.

The choir retunes twice a day. At the exhale the whole floor slides down; at the inhale it climbs. So every name has two forms, a low name and a high name, and to use the wrong one is either an intimacy or an insult depending on precisely how wrong it is. Lovers use the exhale-name at the inhale. Enemies use it flatly, in public, on the switchboard slab, where forty conversations pass and none of them touch, and everyone hears.

And when a wright moves — grows its runner, buds its far trunk, pours itself across two hundred paces over sixty years — the address stays behind. The name is real estate. Someone else will tap that vent, and take that pitch, and be called that, and inherit the grafts that were grown into it and the debts along them and the shading-feud that has been running since before either of them was a bud. There are citizens in the Bell who are feeding, through a pipe they did not grow, an enemy they have never met, on behalf of a person whose empty trunk they are living in. They know this. They keep the pipe. To cut it is to starve. The slow wars of this city are almost all of this kind: not hatred, but tenancy.

The vacant trunks are the cheap housing of the world, and there is a whole class who live in them, and the etiquette is exact: you do not repair the previous occupant's wear. The dishes worn into the stair are their gestures, the polished jamb is the place their fan rested for nine hundred years, and you put your tendrils in the hollows and you are, for as long as you live there, a little bit shaped like someone who left. It is not considered haunted. It is considered furnished.

---

They have no travel. They have handing.

Nothing in the Bell moves itself except a falling slab, and so the cold-salt trade — hot blocks out to the night-side radiators, cold blocks back — is not carried but *passed*: a chain of wrights along a route, fan to fan, hook to hook, a road made of people who each move a thing one span and never go anywhere. Distance is measured in hands. A block crosses the bowl in a season and passes through four hundred lives, and each of those lives knows the block, and some of them know it well, and there are wrights on the cold routes whose entire biography is a list of blocks. The porters' guild is not a company of travelers. It is a sentence four hundred words long, and the words are people, and the thing they say is *cold.*

Their tenses are built accordingly. There is a tense for what is merely knocked, which is the tense of rumor and of love and of most of history. There is a tense for what has been grown, which is evidential and unanswerable. There is a tense — the difficult one, the one children get wrong for a century — for *what is still arriving*: the runner not yet rooted, the bud not yet anyone, the treaty half-grafted, the child. A young person here is not a small adult. A young person is a far end. For forty years the bud and the trunk are one organism and two addresses, both half-empty, arguing with each other in public across the switchboard slabs, and everyone listens, and it is thought charming, and it is the only period of life in which a wright is allowed to be rude.

And their comedy is timing, because their poetry is timing. A sentence is eleven seconds, because eleven seconds is the boom's death and the wake of the boom is where a voice can be heard. To speak into the boom is their idiom for vanity. The high form is the spanning sentence: a thing begun before the boom, held silent through it by everyone who heard the first half, and landed after — so that the audience carries the meaning across the deaf interval themselves and delivers it to themselves. The best of these are famous for a thousand years. The best of the jokes are grown: a stair whose last step, forty years in the making, completes a rhythm begun at the bottom, so that the punchline arrives under the foot of a stranger two generations after the comedian has become the banister.

---

But the wrights are not the only living things, and the question of who else is *anyone* is the oldest argument in the Bell.

There are the mats at the vent-mouths: flat, hot, patient, unindividuated, doing the chemistry that makes the almond oil out of hydrogen and sour gas. They have no root-plate. They cannot knock. They also, indisputably, made everything — every drop of the fluid in every line, every wall of every wright. One sect holds that the mats are the citizens and the wrights are their hands, grown upward for convenience, the way a hand is not a person. Another holds that the salt is the person: *the stone dreams the plan, and we only trace it,* and the wrights are the dream's organs, and the mats its digestion. The third holds that a person is a pressure district and nothing else, and that the argument is a waste of good sap. They have conducted this dispute for longer than there have been eyes anywhere, and they conduct it by building. Each sect has grown its position into architecture, in cubes and octahedra, and you can walk up an argument. It is not settled. It is load-bearing.

There are the lamp-cups, bred in lineages, named, fed, gossiped about, the only possessions that can be inherited without an address. In the sealed dark quarters, after a bloom, the cups nobody feeds go on glowing for centuries, dimmer and dimmer, lighting nothing, and the neighbors know exactly how bright they were when the quarter died, and can date the mourning by it.

There are the sweeper carpets: the largest labor force in the world, mute, passing the yellow dust hand to hand to hand across the plazas forever. They have no plate. They cannot answer. And yet their cilia beat, and the beat can be modulated, and a carpet addressed with a drum will, over a season, alter its tide in a way that is not quite a reply and is not quite nothing. There are wrights — a few in each generation, thought eccentric, thought holy — who spend an entire life on one exchange with a floor. Four questions, four answers, eight decades. They are the only citizens who have ever claimed to have been surprised.

And there are the unknotting filaments, which are not anyone, which dissolve dead structure back into sap and gas, which are kept in slots and released with a courtesy that is not addressed to them. They smell of almonds, strongly. So does everything alive. The smell of life here and the smell of its undoing are the same smell, and no one has ever found this remarkable.

---

Twice in recorded history something came back down the throat alive.

The spores go out on the exhale and lie on the plain as yellow flour and die, all but nothing, in a place the city has no word for except *the place where the spores go* — which is, if you say it plainly, a word for where the children go. Every bloom is a birth and a suicide and an emigration at once: a quarter goes pale, roars its stored sap up as fog, and dies back, and the neighbors seal it and keep it dark a decade and harvest its tube-wood, and the mourning is indistinguishable from the carpentry. There is a sect that blooms often, deliberately, on purpose, believing the plain is real. They are considered mad. Their quarters are dark all along the eastern rind.

And twice, something came back. Wrong color, wrong smell, a stranger's chemistry that the rest of the Bell finds faintly nauseating, rooted in a corner and not removed — because both times the strangers survived a gas spike that killed everything else. They are kept. They are fed. They are married into, reluctantly, at intervals of centuries. Their descendants smell wrong forever and hold, by custom, the offices that face outward: the shutter-priests who listen to the graduated pillars tick as the coin of daylight comes down and cooks the salt, and who have never perceived the light they spend their lives preventing; and the few, in every generation, who grow a runner *up* — up the slant of the throat, toward the cold thread, toward the almond smell going thin — and bud at the top, in the fringe of scorched failed growth, and die there, mostly.

The city calls them a word that means both *insurance* and *nausea*. It does not remove them. It has never once removed them.

---

Go along the closing wall, in the folded districts, where the stairs are ribbons and the rooms are the width of a hand and a green thread of lamp-film runs down a slot in the rock.

Inside is a wright eight thousand years thin. Its ruff is a hand's breadth of black blades. Its fan is nine tendrils where there were ten thousand, and each one is worn to a hook of glassy old sap, and it cannot build anything anymore, and it has not built anything for a very long time. It is amber, then black; it is legible only as a reflection; it returns the light of the quarter across the gulf and is known by which lights it returns.

On the hour it lays its plate against the stone and knocks about the weather. The knock goes out bent and faint through the folded lattice, into the mains, up the terraces, across the switchboard where forty conversations pass and none of them touch.

And somewhere out in the vast dim outline of that city, in a lit room three cups bright, a wright stops its fan — ten thousand hooks going still, which is the only silence it can make — and lays its plate flat, and answers.
