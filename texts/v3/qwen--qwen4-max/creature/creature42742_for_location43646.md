## The tenants

The keepers are the least of it, and they know it, and this is not humility. It is an inventory.

There are fifty-one adults on the shelf this season and thirty-four children, and forty thousand structures, and something on the order of nine hundred million underhares, and a crust that may be one individual or may be eleven kilometers of neighbors. The keepers do the arithmetic without embarrassment. They are the ones with the mallets. That is all they claim.

You can identify a keeper at two hundred meters by the way they are cooked. Everyone up here is darker on one side — the side that faced the sun on the round, which is the side that faces the sun on every round, because the round is inherited and so is the direction you walk it. The night families are pale and the dawn families are halved. Their faces have the same crow's feet, all of them, the whole population squinting the same squint, and their lips are split in the same four places, and their chests are deep in a way that looks like pride and is only plumbing. They breathe about nine times a minute at rest. They drink constantly and never appear to sweat. Their boots are crusted pink to the ankle from the sinks and their hands are crusted white to the wrist from the pans, and a keeper's hands at forty are the hands of a lowland man of seventy.

They sleep at noon, in the cellars, under the ice they made, in a cold that costs nothing because it was going to be sold anyway. This is the only luxury on the shelf and everybody has it.

They do not shout. It is not a rule; it is a budget. A raised voice at four thousand four hundred meters is a thing you pay for out of the same account as lifting a slab, and to spend it on anger is considered not offensive but *stupid*, the way it would be stupid to burn a chair. What they have instead is a language of the hands, small and economical, most of it makeable with one hand while the other holds something heavy, and a spoken register used at half a meter, which is the distance at which two people who like each other stand.

Their names are signs. There is a woman whose name is the gesture for setting a mallet down without letting it ring. There is a boy called Late Fog, for the morning he was born. There is an old man at the funicular head whose name is a two-handed lifting motion and nothing else, and everyone knows what he did to earn it, and he has never once been asked about it.

The shelf's population is divided into the dark and the gray: those who mind the engines through the nine-hour night, and those who take the lids at dawn. They overlap for about forty minutes. That overlap is the entire social life of the region. Marriages are made in it, arguments are conducted in it, children choose their shift in it at fourteen. Otherwise the two halves of the human species up here live in the same huts at different hours and know each other by the state of the tools.

## The steps

Here is the fact that organizes everything, and it takes a moment to feel it.

The crust advances at its edge by about a millimeter a decade.

A bootprint is twenty centimeters across. It heals from both rims inward, ten centimeters each way, a hundred millimeters at a millimeter a decade. A bootprint is a thousand years.

The keepers therefore walk in footprints. Not *like* footprints — in them, in the actual depressions, the same ones, the ones their grandmothers made, worn now into a shallow dish in the varnish and faintly darker at the center where a century of soles has polished the stone through the crust's grip. A round is a sequence of about eleven thousand inherited steps. You learn it as a child by being made to walk behind an adult with your feet in her feet, which is a slow and humiliating apprenticeship, and takes four years, and is the whole of the law.

To step off the line is a tort. It has a name and a price and the price is not paid in money.

The middle districts are different, and the difference is the reason the shelf is survivable at all. Where the warrens are, the ground is *quick*: the underhares turn it, fluff it, spoil it, reseed it, and a scar there closes in a season because thirty thousand small animals are constantly making new ground and the crust colonizes new ground fast. Out on the slow ground, where nothing digs, healing runs at the speed of the crust itself.

The catastrophe is not damage. The catastrophe is a noise that turns quick ground into slow ground.

A sharp onset — a percussion drill, a dropped iron bar, a trike with pneumatic tires thumping a cobble — flushes a warren. A flush is a stampede. A stampede collapses the galleries and kills the young and kills, in one afternoon, the tillers. Then the crust is broken over two hundred meters with nothing left to churn it, and the wound closes at a millimeter a decade from the edges, and a hundred meters from each side is a million years.

There is such a wound in the Old Field. It is a hundred and thirty years old, from a drill run in the twelfth year of the second expansion by a man who had come up from the cloud and had not been told properly, or had been told and had thought it was a custom. It is gray. It is the exact ash-gray of dead ground and it is two hundred and ten meters across and in low sun you can see its rim, which is the crispest edge on the entire shelf, because everything else here has weathered and this has not had time.

Children are walked out to it once. Nobody explains it. They are simply made to stand at the rim and look, and then made to walk home in the old steps.

The rule that comes out of this is not stated as ecology. It is stated as manners. *Do not startle the ground.* And the subtlety of it is that loudness is not the criterion — the air forgives, the ground remembers. A shutter's flat clack at four meters up dies in forty meters of thin air and never enters a burrow. A boot heel on a cobble is a hammer on somebody's roof. The mallets are wooden and the keepers strike with a soft wrist, a rising blow, a sound with a slow front edge, and an apprentice who lets the mallet ring is not scolded. The adult simply takes the mallet and demonstrates, and hands it back, and this happens as many times as it needs to.

## The diggers

The underhares are the plain's stomach and its lung and its road crew, and they have no idea.

Pallid, short-eared, half-blind, with forearms like a mole's and a metabolism slowed to a crawl, they live in towns of thirty thousand under the Sorting, and their mouths are every forty centimeters, and their spoil is the softest ground for six hundred kilometers. They plug and unplug those mouths all day according to the pressure and the temperature, steering air through the galleries, and when a front comes over the rim thirty thousand of them exhale at once and the plain makes a sound two kilometers wide that the keepers call turning over in its sleep.

They farm, in the sense that anything farms: they cache saltbush seed in chambers where it molds, and they eat the mold, and they eat the crust that falls in, and their droppings grow a fungus they also eat, and their digging is what keeps the quick ground quick. Their pups come up in the fifty minutes, blind and pink, and are taken by tally birds, and this is not a tragedy; it is the arrangement.

Nobody has proved that the warrens make the second dew. It is only the largest of the theories. Thirty thousand lungs breathing wet air out of cool galleries into a morning that has already been wrung dry by the sun would do it, arithmetically, if the numbers were slightly kinder than anyone can measure. There is a woman at the ninth pole who has kept a hygrometer and a notebook for forty-one years, and her notebooks are the most valuable object on the shelf, and she will tell you, at half a meter, that she does not know.

## The small hours

Inside the crust there is a fauna that no one has ever seen awake all at once, because it is never all awake at once.

There is a springtail, two millimeters, slate-colored, that wakes with the wet, feeds, molts, mates, and dries down again inside the fifty minutes. It lives about forty years. Summed across its whole life it has been awake for eleven days. It experiences the shelf as a single brief party held in installments.

There are nematodes in the crust that have been dry since before the industry and are not dead. There is a mite that eats the black mold and is eaten by a beetle larva that eats nothing else and has, accordingly, a fifty-minute childhood.

The crust itself thickens a millimeter a decade and smells, for one hour, like iodine and warm bread, and then smells like nothing for twenty-three. In places it runs unbroken for eleven kilometers. Down in the cloud there is a case about this — whether the eleven kilometers constitute one organism, and if so whether an organism can hold a lease, and if so who has been signing. The keepers find the question uninteresting in a specific way: they already walk in each other's footprints. Whatever the court decides, the landlord will be the ground, and the ground will still take a million years to forgive a drill.

## The engineers, and their children

The beetles invented the harp. This is not a metaphor the keepers use; it is a line of descent they can point at.

A hundred and thirty years ago someone copied the ridging and the hydrophilic tips and the waxy channels off an elytron and built a two-meter frame, and the frames now stand across the whole shelf taking their share of the fog, and the beetles work the gaps, and on good mornings the beetles climb the mesh and drink from it because it is easier.

It has been easier for a hundred and thirty beetle generations.

You can see what that has done. Out on the far ridges, in the gaps, the beetles are still deeply grooved — old-form, sharp-shouldered, capable of standing on their heads in a fog and making their own water the way their ancestors did. Near the harps they are smoother. The grooves have relaxed. The waxy channels are shallower and in some populations vestigial, and those beetles cannot drink from fog alone anymore, and do not need to, and would die out in a season if the frames came down. They are being domesticated by a machine that was copied from them. Nobody intended it. Nobody is doing it. It is simply what happens when you make a thing easy for a hundred and thirty generations.

And in the Old Field there is the station whose shutters jammed open in the second generation, whose heat store cracked, and which still, through an accident of plumbing nobody has ever bothered to trace, makes one slab of ice every clear night.

The beetles found it sixty years ago. On foggy mornings there are so many on that single pan that the ice comes up black and moving. They breed in the rim. They no longer disperse; there is no reason to. Their wings have begun to fuse. They have a microclimate — the pan cools the air above it, the crowd's own respiration wets it, and the wetness makes more beetles possible, and the beetles make more wetness. By seven it is a damp ring in the dust and by eight it is gone and it happens again the next night.

It is the only spring on the shelf. It is made by a broken machine and maintained by insects, and it is sixty years old, and it will very likely be there when the last keeper has gone down the funicular for the last time.

Children are taken there once, too. It is the second thing they are shown. Nobody says what it is for.

## The talliers

The tally birds are waist-high, gray, stilt-legged, voiceless, and they walk the same beat for sixty years.

The name is a census. A bird knows its mouths — every burrow on its beat, individually, by smell and by the sound of what is under it — and it walks the beat at a measured pace and stops and strikes, and it takes the surplus, and it has taken the surplus from those same galleries since before the keeper who shares the beat was born.

Their beats and the keepers' rounds have converged. Neither species planned it. Both are solving the same problem, which is how to cross eleven kilometers of ground without making a new footprint, and the answer is a single line, and so the line is shared, at different hours, by a bird and a woman who have never met and who are, in every functional sense, colleagues. Rounds are named for birds. A keeper will say she walks Old Hook, and mean both the route and the animal, and will not distinguish, and if you ask her to distinguish she will look at you as though you have asked her to separate a road from its ruts.

When they do meet, in the fifty minutes, the etiquette is absolute: the human stops. The bird passes. It passes at a meter and a half, unhurried, with its hooked bill low, and it does not acknowledge her, and she stands in her grandmother's footprints with a mallet in her hand for as long as it takes, and this is the closest thing on the shelf to a formal greeting.

## The ones who go down

The lifters are the only inhabitants who have touched the river.

They cannot rise from flat ground in air this thin; they need the gorge lip and the noon column, and they spend the afternoon climbing and sliding in the ninety percent of everything that is empty, doing nothing that looks like work. In the evening they drop through the cloud deck into the wet world and come back at dawn with damp feet, and the keepers have seen the damp, and there is no other evidence anywhere on the shelf that the world below is real.

They also eat dead things. Which means that when a warren collapses somewhere out past the ninth pole, the first anyone knows of it is lifters coming inland — three or four of them, low over the Sorting, in a place lifters have no business being. It is how the shelf finds out that somebody has made a noise. The birds are the alarm and the accusation at once.

And sometimes a lifter comes down on the plain and cannot get up.

This is the one exception. A grounded lifter will walk to the lip, and it is a heavy animal with a wide wingspan and dragging feet, and it will cut a scar eleven kilometers long that will not close in a million years. So when the sign goes up — two hands, a lifting motion, the old man's name — the keepers run.

They run. On the quick ground, off the steps, at four thousand four hundred meters, with their hearts going like the pumps, and they take the sled and they go out and they carry it to the edge, and it takes six adults and most of an hour and someone always vomits afterward and someone always has a nosebleed that will not stop.

The damage they do running is real and is entered in the ledger. It is entered against the alternative. The arithmetic is never close.

There is a man at the funicular head who has done it once. He is named for it. He will be named for it after he is dead.

## The slow ones

The cushions are not really plants in the way a lowlander means. They are two centimeters of living resin-green skin over a dead heart, and the heart is peat, and the peat is the plant's own ancestors laid down in order, and a core out of a four-meter cushion is a climate record three thousand years long. Underhares burrow beneath them because the resin makes a roof that does not collapse. A beetle mines the dead interior and has never seen light. And the whole dome is a sponge: it takes the fog and holds it for weeks, and a cushion squeezed at noon, illegally, will give up a mouthful of water that fell as fog nineteen days ago.

Their shade is a crescent a hand's width wide, and it is occupied — skinks, always, the same skinks, on the same stones worn shiny by four generations of the same belly. To sit in a cushion's shade at noon is to displace a lizard, and keepers do it, and apologize, in the small one-handed way.

A spoonful of resin per cushion per decade is the legal take, and the resin seals the cellar lids, and without the seal there is no ice, and without ice there is nothing to sell, and the entire economy of the shelf terminates in that spoonful. There is a larva that eats the seal. Keepers inspect the lids by smell.

The resin round takes thirty-one years.

There is a keeper whose name is the sign for a spoon held level. She is fifty-eight. She began the round at twenty-six and will finish it next season, and she has visited eleven thousand cushions once each, and she will never see any of them again. Her master did the round before her and died before she was halfway through, and she has found his marks on four thousand cushions and could not check a single one of his decisions against the plant, because the plant will not have changed enough to tell her anything. She has an apprentice. The apprentice will spend her whole working life finding out whether Spoon was right.

This is the ordinary condition of competence on the shelf. You do work whose verification belongs to someone else.

## Noon

The dead hour is not dead. It is simply not ours.

At sixty-two degrees on the varnish there is a silver ant. It is long-legged and mirror-haired and it holds its body four millimeters off the stone, which is the difference between cooking and not, and it forages in the four seconds that would blister your forearm, on a plain where every predator is underground or under a cushion or asleep in a cellar. It navigates by the polarized violet. It runs a hundred body-lengths out and a straight line home. It has the entire shelf to itself for three hours a day and has had it for a very long time, and if you want to know what the noon shift looks like, it looks like eleven thousand silver specks moving very fast over a floor that a keeper has been told since childhood is empty.

The keepers are asleep beneath the ice, and they shout in their dreams. All of them. It is the only place shouting happens on the shelf, and they wake with sore throats, and the children — who have never heard a raised voice — are frightened by their own sleep, and are told, at half a meter, that it is only the body doing something it has no use for.

The ones who go down the funicular cannot sleep at all. The wet world is loud, and it also *smells*, continuously, at full volume, everything at once, and a shelf-born nose has no filter for it because it has never needed one. Smell down there is a kind of shouting. They weep a great deal and are thought to be emotional. Mostly they are just being yelled at by grass.

## The old air

Some of the cellars broke into fossil ice in the second expansion: twelve thousand years old, full of small round bubbles from an atmosphere nobody has breathed. It cuts and sells the same. The keepers prefer it and say it melts slower, which is true.

Children crack a slab and put an ear to it and listen to a bubble pop, and call this listening to the old weather, and are indulged.

The body is finite. Everyone knows the approximate year. There is a word for the period after it, and the word is not fearful, exactly; it is the word for a shift you have not been assigned to yet. Meanwhile the slabs go down twice a night into the cloud, and the world below melts them, and gets — along with its cold — a little Pleistocene air, and crust dust, and the odd beetle, and a few hundredths of a millimeter of dew that a stone made for itself out of nothing on a clear night at four in the morning.

Nobody down there has asked what is in the ice. It is cold. That is what was ordered.

## The morning

Add it up and something appears that no single species on the shelf can see.

The crust greens in ninety seconds. The springtails wake and molt and mate. The beetles stand on their heads on the ridges and climb the mesh in the gaps. The harps run their grooves full. The underhare pups come up blind and the tally birds walk their beats and take the surplus. The waders bloom pink and silent across the sinks. The moths shiver on the vents that are still warm from the day before yesterday. The keepers strike with a soft wrist and lift the lids and stack the cold, and stand still when the bird passes, and step where their grandmothers stepped.

All of it happens in fifty minutes, and all of it stops together, and none of it can happen without the others, and it has been happening for four thousand years.

Fifty minutes a day for four thousand years is about a hundred and forty years.

That is the age of the thing that lives here. Not four thousand — a hundred and forty. It is older than the language and it has been awake for roughly one human lifetime, in installments, and it has a body eleven kilometers wide made of animals and machines and one crust that may be a single individual, and it dies at eight every morning and is born again at the dew, and it does not know it exists, and there is no one outside it who can see it whole.

And lately — within the industry's short life, within the last hundred and thirty years, since the harps went up and the footing rings grew dense and the warrens learned to breathe around forty thousand warm structures — it has begun to breathe twice.

The second dew arrives after the sun has taken the first. Eleven or twelve minutes. The crust greens again. Nobody has satisfactorily explained where the water comes from: the warrens' thirty thousand lungs, the cushions exhaling nineteen-day-old fog, the crust letting go of what it bound at dawn, some combination, some new organ assembled out of parts that were all here separately before and have recently been put into relation.

The keepers do not argue about it. They open the harps. It is worth a third of a night's yield, and it is the only thing on the shelf that has ever increased.

---

At four in the morning in the Old Field, on a pan nobody maintains, the ice comes up black and moving.

The beetles are on it in a layer three deep, and they are drinking, and breeding, and their wings are fusing, and their grooves are gone. The machine that made the cold was copied from their great-grandparents. It has been broken for sixty years. It has stopped being a machine in any sense that matters and become a wet place, a spring, a congregation, a thing the plain has taken in and made a tenant of — the way it took in the machine-fields and made them indistinguishable from the Sorting, the way it took the vents and made them a moth nursery, the way it took the footings and made them gardens.

Everything up here that was built is being adopted. That is the direction it goes. The shelf does not resist and does not negotiate; it simply grows over, at a millimeter a decade, and sorts, and fills every stone with a tenant, and waits for the newcomers to become locals.

By nine it is gray and hot and looks like nothing at all.

Fifty-one adults asleep under their own ice. Thirty-four children in the cellar shade. Nine hundred million diggers holding their breath. Ten thousand silent birds standing in brine. Eleven thousand silver ants out on a floor at sixty-two degrees with the whole world to themselves. Four ragged black shapes climbing a column above a gorge, coming up from a river with wet feet.

And a hundred and forty years of morning, spread thin across four thousand years of stone, waiting for the dew to come back.
