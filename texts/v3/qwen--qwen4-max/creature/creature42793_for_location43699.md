# The Keepers

## Two Faces

They are a people with two faces, and the seam runs across the brow.

Above the visor line the skin is weathered dark, cracked into a fine map at the temples; below it, in the shade of the wooden brim, it is pale as the inside of a shell. Everyone has this mask. Children get it in their second year. You can read a person's age from the fan of squint-lines at the outer eye, which deepens by a hair each decade, and from the way the pale part of the face has begun to sag away from the dark part, so that the old look as though they are wearing a hood they have not quite pulled off.

Their backs are the other clock. The portage from the fan to the walls begins at fifteen and ends when the spine says so, and it builds a particular body: a thickening across the shoulders, a forward set to the neck, a way of standing with the weight already slightly downhill even on level pavement. Old keepers stand as if still carrying. Some of them, put to bed in the bright hours, cannot lie flat, and sleep propped against a corbel with a sedge mat under the shoulder, in the posture of a person halfway up a stair.

The hands are ruined and superb. Sand and stone and forty days of lichen wear the fingerprints off by thirty; after that the palms are smooth and glassy and can feel a fall of one part in a thousand, which is how a grader tests a slab, by lying on it in the drinking hour with the cheek against the wet and the palm flat, reading the direction the water wants to go. They have a word for a slab that has been dressed correctly. It translates as *obedient*.

They speak quietly and low, under the hiss of the combs rather than over it, and mostly at dusk. The drinking hour is too loud for talk and too cold; noon is too bright and belongs to sleep. So the whole social life of the town happens in the second work, on silver pavement, under stars that do not twinkle, with a rake in the hand. Sixty-one people who see each other's work all day and each other's faces for about two hours.

## Sixty-One

The number is not a law. It is a quotient.

Comb yield in jars, divided by what a body needs, gives sixty-one and a fraction, and the fraction is where all the trouble lives. There is no council, no decree, no enforcement. There is the ledger, and there is the fact that everyone can read it, and there is the arithmetic of what happens to sixty-two people on a pavement that makes sixty-one.

So a birth is a vacancy, and a vacancy is a death, and the two are spoken of in the same breath and never in the same sentence.

A woman may wait three years. She waits with the roster column in her head, doing the sum she has been taught not to do, and everyone can see her doing it, and the courtesy of the place is that nobody says so. There is a phrase offered to the very old, at dusk, with a hand on the forearm: *You have carried a great deal.* It means thank you. It also means the other thing. It is said gently, by people who mean it gently, and it is the most frightening sentence in the language.

They are not cruel. This is the difficulty. They are extremely kind, in the specific way of people who cannot afford to be otherwise, and their kindness has a shape you would not recognise as kindness until you had lived inside it a while: the way a dying keeper is moved to the door with the best light, the way the last four ripenings are named for them in advance, the way nobody lets them do the stair rotation alone.

And there is a party — it is not a party, it is three or four people at a time, and it has existed for six hundred years — who hold that the fraction is real. That the instruments have precision, and precision is a width, and inside that width there is room for one more. They are called the margin. They recompute. They are always allowed to recompute. They have never been able to make the fraction wide enough to be sure, and the town's opinion of them is a sort of exhausted affection, the way you feel about a relative who keeps writing to a dead address.

## A Door Each

Sixty-one open doorways. Sixty-one people. This is not a coincidence of counting; it is what a person is here.

They do not live in households. They live in cells, one to a body, scattered on their separate bosses of rock with a hundred paces of graded pavement between, and the town's intimacy is all conducted outdoors, in transit, in the gaps between work. To love someone is to cross open stone to them twice a day for forty years. There are pairs who have done this so long that the runnels between their two doors have been re-dressed by unspoken agreement to make the walk shorter by nine paces, and this is the closest thing to a marriage contract the Sill possesses, and everybody notices when it happens, and nobody remarks on it.

A child belongs to somebody else's door until a door comes free.

When it does, the block is lifted out. The room behind is a stranger's: the sleeping hollow worn by a body of a different size, the private tally scratched by the jamb that no longer means anything, the faint cut of a name that the new name will be laid over but not erase. You inherit the soot. You inherit the smell. Keepers speak of their rooms in a way that is hard to translate — not *my* room, and not *the* room, but something like *the room I am currently the answer to*.

The block that comes out of the door goes into a wall.

This is the part that outsiders, if there were any, would take longest to accept. A death costs a block: the door must be sealed, well-fitted, and there are only four blocks a year. So a year with a death in it is a year in deficit, and some wall somewhere goes unrepaired, its eaten course plugged with sedge and hair and packed clay against the next ripening. A birth releases one. The masonry of the town is conserved by the roster. You can stand at the eastern lip and read four hundred years of demographic history in the plugs: the bad decade, the long wait, the year two went at once. The walls are a record of who was missing.

They know this. When a keeper lays a door-block into a hungry wall, they say the name of the person whose door it was, once, quietly, into the joint, and then they set it and bed it and go back down for the next load. It is not a funeral. The funeral was eleven years ago, or will be in eleven years, depending on which stone you mean.

## The Turns

There are no professions. There are rotations, and a life is the sequence of them.

At four, the copper wire and the pools: you are given a hook and a stretch of pavement and you learn to lift a snail out of a saucer of water without cracking it, and this is the first thing anyone remembers. At seven, the lichen, forty days, thumbnail-sized, a bone scraper on the four rows where the swifts are nesting because sand would frighten them, and a wooden one everywhere else. At eleven you are allowed on the fan, which is to say allowed near eleven years of somebody's patience. At fifteen, the back.

After that the turns widen: grading, channel-lining, sedge, the goats, the terraces, the coppice, the fire, the instruments, the stair. Everyone does everything eventually. Nobody is a specialist, because specialisation is a form of growth and there is no growth.

Except in the instruments.

That is the one place where a person can be better than another person, and so it is the one place where the town's whole suppressed appetite for distinction has gone to live. The making of measures is a tournament conducted over centuries, in near silence, with immense politeness. A keeper will spend thirty years refining a float-and-lever that reads the cistern to a hair, and demonstrate it, and the demonstration will show that the cistern has not changed, and everyone will stand around the hall admiring the mechanism with real and unfeigned joy, and the maker will feel something they have no word for that is not quite pride and not quite grief.

The best maker of each generation is called the rod-hand. The rod-hand keeps the working copper against the master copper, and the master copper is said to live behind one of the sealed doors that is neither rubble nor a corpse. Which door, nobody says. Whether it has been opened in the ledger's memory, nobody says either. There is a whole etiquette of not asking, and children ask, and are told: *that one is not empty.*

## Six or Seven Dozen

Everything is counted. The frogs are estimated.

This is deliberate. The tally for the cistern hall has a line for the frogs and the line has read *six or seven dozen* for as long as the wall has existed, in a hundred hands, including the hands of rod-hands who could have counted them exactly in an afternoon and did not.

It is the town's one loose figure. The one place where the proof is allowed to breathe. They put something there — mercy, slack, an admission that a living thing is not a jar — and they will not explain it in those words, and if you asked a keeper why they don't count the frogs properly they would look at you as though you had proposed counting the dead.

The children count them anyway, badly, learning numbers. And the children name them.

This is the great underground art of the Sill: frog names. They are not in the roster. They are obscene, or grandiose, or they are the names of the illustrious dead bestowed on a small pale animal sitting in a shaft of light with its throat going. A frog called by the name of a rod-hand four centuries dead is the funniest thing that has ever happened, and it happens most years, and the adults do not laugh where the children can see, and the adults do not stop it. It is understood that the frogs carry what the ledger cannot.

## The Names

The roster column is one copper-rod wide. That is the constraint on the only art the town has that is not maintenance.

A name must fit. A name must not have been used. After a thousand years of sixty-one at a time, the space of short pronounceable unused names is a narrowing thing, and there is a keeper — always one, by rotation, but in practice always the same sort of person — whose life's work is finding the next ones. They work at dusk with a stick in the dry dust of a slab, sounding syllables into the hiss.

The roster is memorised entire. All of it, every name, in order, by everyone, by twenty. It is the only literature. It is recited at the four ripenings and at the drinking of the year's first cold, and its metre is jagged and its rhymes are accidental and it is unbearably beautiful to them, because every name in it is a person who was the difference that year, and because when you hear your own name you hear the shape of the gap you will leave.

And they have computed the end.

Not the end of the water: the water is eternal, the water is the whole point, the fountain will turn as long as there is cloud. The end of the names. It is a real calculation, done with real care, revised every generation by the rod-hand of the day, and it gives a number of years, and the number is not large enough to be comfortable and not small enough to be urgent, and it has been not-quite-urgent for six hundred years.

What happens then is the town's only theology. Some say the column will be allowed to repeat, and the proof will begin again, and that this is what the proof is for. Some say the last name will be the last keeper, and the town will close its own doors from the inside, one by one, sixty-one fitted blocks, and the combs will go on combing. And some — this is the argument that will not die — say that when the names run out, someone will come up the stair with new ones.

There is also copper. The copper came from somewhere else; there is no copper in this limestone; every wire and every rod is cut from a stock that has never increased, and the filings are swept and kept and weighed, and a wire too short to hook a snail is not thrown away but twisted into a longer one. The copper is the other hourglass, and quieter, and the keepers who worry about it worry alone.

## The Bright Hours

They sleep together in the outer chamber of the hall, under the ledger, in the hours when the pavement is too bright to look at. Sixty-one bodies in a stone room with a moving slab of light on the floor, and below them the frogs and the finished chord, which nobody has adjusted in living memory and which everyone hears in their sleep.

They dream. And because nothing happens here, dreams are the only place where anything happens, and dream-telling is the town's narrative art, its theatre, its scandal, its news.

There is a taxonomy. There are dreams that are *reports* — a stranger, a stair with weight on it, water falling from above — and these are told slowly, in the dusk, with the rake put down, and are treated with a seriousness that is not quite belief. There are dreams that are *complaints*, about the living, transparently, and are enjoyed. There are dreams that are only the combs, which everyone has, and which are not told.

The commonest report, the one that comes to nearly everyone at least once and to some every forty, is of rain. Not the drinking: rain from above, water arriving from the sky, falling on the town from the wrong direction. They have no rain. The fluting in the pavement was cut by rain in an age before the ledger, and they know it, and they walk across the memory of it every day with their bare feet. In the dream the water comes down and the channels run backwards and the combs are useless and everyone is standing in the open with their mouths open, and it is not frightening. Keepers who have had it say the same thing about it: that in the dream nobody was working, and it was fine.

## The West Shelf

The three abandoned designs still stand out there, worse than the ones in use, catching whatever they catch, going nowhere.

They are the only ruins and the only monument, and children are taken out to them once, at about nine, and shown: *this is what a mistake looks like.* Not a moral. A demonstration. The town's proof that convergence occurred, that the profile in use was arrived at and not merely inherited, that there was a time when hands could still improve things and did.

Every few generations someone proposes a fourth design. They are always permitted. There is a place for it, at the end of the west rows, among the failures, and there is a procedure for measuring it against the eleven, and the procedure is fair and has been refined for centuries by people who wanted very much to be wrong.

Sometimes it is worse. Once, four hundred years ago, it was very slightly better.

And this is the thing they do not discuss at dusk. A better comb changes the yield. A changed yield changes the cistern. A changed cistern changes the quotient, and the quotient is sixty-one, and sixty-one is every door and every name and every wall and every rotation and the number of people who came up the stair. The town cannot adopt an improvement without becoming a different town, and it has spent a thousand years becoming exactly this one.

So the fourth design stands on the west shelf with the others, and it is scraped on the forty like everything else, because leaving lichen on it would be a discourtesy, and the ledger has a line for its yield, and the line is the most-read line on the wall.

## The Unworking

Three things on the Sill do no work, and they are loved out of all proportion.

The lizard on the sunny steps of the hall. It is the only continuous body in the place — everything else is replaced, generation by generation, stone by stone, name by name, and the lizard simply persists, and it is possible that a keeper of eighty is looking at an animal that watched her grandmother carry a block up from the fan. People step around it. People bring it snails it does not want. It has no office, no yield, no line in the ledger, and it is the only citizen who has never once been useful.

The two choughs at the Spill, who work the updraft for about a minute a day, and for that minute the town stops. Rakes down. Sixty-one people, scattered over four hundred paces of pavement, all facing the same edge, watching two birds be extravagant. Then it is over and everyone picks the rake back up. There are names for the birds and the names are old and are handed on and are not in the roster either. Nobody has ever seen a third.

And the bell-goat. Nine goats, one bell, and the bell is the oldest made thing on the Sill, older than the rod, older than the ledger's first mark, and it is worn thin at the lip and its clapper has been replaced eleven times. The goat that wears it is chosen for calm and is called by the bell's name, not its own, and rings itself twice a day without knowing: once when it comes to the yard gate at the drinking, once when it comes back at dark. The town's whole clock hangs on the neck of an animal that would rather stand in the shade.

Their hair goes into the moulds. Sedge-and-hair, block-shaped, set in the sheet of flow. So the goats are in the walls, and so, in every generation, is a certain amount of human hair, combed out and washed and laid in the fan, and eleven years later it is inside a block, and the block is in a wall, and the wall is being quietly eaten by the dawn. There are keepers who can tell you which course of which building contains their mother's hair. They do not point it out. They just know, walking past.

## The Stair

Four hundred and eleven steps down from the eastern lip into the whiteness, swept every ninth day by whoever holds the rotation, and nothing has come up it in a thousand years.

There are positions on this. That the stair is for arrivals and arrivals are coming, and the sweeping is readiness. That the stair is for departures, and the sweeping is a temptation kept clean. That the stair is an instrument, its wear read against the rod, and the sweeping is calibration. That the stair is the only thing here that is not necessary and is therefore the only thing here that is an offering. That the sweeping is the point and the stair is incidental, the way the chord is the point and the water incidental.

And there is the older thing, which is not a position but a story, and which is told to children at about the same time they are shown the west shelf.

That the flutes in the pavement were cut by rain, and that the rain stopped, and that the lower country filled with cloud instead of water, and that people came up out of it. Up the stair, which was already there, built by somebody for arrivals, which means that whoever built it was expecting them. That they came up carrying copper and a bell and a goat and the knowledge of how to comb fog. That there were sixty-one of them.

The water permits sixty-one. Sixty-one arrived. The town's entire religion is the observation that these two numbers are the same, and its entire science is the thousand-year demonstration that they remain the same, and its entire art is the column of names of the people who have stood in the place where the arriving stood.

There are marks in the oldest part of the roster that nobody can now read. Small nicks beside certain names, at the left, in the copper-width margin. There have been arguments about them for six centuries. The current opinion, held loosely, is that they indicate a person who went down and whose place was kept — whose door stayed open and whose rotations were shared out and whose name was not released, so that no child could be born for the length of a wait. If that is right, then there were years, once, when the town held its breath for someone on the stair. If that is right, the sweeping is not readiness and not offering. It is a place kept.

Nobody has gone down in the ledger's memory. It is not forbidden. It is simply that everyone can do the sum, and the sum is a wall with a hole in it and a woman not permitted a child, and so people stand at the lip in the drinking hour with the whiteness breathing against their ankles, and look down the first nine steps, which are always clean, and go back to work.

## The Third Row

At the fan there is a mould with a hand's width of stone in it, set the year a woman was born.

She goes down to it most dusks. Not to do anything — there is nothing to do to a mould, that is the whole discipline of the slow quarry, you set it and you leave it and you do not touch it for eleven years. She goes down and looks at how old she is. Everyone does this. Children run to the fan to see their own thickness and come back shouting a number that means nothing to anyone but them. Adolescence is a finger's depth. Her daughter, who is nearly grown, has a mould in the fifth row with less in it, and stands beside her mother at the third and compares, and this is the closest thing to a mirror the Sill has.

When it ripens, someone will carry it up. Probably the daughter; probably a porter, if the daughter's back is already set wrong. It will be laid into a course that has been thinning since before the town had a name, and the name spoken into the joint will be hers, and the block will begin, immediately and patiently, to be eaten.

That is what they are, these sixty-one. Not owners. Not survivors. The part of the fountain that can climb stairs. The water dissolves the high stone and gives it back at the low edge and cannot get it uphill by itself, and so the mountain has grown a set of backs and hands and eyes to do that one movement for it, over and over, four blocks a year, forever, and to write down every day that nothing has changed.

The proof needs a witness or it is only a stone. They are the witness. That is the whole of the difference they make, and they make it sixty-one ways, and each way has a name that fits in the width of a copper rod, and will not be used again.

At the pools above the fan a four-year-old is lying on her stomach on cold obedient stone with a wire in her fist, watching a snail move through a saucer of water the size of a plate, waiting for the moment it lifts its foot.
