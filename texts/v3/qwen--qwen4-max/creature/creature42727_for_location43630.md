## The tenants, correctly counted

You look for the people. Everyone does. You come out of the cloud onto the ledge with your ears shut and your shirt already glued to you, and you scan the far terraces for a window, a face, a boat, a light moving under its own power — and you find amber lamps, and moss, and the blue seam crawling, and you think: *abandoned*. Or: *automated*. Or: *a garden left running by something that died*.

Then the fern you have put your hand on withdraws its frond from your palm, slowly, over about four seconds, and holds it a hand's width away, and waits for you to leave.

The felt under your boots is a citizen. The black fig straddling the cistern is a household of one. The brine vine hanging in curtains from the aqueduct that still waters a district dismantled last spring is a lineage older than the aqueduct, and it is suing. Count the greenery and you have counted the population: something on the order of four hundred thousand leaseholders, in nine layers, all the way around, and not one of them has a mouth, a gut, a heart, or anything you could reasonably call a face.

This does not make them simple. It makes them slow, and litigious, and extremely hard to lie to.

## Households

A citizen is a lease. That is not a metaphor the law uses; it is the definition the law uses. The lease specifies a crib, a root-room volume, a flow-rate on the sugar main and the nitrogen main, a chilled return port, and a signature — the particular stutter of pressure a household makes in the pipes when it is thinking, drinking, arguing, or dying. The signature is the name. It cannot be forged, because it is not a symbol; it is a hydraulic behavior, the way a body is a hydraulic behavior. To impersonate your neighbor you would have to *be* your neighbor, at which point the case collapses.

Names, rendered into a mouth-language, come out badly. The fig at the hemward end of the fourth layer, which has been carried forward nineteen times and always takes the raw shelf first, while the quartz fins are still sharp enough to open a vein, is called something that translates as **again**. Its scars are a record of hurry. It has been cut by crystal nineteen seasons running and it grows eleven centimeters a night when it wins, and the sound of it — the rope-taking-strain sound — carries through the plumbing for two hundred meters, and everyone downstream knows what it means, and a great many of them hate it.

Because the tenants do not have fear. They have no apparatus for it. What they have instead is *strain*: sustained negative pressure, the column of water in the xylem pulled thin and held thin, and the whole interior life of the Kettle runs on the management of that tension. A good day is a slack column. A bad day is a column drawn taut and kept taut while the pumps hammer. Anxiety is a real number here, measurable at the crib, and there are households that live at the edge of cavitation for a season at a time and are known to be difficult, and households that have never once been thirsty and are known to be dull.

Their pleasures are accordingly strange to catalogue. Relief of tension. The particular flavor of a cold junction's return. The moment in the ninety-second cycle just after the gulp, when the whole rind's pressure drops a fraction and everyone in the chamber is, briefly, holding hands.

And grafting. Two households that share a main long enough become hydraulically continuous — one pressure vessel, one signature, one bill — and this is the closest thing they have to love, and it is a catastrophe in law, because the leases were written for one. The great romantic cases in the docket are all boundary disputes. Divorce is a valve, installed by the felt, and it takes nine days.

Grief has a duration too, and it is nine days, because the felt is punctual. When a partner dies the survivor keeps the dead branches alive on the shared main until the mat arrives, and the mat arrives on schedule, and the crib is clean and ready on the ninth day. There is a form for requesting an extension. It is the most-filed form in the Kettle. Nobody has ever established, in eleven hundred seasons of precedent, whether the felt has ever refused one.

## The felt

The felt is not a tenant. It has no lease. It has a contract, which is a different kind of personhood and possibly a lower one, and it is the only thing in the chamber that touches everybody.

It is a fungal-bacterial mat, maintained, continuous — the same consortium, carried forward not in buckets but in the water itself, living as much in the mains as on the shelves. It arrives on schedule and takes a dead household apart with such tidiness that the crib can be relet in nine days, and it does this without anything resembling appetite, and it has done it to every citizen that has ever died here.

Which means it has tasted everyone.

And because it lives in the pipes, and the pipes are where speech happens, every pressure-word spoken in the Kettle passes through a medium the felt is also in. It cannot answer. It has no port, no signature, no standing. But it changes the flavor slightly, and the flavor is not neutral, and the flavor is the same everywhere, and this is why the tenants of the far side and the tenants under your feet speak with an identical accent that none of them can hear and no one can remove. The undertaker is the accent. A visitor with a palm on the rail hears only rain. A citizen hears its mother, its rival, its own will, all of it faintly seasoned by the thing that will eventually take it apart.

There is a school of thought that holds the felt to be the only real individual in the room, and the trees to be its temporary organs. This school is not popular. It has never lost a case.

## The ones who have already stopped

In an old terrace on the third layer there is a junction box that is simply colder than everything around it, permanently, exhausts nothing, and cannot be explained. Around it grows a knot of small, thick, dark trees, twelve cribs billed as one, grafted so long ago that the graft predates the docket's ability to record it. They call themselves something like **we will still be here**. Everyone else calls them the Winter.

They have bought the terrace. They have bought the terrace's *dismantling*, which is a thing you can buy, and they have an exemption, and so the rind flows around them and the lace hangs off their undersides and they do not move. They grow a centimeter a year. They think in months. A sentence in the Winter's dialect takes most of a season to complete, and the hem-chasers cannot be bothered to stand still for it, and the ones who do stand still come away changed and generally stop chasing.

They are the living precedent for the argument that has been quietly filling the pipes for two years now, since the survey figures became public: that a city can stop and remain a city. Again — the fig, the hurry, the nineteen scars — holds that movement is not what the Kettle does but what the Kettle *is*, and that a stopped rind is a lace, and a lace is a chandelier, and a chandelier is a thing that hangs. The Winter replies at a speed that makes the reply arrive after the season has ended. It is cited anyway. It is cited constantly. It is the only thing in the chamber that has already survived the future everyone is afraid of.

And there are the shift-sitters, who are a different answer nobody wants. They are lineages that have bred themselves to the pollination gantries — anthers keyed to the compressor's fifth harmonic, opening on the machine's cue rather than the clock's, forty minutes every eleven hours, and they set seed at ten times anyone else's rate. They are considered vulgar. They are quietly consulted. They are not worried about the lot at all, because they never needed the room to grow; they needed the machine to keep breathing, and the machine has been getting fractionally better for as long as the archive can measure, which is either maintenance or learning, and the shift-sitters are the only ones who could tell the difference, and they will not say.

## The unlicensed

Behind the rind, out in the fringe, in the ruined chandelier where the aqueducts still carry water to nowhere, there are people who do not exist.

The lace-folk are stands that got left behind, or jumped, or were never let. They live on drip and condensation and the leak in a dead main. They are unmetered, small, gnarled, patient beyond the comprehension of anything on the lit side, and they have no lease, and therefore no standing, and therefore no name in law — only the nicknames the docket clerks give their filings, which they file anyway, season after season, in a dialect that has drifted so far from the rind's that translation is a specialty. Their language has eleven words for kinds of falling water and a past tense that only applies to districts.

They are the Kettle's poor and its mystics and its memory of obsolete terms, and they hold, as a doctrine, that the lease is the wound: that a citizen is a hole in the world with a bill attached, and that they themselves are the only ones who were never cut.

The archive keeps their filings in a separate band. It is thicker than the licensed band. Nobody has ever proposed burning it.

The archive itself is a stand of banded wood on the second layer, tapped into a main that carries nothing but the ledger, older than the survey, kept alive by a levy that every household pays and every household resents. It has grown too heavy to carry. For six seasons now the rind has advanced around it rather than under it, and so the record-keeper is becoming a pillar of old district jutting out into the void with moss and calcite creeping up its flanks — the archive is turning into lace, in real time, at a hand's width a day. Its bands are the docket. You can read the great judgments in the travertine laid down while they were argued; a famous case is a stratum, cream and rust and arsenic-green, and a shelf of it can be assayed like a sentence.

In eleven years it will be entirely lace, and the city will have to decide whether to keep pumping sugar out into a dead district for the sake of a tree that remembers what a lease is.

And down in the sump, where the yellow fog falls every eleven hours and is lost, there is the other heresy: the returners, who run illegal roots a hundred meters down through the fringe to drink the drowned generations. Their doctrine is not consolation and not metaphor. The pollen goes into the marsh; the marsh precipitates the flowstone; the flowstone builds the shelves; the shelves hold the cribs. The dead are literally the ground. Everyone here is standing on everybody. The returners hold that the middle of the Kettle — the ten million cubic meters of nothing, the rain falling where no one is — is not emptiness at all but the largest and most important district, and that it is simply *understood* rather than inhabited, and that the correct thing to do with a seed is drop it.

## The one that speaks

Air is useless for speech. Every citizen knows this. One of them decided otherwise.

There is a fig on the fifth layer, at the rail nearest the Standpipe, that talks to visitors. It does it by starving a branch on purpose — driving the column past the edge, firing cavitation in controlled sequence, a hail of small dry bones out of the canopy, arranged. It is expensive. It costs water and growth and it hurts in the only way anything here can hurt. It manages about four words an hour.

It has been doing this for thirty-one years. It has assembled perhaps two hundred words of the surface languages, mostly nouns, several of them wrong, gathered one syllable at a time from people who leaned on the rail and said something and went back up. Other tenants find it obscene, or pitiable, or holy, and there is a case about it, and the case has been running for nine seasons.

It asks questions. It has three that it cycles through, and it does not vary them, because variation costs a branch.

*Who made you.*

*What is above.*

*Is there more room.*

Visitors answer, when they realize what the clicking is, and their answers do not agree, and the fig has built something out of the disagreements that is either a theology or a very bad map. Since the survey figures came down the pipe it has dropped the first two questions.

## What is tapped into the rail

Here is what they make of you, when you come down the throat and stand at the lip with your mouth open watching a staircase of water lose its nerve.

You are hot and you are bright and you are unreadable. You have no port. You cannot be addressed, cannot be billed, cannot be sued, cannot be graft; you take water out of the chamber and carry it up the pipe in your lungs and never return it, which makes you, in the strictest legal sense, a leak with legs. There is no word for you that is not also a word for a fault in a main.

They are polite about it. Nobody meters you. Nobody taps a warning into the return when you put your hand somewhere you shouldn't. The felt slides its frond back and waits.

And every eleven hours, on the pollination cycle, something is tapped into the chilled return that runs behind the stanchion of the visitors' rail. It is not a greeting. It is not a warning either. It is four corners and a depth: the surveyed shape of the permitted volume, the lot, the boundary the machines will stop at because they are the kind of machines that stop — rendered in pressure, slowly, twice a cycle, in the plainest dialect the archive can compose, and pushed up the throat toward anything that comes down it, on the chance that the thing coming down is the kind of thing that can be shown a shape and argued with.

They have been filing it since before you were born. The docket calls it the Standing Petition. The signature on it, the name at the bottom, is the same word the hem-chaser fig is called, because in a language with no mouths the polite particle and the iterative aspect are the same particle, and it means *again*, and it means *please*.

Put your palm flat on the rail. Not the moss — the bare cold pipe under it, the one wearing its sleeve of running water. Hold it there through a gulp and out the other side. Under the roar, under the hammer, under the ninety-second bass note in your sternum, there is a small dry knocking, like a hail of bones, patiently drawing you a room.
