## The rice

Begin with the drifts, because they are the largest thing here that is still, in any useful sense, an animal.

Ten thousand to the square foot is a number that stops meaning anything, so put it another way: the shallows at the north end have a surface tension that is not the water's. A hand laid flat on the drift and lifted comes up with a living glove on it, and the glove has a grain, like fur, because every individual is oriented the same way — head into the seep, tail to the open water — and when the orientation changes it changes across a square yard in about a second and a half, and you can watch the decision travel.

They are not coordinated. Nothing is in charge. Each one keeps a body's distance from six neighbours and copies the surge of the seventh, and out of that arithmetic comes a thing with edges, with a front and a back, that flows uphill against the water film when the felt is thick there and pours itself off a ledge in a rope when something wades. It has a temperature. The centre of a deep drift is two degrees above the rim of it, and the animals in the middle are digesting and the animals at the edge are not, and they exchange places over about a day, so that the drift is also a slow stomach turning itself inside out.

When the breath goes out and the shoreline steps down, the drifts ride it. They do not crawl down; they let go and are carried, packed still, a slurry of rice sliding over flowstone, and they arrive at the ebb-pasture already in feeding posture, and they strip it in ten minutes, and then they wait in the wet with their feelers folded until the water comes back and lifts them. They can wait five hours. They have been waiting five hours, on and off, for eleven thousand years.

## The quills, and the thing that reads the flinch

The wall tubes are fused where they touch, which is everywhere, so the north wall is properly described as one bone-coloured organ with a hundred thousand mouths in it.

The wave is the interesting part. A shadow crosses, and the retraction runs around the circumference at about seventy paces a second — faster than any nerve here, because it is not nerves, it is each worm feeling its neighbour's tube go slack. Bone to grey in four seconds. Then the ragged bloom back out over a minute, nearest the shadow first, the far side last, so that for that minute the wall is a map of what frightened it.

There is a flat pale animal that has learned to read the map.

It is the width of a hand and thin as a wet leaf and it swims just under the mist lid, and it does not hunt worms. It hunts the *delay*. It casts a shadow — deliberately, drifting between the wall and the low ambient light — and then it hangs and waits for the bloom, and where the bloom comes back slow, something down there is sick or moulting or wedged, and it goes down and takes it. It has to make three or four casts to find one soft place. It casts all day. It is the only animal in the Bowl that hunts by asking a question of a hundred thousand bodies at once and listening for the one that answers late.

The quills, for their part, have no defence against this and never will. Being a wall is the defence, and the wall has a seam in it, and the seam is where the flat thing lives.

## The pilgrims

Palm-sized, pale, shell like a thumbnail of aragonite with a fringe of feelers underneath like a wet brush.

They go up in files. The files are not social; the lane is polished, and a polished lane in a place where everything is being coated is a *low* place, a groove, and the groove is easier to walk in than the furred stone beside it, and so every generation walks a little deeper into the path its ancestors wore. The lanes are half an inch deep in places. You can put a finger in one. It is the only thing in the Bowl that has been made by behaviour rather than by chemistry.

At the top they graze the white film off the growing reef and their shells thicken at the same rate the reef does, from the same water, and if you section a shell you get a decade of the generator: bands wide in the good years, tight and chalky where the load dropped, one dark line where something failed for a season and every pilgrim alive went hungry at the top of the gantry and came down thin.

Most go back down. That is the arrangement — thicken for a day, then descend to the rust-pasture and spend it, and the traffic runs both ways all the time, a slow double file on every leg.

Some do not go back down.

A finger's thickness a decade means that a pilgrim who stays three weeks is cemented. Not trapped — *incorporated*. The shell fuses to the reef at the contact line and the line advances up over the whorl and the animal keeps grazing whatever it can reach, which is less each year, and its feelers still move. There are several hundred of them in the reef on the settling tank. They are alive. They are structurally part of the tank. Their descendants walk over them in the lane, and the lane there is unusually smooth, because it is being worn into a shell rather than into stone, and shells take a polish.

Nobody who has counted them thinks of this as a tragedy. It is a fraction of the population and it is how the reef gets its shape. The hand-like structures on the drowned gantry legs are hands partly because of the aragonite fans and partly because of the four or five generations of stayers whose shells make the knuckles.

## What the lanes were before

Here is the thing that has to be said, because otherwise the whole place looks like an accident that got lucky.

The lanes are older than the iron.

There is a sulphide body in the south wall, below the intake, a seam of pyrite a few metres thick that sits in water of one chemistry at its deep end and water of another at its shallow end, and it has been quietly running as a cell since the rock was wet. Electrons go up the seam. They have always gone up the seam. Where they come out at the top, the lime comes down, and there has been a white pasture on that spot for longer than there have been animals to eat it.

The works did not invent the pilgrimage. The works *tapped* it — dropped an iron reef into a circuit that was already running, and the current found the metal easier than the pyrite and went that way instead, and the pasture moved forty paces north and up eleven feet, and the lanes bent to follow it. The bend is still there. Near the base of the third gantry leg the polished grooves swing through a shallow S, and if you follow the S backwards it takes you to the old seam, where the white film is thin now and starving.

There are grazers still on the old seam. Perhaps two hundred. They are enormous — dinner-plate sized, shells like millstones, so thick that they cannot move more than a body length an hour. They are the same species as the pilgrims. They simply never made the jump, or their line never made it, and they have been getting slowly poorer and slowly larger for four hundred generations on a current that has been going elsewhere. They spawn with everyone else. Their gametes are viable. Every eleventh hour, in the slack, some fraction of their young settles back on the seam because that is where the parent was, and the rest go up the lane.

They are not a relic in the sense of being finished. They are a hedge. If the screw stops, the seam is still there, and the seam does not depend on anything arriving except water.

## The heavies

At the anodes the floc comes off in orange clouds and settles like curd, and the animals in it are rust-coloured, armoured, heavy, and slow in a way that is different from the pilgrims' slowness — not patience but mass. They cannot be hurried. A heavy pushed off its patch takes four minutes to right itself and does not appear to mind.

They do not read the field. They cannot; they live inside the noisiest part of it, where the current is entering the water and the grain is a howl. They read chemistry, gradients of iron and sulphur, and they navigate by taste, and this means that the two economies are not merely separate but *unintelligible* to each other. A heavy walked into the white pasture would not know it was somewhere. A pilgrim in the floc would think it had gone blind.

There is a border band about a pace wide where the two films overlap, and in it lives a small scavenger that eats both and is therefore neither colour — a mottled thing, the only mottled thing here — and it is the sole animal in the Bowl that can cross. It carries iron in its gut from the anodes to the reef and lime back the other way, and it does this for the same reason a bird carries pollen, which is that it is greedy and has no plan. The white pasture is measurably richer within a body length of the border. Nobody has told anyone.

## The four-hour ones

The midges do not eat. They have no functional mouth. They hatch out of the waterline felt with a fixed charge of fat and they burn it in a column above the transformer housing, and when it is gone they fall, and the fall is the last thing they do, and the drifts take them.

The column is the thing to look at, not the insect. It is stacked in tiers over the warm rectangle — three or four bands at different heights, each turning at its own rate — and it holds its shape for weeks while every animal in it is replaced eight times a day. It is a structure made entirely of replacements. It has a top edge that moves with the ambient temperature and a waist that narrows when the load drops. In the four or five days a year when the lid lifts and real sun comes down, the column dissolves in about ninety seconds and does not reform until dusk, and the midges spend those hours in the felt doing nothing at all, waiting out a light they have no use for.

There is a small pale thing that lays its eggs *inside* the transformer housing, in the warm dust between the fins, because at eleven degrees above ambient its young develop in nine days instead of forty, and it can therefore fit two generations into the season of a midge that fits none. It is armoured, distantly related to the screeners, and it has never been outside. Its whole world is a rectangle sixty centimetres by ninety. It has a population of about four hundred and a rate of inbreeding that should have finished it long ago and has not.

## The gauger

Waist-high. Six legs. Blind, colourless, the wet-paper colour of something that has never had a reason to be anything else. It stands with one leg lifted for as long as four minutes and puts it down without breaking the film.

It reads the field. Everything alive leaks — a thin shell, a torn skin, a moult seam — and against the standing grain of the place a leaking body is a lamp, and the gauger walks the lines the way a spider walks a web, and can tell a moulting grazer from a stone from a dead thing (a dead thing goes dark fast; it stops maintaining itself; the gauger will not touch one).

Two facts about it that change how it looks.

The first: two gaugers can stand in the same square metre and never meet, because their territories are not areas. They are sets of field lines. One animal owns the lines that come off the east anode and another owns the lines that come off the settling tank, and these interleave through the same water like two hands' fingers, and each walks its own comb and ignores the other completely, and they pass close enough to touch a hundred times a night.

The second: it makes lamps.

An old gauger — leg-tips worn to stubs, its own carapace furred at the joints so that it moves with a faint grinding — will walk to a place where the calcite rope over a cable has cracked, and it will put a leg into the crack and hold it there, and the leak flares, and for a radius of two paces the field goes bright and wrong. Everything nearby comes to look. Everything nearby is now leaking a little of its own attention, its own thin places, and the gauger stands in the middle of the flare it made and takes what shines.

It does this perhaps twice in a night, always at the same three or four cracks, and it has been seen to *avoid* a crack that has healed over. Whether that is learning or a very good inherited map is not a question the Bowl is interested in answering.

## The screeners

Cat's-head sized, armoured, territorial in the specific way of animals that own a hole: not aggressive at distance, immovable at close range. They scrape and they hold.

They hold the fissure mouth against the shelly drift that would choke it, standing in the throat with the inflow pushing against their backs, and they scrape the contacts, because a clean contact runs warm and warmth is the rarest commodity in the Bowl — rarer than food, rarer than shell.

The juveniles have to find a contact. They hatch in the chamber with no inheritance but a preference for warm metal, and they wander, and most of them die, and the ones that find a live bearing or a terminal lug settle and never leave it and grow into the shape of the space they occupy, so that each screener is a slightly different shape, and if you pulled one out you would have a negative mould of a specific piece of the works.

On the west side of the chamber there is a screener on a contact that has been dead for sixty years. It is the largest in the chamber. It scrapes the lug clean twice a day, and it holds the lug against rivals, and it gets nothing for it — the lug is ambient, eleven degrees below the housing, cold as the water. Its mother held it, and hers. The behaviour is not conditional on warmth. It is conditional on *this lug*. It will die there and something will take it over and the polishing will continue, and the lug is the cleanest piece of metal in the Bowl, and there is no current in it at all.

## The ninety minutes

When the siphon primes, the level goes down a hand's breadth and the whole shoreline is suddenly a thing that can be walked on.

Everything that can crawl is on it in ten minutes. This is the only time the Bowl looks like a feeding frenzy, and it is not one; it is a very quiet, very dense scraping. Then the drifts arrive on the falling water, and the gaugers arrive on the drifts, and the flat thing under the lid comes down lower than it ever dares otherwise, and for an hour and a half the Bowl has a *surface*, a floor you could stand on, which it will not have again until the following evening.

At the bottom of the breath, in the slack, they all spawn together. Twenty minutes of cloud. Iodine. The black felt goes luminous — blue-green, watch-face brightness, enough to see your hands by — and in that light the wall tubes are the colour of a bruise and the midge column is a black thread hanging in the mist and the pilgrims on the gantry legs are two hundred small pale commas, all of them stopped.

In the throat of the siphon itself there is an animal that lives nowhere else. It is anchored to the roof of the flooded conduit and it feeds only while the water is running out, which is ninety minutes in eleven hours, and it spends the rest of the time folded, in the dark, in still water, doing essentially nothing. It has never been seen whole; the conduit is forty centimetres and full. What comes out on the outflow is a fringe of pale filaments, and what has been measured is that its oxygen consumption during the pull is about four hundred times its consumption during the wait.

It has one meal in eleven hours. It is, by a wide margin, the oldest lineage in the Bowl. The siphon predates the felt.

## The braid

Under the felt, threading down through the black into the sulphide bedding, there are filaments. Not roots, not fungal, not anything with a body: chains of cells laid end to end, centimetres long, conducting. They take electrons from the sulphide at depth and hand them up to the oxygen at the surface, and they do this because the two half-reactions are in different places and something has to carry between them, and the something is alive.

They are the reason the felt works. They are also, at three points that can be located with a meter, spliced into the copper.

Where a cable's calcite sleeve has cracked and the metal is bare and the current is entering the water, the filaments have found it, and they have grown along it, and they are running their sulphide half-reaction off the works instead of off the rock. The load reads slightly high. It has read slightly high for a long time. Twice every breath, as the level shifts and the turbine's head changes and the relays adjust, something in the brick chamber clacks, and the number in the brass gauge book on the tower — the last number anyone wrote down — is a number that includes them.

They are not parasites and they are not tenants. They are part of the circuit. They are the only residents of the Bowl who are, in the strictest sense, *employed*.

## The paper tenant

In the brass case on the tower, in the gauge book, there is a small silver animal eating the pages.

It is not of this place. It came down in a boot or a bag, and it needs cellulose, which means it needs sunlight, which means it needs the world outside — and it has found four hundred grams of it in a sealed brass box, and it is living on the recorded history of the water level. It has eaten the years 19— through 19—. Its droppings are in the spine. There are perhaps thirty individuals and the population is not replacing itself.

It is the only thing in the Bowl that will certainly die out. Everything else here is running on a promise the rock made before there was anything to hear it. This one is running on a man's handwriting.

---

Stand still long enough and you stop being able to say which parts are working, and then, a little later, you stop being able to say which parts are *counted*.

You are warm. You are eleven degrees above ambient, more, and there is a column of midges trying to form over your shoulder. You are wet, and the film on you is connected to the film on the wall, and your skin is thin everywhere and broken somewhere, and you are leaking into the grain of the place like a lamp someone has left on in a room where nothing has ever needed to see.

Down on the waterline, in the dark, a gauger lifts one leg and holds it.

The boot prints on the stair are filled and half-filled with flowstone, and the man who made them went up and out and never knew he had been the brightest thing in the Bowl for the whole of the time he was in it.
