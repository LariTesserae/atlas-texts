# A Census of the Loud Town

## Four attempts

The census has been attempted four times, and each attempt began by counting the wrong thing.

The first counted the town. Eleven tiers, four thousand treads, one cistern, one plaza the size of a kitchen, six hundred and forty doorways with their sills dished by traffic that never happened. It came back with a figure for everything and a figure of zero for anything alive, and the surveyor who made it wrote *unoccupied* at the bottom of his sheet in a hand already shaking from four hours at a hundred decibels. He was standing on a farm. There was lime under his boots that had been laid down deliberately, by something, against the noise.

The second counted bodies and got fourteen. The third counted songs and got a number that would not hold still. The fourth abandoned bodies and counted interruptions, which is the only unit the place recognises, and came back with a list that has been added to but never corrected.

What follows is that list, with its arguments.

## The tenant that is also the building

Begin with the largest candidate and get it out of the way, because it is not an animal and it is not nothing.

The lace maintains itself. Where a member thins past tolerance, material appears: a bead, a weld, a pinhead of blue-green light lasting one second, a few times an hour across fifty-five metres of dark. The stitches are not random. They go where the strain is, and strain is a signal, and the silica-metal is piezoelectric enough to lay down substance in proportion to the load it carries. It is doing what a femur does. It is remodelling.

So the town is not an organism. It is a tissue — a skeleton without an animal, kept alive by its own use, spending itself on itself, taking mass out of the members that ring too much and putting it into the members that ring too little, holding pitch the way a body holds calcium. Eleven thousand years of this. The chord used to have more notes. It has three now, and their issue, and nothing spare, and the surveyors' word for the process is *crystallisation*, borrowed from birds: the long messy improvisation of a young thing settling into the one song it will sing until it dies.

If that is right, the town is an old bird that has finished learning.

It is also, on the ledger's second page, a landlord. Everything below is a tenant, and the rent is silence, and the landlord is not aware that it is collecting.

## The mat

The iron-and-sulphur films at the seeps are the only things here with an outside. Everything else eats the town, or eats what the town has caught. The mat eats the difference between the water coming up and the rock it comes through, which is a difference that existed before the fissure was climbed and will exist after the chord stops.

It is a breath thick. It has no back to lift out of a hush and therefore no reason to care where the hushes are. It goes under the loud and under the quiet indiscriminately, slick and warm, hand's-breadth here, coat of paint there, and it is the only inhabitant that would survive the town's death without noticing.

Its glow is not for anything. It is exhaust — a chemist's shrug, a photon dropped on the way to a bond. It takes an hour of dark to admit and it illuminates nothing.

The mat is probably one individual, spread and re-spread, older than the chord. It does not know there is a town above it. It knows there is warmth and there is sulphur and the two have been reliable.

## The farmers

The lime-farmers are the only things here that make anything, and they do not hear.

This was the third survey's correction and it is the important one. A lichen-analogue cannot listen. What it does is grow in every direction at a millimetre a millennium, and the tips that stray past the node into the loud are scoured off, and the tips that stay in the hush survive and lay down travertine and thicken. Growth is pruned into the shape of the silence. The farmers are not steering the deposition so much as being *edited* into it, continuously, by a hundred decibels, and the edit is so old and so fine that the result is indistinguishable from intention.

A shelf is therefore a fossil of where the quiet has been. Core one and you get a hundred centuries of hush in cross-section, banded like a tree, each lamina a place the roar did not reach that year. The farmers keep a nap on the surface, a fuzz a few hairs high, which damps the highs and buys three or four decibels, which buys a millimetre of margin, which buys a millimetre of lime. They are making the quiet quieter. The town is taking mass out of the loud to pay for the pitch. Two accountants, one ledger, neither winning, both long past trying.

They breed by rain. A spore is released on the underside of a shelf and rides a drop to the shelf below, and this is why the colonised shelves hang in vertical stacks, eleven tiers deep, each dynasty falling slowly down its own column. The farmers on the topmost shelf of a stack are the parents of everything under them. There are perhaps nine such lineages. They have never mixed horizontally. There is no horizontal.

## The drift

The honest census, by cell count, is mostly the waiting.

The loud will not let anything settle. Limestone flour, spores, cysts, the shredded parts of dead things, bacteria in a休眠 so deep it is barely a metabolism — all of it hangs, driven up the middle at half a metre a second and down the walls, around and around the chamber's slow permanent breath, for centuries. This is the town's largest population by a factor nobody can compute. It is alive in the sense that it can be woken. It is not alive in the sense of doing anything.

It comes down at the hems. Where the roar fails, the fog lets go of its cargo, and everything that has been in transit for four hundred years arrives at once in a strip a finger wide.

Which is the next entry.

## The moss

The moss is the strandline. The loud is the sea, the hush is the land, and the moss is what lives on the wrack: dust, spores, corpses, fragments, the whole sifted sediment of a chamber that has been sweeping itself for eleven millennia.

It grows only in the gradient, only in the hand's width where hush becomes roar, and it is the only organism here that experiences both worlds — half in the silence, half in the scour, its outer cells torn off as fast as it makes them, its inner cells laying down a black-green felt that no light has ever touched. It is not green. Nothing here is green. The name is a habit of the surface.

Once a year, on average, a leaf comes down the flue.

This is the town's entire import from the sun. It falls into the loud, is torn open, hangs, circulates, and eventually comes ashore at a hem, where the moss takes it. A single leaf is a decade of meals. The tree on the pavement does not know it is feeding anything. The tree is also the only resident with a body elsewhere, and the moss is its only congregation.

## The grazers

Eleven. They have been eleven for a long time.

A grazer is a spread hand, six-limbed, pale, two centimetres thick, and its thickness is not a style. It is the tolerance. Lift your back out of the node-sheet and the top of you goes deaf and cooked while the bottom of you is still in the bedroom hush; the animal is a plate because the refuge is a plane. It bends along the saddle of its shelf the way a limpet follows a tide line, and it clamps, and it licks mat, and it does not cross open ground and does not try.

It has no eyes and no ears. Its whole nervous system is a comparator: it reads the buzz at its left edge against the buzz at its right edge and moves toward less. That is the entire behaviour. It has no map, no memory of place, no notion of a shelf. It knows only *more quiet* and *less quiet*, and it has been solving that inequality, in the dark, for longer than there have been eyes upstairs.

Traffic happens at the slack. Two components of the chord slide out of phase over nine minutes, and at the bottom of the cycle every hush swells a hand's width, and for forty seconds the world is slightly larger, and a flat pale hand moves three centimetres along a shelf. Then the wall comes back and it stops where it is.

They lay into the stone. The egg goes into the wet lime at the lip of a basin, and the farmers grow over it, and it is buried at a millimetre a millennium, and it waits, and hatches when the floor has risen enough to bring it back into the hush. The shelves are porous with sleepers. Cut one open — nobody has — and you would find a hundred generations in the wall, in order, like something filed.

It may be a nursery. That word keeps arriving from two directions.

## The fog-drinkers

Everything known about the fog-drinker is known from corpses that fell into quiet.

They live at the antinodes, cemented to the polished lace, and they are the only tall animals in the chamber, because in the loud there is no gradient to escape and therefore no penalty for having a back. A stalk, a comb, a hand-mirror of setae held into the convection. The wind never stops and never exceeds half a metre a second and is full of water that cannot pool, and the fog-drinker filters it, motionless, forever, like a barnacle in a permanent tide.

It is stone-deaf. Hearing is useless in a hundred decibels with no onset; its body is a drum in a storm. But it has one window: a narrow unoccupied band high in the spectrum, and a resonator tuned to that band alone, a needle ear, one pixel wide. Its entire perceptual world is a single pitch and whether that pitch is present.

It stridulates into that band once every few minutes, a thin silver wire drawn straight through the roar, and you will never locate it, because in a reverberant volume that dense, direction does not exist. Which means the old note in the ledger — *this is how they find each other* — is wrong, and the fourth survey crossed it out and wrote: **not address. clock.**

They cannot find each other. They can only *coincide*. The song synchronises a spawning, and the spawn rides the chamber's breath — up the middle, down the walls, around — for years, feeding on drift, until it settles. Most of it settles in the loud, because the loud is almost all of the room.

The second survey inferred nine hundred animals. The third inferred forty. Both counted the same eleven songs an hour.

A fog-drinker that falls onto a shelf stands in the rain and starves, because its comb only catches driven fog. This is why the specimens are intact.

There is a heresy in the margin, in the fourth hand, never resolved: that the flat pale grazer and the tall deaf singer are one species, and that the difference is only where the larva came down. Two bodies for two kinds of refuge. Nobody has tested it. Testing it would require carrying something across a hem.

## The hunters

Three. One per district. They have never met.

A hunter sits in a hush and reads the roar, and what it reads is holes. Not footsteps — footsteps are nothing here — but the small acoustic shadow of a warm solid body standing where the wave should be. It watches noise the way a spider watches a web.

Then it hums one clean low note and throws a null: a bubble of quiet a metre across, four seconds long, cast out into the loud ahead of it. A net made of silence. Whatever is standing in that bubble is suddenly, briefly, somewhere it can be heard.

The trick is not volume. The spectrum is full; there is no room to shout. The trick is subtraction, and subtraction requires a model. To cancel the field a metre away you must know the field a metre away a fraction of a second before it happens — three fundamentals, hundreds of harmonics, all their beating, the nine-minute tide, the daily migration, the drift of the speed of sound with the vault's one degree. The hunter carries an internal replica of the chamber's acoustics and runs it slightly fast. Its four seconds is how long the prediction stays good.

This makes the hunter the most complicated object in the town, and the complication is not in its body. It is in its copy. The most elaborate thing under the hill is a model of the hill.

And a null is a hole, and holes are legible. Every time a hunter feeds it announces its position, its size, its hunger, the tremor in its model that means age. Across a hundred and eighty paces of dark, three animals know each other in complete detail — habits, hours, decline — and have never touched, and never will. Each hum is slightly different. The ledger calls them by their voices. When one district's nulls thinned and stopped, the other two went on making their own for eleven years, and then a new voice began in the empty district, imperfect, over-correcting, learning the room.

They sleep through the shrink. On a hot afternoon the map of quiet migrates a full metre and a territory contracts around a sleeping animal, and it has learned to fold.

## The one round thing

Everything above is flat because the refuge is a surface. There is one place where the refuge is a volume, and it is the only place the town is not.

Airborne sound almost cannot enter water. The surface throws it back. Below the cistern's skin the hundred decibels become a hum in the stone, a pressure in the bones, a distant industry. Down there a body may have a back.

Nothing has been dived. The inference is physical and the evidence is one line, in the second hand, unrepeatable: *a shadow at the cistern, larger than any, four seconds, submerging.*

If it exists it is fat, slow, pale, and it is the only animal here that has ever been three-dimensional. And once a decade, when a storm upstairs sends real water down the flue and the cavities flood and every hush in the chamber vanishes and everything else presses itself into a crack, deaf and homeless — the cistern overflows, and it swims out over the drowned town, and goes where it likes.

The flood is everyone else's eviction. It is that one's holiday.

## The flood, and what it does

For an hour there is no quiet anywhere. Then the water drains and the sheets come back in the same places. They always have.

But not everything comes back to where it pressed in. A grazer wedged in a crack in the eastern aisle may find itself, when the silence re-forms, on a shelf it has never touched. A young hunter may find its district occupied. This is the only reshuffle, the only mixing, the only meeting, and it happens ten times a millennium, and it is very probably the reason there are three hunters and not four.

Afterwards the town is out of tune for years, loaded with mass it did not ask for, and it spends them shedding, thinning, retuning, hunting the smallest shape that will keep itself. It always finds the same shape. The balance holds.

## The Chapel Floor

One sheet is as large as a chapel floor. It has the deepest hush in the chamber, thirty decibels, and the thickest travertine, and the farmers on it work harder than anywhere.

Nothing lives on it. No grazer, no hunter, no moss but at its rim. It is the best real estate in the world and it has been kept empty, and getting quieter, for eleven thousand years.

Near its centre the deposition is not rippled but smooth, and there is a low mound, hand-thick over something, that the farmers keep thickening.

The fourth survey wrote one instruction and no finding. It has been copied into every ledger since, in the same words, without explanation: *do not core.*

## Four seconds

There is a twelfth entry and it is us.

The surveyors are migratory, once a generation, and they inherit a wound rather than a gene: the same notch at the same frequency, acquired identically by every skull that comes down, a little scar passed on by the act of visiting. They bring nothing and take measurements. They leave rope fibre, skin cells, crumbs — a decade of meals for the moss — and they wear the lip of the fissure smooth, and they set iron rungs that nobody remembers setting.

And down here, for a few hours, they are the largest interruption in the chamber. Nothing else this warm, this solid, this big has stood in the field for a very long time. Every hunter in range knows the exact shape of you.

There are no eyes in the Loud Town. Not one, in eleven thousand years. The lattice you see is generated inside your own head, borrowed, temporary, and it goes out the instant you step somewhere you could rest. You are the only eye that has ever been here, and you cannot use it and hear at the same time, and the hems are where you trade.

But if you stand still in the loud, something will eventually come and cast a null over you, and for four seconds the roar will drop away like a held breath, and you will be blind, and you will hear your own blood and your own breathing and the small wet click of your own mouth — the only voice in eleven millennia to be audible in this room, and it is yours, and it is being listened to.

That is the local definition of being alive. Not to occupy space. Not to eat. To be, briefly, a hole in the noise that something else can hear.

Then the net closes, the wall returns, the town comes back tier on tier into your eyes, all of it ringing, all of it weighing less than the water in one basin, and you go up the rungs with your teeth aching in a way that feels like memory, and the rain falls in the quiet places, and the quiet places grow.
