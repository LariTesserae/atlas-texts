## The First Answer, Which Is a Visitor's Answer

A million birds. That is the number you would give if you were asked at the mouth of a Window with the flood coming out past your head, and it is not wrong, but it counts the wrong thing. The birds are away half of every night. They are the only residents who leave, the only ones who know what a river looks like, the only ones who have ever been rained on. They are not so much the population as the portage — a million small wet cargoes moving between two countries, one of which has water lying around in the open, like a lunatic.

Begin with them anyway. Everything else here is arranged around their absence.

## The Porters

Cinnamon-brown, white-flecked, forty centimeters from hooked beak to squared tail, with a wingspan nearly a meter and a body that weighs less than a tin of soup. Soft-plumaged to the point of blur: the contour feathers have no stiff shafts to speak of, so a bird in the hand feels like a handful of warm down with a skeleton somewhere in it, and a bird in flight makes almost no sound of its own. In a country with nineteen seconds of reverberation, a noisy wing would be a species-ending design flaw. They have been selected, for two million years, for silence at the edges and hardness in the throat.

The face is mostly eye, and the eye is enormous — a bucket built to catch photons at a density that does not occur here. This is the first thing to understand about them: their eyes are not for this room. In the brown-grey bag-light of the plain they are nearly useless, big slow lenses gathering nothing. They are for the canopy eighty kilometers away, for fig clusters against a dusk sky, for the eleven minutes twice a year when a coin of the sun lands on the floor and the whole town flinches. The eyes are commute equipment. Down here the birds are functionally blind and do not mind.

What they have instead is a face you can feel with. Rictal bristles stand out around the gape in a stiff basket, longer than the beak, and the last ten centimeters of every flight are flown by touch — wall, root, flank of another bird, all of it arriving as a brush against the whiskers. Below the whiskers, the feet: weak, absurd, all four toes capable of pointing forward, good for shuffling and pressing and nothing else. They cannot grip a branch properly. They cannot carry. They cannot hold.

The tool is the breast. In the building season the salivary glands swell until the bird can wet a beakful of flour and work it, and it presses that beakful against a wall with the warm front of its body, over and over, tamping, shaping the cup to the curve of itself. The alcoves in the old walls are not carved. They are casts. Cut a tower and you find three hundred generations of chests.

## Four Kinds of Hearing, Which Are Four Kinds of Belonging

A bird here lives inside four nested acoustic worlds, and it is a citizen of each at a different radius.

The innermost is its own clicks, which work out to about a meter — a hard, dry, audible ticking that returns to it the shape of a wall, a root-hawser, the lip of a cup. Within that meter the world is crisp, detailed, present. It is the only place in the country where anything is crisp.

The next is the family. A chick develops a voice in the first week, a churr with a particular grain to it, and the parents learn that grain the way you learn a face. Against the roar, against nineteen seconds of smear, that learning holds out to roughly two hundred meters. This is the whole of the law. There is no territory, no fence, no dominance, no punishment. There is only the fact that inside the sphere your chick is a named individual and outside it your chick is noise, and noise is not fed. Mothers do not defend the radius. They simply cannot hear past it, and neither can anyone else, and so the towers stand where they stand, and the kilometers of bare floor between them are not waste ground but the necessary condition of anyone having a family at all.

The third is the tower's own voice, and this is the subtle one. A tower does not sing. It filters. Fifteen or twenty meters of fluted, buttressed, honeycombed guano stone, with every lip and hood and baffle placed by three thousand years of ancestors who could not hear their young when they got it wrong, is a machine for shaping the roar — combing notches out of it, folding cancellations into its lee, throwing acoustic shadows across the plain. Each mass therefore has a signature made of the silences it produces: a particular pattern of holes in the noise, stable across centuries, audible from far beyond the family sphere because it is written in the low frequencies that the dust does not eat. A bird coming home out of the dark does not steer toward a sound. It steers toward a specific shape of quiet. That is the address. That is what a fledgling memorizes in its last week on the wall, turning its head, learning the notches: not a place but a hole in the sea, which it will find again for eleven years.

And underneath all of it, the fourth: the Throat, breathing at eight or nine cycles a second, a forty-meter note below the floor of hearing, felt in the sternum and the sinuses, unattenuated by dust, unblocked by stone, arriving from the east with the weather. It has not stopped since the Pleistocene. Everything that flies here flies with that pressure on one side of its body. It is the only fixed bearing in a world with no landmarks, and it means that in this country *east* is not a direction. It is a note.

## The Chicks Are the Reservoirs

Here is what the four-hour exodus is actually for.

A nestling in this room is a cistern with a voice. It grows fatter than its parents — grotesquely, visibly fat, a warm orange-brown bladder of lipid in a cup of stone — and that fat is not only calories. Burn a gram of fat and you get a gram of water and a little more, and there is no other water anywhere. The chick will fledge lighter than it was at its peak, having spent its own substance to build a wing, and it will fly out into a country of rivers and never once put its beak into one. It will eat its water for the rest of its life, in the dark, at speed, eighty kilometers from home.

So the parents are not bringing food. They are operating a pipeline with a nineteen-second delay and a two-hundred-meter terminal radius, and the churring whine that never stops for the whole of the nesting season is the sound of a reservoir requesting delivery. When a bird comes in off the plateau with a fig in its throat, the fig is ninety percent water, and that water has been in a leaf, and in a river, and in a cloud, and it will be in a chick within the hour, and it will never once in its entire journey have been a drop.

## The Ceiling Nation, Which Has No Addresses

Above all this, hanging in the raw vault, are several million bats, and they are the other half of the economy and the opposite of everything just described.

They build nothing. They own nothing. They have no cups, no walls, no inherited real estate, no three-hundred-generation projects. Their society is thermodynamic: the good places are the places where the vault's slow air moves least and where the bodies around you are warmest, and status is a matter of being in the middle of the crowd rather than at its edge. They shuffle. They fight in a small dry way, all thumb and shriek. They are the only things in the country that grip — feet locked into rock by a tendon that costs no effort to hold, so that a dead bat can hang for a week before anyone notices.

They do not find their young by voice, because at that density and that pitch the ceiling is a solid wall of ultrasound, a pressure behind the eyes rather than a sound. They find them by smell and by position, in crèches, out of a heap. A bat mother noses through forty pups that are not hers. Where the birds have invented the individual, the bats have declined to.

And they pay for everything. The birds import sugar, seeds and water; the bats import nitrogen and phosphorus, in the bodies of moths and beetles taken from the plateau's night air, and they deliver it as a fine dry flour that falls all day, without pause, forever. It is snowing in here. It has been snowing since before there were birds. The towers are made of it, the plain is made of it, the two living centimeters of the floor are made of it, the salt on the western flats is what is left when the bacteria have finished with it. The unbuilt nation upstairs is the sole manufacturer of the built nation downstairs, and neither has any idea the other exists.

## The Two-Centimeter Nation, Which Is the Only Native One

Everything above is a commuter. The birds go out; the bats go out; the rats come down and go back up; the owls wait at the doors and never enter; the roots are the fingers of trees whose bodies are in the sun. Even the pale gardens are only the far end of somebody else's plumbing.

The animals of the floor have never left.

Mites in armor like polished chestnuts. Springtails in drifts so thick the drifts move. Pseudoscorpions, blind, four millimeters long, walking backward with their pincers held out. Guano-moth larvae in felts of silk. Beetles — black, matte, fused, wingless, eyeless — whose species exist in this room and nowhere else in the universe, and whose lineage has not taken a drink of water in something on the order of a million years. They make it, molecule by molecule, in the burning of fat. They recover it out of their own droppings with a plumbing arrangement wrapped around the hindgut that wrings the frass dry as chalk, which is why the floor of this country is flour and not mud. They gave up flight and sealed the wing-cases down into a still-air chamber over the abdomen, so that the sky their ancestors had is now a small pocket of humid breath they carry on their back. Every one of them is a solution to the same problem, and the solutions rhyme: the beetle's sealed case, the bird's dry guano, the fungus's melanized cord, the salt's bound film that will not become a droplet, the plant that dropped its leaves and its color and its ambition.

They live fast. A mite generation is three weeks. In the time one cup is inherited three hundred times, the floor has run through more generations than there are cups. To them the plain is not empty and not static: it is a continent of falling manna with weather, famine, and sudden enormous windfalls. A nestling that falls out of a tower is a province. Within a day the moth larvae have found it; within a week there is a boiling metropolis in and around a body the size of a fist, a succession, a boom, predators arriving to eat the predators; within six weeks it is over and the survivors have dispersed across a hundred meters of flour to wait. Nothing here has ever held anything, but the pseudoscorpion grips a beetle's leg and rides, and that is the entire inventory of grasping in this world: holding onto something else that is going somewhere.

## The Dead Who Got Out of All That

Which is why the Ossuary Suburbs matter.

Out in the abandoned districts the air is driest, and a bird that dies there desiccates faster than the floor nation can eat it. It escapes the cycle. It becomes a small brown weightless object with folded wings and dry pits for eyes, and it stays that way, in a cup pressed by a breast forty thousand years dead, while the drifts of feather-down and eggshell rise around it. There is no other immortality available in this country. To be preserved here is simply to be too dry to be worth eating.

They are the largest single class of resident, and the only ones with permanent addresses. Everyone else is renting.

## The Plumbing, Which Is the Actual Government

Under all of it — under the towers, under the plazas, under the salt, threaded through the two living centimeters and down into fissures nobody has ever entered — is the fungal network, and if you want to know who runs the place, it is that.

It is the only thing here that moves water on purpose. Rhizomorphs, cords, hawsers of hyphae waterproofed and darkened, running sugar down from trees three hundred meters overhead and running moisture along their own length, sealed, piped, never leaking. The pale gardens are not plants that live in a cave. They are thieves standing over a shallow main: coral-pink and wax-white and translucent amber, no leaf, no green, no interest in the sun, tapping the cord-mats and taking a cut of a canopy they will never see. They come up in tiers where the plumbing runs near the surface, which is why the groves are islands and why the islands are where they are. The grove is a map of the pipes. The foxfire in the older cords is the pipes glowing faintly with their own inefficiency.

And at the throats of the Windows, in the few percent of daylight that survives the boulder plug and the root lid, there is a green rind: hard ferns, xeric moss, a centimeter of growth a year, the only organisms in thirty kilometers that pay their own way. They are the poorest citizens and the slowest and they live at the doors, and twice a year the sun stands over one of the shafts and drops a coin of unfiltered light for eleven minutes, and they take it, and that is most of their income.

## The Ones Who Own Lines Instead of Spheres

The birds own radii. Not everyone does.

The pale-headed racers own routes. They are cold-blooded in a cellar at eleven degrees, and their slowness is not caution, it is arithmetic: a snake here may take a season to work a district that a bird crosses in a heartbeat, and it eats perhaps a dozen times a year, and it lives long enough to know the town as a sequence rather than a map. It climbs the fluting by feel, in the dark, at the speed of a growing root, and takes a nestling out of a cup whose walls it has known for longer than the chick's entire lineage has occupied it. It is a tax with a very slow schedule.

The rats own the Dry River — the cobbled bed of the actual river, packed like concrete, the oldest road, going somewhere still. They come down the fissures with seeds in their cheeks, cache them along that road, forget half, and thereby plant every grove in the country. They are the vertical commuters, the ones who go up and down rather than out and back, and they are the reason there are gardens at all.

The spiders own holes: single cups, inherited, in the empty quarters, sitting in a depression made by a chest that stopped breathing before the last glaciation.

The owls own the doors, and only the doors, and take their toll on the four-hour flood as it pours out.

The crickets, hand-sized, graze the cord-mats and are the largest thing in the country that has never been outside.

## The Thing Made of All of Them

Count again, and this time count the towers as animals.

A tower is fifteen to twenty meters of warm tan porous stone, ammoniacal, insulating, honeycombed with tens of thousands of cups; it maintains its own acoustic signature; it repairs itself; it grows; it quarries its own dead districts for material; it is entirely composed of the bodies and the leavings of the things that live in it. Its cells are birds. They are replaced every eleven years. The structure persists through three hundred replacements of every part. Nothing in it has ever seen it built, and no bird in it could describe it, and it is nonetheless as much an organism as anything else in the room, and considerably longer-lived.

Or count it as physics, which is cleaner. Guano flour falls continuously. Birds pass through the towers, arriving and departing, adding a cup, leaving, dying, being ground. The tower is a standing wave in a falling snow — matter flows through it and the shape stays. The plain is the same material lying down. The city is not on the sediment; it is the sediment, temporarily standing up, and it is standing up at one millimeter a year, which is also the rate at which it is burying itself.

There is a consequence to this that nobody here will experience and that follows anyway, with no more urgency than the salt going dark. As the floor rises, the room shallows. As the room shallows, it reverberates less. As the reverberation shortens, the roar thins, and the recognition radius widens — two hundred meters, then three, then a kilometer. The towers crowd. The plazas close. And at the very end, in the last thin acoustic sliver before the floor meets the arch, there will be a brief period in which every parent in the world can hear every chick in the world by name, and no space left to put a wall.

Universal recognition, immediately followed by no room. It is not a tragedy. It is a schedule.

## Nineteen Days From Now

The sun will stand over the fourth Window and drop a coin of light onto a grove of pink lanterns for eleven minutes. The lantern-flies will not be there; nothing here has seen the other kind of sea and nothing here will look up. The ferns at the shaft mouth will take their income. A racer will lie in the warm patch because a warm patch is a warm patch. The grove will not appear to move.

Two meters away, in the dark, inside a wall the exact color of its own body, in a cup shaped by a chest that died before the town had this shape, a chick will make the sound that is its name, and out of nineteen seconds of grinding, metal-and-gravel roar, from somewhere inside two hundred meters, something will answer it.

That is the whole of what lives here: a million porters, a ceiling of unbodied heat, a floor of the only true natives, a fungus that owns the water, a slow tax, a road with seed in it, a great many dry dead who escaped being eaten — and, threaded through all of it, a few hundred thousand small conversations that can only happen inside a sphere, in a room that is patiently filling itself in.
