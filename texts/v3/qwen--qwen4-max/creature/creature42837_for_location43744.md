## Stalls

Ask who lives here and you will be answered in stalls.

The beasts have no species word. Pressed for one, a herdsman will use the word for *warmth*, which is also the word for household, which is also what you would give a courier as your address. Otherwise the animal is called by where it stands: Third-North, the one by the ramp, the old cow under the dye wall. A child born on the night a beast calves takes that beast's stall-name and keeps it for life, so that in any room you can name four people and know what was happening underground when they arrived.

They do not lie down. There is no room, and after four hundred generations there is no habit either. They sleep standing, in shifts, in a fug of ammoniac steam, and they make a sound below hearing that comes up through the corbelling and into the soles of your feet: the byre hum, which is not the breathing but underneath it. Byre-folk are white as cave fish and speak with their mouths an inch from your ear even outdoors. They know every animal by its exhale. They can tell you which stall has a cracked tooth from the change in the room.

Above, the floor is warm in patches. Children learn the map of it barefoot before they learn their letters, and it is how they get the news: a cold spot spreading under the west corner means a beast has died in the night, and the household will be quiet at breakfast, and no one will have to say why.

There is one law about this that has no court because it needs none. **A byre may not be emptied.** Beasts are replaced one at a time, old out and young in on the same morning, so that the heat never drops. To empty your stalls is to chill your neighbours' walls, and their children's lungs, and their mortar, which has not yet had its winter. Warmths sue over leakage. Felt is hung between byres in disputed thicknesses. A house that goes cold is not a house that has lost its money; it is a hole in the fabric, and the quarter feels it the way a mouth feels a gap.

## The people

Barrel-chested, thick-blooded, slow at the wrist and quick at the eye. Their hands are ruined at the tips and their nails are ridged like the shells underfoot. Above the wall they wear visors of slit bone or horsehair, because the limestone throws the sun back up at you and the Open can blind a man in an afternoon; quarrymen who forget go grey in the middle of the eye by fifty.

You can read a person's day off their face. The roofs are the sun-place and the byres are the dark, and a woman who spends her mornings below and her afternoons at the loom above is burned on one side and pale on the other, and everyone can tell at a glance what she does and roughly what she is worth.

They have three names and give them in order of importance: the warmth, the stall, the small name last. They speak the stone voice, low and close, lips near the ear, and there is a whole grammar of distance — how near you may stand, at what point a conversation becomes an intimacy, at what point it becomes a threat. Their word for *shout* is their word for *fall*. It is not a metaphor. It is the same event described from two ends.

They are full of stone. The water that lays a rind in every gutter lays one in them too, and the gravel is the common death: a slow blocking, a hard thing worked out of a soft place. The word the water-guild uses for re-cutting a channel is the word the surgeons use for opening a man. Apprentices learn both trades in the same yard, on the same slab, and a good re-cutter is expected to be able to do either. The stones taken out of a person are counted, washed, and put into the wall packing or the soil beds, because nothing here is thrown away, and because it is the same stone that is in the cistern lip and in the paving and in the shell beside your foot. A man is a place where the water slows.

Time is measured in courses, stairs and cisterns: a year, a lifetime, three lifetimes. A promise is dated by masonry. *I'll see it when the third course is laid.* Two cisterns ago is living memory in the way that a grandmother is living memory elsewhere — that is, it is argument, not history.

Their music is at the pitch of the byre. From the lane you hear nothing at all. From the wall, with your palm flat on it, you feel the whole quarter singing.

## The scraping hour

Children own the dawn. They go out before light with scrapers of rib — a rib is the right curve and takes an edge and does not spark — and take the rime off the nets and the north walls and the standing stones, and push the shavings down into the gutters, and the gutters take it to the cisterns. Ten thousand small spoons on ten thousand stones.

A child's last scrape is the day their hands are too big for the gutter-mouths, and it is a bigger day than any wedding. The scraper is hung above the door of the room they were born in, and thirty years later a smaller hand takes it down. Some of these ribs have been in use for six generations and are worn to a translucent sliver, and there is a family in the eastern quarters that will tell you, with the flat certainty of people stating a measurement, whose bone it was.

Then the roofs: the looms, the dung mosaics, the felt flags marking whose water falls where, the traffic of hooves on the spiral ramps, the marmots. Then the midday hush of the thaw months, a million people sitting still in blue dung smoke while the hill above them drips, and every hand in the city turning a spindle. Then the evening pour, and the quarrymen going up with their skins of meltwater in the last light, and coming home, and the whole place smelling of wet rock at breakfast.

They eat curd and whey and butter and blood and old beasts and pond-hay bread and, for six weeks a year, fly-bread, which is dark and oily and faintly sweet and which everyone pretends to be tired of by the fourth week and misses for the other forty-six. They play knucklebones on felt. They go, once in a life, three days east, to stand among willows up to the knee.

When they die they are carried out to the pavement beyond the last wall, and the bearded vultures come down and take the femurs up and drop them on the flat rocks until they crack, and the marrow is gathered and eaten and the splinters are gathered and drilled, and a splinter of long bone is a needle. So the fleece in the wall was sewn with the grandmother of the woman who packed it there. Nothing leaves the Sill but cheese, dye, needles, and couriers.

## The other tenants

The walls are inhabited. The packing — hair, ash, ribs, hair — breeds bone-beetles and a small grey moth, and the slots left for the doves admit both, and the doves eat both, and the children take the doves with a hooked wire. Opening a wall for repair is a harvest as much as a job: the smell comes out first, then the swarm, then the children of the courtyard with their wires, and the mason stands back and lets them work, because it is their right and because he was one of them.

The roofs belong to the marmots. A city that must not shout has outsourced its noise to something that cannot be sued. Their whistling is the loudest thing on the Sill and it is a language with about nine words in it that people actually use: the shadow-note, the two-note for the cat, the fast series before a hard freeze, the one that means a stranger on the roof, and the flat single note that means *the hill is moving*, at which the whole quarter stops and then walks, quietly, in one direction. Colonies have accents. Young marmots cry wolf and there is a word for a young marmot, and it is not a kind one. In every quarter there are two or three old women who are listened to about the whistling, and the dogs — thick, slow, bred four hundred generations not to bark — are the translation layer for everyone else. A dog gets up and leans against your leg and you go inside. That is the entire alarm system and it has never failed.

The ash cat has an account. She takes a beast a week from the cliffs above the eastern quarters, and the loss is entered, and the household is made whole out of the quarter's common stalls, and no one has hunted her in living memory. Her line was on the escarpment before the first byre was dug. When she kittens, the rolls note it, because the rate will change.

The choughs at the Lip are the only thing here that does something for no reason. They drop and catch, drop and catch, all afternoon, in the updraught, and people watch them from the roof-walks the way people elsewhere watch a fire.

In the made soil there is a worm. It is a lowlander. It came up the stairs in a bale or a root-ball nobody remembers, and it has naturalised, and it is now the only lowland animal that lives here without permission. Soil is dowered by weight and by worm-count, and a bed without worms is dead soil and worth a tenth, and there are soil-thieves who take the top hand's depth off a walled bed at night and are pursued with a seriousness that outsiders find comic until they understand that the bed took four generations to make.

## Those who don't fit

**The stair-folk** live on the nine thousand steps, in landings and cut-backs in the wet cleft, among the ferns of the first thousand and the bare stone above. They keep goats, they cut and re-cut the treads, they carry grain and salt and copper up and dye and felt and needles down, and they are counted as neither Inside nor lowland. They shout. They can: the gorge is already loud, and there is nothing above them that has not already fallen. Their children are born on the stairs and their dialect is a joke in both directions. They are the only people who have seen the world from outside, and Inside does not entirely believe them.

**The outstood** are the cast-out, put above ground. They live in the abandoned outer quarters where the Lip is going, in rooms nobody will ever finish, with the shells still crisp in the thresholds and the frost splitting the corners a hair a night. They cannot keep stalls; there is nothing under them. They burn dung in the open and steal soil and die at forty. And they shout, too — at night, out there, across the empty lanes, because no hill is left above them to bring down — and people in the last inhabited quarter lie in the warm and hear it and say nothing, and the sound is the worst sound on the Sill. Their children are sometimes taken in at the scraping hour, if they can hold a scraper. That is the whole of the city's mercy and it is not small. And their dead are carried to the pavement with everyone else's, because the pavement is common, like the ponds. You may be put above ground. Your femur comes home.

**The couriers** run relays for forty days and are the only minds here that are wide rather than deep. They marry rarely. They come back with words — a lowland word for a colour, a far-warmth word for a kind of cousin — and the words spread along the ribbon at about the speed of a rumour, which is fast, which is the fastest thing here except frost. All of them can imitate the far accents badly. One in a generation claims to have stood at the end, where the damp band pinches out against a dry range and there is a last house with a last gutter and nobody beyond it. Nobody believes them. The city's cosmology is a rumour of its own edge.

## The slow tenants

The oldest citizens are the cushions. A plant the size of a cartwheel is a thousand years old, hard as a doorstep, spreading a finger's width in a decade, and it has a name and a boundary right, and disputes about it are heard by people who were not alive when the last dispute was heard. There is a register of the ones that have gone over the Lip. It is read aloud, in the ordinary voice, on the same day each year, and it takes about a quarter of an hour, and it is the only ceremony here that makes people cry.

The lichen walls are cropped once in a lifetime and the dye goes down the stairs and buys the grain. A man may own a dye wall his whole life and never see it cropped. He walks past it every day. He is not waiting for it; his granddaughter is. Nobody plants lichen. You only wait, and the waiting is the whole of the agriculture, and the cropping is attended by everyone descended from whoever planted nothing, which is everyone.

The rind is a tenant too, and it keeps records. Cut a channel-lining across and you get bands: wet years, dry years, the year the fog failed, the year of the ash. The re-cutters read them the way a lowlander reads a tree, and they are always behind, and the rolls say which quarter is due in which decade for the next hundred years, and everyone knows the schedule is a lie of a very useful kind.

And in the deep cisterns, below the last of the tallow light, there are pale things. Blind, jointed, slow, the length of a thumbnail, living in the water at the bottom of the world. The re-cutters meet them when they go down with a lamp and a copper pick. Opinion is divided. Some say they came up with the fog; some say they were in the seabed all along, waiting out four thousand paces of lift in the dark, which is not possible, and they say it anyway. What is not in dispute is what they are waiting for. The city is being turned into a cave by its own plumbing, a hair a year, and when Inside has moved inland and the roofs have fallen in and the lanes are roofed forever by their own drip-columns, something will already be living there, and it will not have had to change at all.

## The warmth

Here is the correction, and it takes a while to arrive.

The resident is not the beast and it is not the person. It is the warmth: the continuous body of heat that has been running under that patch of stone since the byre was dug, three cisterns ago, by a woman whose mark is still on the second course. It eats lichen and pond-hay and a fist of bitter grain. It excretes milk, dung, ash, whey, bone, and people. It has a name, a ledger, standing in the water-rolls, and rights of drainage against its neighbours. It can be inherited, divided, merged by marriage, and left. It outlives every family that has ever belonged to it. A person is the part of a warmth that can be sent on an errand.

When a warmth's people die out entirely, the quarter keeps it lit. There is a common fund for this. Beasts are bought, stalls are filled, some outstood child is given a stall-name and a scraper, and the heat goes on, because the alternative is a cold house and a cold house is a wound in everybody. This is the closest thing they have to a religion and it is not one. It is thermodynamics with a very long memory.

## Inside

And then, standing back — which you can only do on the roof-walks, at night, above the knee-deep fog draining downhill along the lanes — the whole ribbon looks like one more animal.

It has a mouth at the quarries and a gut below grade and a skin of turf and felt on top. It drinks fog. It is warm-blooded, at the temperature of a mild autumn, on the strength of a hundred thousand rumens. It moves inland at the speed of geology, a pace or two in a lifetime at the front, one warmth per hundred years at the back, and it sheds its skin behind it: empty rooms, frost-split, crisp shells in the thresholds, then not standing. It has three archives and no history — the rind in the channels, the rolls in the guild halls, and the knotted cords and pegs of the measurers out at the Lip, men who spend a career watching a line move less than the width of a hand and who can tell you, to the pace, how far their grandmother's house is from the edge.

But it does not want anything. That is the last correction, and the important one. The only things on the Sill that want anything are the size of a person or smaller, and what they want is small: a stall, a warm floor, a daughter who can hold a scraper, the third course laid before they die, six weeks of fly-bread, one trip east to stand in the willows.

Tonight the fog is in the lanes and the roof-walks are clear. The byres breathe. Somewhere a mason's daughter is asleep in a room her son will finish, with a rib above the door and a needle in the wall, and out on the terraces the day's water is freezing in ten thousand drilled holes, doing the only fast work there is. And at the bottom of the Ninth Cistern, in the dark, in the stone the water is patiently putting down, something blind and the length of a thumbnail moves a little, and settles, and waits for the cave.
