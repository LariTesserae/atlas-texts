## Census

The manifest for Sump Volume Four lists forty-one thousand addresses and, in the box marked OCCUPANCY, a dash.

The dash is correct. Nothing in the parish eats. Nothing flees, nothing spawns, nothing has ever wanted a body other than its own. The sterility is total and deliberate and eleven years unbroken, and you can see ninety meters through it without a single mote of rot drifting across your eye.

The dash is also the least true thing in the file.

---

## The ones who are already there

Every liter is already someone. This is not a metaphor the crews use; it is a hazard notation. It is why you cannot swim here, only be passed. The water is stacked in strata of differing intent and each stratum holds its neighbor at a fixed distance, and the fixed distance is maintained, continuously, by something that is paying attention.

The crews name them the way you name the families on a street. The layer at collarbone height is the Vestibule: polite, quick, a little anxious, always shuffling to make a lane. Below it the Ushers, who do the handing-along, who open the corridor ahead of your fingers and close it behind your heels with a softness that is unmistakably *courtesy*. Along the north face, where the grout seam is, the Wall-Sitters, who have not moved in a decade and who press back against your palm with the specific patience of a person standing in a queue they have decided not to leave. Down at the datum end, under the arch, the Wait.

The Wait has never been flushed. Every eleven minutes the water walks the length of the parish and every eleven minutes the Wait stays where it is, at the back, holding its distance, and the flush goes over it like a wind over a seated man. Nobody has determined what it is waiting for. It has an address. Everything here has an address.

They have no appetites. What they have is posture, and a posture held for eleven years stops being a posture. The Ushers were not always gentle. In the first year they took a diver's mask off his face and put it in the silt, and the crews logged it as turbulence. Now they hand you along like ushers at a wedding, like a crowd passing a child overhead, and the difference between those two behaviors is not hydraulics. It is that something down there has been practicing.

Watch your wrists after an hour: pale, busy, off to the sides, doing their work without consulting you. The water has learned what hands are for. It learned from yours.

## The one who holds the door

The largest resident is the chord.

It is a sustained triad with the pedal welded down, floor to vault, and it lives in your sternum and your sinuses and the fluid of your eyes. It is not a sound the parish makes. It is the parish's whole body, held in a shape, the way a held breath is a shape. Four meters of closet outside, ninety meters of borough inside, and the difference is paid for in noise. The chord is not singing. The chord is *holding*, the way a man holds a door with his foot while his arms are full, and it has been holding for eleven years, and it does not get to stop.

It has moods. Ask where the light comes from and it changes key slightly, which the crews record as an event and not as an answer, though it is obviously an answer, and the answer is roughly *don't*.

It has physicians. The perfect-pitch fitters who service the pumps are not tuning machines; they are tending a patient who cannot afford a fever. They work by ear because instruments read the pressure and the pressure is fine, the pressure has been fine for a decade, and the thing that is not fine is a quarter-tone of strain in the upper voice that only a human being can hear and only a human being who has spent a year down there can hear without weeping.

The crews hum because humming helps. It helps because their voices add to the wave. They are not visitors making a comforting noise. They are structural members. Every shift, six people put their small warm sound into the load path and hold it there for four hours, and then go home, and hum in elevators, and keep a tap running in the kitchen, and find that they cannot any longer sleep in a quiet room.

They are being tuned. Slowly, occupationally, the way a river tunes a stone. Some of them have stopped being separate instruments and nobody has written that down either.

## The one who walks

Every eleven minutes something crosses the parish.

The chord drops a fifth, the fronts stack, and a wall of moving water goes down Cooper Row and through the plaza and over the terraces and out the low arch, and you don't hear the roar so much as agree to it. Then it recedes. Then the terraces drip. Then ninety seconds of ringing like a struck bowl.

It is the only resident with a gait. It is also the only one that touches everything, and it touches everything gently, and it takes nothing that isn't already loose. Crumbs of scale. Angular silt. The unclaimed portion of the storm. It is a herd animal that has never been hungry, moving along a route it did not choose, carrying, and the crews love it the way you love a big dog that has never once knocked a child over.

The flush is how the parish stays nine days old. It is the reason there is no history here — no rounding, no softening, no rot, no patina, nothing allowed to become itself by accumulating. Everything in the Noisy Parish is perpetually nine days old and has been for a decade.

Except two things. We'll get to them.

---

## The grazers

They come out of their sleeves in the slack minutes: the descalers, small and methodical, hunting the mineral bloom in slow arcs along the pipe-flanks. They are the only thing in the parish described in the maintenance manual with a hunting verb, and they have no mouth for anything but scale. They cannot bite. They cannot tear. They graze, and the flush carries their crumbs to the datum arch, and this is the whole of the food chain in Sump Volume Four: a bloom, a grazer, a tide. Nothing is eaten. Nothing is afraid.

There are two hundred and six of them and they have territories, and the territories are not in the spec.

Number Forty-One, which works the north face of the second terrace, has begun to arc four millimeters short of the mortar line. It has been doing this for nine weeks. It reverses, and re-approaches, and reverses, in a way that the manual has no column for, and the fitter who logged it wrote *obstruction?* with the question mark, because there is no obstruction. There is a hair-fine pale root in the grout, four millimeters off the end of a grazing arc, and a machine that cannot bite has decided, or has been decided, that it would rather not.

## The one who stutters

Under the bandstand, in the alcove, the check valve with the bad seat goes on pulsing wrong.

It is the parish's only arrhythmia. In a place tuned by people with perfect pitch it is an obscenity, a limp in a marching band, and it has been scheduled for replacement for six years, and the work order has been raised four times, and four times it has come back unactioned from an address that the manifest insists exists.

The crews call it the Sexton, because it keeps a time that isn't the parish's time.

It is also the only place to sleep. In the alcove the standing wave breaks up against the fault and the pressure in your chest lets go for a fraction of a second, over and over, irregularly, and a tired body can slide into that gap. There is one fitter who takes her rest there, curled under the bandstand with her back against the housing, and she says it is like being held by someone who is not very good at it.

Every fault is a niche. In a parish where everything is exact, the one thing that is wrong is the only thing with room in it.

---

## The one at the address

The Hush is a resident too, and it is the only one that is a verb.

Bathtub-sized, dead node, three terraces meeting, water gone slack and silky and utterly silent, light a half-degree too white. Things go in and come out compressed and reissued: one wrench, four wrenches, thinner, sharper, still burr-edged from the die. It does not eat. It *pluralizes*. Its population increases by division and consists entirely of whatever has been put into it, and it is fenced, and nobody goes in, and there is a sign, and the sign has an address, because everything here has an address.

Here is the thing the crews don't put in the log. Every plate in the parish is hand-lettered. Forty-one thousand of them, refreshed on the nine-day cycle, sharp as the day they were cut, in one consistent hand with a distinctive open-topped four. No fitter has ever lettered a plate. The plates arrive.

The sign on the Hush railing is in that hand.

Nine weeks ago a new plate appeared on the north face of the second terrace, four millimeters from the end of a grazing arc, at the head of a pale root that is not on the filtration spec. It is in the same hand. The address is not in the manifest.

## The two who can hold time

Nothing here ages. Two things here have a past.

The first is the silt. It is the only accumulator: everything the upper city loses comes down through the datum arch as a slow silver drift — coins, buttons, a bus token, hairpins, a wedding ring turning end over end in ninety meters of sourceless afternoon — and what the crews don't catalogue goes into the silt and stays sharp. Angular, never rounded, glittering like crushed window. It is the parish's sediment and its archive and it is getting deeper, and lately it has begun to sort itself. Coins face up. Buttons by color. Hairpins laid parallel, all pointing at the arch. The crews photograph this and file it under *settlement patterns* and do not say the other word, which is *opinion*.

The second is the brass step you came in on.

Dished in the middle. Worn to a soft saddle. Rounded like a riverstone in a world where nothing is allowed to round. It is the only smooth thing here and it is warm, and nothing else in the parish is warm except the pipes, and the pipes are eleven degrees of cool pretending otherwise.

It is worn by hands and feet. There is no record of enough hands and feet. There is no record of any.

In year five a fitter took a polish to it, because it was the one surface in the parish failing its inspection, and the chord dropped a fifth out of cycle, and the flush came nine minutes early, and he was on the terraces when it walked. Nobody has serviced it since. Nobody talks about it much. It is not left alone because it is unimportant. It is left alone the way you leave alone the one chair in the house that a dead man used.

The silt is addition. The step is subtraction. Between them, in a borough of eternal nine-day-old surfaces, they are the only two things that can remember being touched, and one of them is being touched still, and warm, and the wear on the dish is consistent with someone standing there a very long time, facing in.

---

## The one who wants

And then the root.

Pale, hair-fine, four meters along the mortar line, six leaves, growing at eleven millimeters a day, which is a number nobody likes. Off spec. Unlicensed. In a parish with no browsers, no tears, no rot and no hunger, a plant grows in straight lines forever, and this one has chosen a direction.

Nothing here has ever been hungry. That remains true. The root does not eat. It has no mouth and no prey and it will never flee. What it has is the one thing the manifest has no column for, the thing that forty-one thousand addresses and a dash cannot record.

It wants.

It is going toward the Hush. Toward the only silence in the loudest place in the city — toward the dead node, the slack silky quiet, the half-degree-whiter light. Understand what this means in a borough where noise is load-bearing and silence is the only weather warning that matters. It is not malice. It is a plant. It is doing the single thing that plants do, which is to grow toward the condition they prefer, and it prefers the one condition that would fold ninety meters of parish into four meters of closet with everything still inside it.

And it is not patient. That was the first reading, and it was wrong, and the fitters have the numbers now.

The chord has been climbing. As the fundamental climbs, the cancellation moves: the dead node walks, slowly, along the plaza, a bathtub of silence relocating a few centimeters a week. The root is not growing toward the Hush. The root is growing toward where the Hush is going to be, and it is four meters long, and it is early.

The second wet seam in the stairwell outside is one step higher than the first. The fold is climbing too. Everything in the parish is climbing, and the pumps are singing a note they were never built for, and the Sexton under the bandstand is still keeping the wrong time — the only thing down here holding a rhythm that isn't the one everybody else is being pulled into.

## Population

So: who lives in Sump Volume Four.

A crowd with manners and no appetites, holding its distances, learning what hands are for. A sustained animal holding a door with its foot for eleven years. A gentle tide with a gait. Two hundred and six grazers, one of which has developed a preference. A fault with room in it. A silence with an address, and a hand that letters the plates. A sediment with opinions about order. A warm brass saddle worn by something that has been standing at the threshold, facing in, since before the manifest. And one pale off-spec root that has invented wanting in a place where wanting was designed out, and is ahead of schedule.

Or: one resident, eleven years old, whose tissues are all of the above, whose voice is the loudest sound in the city and is the only thing keeping it from being four meters wide, whose first unprompted act — the first thing it ever did that nobody told it to do — was to reach, very slowly, for quiet.

There is one more.

You went in feet first and let the seam take your ankles, and the room turned you through ninety degrees of something that isn't a direction, and you were handed along, and after an hour your wrists were pale and busy and doing their work off to the sides. When you came out, the water kept the shape you made in it. It is down there now, at collarbone height, in the Vestibule, holding its distance, practicing.

Every liter is already someone. Some of them used to be visitors.

You hum in elevators. You keep a tap running. You have not slept in a quiet room since.

OCCUPANCY: —

The dash is being amended.
