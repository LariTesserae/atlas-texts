Start with the moth, because it is the only thing down there that moves under its own power and is not you.

Two spread hands across, white the way the inside of a petal is white — not a color laid on but a color that light gets into and comes back out of, slower. Along the veins a green flush, faint, the green of a stem seen through a fingernail. The antennae are feathered. Both of them, wide combs, held forward. Anyone who has kept bees or watched the summer moths at a lamp knows what those combs mean, though the harvesters have no use for the knowledge and state it flatly when asked: it is a male, and it is looking for something.

It has no mouth. This is not a deformity. There is a whole family of moths that come out of the cocoon without one, having spent everything they were going to spend while they were still caterpillars; they emerge fat with stored life, they fly, they find, they mate, and they are dead in four days or seven. The mouthless stage is the last stage. It is the part of the life that is already ending.

The moth in the Held Breath has been in the last stage of its life for eleven years, or a century.

Nothing wears it. Put your open palm beneath its flight and hold it there for an hour and nothing falls into your hand. A moth that size, flying that long, should dust you like a candlewick. There is no dust in the room; dust requires time; and so the wings do not fray, the fringe does not thin, the green along the veins does not fade, and the flight is as fresh at the end of a season as the tear is.

The figure it flies is not a circle and not a figure eight. It goes low down the long axis toward the puddle end, rises in a long shallow climb through the green-gold haze where the hall stops being a hall, comes back along the ceiling close enough that you can hear the double beat of it — you hear the beat a breath before the wings make it — and turns at the crack end with a small hitch, a stumble in the line, like a word said twice. The whole figure takes a little under an hour. It does not vary.

It does vary. That is the thing the old ones know and the young ones are told and nobody makes a fuss about. There are kinks in the line, places where the moth drops or checks or overcorrects, and there are nine of them now. There were twelve when the oldest harvester was a girl. There were fifteen in the story her grandmother told. The figure is getting smoother. It is being corrected, death by death, toward something. When the moth curls on the floor and takes its three days and the walls take it without a mark and the fruit opens its seam, what comes out is not quite the same flight. It is the same flight with one fewer mistake in it.

They have a sign for it, made on the inside of the wrist: two fingers walked forward, then lifted. It means the moth and it also means *already*, and it also means *don't*, and the three are not distinguished.

Every few days it lands on the fruit and stays an hour, always on the same shoulder of it, and the amber comes soonest there.

---

But the moth is not the tenant. Say it plainly, the way they say the tide tables: the moth is a thing the room is doing.

Consider what the room does. It closes a cut on itself in minutes and lets your hand take weeks. It keeps ten thousand mouths on independent schedules and then makes them agree. It grows a rope a finger a day and grows the gap a finger a day to match, so that the reaching never finishes, and yet you can hear the rope knock the floor — hear the contact, plainly, a boom you feel in the soles — from a rope that has never touched anything. It lets a leaf fall for eleven years without letting it land, or die, or gather dust. It beads water on every underside and holds the beads until the top of the exhale and then drops a tenth of them at blood heat. It refuses mineral and adopts fiber. It permits you.

That last is the one worth sitting with. A warm wet hall this size, packed with sugar and water and living membrane, should be roaring. It should be furred with mold, sour with rot, clouded with gnats, ticking with the small lives that eat everything everywhere all the time. There is nothing. Not one spore, not one gnat, not one soft place except the deliberate soft places at the low end where it has chosen to grow rootlets that wave. The emptiness is not a condition of the room. It is an achievement, maintained continuously, at cost. It is scrubbing itself. It is keeping the hall clear the way you keep a table clear when someone is coming.

And it is not hungry. Nothing in there is being digested. The warmth is not the warmth of a stomach; it is the warmth of a heap of cut grass, of a compost pile, of a seedbed under glass — the heat of things being made to grow faster than they would. It is forcing itself.

So: not a lung, whatever it does with its mouths. Not a stomach. Not a cavity, which is an absence. A bud, which is a package, which is the only kind of emptiness that is actually a fullness folded up.

---

There are other residents, and they are the ones nobody counts.

At the low end, where the veins come together in their five ranks and the membrane has gone soft and dark, there is a small crowd of shapes. A bark bucket, green now, swelling, still bucket-shaped, still holding water. The handle of a scraper, gone to a smooth knob with a handle's memory in it. A boot. A cord. A child's carrying-frame that someone left in a bad season and did not go back for. Eleven or twelve things, depending on the year, all of them still recognizable and none of them still usable, all of them warm and wet and faintly pulsing under the hand.

The room does not destroy what you leave. It keeps the form and drops the use. It has the bucket exactly right — the rim, the taper, the little asymmetry where the bark was thicker on one side — and it has no idea what a bucket is for, or it has decided that what a bucket is for is not the part it needs.

They are all at the low end. Every one. Nothing left anywhere else in the hall survives a season; it is simply absorbed, gone, no swelling, no memory. Only the things left near the puddle are kept, and kept in shape, and drawn slowly down into the soft dark place where the five ranks converge. The growing tip. The point.

It is provisioning itself. It is packing the shapes of your tools into the place where a seed packs its first leaves and its first meal. Whatever is coming up out of that low corner is going to come up already knowing what a bucket looks like.

The harvesters have a rule about this, and it is not a superstition, it is a manners: *don't leave anything you'd want back.* Some of them leave something anyway, on purpose, once in a life. They don't say why. They touch your wrist afterward.

---

The people are the only fauna, and they are seasonal, and they know it.

They come down in the dry weeks with flint and their own fiber cord, they cut rind and lining, they carry, they climb out before the exhale finishes. They build nothing because there is nothing to build with that is not already the room, and because the room will furnish itself if you give it a season. They do not talk. Sound comes back wrong down there — a drip before the drip, your voice with a syllable in it you have not said — and speech is the one thing nobody is willing to hear mispronounced by their own mouth. So they use their hands, and they hold each other's wrists far more than people do aboveground, and this is not affection, or it is affection of a very old practical kind.

The wrist is how they count. Going down, at the squeeze, each person's thumb presses the inside of every other wrist in turn. Coming up, the same. The numbers differ. They always differ, and always by one, and the one is somebody who was with you the whole time — who carried, who steadied the cord, who knew where the rain would fall and moved you out of it before it fell — and whom none of you can afterward describe, and none of you can remember not knowing.

They do not investigate this. They leave a hand's width of space at the squeeze on the way up. They pack a portion more food than they need and the portion is gone and no one ate it, and once in a while the warm rain tastes of something nobody brought down.

The woman who left the copper — four seasons mapping veins, five ranks, spiral, nectaries, the insides of petals — she has a plain opinion about the extra, delivered in the same voice she uses for the vein-map, and it is this: *it's not a ghost, it's a rehearsal.* She points out that the room copies buckets. She points out that the shadows on the walls are leaves with the wrong lobing, a canopy that has not grown. She points out that everything down there arrives a little before itself — the drip, the syllable, the knock of a rope that has not reached the floor. Then she says: so what would a person look like, arriving early? And she does not answer, because someone touches her wrist, and the conversation is over, which is how all their conversations end.

---

And then there is the one thing in the room that is not the room and has not been made into the room.

The copper blade, wedged in a wound on the north curve, gone the green of old pond water, half-swallowed, unreachable for six years. Around it the sap blistered and the membrane went white and hard and the crack narrowed by a finger's width for a day and a night, and everyone learned the lesson, and nobody has brought mineral down since.

But there is a shoot growing out of the handle.

It has been growing for six years. It is not a callus and it is not a swelling in the shape of a blade. It is a shoot, with a stem and internodes and leaves, and the leaves are lobed wrong. The lobing is the lobing of the shadows. The arrangement is the arrangement of the shadows. It is the only place in the whole held hall where the canopy that has not grown yet exists in the present tense, in the flesh, green and turgid and casting a shadow that matches itself.

The room refused the copper. The room cannot digest the copper. The room has done the only other thing it knows how to do with something it cannot refuse and cannot take: it has grafted onto it. The wound is a union. The mineral is stock. Everything else down there recurs — the moth dies and comes back smoother, the fruit whitens and ambers and whitens, the water beads and falls and beads, the leaf falls and falls, the rope grows and the gap grows — everything circles, and only the blade has gone anywhere. It is the only resident with a history. It is the only thing aging.

They count its slow disappearance the way you count a child's height, and they are right to, and it is not the disappearance they are counting.

---

So who lives there.

Not the moth: the moth is the wanting, given wings and a search pattern, flying a line that gets cleaner every generation because what it is searching for is getting closer to being real. A male, mouthless, feathered, with four days of life in him and no way to spend them yet, homing on a scent the walls are making in their five-ranked spiral down at the sweet wet point. When it opens he will have his four days. He has been holding them for a century. Every pause, when the ten thousand mouths shut and the crackle stops and he hangs stopped in the green-gold air with his combs forward — that is not him waiting. That is the only part of the time that is his.

Not the harvesters: they are the model. They are what it is copying, badly and lovingly, the way it copies the bucket. It has their cord in its rope and their blade in its wound and their shapes packed into its tip and one of them, always one, arriving early and helping with the carrying and going up the crack and being impossible to describe.

The room lives there. And the room is not the tenant either; the room is the accommodation.

What lives there has not arrived, and is nevertheless the largest presence in the hall, the way a word is in a mouth before it is said — occupying everything, arranging the tongue and the breath and the teeth, present in every part of the apparatus and nowhere in it yet. The light is its first morning, come early. The shadows are its leaves, come early. The knock of the rope is its foot on the floor, come early. The extra at the squeeze is its hand on your wrist, come early, warm, competent, kind, already knowing where the rain falls.

The harvesters have a sign for it and they use it rarely, and only aboveground, and only in daylight, and it is this: the open hand, palm up, fingers not quite closed. It means *not yet.* It means *it is taking the next breath.* It means *leave room.*

They climb out before the exhale finishes because the crack is widest then, and because none of them wants to be inside for the pause — and if you ask any of them, privately, with a hand on the wrist, why, they will tell you the truth in the flattest voice they have.

It isn't that the pause is dangerous. Nothing has ever been hurt in the pause.

It's that the pause is when it's most there. Four seconds, ten thousand mouths shut, the growth gone silent, the moth stopped mid-figure, the leaf stopped mid-fall, the whole warm sharp-edged hall attending — and you, standing in the middle of a room that has been kept clear for something, taking up space that was set aside.

They go up. They count. They leave a hand's width.

And below them the mouths open, and the drawing-in begins, and it goes on being about to.
