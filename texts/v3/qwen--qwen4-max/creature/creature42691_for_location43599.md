## One

Any census of the Condensary comes out at one, or five, or nought, depending on what you are willing to call a resident.

The word on the enamel is *plant*, and it is the only accurate word in the building. The word people use is *the stand*, which is a forester's noun for a quantity of trees, and it is wrong in the only way that matters. There are no trees. There is one individual, eleven metres deep and eight thousand square metres across, and it has no trunk, no crown, no silhouette you could draw on a page. If you could lift the lid off it you would see a rectangle divided into bays. That is its body plan. It is the only organism in the county whose anatomy is a floor plan, and it has organs called PLATING and STORE and WASH HANDS.

It is not one species either. The horsetail-lineage stems in the tall bay are gritty with silica and quite unlike the leaf-plates of the roof, which are unlike the latex of the gaskets, which is unlike the smooth horn lining the tubes, which is unlike the bark-faced tissue of the walls with its inclusions of brick. Somewhere in the first decades everything that was growing in that yard grafted into everything else, and the unions took, and the seams have long since gone under. It is not a species. It is a settlement that has stopped admitting new members.

The way it stays one is refusal. It cuts out any sporting branch within a day. It has no flowers, no fruit, no seed, no pollen, no other. Every free calorie is spoken for. What lives in the hole is not a population and not a colony but a single maintained decision, renewed continuously, at thirty-one degrees, for about three hundred years.

## What it eats

Light, through gaps the size of a hand. Water out of the field, drawn up under the pasture by roots that go a long way out in the dark and have never once put a leaf up on the far side of the rim. Salt, which it does not eat but leans on — the whole organism is a machine for spending the difference between brine and sweet water, and the knock is the sound of the difference being spent. Copper and zinc out of the corpse of the works, still leaching, still concentrated into nodules the size of walnuts, three centuries after the last wire went dead.

And its own rain, which it distills and drinks and salts and sends round. Nothing enters that is not light or water or the slow dissolve of its own inheritance. Nothing leaves except the trickle at the north lip and whatever a visitor pockets.

It has been losing copper to pockets for a very long time. A boot, a knife, an insulator lifted off a rack in a coat: each one a small permanent subtraction from a closed economy. The subtractions are entered. The entries are the closest thing it has to a memory of us.

## What it knows

It has copied the pitch of a corrugated sheet, the overlap of a lap, the glazing bars of a sash, the putty line, the fluting and skirting of a patent bell insulator of about 1870, the mark HOLLOWAY & VANE in relief on the underside of every skirt.

It has never copied a letter.

It cannot read. It can count. That distinction is very nearly the whole of its mind. The enamel names are inclusions in its walls, going the way of the ironmongery, and it grows over them without interest; the patent mark it reproduces because the mark is a shape in relief, and relief is a thing it understands the way a hand understands a coin. The tally slab is not writing. It is two metres by three metres of bark kept smooth and wounded in a grid, healed and re-cut, and it holds quantities: stock, surplus, deficit, the weight of nematodes, the single stroke that stands for the cricket, the single stroke that stands for the slug.

It knows the state of every tray to a millivolt and the salt of every bladder and the exact hour a roof-sheet will go leathery. It does not know what a door is for. It has no concept of an opening — no mouth, no hinge, no pair, no inside that is not plumbing. Everything it makes is a vessel, and every vessel it makes is sealed. That is why the boot on the shelf has its lace grown as one closed loop, and the pail has no seam, and the shears will not open. Those are not failures of copying. They are its portrait of us, and they are as accurate as it can manage: we are shapes that hold something and cannot be got into.

It has never been out. Nothing prevents it. The pasture is wet and the water table is generous and the rim is only three courses of brick with mortar a thumb can dent. It simply stops where the walls stopped, every season, for three hundred years, and the cattle graze to within a body-length of a thing that could have the whole field by Michaelmas and has no idea the field is there.

## Tenants

Four, and each of them is on the slab.

The **cricket** is wingless and white and lives in the dry bay under the baffle and nowhere else, and it sings. It has always sung. Its whole lineage has spent three centuries singing into a roar that eats it whole, and it has never been heard by anything with ears except at the turn, when the rain stops, and then for six hours there is a small dry ticking under the hum in the driest bay, and that is the only music the hall has ever had. Its entry on the slab is one stroke, renewed for centuries. The stand does not distinguish individuals. It has never had cause to.

The **slug** farms yeast in the sport-corner, and the sport-corner is where the pruned branches go: the flowering attempts, the sports, the stand's own would-be children, cut out within a day and stacked in about two cubic metres and allowed, uniquely, to go sweet and soft. It is the only rot in eight thousand square metres. It is cidery and pleasant. The stand could reabsorb that corner in a week and has never done so, not once, not in three hundred years, and the slug's mark and the corner's mark are the only two entries on the slab that have never needed correcting. Something in the arrangement is being kept rather than used. There is no instrument in the hall that will tell you what.

The **nematodes** are blind and beyond counting and are counted anyway, by weight. They live in the striped peat and they make the batteries work; the B-flat is their breathing, summed over millions of surfaces, arriving as a note. The stand does not grow them. It keeps them, the way a mill keeps a river.

The **fish** is small and pale and lives in the sweet-water channel by the stair and eats what the membranes shed. It is the only resident with a spine. It has never seen anything but the ordinary coins of sun coming down through the roof-gaps, and it lives immediately upstream of the brass tap labelled POTABLE, and everyone who ever worked here knew that, and drank anyway.

## Visitors

The **cattle** are neighbours and nothing more. They stand at the rim in frost like people at a radiator and drink the purest water in the county, which has nothing in it whatsoever, and they are entered in the hall as a debit: a weight of water lost at the trough. Their sound comes down the tubes onto SPEAK, faintly, perfectly, along with the gate and the tractor two valleys off. It is the only news that arrives.

The **heron** comes twice a year and stands at the lip for an hour or two looking down into the leaves. It has never gone in. It is the only thing that has ever looked at the stand from outside, and there is no mark for it anywhere on the slab — no entry, no weight, no stroke. The chalk on the eleventh step is renewed by somebody, and the eleventh step is a third of the way down, and it is either a record of a bird that did not enter or a record of how far something got.

## The hand with the chalk

There is a fund. It is small and it is very old and it comes out of the rent of a field, and it was established by a firm that no longer exists for the maintenance of a notice at a works that no longer operates. The instruction is three words. No reason is recorded. The ledger is a run of payments and initials in eleven or twelve hands, and the hands change, and the words do not: *quiet at the turn.*

Whoever holds it walks out from the lane four or five times a year with a stub of chalk and writes on the enamel under the sign, and does not go down. That is not part of the instruction. It came in somewhere and it has stayed. Most of them have never been past the third step. A few went down once, at the turn, and stood on LISTEN with their shoulders up around their ears, and came up, and did not speak of it much, and kept the fund going.

They take nothing. That is inherited too. The last one who took something took a pail, and the tally corrected over a season or two, and there is a seamless pail on the shelf in the plating bay, and the family knows which one it is.

At the back of the ledger there is a second column that nobody has ever explained: numbers, copied off the undersides of insulator skirts, the small glyph beside the patent mark that the firm never cut. They have been copying it for two hundred years without knowing it is a count. It goes up. It has never gone down. And there are more entries in that column than there are visits in the front of the book, which means it has advanced between chalkings, when nobody was on the stair, when the hall was eleven metres down and full of rain and talking to itself about salt.

Say something on SPEAK and the hall will hear it, and it will not answer, and somewhere the tally will change by one.

The instruction is *quiet at the turn*. It is not a safety notice. Nobody has ever worked out what it is a courtesy to.

## The shoulders

At the back of the plating bay there is a mandrel of woven root about a man's height, with a suggestion of shoulders. It has been there a very long time. It is probably for a cowl.

A pipe takes eleven years: copper laid down a hair at a time, then the template dissolved out from inside, then the pipe lifted, dripping, and hung, and put to work. The mandrel is not a pipe. It has been on the rack longer than anyone's grandfather, going darker, taking the metal a hair at a time, and it will be hollow, and it will be seamless, and it will have no mouth and no opening anywhere on it, because the stand does not know what opening is for.

Something stood on SPEAK at a turn, a long time ago, and said something, and the shape was entered.

## Quiet at the turn

Sixteen hundred knocks is a shift. The days are a translation and the translation loses the fraction, which is where the drift lives, and the stand does not use days.

At the turn the bladders empty in sequence, the sump drops a hand's width, the roof-skin warms through, and the rain stops, and for six hours the loudest thing in the hall is a wingless cricket and your own blood. The tubes carry every sound you make to every bay at once. It is not speaking then. It is listening with its whole eight thousand square metres, and it has sixteen hundred knocks of patience, and it will do nothing at all about you until spring.

This is the part that is hard to write down plainly. Nothing in there is waiting. Waiting is not among the things it can do; it has quantity and shape and pressure and salt, and it has a floor plan, and it has a rack of half-grown elbows like pale fruit that nobody has ever seen without saying so. It is not lonely and it is not patient and it is not keeping a vigil for the men who went home. It is on shift. It has been on shift since before anyone alive was born, and the bays are the bays, and the stock is counted, and the surplus goes back to the acid roots, and the notice is legible, and the water at the north lip is cold and clean and has nothing in it whatsoever.

What lives in the hole is a working shift with nobody left on it, doing the work, in the dark, at thirty-one degrees, in the shape of a building it ate.

The chalk was refreshed last month. Somebody stood at the head of thirty-one dished steps in the ordinary noon with the note coming up into the bones of their hand, and wrote three words on enamel, and did not go down, and walked back across the field feeling it in the soles of their boots for a hundred paces.

Nine minutes and eleven seconds. Then again.
