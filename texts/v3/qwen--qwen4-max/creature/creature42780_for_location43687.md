Begin where nothing is supposed to be able to live: in the hand's width.

The setter's underside is a matte black plane held ninety millimeters above the stone, and that gap is warm — nine degrees above the air, and wet, and dark, and it moves at the speed a fingernail grows. Things live in it. The crews call them unders, which is wrong, and the counter has stopped using the word. They are not *under* anything. They live in a thickness the way a fish lives in a depth. They are eleven millimeters thick and cannot become twelve; the ceiling does not negotiate. They have no eyes. They have a rim of wet cilia along the flank that reads the distance to the plane and keeps them centered in the warmth, and they graze the iridescence — the open lattice, the film the stone cannot use — and they lay eggs in the flaws, in the little ticks of thermal stress, so that a setter's ticking is, if you are eleven millimeters thick, the sound of housing becoming available.

They cannot cross cured stone. Cured stone is cold and dry and has nothing on it. So their entire world is a strip a hundred meters wide and nine centimeters high, traveling east along the front at the pace of the works, and a lineage can go forty generations without ever encountering an edge. When a setter is jacked up for plane service the strip stops, and the animals walk. They walk in files, in the dark, following the warmth of the next machine eight kilometers off, and most of them die on the way, and enough arrive. There is a man who lies on his back on a padded sled and moves them out from under a lifted plane with a squirrel-hair brush. He is the only person on the plateau who touches stone an hour old. He does it through a glove, with a brush, at a rate of about two hundred animals an hour, and he has never once been written up for it, because there is no rule about this. There is no rule about this because they are not on the schedule. Nobody planted them. They came.

Their eggs are spheres. Four tenths of a millimeter, perfect, laid by the thousand in the cracks. Nothing on this plateau is round except what is alive.

The woman who counts springtails works the nine-year terraces, which is to say she works the middle of the world. Walk out of Sixth Grade and you walk backward through the ages of the ground: ninety-one years under the town, where there is four centimeters of dirt and the moss holds water like a sponge; nine years on her terraces, where there are cushions and lichens and the first four millimeters; sixty days at the curing pans; and then, this morning, the green.

She takes forty cores a day, each the size of her palm. Four hundred animals in a lean week. Nine hundred in a bloom. Out at the front, a hundred times that, but the front is not hers. She counts by eye, with a lens and a tally clicker, because the automated count drifts and she is the thing the automated count is checked against. She has names for the states of the carpet that are not in any manual: soup, shiver, the grey, going quiet. She can smell a lattice fault two days before the gleaners thin. Twice she has stopped the front by telephone, from a hut on jacks, in her socks.

She is very good company. She tells the story of the wrench — the woman on the lower terrace, the second bounce, the third, the whole plain turning to look — and she tells it as though the joke were on the plain, for having nothing better to do than listen. The adults call her the Count. The children call her Aunt Sixty, after the rule, because she is the person who explains the rule, and because she has been out here six years and they assume she is made of the same number. Her name is on a duty board on Level Two, misspelled, and she has never had it corrected. Correcting it would require a form, and the form would go into the drawer, and she has opinions about the drawer.

She has not left the terrace in six years, and the reason people give for this is that she is dedicated. That is not the reason.

The sump hens were designed for a world with grit in it.

They crop the mats — sedge, moss, springtails by the mouthful — and a bird that crops needs stones in its gizzard, and there are no stones here smaller than a door. So they swallow the chips. Conchoidal flakes, the little crescents that come off a slab when it cracks, sharp enough to open a wrist. The gizzard is a muscle and it works them, and mostly they work it back. Hens perforate. Hens die at three years in a place where the design life is eleven, and there is a breeding line for gizzard wall thickness, and the vet's ledger has a column for it, and the column is going the right way slowly.

When a hen dies they open her. In the gizzard, in the dark, at body temperature, there is sand.

Sixty grams. A hundred and forty in an old bird. Bright, fresh, glassy, the corners just taken off — not the frosted pitted grains of an old world but new work, unfinished, a pane with its arrises softened. It is the first sand on this plateau and it is being made continuously, by an animal whose entire scheduled function is to defecate on crust, in a compartment nobody thought about, at a rate nobody has authorized.

The assay is by mass and the pins are numbered, and sand has no number. So it goes into a category. *Fines, biogenic.* There is a line for it on a form, and the form goes into the drawer on Level Two, and the drawer is sixty-one years deep.

The children know about the gizzards. The children know everything. They are the first generation of human beings with no memory of a pebble, and they have a word, *hol*, that means round and also means before, and also means a lie told kindly. They skim slabs on the sheet-flow, which is a game entirely about grade. They are permitted eggs, eyes, drops, the moon, and the school's globe, which is a sphere forty centimeters across showing a planet with a real sea on it, and which comes out of a cupboard four times a year. A ball is contraband. Two boys were caught with a ball. Nobody remembers where the ball came from and the incident is now a story with a moral nobody can reconstruct.

They name the foxes. They feed the foxes, which is forbidden, under a hand-lettered sign asking you not to feed anything, which is re-lettered every spring by whoever has the best handwriting, and which is the town's actual constitution. There is a lineage of pale foxes under the school. The school has moved four times, at night, at the speed of a walking man, and the foxes went with it, and their kits are born under the same door the children come through at the same hour, and have been for four generations. They are the only animal on the plateau whose habitat is a timetable.

The herd has begun to have preferences.

They were specified as a function: heavy, slow, splayed hooves, break crust, dung on it. They have no wild ancestor and no predators and, until recently, no opinions. Now they walk the warm lines. They return to particular cracks. They go single-file along the terrace lips where the pans are, and they have been doing it long enough that the track holds water after the shower has stopped, and water that is held follows the grade, and the grade falls east.

The surveyors measure this monthly and are pleased. What they are measuring, though the form does not have a box for it, is the first draft of a river. A channel is forbidden. Four millimeters a year of hoof-worn crust that happens to lie in a line is not a channel; it is erosion, which is the point, which is scheduled. The herd is cutting the river sixty-one years early and doing it so slowly that it is legal.

The river itself lives in a cabinet on Level Two. It has a designation and a guardian and a file, and it is fed monthly: sheet-flow depths, pan temperatures, the herd's tracks, the moss's water-holding capacity. Four custodians deep now. The current one is thirty-four and will be ninety-five in the year the hydrology board releases the plan, and she has a daughter who has been taught the file the way other children are taught a hymn. On the drawing the river is a blue line with a name chosen ninety years ago by a committee, and the children call it the wet, and they have seen it, in the way you have seen a country.

Everything here is ahead of its water. That is the local condition. That is what it does to you.

Forty kilometers downwind the wall stands where it has stood for ninety years, and there are birds in it that have not landed since the season before last.

Not a species. A caste, a habit, a hundred thousand gleaners that ride the standing updraft and drink rime and sleep on the wing in shifts, and come down only to lay, on the green stone, at the front, where the film is thickest. Their world is exactly as old as the works. It is the exhaust of a continent, and it has a completion date, and when the front closes the wall will thin and go, and the habit will have nowhere to be. Nobody has written this down. The counter has written it down.

And some of them go east.

Every year a fraction of the gleaners leaves the works and flies out over the grade, eleven hundred kilometers, to the basin that has been dry since before the works began. Most do not come back. Some come back in the wrong season, thin, and the counter has been opening their pellets for six years, and in the pellets there is sand.

Not made sand. Found sand. Frosted, pitted, rounded by water a hundred thousand years ago. Because the basin is not entirely dry — the sheet-flow arrives there, four centimeters of fall per kilometer, forty-four meters of total drop across eleven hundred kilometers, and in the low corner there is a stain, a damp pan a few centimeters deep that blooms with everything the lattice could not use, and it is the best feeding on the continent, and under it and around it there is a beach. An old one. A fossil beach, from the sea that was there before, the sea the plan is quietly proposing to rebuild.

The birds are commuting to the ghost of the coast their grandchildren are promised. The plateau's future and its past are the same place, eleven hundred kilometers east, and there is an animal that has already found it and does not know what it has found.

That is why she has not left the terrace. She is waiting for the wrong season, every year, for the thin ones to come back.

Contraband here arrives from two directions. From the west, in the boot-treads of the feedstock crews, who are the only people who ever look at the mountains, and who are washed and brushed and assayed at the gate because old dirt is a contaminant and the pins are numbered. From the east, in the crops of birds, unlisted, unweighed, unowned.

She keeps a tea tin on the warm rail.

In it there are two hundred and six grains, and at forty magnifications she can tell you which is which. The made ones are bright and unfinished, corners taken off and nothing more, the work of a muscle in the dark. The found ones are frosted and dull and have a history: each one has been rolled by a sea, and the sea has a date, and the date is before.

This is the only unscheduled object on her terrace, and she is the assay, and so it is the one place on the plateau where the assay has a secret. She is not smuggling sand. Sand is the goal; sand is what four millimeters of dirt is *for*; sand is the whole reason the herd exists and the moss is permitted and the surveyors are pleased. She is smuggling *early*. She has taken the future out of the drawer and put it in a tin, and the difference between her and the hydrology board is sixty-one years and the question of whether a thing can be allowed to exist before it is released.

Once a season, at the shift change, with the tea going round and the fog coming up through the terraces like something being poured, she walks to the east lip and puts one made grain into the sheet-flow.

Two millimeters of water, no channel to argue with, going where the grade tells it. The grain rolls when the sheet is thick enough, which is perhaps eleven days a year, and travels perhaps a meter a night on those days, and stops. She has done the arithmetic. She is very good at arithmetic; it is the entire job. The number that comes out is larger than the plan, larger than the plan's plan, larger than the lifetime of the office that guards the river. The grain will not arrive. It will be buried in moss, or eaten by a hen, or lodged behind a survey pin and stay there while the pin rusts and the terrace cures and the school moves twice more.

She puts it in anyway. It is the slowest thing anyone on this plateau has ever done on purpose, and it is the only thing here that is not for the assay.

Last season a girl watched her do it. Nine years old, born on cured ground, *hol* in her mouth like a stone she wasn't allowed to swallow. She asked what it was and the counter said sand, and the girl said there isn't any, and the counter said there is a hundred and forty grams inside every dead hen on the lower terrace and about a gram in the crop of a bird that has been to the sea. Then she said: it's going east. It's ahead of the water. Everything here is ahead of the water.

The girl asked how far it had got.

The counter said: about nine meters. Then she said the number properly, in meters per year, with the eleven days in it, and let the girl hold the whole enormous disappointing figure in her hands, because that is what the tin is for, and that is how you make someone who will still be here in sixty-one years.

Above them the setters ticked. Out in all that room a flock turned and made its sound like distant applause, and under the nearest plane, in ninety millimeters of warm dark that no rule has ever described, something eleven millimeters thick laid a perfect sphere in a crack.
