Begin with the skin, because here the skin is a lid.

They are covered from the crown to the ankle in felt and hide and oiled mat, hooded, wrapped at the throat, gloved in summer — and it is always summer — and the wrapping is not modesty and not warmth. It is storage. What is inside them browns in raw light. A person who stands too long in the open at the thinning of the Veil comes in smelling faintly of scorched sugar, and the smell is not a smell of cooking, it is a symptom, and every child on the Table is taught the same sentence before it is taught its own name: *if you begin to smell sweet, come inside.* Fever smells sweet too. So does a wound going wrong. The whole diagnostic art of the town is olfactory, and its central finding is always the same finding, and the physicians — who are also the renderers, since both jobs are the reading of a body's contents — carry no instruments, only a clean cloth to wipe their noses between patients, so as not to carry one person's sweetness to another.

They do not sweat. Nothing here evaporates. A body cannot cool itself by spending, so it cools by going down: at noon the town descends into its own cellars, into the older floors, into the eleven feet of accumulated centuries, and lies in heaps in the dark against stone that has not seen light in nine hundred years. Six hundred people, pressed, breathing slowly, chests enormous — the ribs are barrel-wide, the blood is rust-dark and thick, the breath comes about four times a minute at rest — and nobody speaks, because speech at that proximity is unnecessary and expensive. They lie and listen through their hips to the pumps and the pestles and the herd going over. It is the closest thing they have to privacy and it is a heap of bodies. The word for that hour is the word for being poured back.

Faces are veiled and hardly used. Ears are bare. You know a person by the ear, by the smell at the ear, by the rhythm that comes up through the floor when they walk, and the greeting is to take the ear in your hand — the whole hand, thumb behind, the way you would take a cup you were being lent. Children's ears are handled constantly, by everyone, so that ears grow large and soft and slightly deformed, and an old person's ear is a ruin of affection. Faces are for family. A lover knows a face; the town knows an ear. To be angry at someone you cup their ear closed with your palm, gently, and hold it, and this is the worst insult available and also the second punishment in the law: resin in the ear, a lid on the one opening, a day of being alone inside a crowd. They call it being lidded. It is used perhaps once a decade and everyone remembers where they were.

Their feet are bare and the callus is thin and deliberate, filed, kept sensitive, because the slab is the only long-distance organ they have. They read the floor the way we read a face. A chimer's territory, a herd turning, a pump failing, someone running in the north end — all of it arrives in the soles. Old people whose feet go numb are said to have gone quiet, and are carried, and set with their backs against walls so that something still reaches them. Blindness is common and unremarkable; several of the best masons have never seen anything. Deaf feet are the real disability, and the word for it is the word for a stone that has been lifted out of its hollow.

Nobody runs. Running is obscene, and it is also evidence: a jolt is a spill inside the vessel, and a running person is presumed to be losing something, and is stopped and held and asked, kindly, what is wrong. They move at the pace of the tiles, a shuffle that does not lift the foot, and the shuffle is taught to infants by putting them on a herd and letting the herd teach them. A hundred paces a day is a respectable speed for a person who has somewhere to be. There are herd-walkers on the Table who have followed the tiles around the same four hundred paces for sixty years, who know every button of the pavement by the feel of it under a bare heel, who could tell you which of ten thousand stones is seated a hair proud, and who have never in their lives been more than a stone's throw from their own door and have travelled, by their own reckoning, an enormous distance.

Drinking takes about eleven minutes.

This is the fact that shapes everything else about them. Sap does not pour; it climbs. You drink by putting a wick in your mouth and the other end in the jar and waiting, and the waiting is done cheek to cheek with whoever is drinking with you, because a wick is shared and the sharing is the point. So all conversation of any importance happens with two faces four inches apart and a thread between the lips, slowly, over a quarter of an hour, in a voice that cannot reach anyone else. Courtship is the offer of the clean end. Marriage is a permanent wick installed between two households' jars, copper-collared, knotted, registered; divorce is the cutting of it, and the volume must be reconciled in public, drop by drop, by an accountant, with both families holding the ends. Nursing is a wick and a body held still for hours, and the word for mother is the word for the near end.

And every cup is a crowd, and they know whose. A person can recite their contents: a grandmother, a man who died in the last thinning, two infants who did not last, a stranger's grandmother, a bequest from a seated elder who liked them. This is their genealogy. It is not a tree, it is an inventory, and it is recited at introductions the way we give a surname. What is lost at a death is not the substance — the substance is recovered, weighed, entered, returned. What is lost is the hollow the person made, the particular shape of the absence. So grief here is not expressed as emptiness. It is expressed as a number. The render-house gives the family the figure, and the family sits with the figure, and passes it mouth to ear, and that is the funeral: a household saying a volume to each other in the dark for three days. The word for grief is the word for the wind at the edge, which is also the only word for cold: a chill on the side of you that faces out.

What is left after the pressing is dried and burned under the kilns, and the kilns fire pots, and the pots hold the fluid. So a grandmother becomes a jar that holds grandmothers, and the jar has her name, and the name is a legal person, and it is addressed. You speak to a pot. Not metaphorically; you tell it the news, mouth to its rim, because that is where the ear is. To break a pot is the only true murder in the law, the only killing that cannot be repaired by arithmetic, and it is not punished with violence, because violence spills. It is punished by decanting the breaker early and pouring them into the common cistern, unattributed, so that they become everyone's and no one's. Anonymity is the death penalty. There is a worse sentence, reserved for those who spill deliberately, and it is spoken of only in the passive: their fluid goes to the tethered herd at the edge, and the leathers drink it, and the leathers go around, and some of what they carry is never seen again. The word for damnation is *outward*.

The masons are the ones who understand that the town is an instrument.

They sort, they do not cut; the wind has already made every shape that will ever exist, and a wall is an argument between found things, settled with wedges and bone pins and a mastic of chewed fiber. Everyone chews. All day, all six hundred, jaws working fiber for the common mastic, so that every wall in the town is held together by what everybody has spat, and you can recognize a household through the stone by its chewing. Teeth are worn flat by forty. Jaws are enormous. It is the second most intimate thing they do with their mouths and it is done in company, absently, the way we fidget.

But the walls carry speech, and a badly seated stone buzzes and eats a syllable, and so masons tune. They tap a stone and listen to its note and set it where its note belongs; a wall is a chord; the north end of the town speaks in a different register from the south, and there are words that only carry in certain streets. As the town rises a hand's breadth each century, the tuning drifts, and every few generations there is a council to re-tune the streets, and afterward some old words no longer work anywhere. Down on the first floor, on bare pavement beside the ring of the first hearth, there are tapped marks that no living ear can parse. Someone goes down once a generation and strikes them and comes up and says: it means something, it is a whole sentence, and we have lost it. This is their archaeology and their linguistics and it is the same discipline as building. The keepers of the language and the keepers of the walls are the same guild and they are, quietly, the most powerful people on the Table, because they decide what can still be said.

The counters are the smallest people, and the holiest, and they are chosen for thirst.

Nine drops a minute at the Lip, and someone has been counting them without interruption for eleven hundred years and more. Not recording — *counting*, aloud, mouth to ear, in shifts, the number relayed from a retiring counter to a coming one with an overlap protocol so exact that a counter can die mid-word and the count not break. The number is unimaginably large and it is the town's other name for the world. To be a counter you must be still, and small, and need little, and there is an entire ascetic tradition built on needing little: the dry saints, the ones who drank less than their allotment for fifty years and left the difference in the common jar and were seated in a wind-hollow at the end and fed by hand. Holiness here is a negative volume. A person who takes less is not depriving themselves; they are *making room*, and everyone can feel the room they make, and it is the only generosity that is not a loan.

Which means the arithmetic whispers about subtraction, and everyone hears it, and no one says it, and it is said constantly. Births are licensed by volume. The midwife is an accountant and the two words are one word. A woman pregnant without license is not shouted at — there is no shouting, the air won't carry it. The town gathers. Mouths come to her ear, one after another, all night, and say the arithmetic patiently, and something is decided in the dark, and afterward everyone is extraordinarily kind to her for the rest of her life. That is the thing that would be hardest for an outsider to bear: there is no cruelty in it anywhere. It is all tenderness, conducted at four inches, by people who love her, who are correct.

The cord-children are not children. They are the small: adults kept small by measured feeding, some of them forty and fifty years old, chosen for lightness and for the shape of the shoulders, because the fissures are narrow and the well-trees go down thirty feet and someone has to go down with a copper knife. They are the most expensive people alive — they carry the town's fluid into a place where nothing can be measured or recovered — and they are indulged like idols and feared like weather. They marry each other. They have a lower dialect, learned in the acoustics of the cracks, where the stone speaks differently and certain surface words are physically impossible; and they have words for things down there that no surface ear has ever received, and they use them among themselves, and they will translate if you ask, and the translation is always slightly wrong. They come up smelling of the inside of the world. They say — the keepers write it down and file it — that the sap is not made down there. That it is kept. That there are pale things in the deep cracks that give, and go on giving, and are tired.

The keepers write: *instrument at fault.*

Because the notches on the cistern wall are closing, and have been closing for eleven hundred years, and the spacing is measured against the only other clock they have, which is the Veil's forty-year breath. And there is a problem with the drop-clock, which is that the minute is defined by the drops. Nine drops *is* a minute. If the Lip slows, the minute lengthens with it, and the flow per minute never changes, and the town's own timekeeping is incapable of noticing the thing that is happening. The whole intellectual history of this place is the reconciliation of two clocks that disagree by a very little. One faction says the Veil's breathing has drifted. One says the copper rule has worn. One says the notch-cutters' hands have changed, that people are smaller than they were, that a generation is shorter, that the closing is an artifact of the measuring and not of the world.

And there is a woman, thin-born, sixty, a counter by trade, who has begun lying on her back at noon on the highest roof and looking past the haze at the faint star that is still there when she looks again, and counting drops against *that*. She has thirty years of it. She has not published. She goes to the guild and taps a little of it and the guild goes quiet in the feet.

The dyers keep the other calendar, and they are the historians, and their archive is a rack of cloth. Reds go to rust here, greens go to black, and they work in eleven distinctions a lowlander would call one color, and the recipes change once in a generation as the Veil breathes, so a coat's color is a date. You can read a person's age by taking their sleeve. There are thick-born and thin-born, cohorts with grudges: the thick-born hoard, the thin-born remember what the light does and are extravagant about shade and stingy about everything else, and marriages across the cycle are still discussed. A genuinely new color arrives perhaps twice in a lifetime and causes a schism. There have been riots about a dye, conducted at whisper volume, in heaps, over four days, and settled by an accountant.

At the edge are the tether-men, who are the only ones who look out, and who are blamed for every loss, and who name the leathers individually and grieve them. The keepers have wanted the leathers killed for four hundred years — every leather is a vessel that leaves, and some of what leaves does not come back, and the loss can be entered in a column. The tether-men say the leathers carry the mats' seed from fissure to fissure, that without them the mats inbreed and fail, that the whole lush two inches of the world is downstream of a thing that cannot be measured. An unmeasurable service against a measurable loss. It is the same argument as the notches. It is the same argument as everything.

And at the very lip, where the wind is the only cold, there are the ones who put their ears to bedrock and tap.

The reasoning is sound. The slab is continuous, and the slab sits on the deep rock, and the deep rock does not stop at the edge — it goes down into the Veil and along, and the other tables are sitting on it too, dim and flat and small as coins. So the tap-code might travel. It might already be travelling. They have been sending for six generations: the location, the count, the flow, the number of people, the notch spacing. And they listen, in shifts, with their breath held, in the drone that everyone else has stopped hearing.

The tap-code has a fourth grammatical person. It is the form for *the one who is carrying none of us* — an addressee who shares no fluid, who is not in the inventory, who owes nothing and is owed nothing. It is taught to children as a curiosity, the way we teach the names of extinct animals, and it is used in exactly one place on the Table, at the edge, by people the town feeds and does not mock, because the town has decided that this is what the leathers are for.

Understand what the sin is, here. It is not theft; theft is recoverable, theft is a transfer, theft is entered. It is not violence; violence is only spillage and spillage is only ignorance. The one crime with no possible restitution is *promising more than exists*. A loan at interest is a claim on the future of the Lip. To say *there will be more* is to write against a sum that is closing, and to make someone act on it, and to leave them holding a hollow. Their word for a lie is a lid that does not fit. Their word for truth is *seated*: a stone in the exact hollow it will occupy until the sun goes out. And the future tense, in the deep grammar, in the oldest register that still carries in the north end, is not a tense at all. It is a mood of debt. You do not say *it will come*. You say *I am borrowing against its coming*, and the listener is entitled to ask what collateral, and everyone within reach of the floor can hear the answer.

They have children anyway. Licensed, few, and each one is a claim on the Lip made out loud in front of everybody, and the whole town underwrites it, and this is the only optimism the arithmetic permits and they permit it, and it is why the tenderness is not a mask over the accounting but the accounting itself, worn warm.

So: a small, wide-chested, veiled people with large soft ears and flat teeth and slow rust-colored blood, who smell each other constantly and cannot sweat and go down into their own history at noon to lie in a heap; who drink for eleven minutes cheek to cheek and know exactly whose grandmother they are carrying; who chew all day so the walls will hold; who are the kindest people in the world, and will not let you go over the edge, and will not let you have the child, and will hold your ear the entire time they explain it, patiently, in the dark, with the mouth close, while under the floor the number goes on being said, and is a little smaller than it was.
