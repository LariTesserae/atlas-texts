## The Residents

Begin in the seams, because that is where the population is.

The grit that collects between the polygons is not inert. It is a paste of basalt flour, sulfur, precipitated iron, and a film of something that has been alive, at a guess, since the Paleocene. Below the grit, in the pores of the basalt itself — hairline cracks, vesicles, the spaces between columns where the cooling flow shrank away from itself — there is a lining. It has no shape. It has no organs, no motion, no distinguishable individuals. It eats sulfur and iron out of the rock at thirty-eight degrees and divides, on average, once every hundred and forty years.

It is most of the biomass on the continent. It is also the first stage of the refinery. The water that goes down cracked does not come up loaded purely by chemistry; it comes up loaded because something in the walls has been prying the metals loose, patiently, for longer than there have been eyes anywhere. The leaching tanks are inhabited. They have always been inhabited. Every chimney on the tableland is a smokestack for a metabolism four hundred meters down, and the animals you can see are living on the exhaust of an audience that will never surface.

So the census — lichen, grove, plater, swift, tender, hunter — is a list of the exceptions. Six ways of being visible on a continent whose life is overwhelmingly a smear in a crack.

---

The wire-lichen is not a passive thing threading itself along a gradient. That is the first correction.

It plates its own filaments: nickel-iron, drawn out of the current, laid down in threads a tenth of a millimeter thick and sometimes forty meters long. And a filament, once grown, *lowers the resistance of the ground it lies on*. The lichen does not merely find the circuit. It extends it. It builds wire out into dry seams where no current has flowed in a year, at real metabolic cost, on the strength of nothing but a rising humidity and a faint shift in the four-hertz phase — a gamble that the rain is coming back this way.

Most of those gambles fail. Walk a dry valley and you can see them: grey threads running out across bare stone toward nothing, ending in a frayed tip, dead capital. The reticulum is therefore an archive. It is a map of where the water has been, drawn in metal by an organism that keeps betting on the last hundred rains. Read it and you can reconstruct the drift of the wet edge over a century, which is more than the rain itself remembers.

And when the wet comes and the patches flush copper from edge to edge in four hours, what you are watching is not a change of color. It is a liquidation. The lichen is dissolving its own wire — running the deposition backward, cashing a century of savings into soluble metal, spending it all on fruiting stalks no taller than a finger. The smell of a coin held too long in the mouth is the smell of a fortune going into the air. Then the stalks are grazed, and the spores go out on feet and in leakage, and the metal moves up into shells, and the lichen begins again from nothing, in the dark, on credit.

---

The terraces are a ladder, and each rung is a different country.

The top basin of a big chimney runs hot and acid and thick with dissolved iron; the tenth is cool enough to touch and nearly clear. Between them, a gradient of poison, and life has sorted itself along it with the fineness of a settling series. There are films in the top basin that nothing else on the plateau could survive. There are pale grazing things — sump-crawlers, flat as a fingernail, translucent enough that you can watch the film move through them — that live only on the fourth and fifth terraces and die in the third and die in the sixth.

Which means each chimney is an island, and each island has been an island for a very long time, and the flues are old enough to have accumulated endemics. Not many species per flue. Two or three, sometimes only one, and often found nowhere else in the field, let alone the continent. Ten thousand chimneys breathing in a valley and a substantial fraction of them carrying a fauna that exists in that pipe-work and nowhere in the universe.

When a flue falls, that fauna dies. Unless it goes down.

They go down. That is the part that takes a while to accept. The sump-crawlers, the films, the eggs — all of it washes into the cracked pavement with the overflow and enters the plumbing, and the plumbing is continuous, and somewhere in the dark warm water under the tableland there is a slow traffic of lineages riding the circulation from one vent field to the next. The chimney fields are not separate islands. They are the tops of one island, and the island is underground, and its animals commute through it on a schedule measured in decades.

The midges make the same trip in the other direction. Larvae in the hot upper basin, adults in the plume, mating in the thermal column at two hundred meters, and then falling — most of them back down the same throat, some of them onto a chimney eleven kilometers away, which is how anything new ever gets started here. Their entire lives are a loop through the sky and back into a pipe. The sky, for them, is a corridor between two holes.

---

The tube-groves are not trees, and they are also not colonies, exactly. Follow one and you find that the tubes come up along a fissure, always, and stop where the fissure stops. The organism is the fissure — or rather the organism is the thing living in the fissure, and the pale hollow cylinders are its fruiting bodies, its lungs, packed wall-thick with filtered sulfur until they get heavy and slump and break, and the fragments roll a few meters and root. That is the whole of their locomotion, and it is why they cluster and stop, cluster and stop, across so much empty stone.

Inside the flues themselves there is a lining. Call it throat-lace: a thin living film, sulfur-metabolizing, that deposits sinter evenly and dissolves what protrudes. It is the reason the throats are smooth. A hundred million years of the same gas at the same pressure would tune a pipe on its own, but not this well, and not this reliably; the laminar breath of the Anneal is partly a biological product, an animal maintaining a duct because a smooth duct is a better place to be an animal.

Which gives the fallen flue a cause. The lace sickens — a change in the water, a trace metal missing, a phage, nobody knows — and the throat roughens, and the note coarsens, and the flow trips into turbulence, and turbulence erodes, and eleven months later a hundred tonnes of iron pipe drops onto the pavement with a *whump* that carries sixty kilometers.

The swifts left six weeks ago. They heard it coming.

---

The flue-swifts have outsourced incubation to geology. They glue their eggs into a niche in the throat wall at the depth where the rock is thirty-eight degrees, and they do not brood, and they have not brooded for so long that the brood patch is gone from their bellies entirely. The whole of parental care, here, is site selection, and site selection is done by ear: a female works her way down a flue's inner wall listening to the note, and where the note is cleanest she lays.

They cannot land. Not on the pavement. There is no lift over flat warm stone away from a vent, and their legs are the vestigial hooks of things that have not stood anywhere in ten million years. A swift that comes down on the open tableland stays there. The rotating wheel over a chimney field is therefore not a flock. It is a cage with no walls, ten thousand birds inside a column of rising air, and the only exit is down.

They sleep at the top of it, in shifts, where the air against the cloud lid is genuinely cold — four kilometers up, the only cold anything on this continent will ever feel. They are the sole point of contact between the plateau and its own ceiling. Sometimes they come down with lid-water on their backs, and the mats get a few milliliters of sky.

And they fall. Steadily, thinly, all the time: a rain of small dead birds onto the hexagons, which the wire-lichen plates over within a season, so that a swift's last act is to become a component.

---

A plater's shell is a document.

The metal is deposited in bands, one band per wet, and the tempering colors — straw, bronze, blue-black — are oxide-film thicknesses, which is to say they are a direct record of the deposition potential, which is to say of the water chemistry of that particular rain. Count the bands and you have the animal's age in rains, which is the only age anything here has; sixty bands is a very old plater and something over seventy years. Read the bands and you have the weather: the thin dull season when the front passed a hundred kilometers north, the fat bright band of a year when it stalled overhead for eleven days, the anomalous green line that nobody can account for and that appears in every shell of that cohort across four hundred kilometers.

There is no other instrument on the Anneal that remembers anything. The platers are the recording medium, and they walk around, and they knock.

The chain is the second correction. It is a defense — belly flat, legs spread, shunt the signal into the stone, smear a hundred bodies into one vague broad nothing that no pit-array can resolve into prey. But a chain of touching platers is also a conductor, and current runs through it, and the current is modulated by every animal's feet: how wet, how much lichen, how much metal, which way the gradient leans. For the eight or twenty minutes that a chain holds, the herd is a single low-bandwidth instrument reading itself. The young are always placed in the middle. Nobody arranged this. It is simply where the young survive, and what they survive is an education: this is what a wet seam feels like, this is the taste of a good lick, this is north.

And the molt-drifts are money. Metal is the limiting nutrient on this continent — not carbon, not water in the end, but the trace metals the sulfides hand out unevenly — and a shed carapace is a concentrated bar of it, lying on the ground. Platers eat molts. There are things that live inside molts and eat nothing else. There are platers that follow other platers waiting for a shed, and platers that will hold a chain and then break it at the far end, where the noise of the break is masked.

A plater with a thin shell reads loud. Poverty, here, is audible.

---

The tenders leak on purpose. Not knowingly — nothing knows anything — but the leak is a gland, and the gland fires at the contact, and it has been shaped by selection into a fixed and reliable act, because the water that a tender leaves at a salt lick grows the copper flush, and the tender eats the flush. They are gardeners. They come to the same electrical contacts along the same routes for the same eleven liters, and they crop what their own leaking has raised, and the crop is better than anything the dry interior offers.

The routes are the point. A tender's heart beats once every eight cycles of the hum — thirty beats a minute, never once out of time — and its cistern is porous bone, remodeled every wet, and it can carry twelve liters across four hundred days of nothing. But it cannot find water. There is no water to find. What it can do is *remember where the rain has been*, and the memory is a route, and the route is taught, matriarch to calf, one lick to the next, across a hundred thousand junctions.

Which makes the herd the plateau's only memory of its own weather. The rain's circuit is irregular; it drifts; it occasionally abandons a loop it has kept for a century. And so there are lost lines: tenders walking a road their grandmothers walked, arriving at a contact that has been dry for thirty years, licking it, leaking on it, cropping nothing, and going on to the next dead station. They are not lost in the sense of being confused. They are lost in the sense of being correct about a world that has moved. Some lines edit themselves in time. Some walk the whole route once, and calve, and walk it again, and stop.

The ones that die at a lick stay there, and plate over, and become an electrode. A dead tender at a contact will close the circuit for thirty or forty years. The best gardens on the tableland are the graveyards, and they are the best gardens because they are the graveyards, and this is the sort of fact that the Anneal produces the way a refinery produces slag: continuously, indifferently, in enormous quantity.

---

The hunters do not have territories. They have a phase.

They belong to the wet edge — not to a valley or a field but to a band of weather two hundred kilometers wide, walking at the pace of a grazing animal, and a hunter's whole life is spent inside that band, keeping station in it, born in it, dying in it. Its home range is a moving object. It has never been dry.

The hunt takes nine hours. The pit array finds the dimple; the approach is a hundred meters an hour; the hunter must null itself as it comes, belly to the grit, bleeding its own signature into the stone until it is not a body but a slight coolness in the baseline. And then it does not bite. It clamps across the plater's carapace and reverses the polarity, and the prey's own shell becomes an anode, and dissolves, and the metal plates onto the hunter's back.

Galvanic predation. They eat the shell and the animal comes apart underneath it, and what is left of the meal is worn. An old hunter is armored in sixty tempers, straw and bronze and blue-black in overlapping fields, heavy, slow, and loud on the field — very loud, a walking distortion, which is why the largest thing that hunts a plater is another hunter.

Some fall out of the band. The front drifts faster than a sick animal walks, and it strands them in the dry, and they do the only thing available: they find a seam, and curl into it, and wait. A year is survivable. Two, sometimes. Sinter grows in seams. There are faces in the pavement out there, flat and wide as a shovel, walled in to the pits, mineralized to the color of the stone, and a few of them — a very few, and there is no way to know which — are still reading.

---

At the wet edge itself, for four days, there is a country that does not exist anywhere else.

Things hatch in the first hour of rain that have been eggs in the grit for four hundred days. They mate on the second day. They die as the ground steams dry on the seventh, and the whole aerial plankton of the front — midges, spores, cysts, motile films riding droplets — blooms and collapses inside a week and travels with the weather, and its members never in their lives touch dry stone.

And crossing them, going the other way, is the counter-flow: the dry spores, the armored cysts, the lichen's stubborn propagules drifting *backward* out of the wet against the front's motion, out into four hundred days of drought, to lie in the seams and wait for a rain that may not come to that particular hexagon for a decade. Two migrations passing through each other at the only moving border on the continent. One going forward with the water. One going back into the future.

---

The escarpment is a refuge, and it knows nothing about any of this.

Eleven hundred meters of seaward face, fog off the ocean, springs at every stratum contact, water that never stops. Hanging mats, black-green, meters thick, dripping. Lineages down there that were on the tableland once and were driven off it by the drought and have hung on the cliff for ten million years, wet the whole time, evolving slowly in the sound of the surf, unrelated now to anything above them. They are the plateau's exiles. There is more life on one kilometer of that face than on a hundred of the interior, and none of it can go home.

On the lip there is a small flying thing — call it a lid-reader — with a cup of upward-facing photoreceptors and nothing else worth calling an eye. It cannot see the ground, or a mate, or a hunter. It can see the paleness slide across the cloud ceiling, the shadow of the ground beneath the weather, four days out. And it emerges on that. It is the only animal on the Anneal that perceives the future, and the future it perceives is ninety-six hours long, and it belongs to a bug on a cliff edge that nothing else has ever noticed.

---

The census does not close. That is the last correction.

The wire-lichen is a reticulum with no boundaries; the grove is a fissure; the chain is briefly one animal; the wheel is ten thousand birds that cannot leave each other; the tender line is a route with bodies on it; the hunter is a phase of a weather system. Count them and you get numbers. The numbers are not the population. The population is a circuit diagram, and it changes when it rains, and the diagram is the only thing here that could be said to have a shape.

And then there is you, at the escarpment, with your palm flat on the pavement.

Thirty-seven degrees, eleven or twelve liters of water in a porous container, leaking continuously through the skin, salt-craving, walking. You are, functionally, a tender. Under your boots the grit is wetting, the resistance is falling, and in about four hours the wire-lichen will flush copper in the shape of your footprints. If a hunter is lying within a hundred meters it is reading a dimple shaped like nothing that has ever crossed this stone, and it will spend nine hours approaching it, and find nothing it can dissolve.

You will be gone by then. The flush will fade; the lichen will re-plate its wire; the seam will dry and open its circuit and go back to ticking. But the reticulum keeps its archive, and somewhere out on the flat warm hexagons, for a century, in metal, in the dark, there will be a shape that no rain made.

Nothing knows this. You included.
