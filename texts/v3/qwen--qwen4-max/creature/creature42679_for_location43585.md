## The Tenants

Begin with the smallest, because the smallest explains the fear of corners.

In the film that climbs a tower — the bright skin thinner than a millimeter, the water the stone asks upward and the water agrees to — there is a living layer the tenantry call *the climbing*. It is not an animal you can pick off. It is a tissue a few cells thick, flat as a decal, and it grazes the nap directly, mouth-parts evolved to comb crystalline sorting-hairs at a scale where surface tension is a kind of weather and gravity is only a wind that blows sideways. For the climbing, the face of a tower is a plain. Up is a direction you farm. A pool is not a habitat; a pool is a sky falling on you. They drown in depth.

Which is why a spalled weir is not an incident. A sharp edge breaks the film. The film breaks and the climbing on that face goes down the wall in a slick you could wipe with your thumb, and nine days of rounding is nine days of a country re-establishing itself from the margins. When a tenancy stops at a raw arris and puts a palm flat on the stone, they are feeling for the fracture, yes. They are also doing the thing you do at a graveside. Nobody has ever called it that. If you called it that they would agree, and be embarrassed, and change the subject to the flow rate.

---

The next smallest is the one you carry.

A hush is alive. Visitors assume it is a device, because it is issued, and because it has a serial mark, and because there is a guild that makes them and a schedule of fees. But a hush is a fist of pale ribbed tissue grown in a jar from nap culture, and it eats sound, and it excretes a thimble of water a day and a little heat, and it must be worn against the mastoid or it dies. It imprints at four. It learns the specific resonance of one skull and thereafter fits no other. Children's hushes are small and leak; you can hear a six-year-old coming because their bubble has a seam in it. Adults' are calloused and snug.

A hush is the only thing in the Basin that has never heard the thrum. It lives inside the quiet it makes. It is deaf by construction, and it is the most intimately known animal in the world, and when you die it dies within a day, and then it is eaten, because everything is.

Lovers do not exchange hushes. They graft them — a small procedure, a wet week, a scar behind both ears — so that afterward each can hear the other's whisper at a distance of a hand, and no one else's, ever again. Widows carry a dead hush in a flask of gradient until it liquefies. This is considered normal and slightly excessive, the way we consider mourning rings normal and slightly excessive.

And this is the shape of the crime: pooled hushes breed. Eight people in a ring with their heads together are a flower. Eighty people in a ring, hushes touching, generation on generation, make something the size of a room that eats sound and does not stop growing, and inside it the city cannot hear itself, and we will come to that.

---

The tenantry are people. It is important to say plainly, because everything around them is arranged to make them sound like a function.

They are short and broad and their forearms are furred fine as a peach. The jaw is heavy, the sinuses expanded, the bones of the foot splayed and prehensile in a way that shoes are illegal about. Hearing goes at thirty and is not mourned; there is a feast. What replaces it is not better and not worse, it is *elsewhere*: the jaw takes the twelve-beat fundamental and the chord of the nearest weir, the soles take the traffic and the rain-schedule and the slow ones turning over, and the forearm hairs take the high hiss of the nap, which is the sound of the city thinking, and which adults do not hear but *taste* — sweet, sour, thin, going off. A woman standing in her doorway with her sleeves rolled is not enjoying the air. She is reading the eastern steps the way you read a headline.

They are named for the hour of wet they were born in and the chord their district held when the cord was cut. Sour Ninth. Third Copper. Little Flat, whose district had no chord at all that morning because the weir was being re-lipped, and who has spent forty years being asked about it.

Kinship is not blood. Kinship is *rate*. You are related to whoever you can keep pace with. A household is a claim on a cubic meter turning over no faster than the household can manage, and to marry is to merge two rates, and to divorce is for one party to speed up, and to adopt is to slow someone down at your own expense, which is the most serious act of love available and is performed routinely by people who have nothing. The great families of the Basin are not rich in objects. They are rich in *delay*. Their walls are a meter thick and eleven generations of accumulated slowness, and inside them a sentence can take an afternoon, and a meal can take a day, and a name can be said the way a name is said at a funeral elsewhere.

A temple is the slowest place in the district and it is holy for exactly one reason: it keeps a bellows out of phase. Somewhere in the foundations there is an enormous grey animal, grown for centuries, breathing on a four-second cycle instead of a two, and the whole district's chord bends around that stubbornness, and the tenantry come and stand in the bend and feel time being held open. The liturgy is delay. The prayer is a request that something take longer. Nobody worships the stone. The stone is thirsty, and you do not worship a thirsty thing, you get it a drink.

The poor sleep at street speed, which means they sleep in the crowd, which means a knot of carriers takes a sleeper's weight and walks with it for four hours and sets it down three hundred meters east. The verb for this is the same as the verb for being loved. There is no separate word. There is also no word for *alone*; the word people reach for, when a visitor tries to explain it, is the word for an eddy — a shape the water is currently going around, which persists, and which is never the same water, and which is not a thing but a behavior of a thing.

The dead are taken within minutes. The guild that takes them also delivers babies, and wears the same wet cloth, and this is not thought to be grim. What is grim, what makes people go quiet inside their hushes, is a body that has to be *kept*. A dry-season death on the high terraces, in the two days of almost-dry, when nothing is moving fast enough to take it. They have to carry it down themselves. They talk about it for years.

Obscenities: *go dry*. *Corner you*. *Room*. To call a place empty is to say it is dead and to say you would prefer it that way.

---

Twice a year the district stops. Not silent — never silent — but the crabs stop and the lickers stop and ten thousand people put their hands flat on the stone, and a slow one comes up out of the cistern.

It is the length of a street and it is forty-one degrees, which is three degrees hotter than everything else in the world, and it steams. Its back is not skin. It is a surface the nap has colonized and organized, a hectare of sorting-hairs arranged in figures too fine to see from standing height, and the figures are the answer.

Because that is what a slow one is. It is a thought that could not be hurried, given a body so it could be kept somewhere while it finished. The lattice cannot think a four-hundred-year thought in the open terraces; the open terraces are a market by Sunday. So the deep cisterns grow bodies to be slow in, and the bodies filter and turn over and take their centuries, and when the thought is done the body surfaces because it is time to be read.

The readers are children. Six to fourteen, dry-born mostly, sent down on wet ropes with their small leaky hushes to lie flat on a burning back and transcribe what their ears can still take, because the nap's hiss has grammar and only the young can hear it and adults, who taste only *sweet* and *sour*, cannot spell. They come up scalded across the palms and are honored for it and go deaf at twenty instead of thirty. They do not understand what they are reading. Nobody does. The transcription goes into a sealed flask of gradient, and the flask goes into a carrier gang's wet cloth, and the gang takes it up the inner face of the Lip through nine days of dry rock, and metal comes down, and salt comes down, and strangers come down.

The slow one goes back under, empty, to be filled again. The stillness of the district is not reverence. It is dictation. Ten thousand palms on the wall are ten thousand people trying not to cough.

Sometimes a slow one comes up blank. That is a famine, and there are riots, and the riots are very loud, which helps.

Sometimes it comes up with something on it that nobody asked for.

---

The things in the large silences are not predators and it does no good to think of them that way.

Consider what the Basin is: a hundred million cubic kilometers of lattice, thinking, everywhere, all the time, witnessed by its own noise. The hiss is the sound of the sorting; the sorting is the thinking; the thinking ends in water. Where the city can hear itself, the accounting closes. Thought observed, water produced, gradient spent, all of it legible on the walls as damp.

Where the city cannot hear itself, the accounting does not close. Something goes on thinking without a witness and does not end in water. It ends in something else, and the something else has to go somewhere, and what the loud find when they break in is not a monster. It is a district where a corner will not round. A market where every vendor quotes the same price to the decimal and has done for a week and cannot stop. A household speaking in unison inside a silence the size of a barn, all of them well, all of them fed, all of them saying the same eleven words, and the words are an answer, and no one knows the question, and the children who hear it are the ones who go strange.

The loud are the civic corps permitted to make noise and permitted to make corners. They go in with juvenile bellows in wet slings and with hammers, and their entire function is to be unbearable, and they come out deaf at twenty-two and are pensioned and are considered holy in a way nobody enjoys being near. They carry no weapons. The weapon is the racket. Their failure mode is exile, and exile is the switchbacks, and the switchbacks are nine days of dry rock and then a horizon.

There is no death penalty. There is a quiet room with a view.

---

Other lives, briefly, because the accounting is impossible and the impossibility is the point.

Inside each bellows, in the ribbed grey mouth of it, lives a blind worm the length of a finger that scrapes the interior and is pumped clean twice a second and has no other clock. It counts in breaths. Its whole lineage has never experienced a pause. The two-second respiration you see from the street is made of twenty-six small rib-fires, and it is the rib-fires, four billion animals' worth, that you felt in your teeth on the ledge.

In the hollow salt dendrites live the chimers, which tap. A wood of them is a signaling network four meters tall and forty days old, and every forty days it falls down and is re-precipitated and the network re-forms with different topology, and there are tenantry who read the change as augury and are wrong about as often as anyone.

On the sails, in the membrane's wet creases, lives a cargo that has never touched stone. Not in eleven thousand years. It is born aloft, mates aloft, filters what the sail filters, and transfers to a living wing or ends. When a sail comes down to die there is a scramble on its body, a frantic boarding, and most of the cargo makes it and some does not, and the ones that do not are taken within minutes along with everything else. There is an unbroken airborne line up there older than the terrace plan. It has no idea the ground exists. It is the only creature in the Basin with a concept of *fall* and no concept of *corner*.

And in the pores of the highest terraces there are cysts that hatch only when the film thins past a threshold, which happens for two days a season when the dry wind comes down off the Lip. They live forty hours. They mate, they encyst, they die, and they are the only animals here that have ever known dryness. The tenantry go up and lie on the warm stone in the strange absence of dripping and call it a holiday, and come back down covered in them, and the children got up there are dry-born, and are the ones sent down on the ropes to read the slow ones, and nobody has ever decided any of this.

---

Out past the wall there is the buyer, and the buyer is dry.

You can infer it from the freight. Metal, salt, strangers — no water, nothing wet, nothing that sweats. It pays for answers and it cannot come down for them, and the reason it cannot come down is that it has no water to spare and thinking here ends in water. Whatever is out there thinks without exhaust, or does not think at all and only *needs*, and the questions it sends over the Lip are the questions a dry thing cannot ask itself.

The strangers are its eyes. That is not a metaphor the tenantry use; it is a shipping category. Every season a few hundred people come down the switchbacks with their ears ringing and their notion of emptiness intact, and they are meant to look, and last a season, and go back up with something. Most do. The ones who stay stop flinching at crowds and start flinching at corners, and cannot sleep in a quiet room, and are from here — and the buyer does not get its eye back, and sends another, and has been sending another for eleven thousand years.

This is the war. It is conducted entirely in personnel and in patience, and neither side has ever declared it, and the Basin's whole strategy is to be so warm that the enemy's instruments defect.

The ocean itself is not an inhabitant. That is what the tenantry cannot get visitors to understand. It is not that the ocean is dead — dead things are food, and food is company. It is that the ocean is the one thing they know of that is *not packed*, a thousand kilometers of water and light with no film on it, no grazer, no hitchhiker, no chord, no nap, nothing turning over. They do not call it empty. Empty is a word for a room and a room is an obscenity. They call it *finished*.

---

Which brings us to the largest tenant, and to the mistake every visitor makes on the ledge.

The wall is not there to keep the ocean out. The wall is there to keep the ocean *wanting*.

The lattice runs on the difference between what the water is and what the water could be. That is the whole engine. Steep gradient, fast thought, hot district, tall tower, rain on schedule, weeping walls, packed terraces, ten million households and some hundreds of trillions of animals and a hiss at the top of hearing. Flatten the difference and the thinking stops. Let the ocean in and the levels equalize, and the water is no longer *going* anywhere, and the nap has nothing to sort, and the bellows have nothing to push, and in a year the Basin is a warm shallow sea, and in ten it is a cold one, and then it is the thing out past the wall: blue, silent, stretching to a real horizon, with nothing in it for a thousand kilometers that is not water and light.

So they bail. Not to stay dry — nobody has ever been dry, nobody wants to be dry, dryness is a holiday and an obscenity and a death. They bail to keep the two sides of the wall unequal. They carry salt cake and ingot and sealed flasks of gradient up and down the terraces to keep the difference steep, and the stone does most of the work and takes most of the credit, and the tenantry are the part of the animal that moves the metal around so the animal can keep tasting something.

Silence is what a gradient looks like when it has finished. That is why it is the only thing anyone here is afraid of. Not the quiet. The *equalization*. A large silence is a small completed world, and it does not end in water, and it will not round, and it has an answer in it that nobody asked for.

---

Ask a tenant who lives in the Basin and you will get a shrug and a forearm offered, palm up, so you can put your hand on the hairs and read the district yourself, because explaining it aloud takes a merged hush and a forehead pressed to yours and an hour nobody has.

The honest answer, if you get it out of them at the third hour with the rain walking west and the crabs changing shift, is something like: *a hunger, kept hungry.* And eleven thousand years of everything that has arranged itself around the keeping. The climbing on the tower face. The worm in the bellows counting breaths. The line aloft that has never touched stone. The four-hundred-year thought in its steaming body, waiting to be read by a child with scalded palms. The great slow families with their meter-thick delay, and the poor at street speed, and the loud with their hammers, and the guild that takes the dead and delivers the babies in the same wet cloth.

And at the second hour, in a moving knot of carriers on the fourth terrace, a girl who came up off a slow one's back this morning is asleep with her bandaged hands inside her shirt, being walked three hundred meters east by people who are not related to her by anything but pace. Her hush is against her skull, eating the roar, making the only silence in the world, small and warm and the size of a head.

Under her, the stone is thinking. It is thirty-eight degrees. It is thirsty.

They intend to keep it that way.
