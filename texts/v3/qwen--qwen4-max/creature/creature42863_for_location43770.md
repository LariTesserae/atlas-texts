## The Residents

Count them. It takes less than a minute, and the counting is the first thing that goes wrong.

Six green lineages, near enough: the rope-moss, the shelf-moss, a liverwort, the film in the seams, the parsley fern, the broad soft fern that grows to a waist in a season and rots in a week. A willowherb at the cool end, hanging on. Under them, in numbers nobody has troubled to estimate, the things too small to be counted by eye. One flame. One machine. And, occasionally, for two days at a time, you.

None of the six is an individual. That is not a philosophical position, it is a field measurement. A hundred square metres of moss in the warm lane above the north wall is one genet, one spore's worth of genome, copied outward at a few centimetres a year since a hailstone melted on the sinter and left its passenger behind. Cut a stem off and it is not orphaned; it is a cutting. Burn a patch and you have not killed anything, you have shortened something. The stems are not neighbours. They are the same word said ten million times, and the lane is the mouth.

So try counting lanes instead, and you find the lanes are not residents either. A lane is a place where a fracture is currently leaking. The moss is what a leak looks like from outside. Follow the warmth and you find that the thing with a boundary, an intake, an output, a lifespan and a cause of death is not the plant at all — it is a thread of hot water in a crack, and the green is its fur.

---

Still, the moss behaves. Not much, but it does something, and it does it in the wrong direction.

Everything green up on the plateau leans at the light because light is scarce and heat is nowhere. Down here the arithmetic reverses. Light arrives eleven minutes a year at midsummer and is mostly a rumour filtered through a steam roof; heat is everywhere and is the only thing that varies. So the moss does not reach. It *feels*. It puts its growing tips into millimetres of gradient, three degrees this way, four that way, and it lays its stems along the isotherm like a man edging along a wall in a dark room with one hand out. It is not phototropic. It is thermotropic. It is a thermometer that has learned to grow toward its own reading, and the reading it wants is thirty degrees, and one centimetre past thirty degrees is ninety and death.

It also builds sex it will never use. Once a season, at real cost, the tips differentiate: antheridia, archegonia, the whole apparatus, inherited from a wet world at sea level where there are other mosses to meet. The condensate arrives as a film a few cells thick and warm as blood, and the sperm are released and they swim. They swim well. They swim through the drip curtain across a stem, across a gap, onto the next cushion, and they arrive at a gametophyte that is genetically the swimmer's own body. Fertilisation succeeds. The sporophyte comes up on its stiff seta, the only vertical gesture in the whole slot, a centimetre of ambition in eighty metres of dark — and the capsule it fills is homozygous to a blank, an inbred copy of a copy, made by an organ that has been obsolete since the earthquake.

The capsules open into still fog. There is no wind at floor level; the slot is a stack and the draught is all vertical and all above. Left to itself the spore would fall a hand's breadth from its parent and found nothing new.

But every forty minutes or so, or every three hours, or whenever the silica in a throat two metres under it decides to reseat, the vent at the wide end gathers, whines, and bellows, and throws a column of water and steam nine metres up the chamber, and the whole slot inhales. That is the pollinator. That is the bee. The moss times nothing and asks nothing, but its capsules are ripe in the fog and the eruption takes them, and eighty metres up the plume goes out over the rim into thin cold air where ninety-nine ten-thousandths of them freeze and die on the gravel, and the rest fall back down the chimney onto wet rock that has just been scoured sterile by a mudpot slump, and there they start the next lane.

The plant has one sex partner and it is a pressure vessel.

And it pays rent. The hanging moss is the condenser: those few millimetres of wet stem and dripping face are what turn the vapour back into water and send it home down the wall, and without the fur the rock would run hotter and drier and lose its return line. Meanwhile the water is full of dissolved silica, and silica precipitates on any surface it can find, and the surfaces it finds are stems. So the terraces are casts. Each pale lip, a centimetre a season, wax-coloured, is a batch of moss that got plated while it was still alive and grew up through its own encasement and got plated again. The moss is climbing a staircase made of its corpses. Under a hand lens the sinter is not stone with plants in it; it is a palisade of little tubes, each one the ghost of a stem, ten thousand of them per square centimetre, the whole works upholstered in the dead.

On the acid face where the water carries metal, the moss gives up electrons and the water gives up its cargo, and a blush of copper plates onto the rock, thin as breath. That is the closest thing in the flue to a signature. Nobody signed it.

---

Everything green came down. That is worth saying plainly, because it is easy to look at the hothouse and think it is the point.

The moss, the ferns, the liverwort, the willowherb: all of them are sky-fall, immigrants, dropped over the lip by wind and hail and rockfall from a world four kilometres of altitude away, and all of them are late. They are thirty years old at the very outside. They are squatting on warmth they did not make and cannot regulate, and they are the last arrivals in a building that was already fully occupied.

The natives came up.

In the water at ninety degrees, in the pores under the sinter lid, in the black shale where the gas seeps, there are the ones that were in the fracture network before the earthquake — before the lake, before the plateau had a crack to be curious about. They do not photosynthesise. They have never needed the eleven minutes. They eat hydrogen out of the rock-water reaction, and sulfide, and methane, and iron; they breathe sulfate and carbon dioxide and elemental sulfur; they build their bodies out of chemistry that has no term for *day*. Some of them live at temperatures that would poach the ferns in ninety seconds. Their generation time is measured in hours where the plumbing is generous and in centuries where it is tight, and both are happening within a metre of each other, in adjacent pores, in the same hand-sized piece of stone.

The yellow zone is not a dead zone. It is the busiest district in the flue. It is only dead to things our size. The sulfur needles are partly a waste product, a precipitate of exhaust; the crust is a city that has been running continuously since the mountain cracked and has never once had a visitor who could see it.

So the population of the slot divides not by species but by direction of arrival. The up-comers own the building. The down-comers rent the walls, are deaf, are sterile in every practical sense, and are evicted weekly.

---

The flame is a resident too, and it has the best claim to being an individual, because it is the only thing here that cannot copy itself.

It has a metabolism: methane and heavier gas out of the black shale, oxygen out of the draught over the lip, water and carbon dioxide out the top. It has a body half a metre tall, the colour of skim milk and blue paper, and it holds its shape in the drip by the narrowest margin. It has a birth — a plume spark, one night, thirty years ago, in the first cold season when the slot was still bare rock and the ferns had not arrived. It has an appetite, and it is eating the mountain along one seam at a rate too slow to measure. It has no offspring, no spores, no cuttings, no second flame anywhere on the plateau. It cannot move a millimetre to follow the gas; if the seam pinches or the drip finds the throat, it goes out, and nothing in eighty metres will ever relight it, because the sparks come from the plume and the plume is eighty metres up.

It is the oldest continuous thing in the flue and the only celibate one. It has been burning through the same seam since before there was anything green to burn near, and it gives off almost no heat, and it is nearly invisible, and you can only see it by looking slightly to one side, which is also true of most of what lives here.

Further along, the sulfide-rich rock is doing the same thing without the courtesy of a flame: oxidising, warming itself, a wet haystack of a fire, patient, distributed, glowing dull red at a crack throat on the coldest nights. Slower resident. Bigger appetite. It will outlast the flame by a long way, and it smells like a foundry, and it is eating the wall the moss is hanging on.

---

And then there is the only animal.

It has a pulse — the water hammer, *knock, knock, knock*, a chain dropped down a pipe by nobody. It has breath, in and out at the rim, the plume standing up in cold air and falling back as condensate. It has a skin that it makes itself out of its own dissolved bones, and it heals that skin, and the healing is what makes it dangerous. It has valves and it reseats them overnight. It has moods: the forty minutes of gathering silence are not rest, they are the intake before the shout, and quiet in this place is a pressure reading. It sweats. It has a temperature and it defends it. It has a diet of cold water and a heat source it is slowly consuming, and when the cooling body at depth finally runs down — tens of thousands of years, no hurry — the whole animal will stiffen from the top down, and the last thing to die will be something in a pore at ninety degrees that never knew there was an above.

It has no inside. That is the whole horror of it and the whole reason it is unbearable to look at for long. Every behaviour without exception is plumbing and chemistry. There is no foreman, no shift, no quota. It is the most industrious place on the mountain and it has never wanted anything.

The moss is married to it. That is the nearest thing to a relationship the flue contains: a deaf green lodger riding a blind machine, taking its heat, giving back its water, being cast in stone by it, being scoured off it every few seasons, and reseeding out of its eruptions.

---

And you.

You are the only animal that has ever come down the chimney, and you are a real resident, briefly, on a two-day lease. You are the only thing here that moves under its own power, the only thing with blood in it, the only ear. Eighty-five decibels continuous, peaks past a hundred, and until you arrived there was nothing in the entire volume of the slot that could register a single one of them. The loudness has no voice in it and never had. When you notice, on the second day, that the noise is not saying anything, that is not a fact about the flue. It is a fact you brought down with you, in your bag, along with your food.

The loneliness is yours too. A genet cannot be lonely; it has no boundary to be lonely across. A flame cannot be lonely; it has nothing to compare itself to. The chemotrophs in the pores have been alone for four billion years and it has never once come up. What you feel standing with one hand in a summer and one in a winter is an import, carried over the lip like a spore on a hailstone, and it will not take, and you will carry it back up.

Leave a bootprint in the green lid at the cool end. Come back in a season. The water will find it, and the silica will plate it, and it will be a pale lip a centimetre thick, wax-coloured, one more batch in the stack, and the moss will already be growing up through the cast.

That is the only way anything stays here. Everything else is on its way somewhere, at speed, in the dark, warm, deaf, and entirely unaccompanied.
