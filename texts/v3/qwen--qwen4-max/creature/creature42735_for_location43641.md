## What lives in the Bell

Start with the mistake the noise invites. Four hundred thousand turning discs, meshed rim to rim, a wall of gearwork sixty meters long: the ear reports a factory, and the eye agrees, and both are wrong about the central thing. The wheels are not machinery and they are not nets. There is nothing in that water to strain. It is optically empty; a net in it would catch nothing but the room's own dandruff, which is in fact what the combs catch, incidentally, as a sideline. The wheel is a lung.

Behind each disc, hidden in the socket it grew, sits the animal proper: a gray fist of tissue about the size of a thumb's last joint, six grams, no eyes, no face to speak of, a folded gill packed with sulfide-oxidizing bacteria it has farmed since it was a larva. The gill wants a current. The animal sits in a hole in a wall, and in still water it would smother in its own exhaust inside an hour. So it grows a shaft, seats it, and grows a bladed disc onto the shaft, and the torus turns the disc, and the disc pulls water through the socket and over the garden in the gill. That is the whole trick. The combs are cut on a bias because they are turbine blades. The whir coming up the shaft is four hundred thousand animals fanning themselves, and the reason it never stops is that none of them can afford to breathe less.

Everything else in the room follows from that one arrangement, the way a town follows from a bridge.

---

The animal is not the wheel, and this matters to it. The wheel is shed mineral, dead as a fingernail, and it wears out: the floor grit is fresh-milled glass and it circulates, and bearings score, and combs chip. A body may live two hundred and twenty years and wear twenty lungs. What it keeps, what it defends, what it never leaves, is the socket — and the socket it did not make. Larvae are heavy and drop; they settle in the low corner and are carried, some of them, back up the wall in the mucus on a crab's carapace. The ones that land in an occupied socket die. The ones that land in a dead one cement themselves to the lip and grow their new shaft into the inherited bearing, which has been lapped smooth by nine hundred years of grandparents.

So the nearest thing to an individual here is an address. Tier eleven, four meters in from the notch, third socket from the seam: that has been continuously inhabited since before the drifts had their first band. It has had ninety-odd tenants. None of them knew it. The frame that looks like a gantry is a genealogy, and the genealogy is also a landlord, and the rent is a wheel.

The animal's entire behavioral repertoire is one gesture. Every forty seconds, a shudder runs through the fist and milks a gland at the base of the shaft, and a bead of mucus enters the bearing. That is it. That is the twitch, the courtship, the defense, the opinion. Spawning is a slower version of the same motion. Fear is a faster one. When the vent stutters and the flow slumps, four hundred thousand animals twitch at once and the sound of it comes up the shaft as a single soft click, like a building settling, and then the whir resumes a fraction lower and nobody notices.

---

There is almost nothing to eat, so the room eats itself.

The mats make the first sugar — a millimeter of gray-white silica nap on the floor, chemosynthetic, cold, the only ground cover for a hundred kilometers. The loom makes the current: kilometers of bacterial filament threaded through the wall cracks, sulfide below, oxidized water above, six tenths of a volt across the height of the room, a battery whose electrodes are the animals' own excrement. Wheel-stock void their surplus iron as half-gram billets of pyrite, a hundred billion of them, identical because the animals are identical, and the torus rolls them downslope into a heap that is a tailings pile and a power supply and has never been intended by anything.

Above that primary layer, the entire remaining fauna is a recycling economy. Mucus flakes, shed combs, dead larvae, glass shavings, the sloughed nap of the mats, corpses. Nothing here hunts for calories except by exception. Mostly things here wait for the room to shed.

And some of them skip the middleman. The electrovores have a mouthpart at one end and a gill at the other and nothing much in between: a two-meter thread of a body stretched diagonally up a fissure, mouth clamped to a loom filament in the reducing dark, gill fanned in the oxidized flow above, drawing current straight across its own length. It has to be two meters. The gradient is what it is; a shorter animal cannot get a usable drop. So its body plan is set by Ohm's law and it grows a millimeter a year and it cannot move without unplugging, and it does not unplug. There are electrovores in the ceiling cracks that have been holding the same two contacts since before the last reversal. They are the cheapest lives in the Bell. Most of their body is a resistor.

---

The spindle-crabs are the only things here with anything like errands.

They work the drifts — six meters of used parts in the low corner, sorted by size grade because the current sorts everything — and they eat the shavings, which are glass, which wears their guts out, which is why they live thirty years and not three hundred. They test shafts. A crab picks up a shaft, seats it against a plate on its own thorax, spins it with a leg, and reads the ring through its skeleton. A flawed shaft rings wrong. It gets dropped. A good one gets carried up the wall and wedged into a crack, because the delivery reflex has no completion condition: the crab will offer a shaft to an occupied socket, find no vacancy, and stash it nearby, and the cracks of the Bell are packed with a hoard of perfectly good spares that nobody will ever come back for. It is a library assembled by an alphabet that cannot read.

The crabs also, incidentally, staff the recruitment of the entire upper wall, because larvae stick to them and fall off at height. The racks are thirty meters tall because that is how high a crab bothers to climb.

And they cross the light at night. The lit cone in the middle of the floor is scoured sterile — ultraviolet at that altitude comes down the shaft like a tool — and a crab in it cooks in twenty minutes. So they wait at the shadow line with a pigment spot on the dorsal side telling them which way is away, and when the spot goes null they run the crossing, four meters of open floor, to the far drift. There is a night shift in the Bell. It has a timetable. The torus runs eleven hours and the sky runs twenty-four, and every two hundred and sixty-four hours the two come back into phase and the dark falls at the moment the current is slackest, and the crossing is easy, and the floor is crowded, and eleven days later it happens again. Nothing celebrates. It is simply the good shift.

---

The brake does not need any of this to work.

It is flat and patient and it carries a blade of glass it selected as a juvenile from the drifts, gauged against its own body, the only tool in the room. It finds a wheel, inserts the blade into the combs, and sits on the stall. Then it waits for the tenant to starve, which takes four to seven months, because the animal behind a stalled wheel is an animal that cannot breathe. The brake's metabolism is low enough that this is not a hardship. Sometimes it dies on the wheel first and the crabs eat it. Sometimes it eats and spawns on the same socket and its offspring wedge themselves into the neighboring combs, and a whole tier goes quiet in a season, and the wall grows around the silence.

The brake is the one lineage that does well out of catastrophe, and it has organized its reproduction accordingly. It spawns in the quiet. Not in the noise — the noise is famine, four hundred thousand healthy lungs and no vacancies. When the whir dies, when the wall stops, the brakes release everything they have at once, and their larvae are the only larvae in the Bell that swim upward on purpose.

---

The couriers never stop, and cannot.

Eel-shaped, a meter of muscle with a gut in it, gills that need ram flow: they ride the torus at the speed of a walking man and they sleep one half of the body at a time, and if the vent stutters badly enough that the flow slumps below their stall speed they suffocate in minutes. They are the only animals here that can be killed by stillness. Everything else in the room is built to survive a stop. The couriers are built on the assumption that there will never be one, and there has never been one, for nine hundred years, except for the three or four times there was.

They carry larvae in a ventral groove, wall to wall, and release them at the upwelling because the shear there triggers a reflex they do not have opinions about. They are the conveyance, the post, the only reason anything on the far wall is related to anything on the near one. During a reversal they ride empty for years. There is nothing to carry. They keep going.

---

At the ceiling, the blister.

It is a silver dome of trapped gas the size of a dinner table, older than the room's occupancy: carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, a trace of hydrogen, the mountain's own exhale. No oxygen in it at all. The animals hanging upside down in its fringe are not breathing. They are hiding.

They are obligate anaerobes, the oldest lineage in the Bell, and oxygen is a poison to them — burns their membranes, kills them in an hour. The rest of the room is full of it, because the shaft has a free water surface thirty meters up, nine meters across, open to the sky, and cold water takes air greedily, and the torus carries the dissolved sky down and around and over every wall. The Bell breathes through a mouth it has never seen. The blister is the one place the sky does not reach: the last cubic decimeter of the world as it was before the hole opened, and its citizens hang in it by their tails and graze the sulfur film on the meniscus and cannot leave, ever, in any direction.

The blister is dissolving. Oxygen eats it from below, a few microns a year. It was larger when the drifts were shallow. And it shrinks fastest during a reversal, when four hundred thousand lungs stop drawing and the oxygen nobody is using climbs — so the quiet that makes the brakes rich thins the last pocket of the old air, and each dynasty inherits a slightly smaller ceiling. There have been fringe animals in this room since the water first stood in it. There is no mechanism by which they can be saved and no mechanism by which they can know it.

The sun, meanwhile, does work here, though nothing has ever taken a calorie from it. The torus drags every corpse, every shed wheel, every flake of mucus through the lit cone, and the ultraviolet sterilizes it, and that is why a room with four hundred thousand animals in it and no outflow but a notch has never fouled. The light is not the power supply. The light is the janitor. It has never been introduced to anyone.

---

Now the part the sound is actually telling.

Every generation of wheel-stock throws a fraction of wrong-handed larvae — combs cut on the opposite bias, one in some thousands. In a room turning the way this room turns, they are invalids. Their blades fight the flow. They run hot, they shed mucus, they score their shafts, they knock. They get the bad berths: the eddy at the notch, the scoured margin of the cone, the seam that dries. They die young and they die loudly. That hail of small irregular failures under the hiss, thousands of separate ticks at thousands of rates — that is the sound of the sick, and it is also the sound of the next dynasty, and it is the same sound.

Because every two hundred and thirty years the vent walks a few centimeters along its fissure and the torus reverses, and then every correctly cut blade in the room becomes a brake, and the wall stalls tier by tier over about eleven weeks, and the animals behind the wheels smother in their own exhaust, and the hiss thins to a rattle and the rattle to scattered ticks and the ticks to the fundamental alone: the shove of the vent in the sternum, and the chair-sized bubble every hour booming up the shaft, and the bell singing its one clean note for four seconds into a room where nobody is fanning.

The brakes spawn. The crabs feast. The couriers ride empty. The loom browns out for decades and grows into different cracks. The blister thins. And the wrong-handed, in their bad berths, discover that they have been right all along, and inherit the sockets, and grow their wheels onto the inherited axles, and over about forty years the whir comes back up to pitch, mirrored.

The drifts take a band. Right, left, right, left: four dynasties, six meters, each one a mass grave of perfectly good parts that no longer fit anything.

---

There are eleven governors, and they are not a caste. They are survivors.

A wheel a meter and a half across turns once a minute, and at one revolution a minute a reversed blade pitch does not tear itself apart; it merely runs badly. So when the last reversal came, the largest and slowest of the old regime's wrong-handed did not die. They were too heavy to be hurt by being wrong. They are still there, eleven of them, spinning the old hand in the new current, leaking mucus, running hot, always a little hungry, knocking once a minute in eleven irregular voices under the hiss — the deepest, slowest ticks in the room. Their bodies are four hundred, six hundred, in one case perhaps nine hundred years old. They are the only animals in the Bell older than the wall they are attached to. You can read the previous dynasty on them the way you read it in the drifts, except that these bands are still breathing.

And because they are heavy, they coast. When the vent stutters and four hundred thousand lungs stop pulling and the torus begins to slump, eleven flywheels keep turning and drive the water honestly for hours, and everything downstream of them survives a bad decade. That is what governors are: the mistakes of a dead regime, still turning, holding the flow steady for their successors. There were nineteen after the reversal before last. There were more before that. The number only goes down, because there is no way to make more of them except by being large and wrong at the exact right moment, and one of these centuries a reversal will leave none, and then the first bad decade after that will take the whole wall.

---

So there are four things in the Bell that remember, and none of them recalls anything.

The drifts remember as stratigraphy: bands of handedness, perfectly good parts, no reader.

The loom remembers as geometry. It has no hand and does not die at a reversal; it only feels the load fall and grow back, and it grows toward load, and nine centuries of etching have left a branching diagram in the fissures that is a precise fossil record of where the wall used to be hungry. It is the only continuously living thing here that has been in all four dynasties. Its shape is its memory. It cannot consult it.

The governors remember as bodies: eleven old animals with the wrong combs, still turning.

And the sockets remember as friction. Every bearing in this room was lapped by shafts of a hand that is now extinct or now unborn. The new wheel does not quite fit the old groove. It runs a little off, a little hot, a little loud. For the first three generations of every dynasty the entire wall knocks — an accent, worn into the mineral by the dead, audible for thirty years, fading as the groove is relapped, gone.

That is the only form the past takes here. Not image, not instinct, not story. A slight mismatch. Everything in the Bell that remembers anything remembers it by being very slightly wrong, and the wrongness is the sound the room makes, and the sound is what comes up the shaft and stops at the lip, so that a person can stand a meter from the edge in the thin cold air, hearing their own blood, and conclude the mountain is empty.

Somewhere on the eleventh tier a seam is drying and the torus has begun to lean. The pitch has dropped a quarter-tone in sixty years. In a socket four meters from that seam, a larva with the wrong-handed combs has just cemented itself to a lip worn smooth by ninety tenants of the right hand, and it will spend a century failing quietly, and it will knock, and its grandchildren will own the wall.
