## The coat

Ask first what counts as one of them, because the answer is not obvious and the locals do not appear to care.

The rind reads, from the slot, as a forest: canopy, understory, ground cover, trunks fluted like organ pipes, leaves cupped to catch the falling oil. Six hundred meters of it, wrapped around the inside of the hollow, every frond pointed inward at the emptiness the way a sunflower points at a sun that isn't there. Walk it — boots deaf, hands on the wall, the whole world arriving through the cheekbones — and you will start counting species and stop somewhere around four hundred, not from exhaustion but from a growing suspicion that the counting is a courtesy you are extending to something that has not asked for it.

Nothing in the coat has a pump. Every stem is a pipe open at both ends to the weather, and the weather does the circulating: overpressure up one flank, rarefaction down the other, a hundred thousand strokes an hour, forever. Which means every plant in the chamber is plumbed, through the air, into every other plant. Cut a stem and it whistles. Cut two stems a hundred meters apart and they whistle a chord, because they are the same circuit sounding at two places. Individuality here is a valve — a local habit of resistance, a way of keeping your sap slightly more your own than the neighbors'. Some species keep it fiercely and are unmistakably themselves: the black strap-tongues, the bladder-vines that inflate and deflate with the eleven-hertz like a wall of slow lungs. Others have barely bothered. There are stretches of the western rind, warm and wet with condensate, where a single graft-network runs for eighty meters and fruits in six different shapes according to what the local partial is doing to it that decade.

So: the coat is one thing, or four hundred things, or one thing temporarily wearing four hundred. Its behavior is real but slow. It leans into modes. It grows toward the shapes the wind wants and then the wind takes those shapes, and after a century the argument about which one started it has no content. It is the tone-shaping of the instrument and it is also the instrument's audience, and it has been both since before anything on this planet had a spine.

## The felts, and the animals that comb them

Under everything: the wax-molds, grey-copper felt in the sumps and along the shaded flanks of trunks, digesting spent oil back into oil that can be used again. They are the reason a closed cycle running for two hundred thousand years has not turned into a tar pit. They fruit in the antinode sinter, small pale cups that release spores tuned so coarse they gather nowhere and go everywhere — the only broadcast breeders in the chamber, the ones that gave up on notes.

The combers graze them. Palm-sized, flat, six-legged, mouthparts a pair of carding combs that draw the felt off the stone in neat windrows. They are the crowded ones, the gossips, packed into the understory at densities that would appall a flier. Everything they say, they say by touch: antennae, drum-pads on the forelegs, substrate vibration up in the megahertz, so far above the roar that the roar cannot even lean on it. A comber colony is a continuous shimmering conversation conducted through the rock at frequencies no visitor will ever feel, about — as far as anyone can reconstruct — the condition of the felt, the location of good felt, and who has recently died.

They farm. A comber will scratch a patch of stone at a particular rate and come back three days later to a thicker crop; the mold grows toward the scratching. Different colonies scratch differently. The dialects are agricultural.

## The pipe-fauna

Inside the stems there is a whole second fauna that the first surveys missed entirely, because you cannot see it without cutting the forest open and making it scream.

Thread-things, translucent, a resonant bladder at the head end, living in the sap-column and riding the pressure stroke back and forth, back and forth, their entire lives a commute of eleven centimeters. They eat the ester-sweet fraction, the bruised-fruit smell, which is the only part of the oil that anything here finds delicious.

And there is a borer — small, dark, wedge-headed — that chews a precisely sized hole in a stem wall and plugs it with wax, and by doing so changes the note the stem sings.

This matters more than it sounds. Every plant breeds by resonance: its seeds are tuned, mass and drag and a little bladder, to gather at the node of a particular partial, and to meet there the seeds of everything else that shares that note. Retune a plant and you have not injured it. You have changed who it can marry. You have moved its entire reproductive future four hundred meters sideways into a different neighborhood of air. The borer does this and then lays its eggs in the resulting fruit, and its larvae eat the mixed brood, and it has been quietly editing the genetics of the Bell for longer than there have been mammals outside. Species here are not lineages. They are subscriptions to a frequency, and the frequency can be reassigned by an animal the size of a grain of rice.

## The fliers

The abundance, and the loneliness.

Kite-shaped membrane animals, one to two meters across, taut as a drumhead, a lateral organ along the leading edge that reads pressure gradient the way a trout reads current. They are born in the air and die in the air and never once touch a surface. There are hundreds of thousands of them in forty million cubic meters and you will see four, or five, turning far apart in all that lit volume, each alone in a cathedral, each solitary the way a lighthouse is solitary.

They are not lonely. Density is death here — the notch-hunters work the crowded layers — and the fliers have made a virtue of it so thoroughly that they cannot now breed in company. They mate in the shear at the crown, in the cold updraft where the Eye pulls, two of them arriving from opposite ends of the chamber on a rendezvous neither of them signaled, because signaling would have brought something else. Contact lasts under a second. Then they peel apart and are a kilometer of roaring nothing away from each other in a minute and a half.

Their eggs drift. Their dead sink. A flier molts its leading-edge organ once, at the end, and goes blind — deaf was never the word — and loses the ability to know where the next curl will open, and falls. The floor-rind waits under the fall-lines. There are places on the floor, meters across, cupped and glossy and hungry, that are shaped exactly like the average death of a kite, and have been for a very long time.

At night they crawl with small blue discharges at the trailing edges, friction-charge bleeding off into the sour air, and the emptiness is sparsely populated by drifting sparks that are alive, and it is the closest thing to a sky this place has.

## The cutters

Now the ones that took longest to understand.

Squat, armored, thorax bearing a resonating plate that is not a loudspeaker but a *harvester*: it takes the ambient roar, inverts it, and returns it. They do not pay to speak. The storm pays. Their voice is powered entirely by the thing their voice removes.

What they make is a notch — a hole in the roar, a few centimeters of engineered quiet, traveling with them like a held breath. To another cutter, that hole is a word. Names are holes of a particular shape held steady over time. Courtship is two animals cutting a shared hole and modulating it together, which requires them to come close and stay close and make a bigger, more perfect, more conspicuous silence than either could alone. Intimacy here is exposure. To love something in the Bell is to stand with it in a lit room and be seen.

The predators counterfeit. There is a heavy, slow thing in the understory that can forge a come-hither notch to within a tolerance that fools most juveniles, and there are the patrols that make nothing at all and simply drift the roar listening for holes, and there is an arms race in the shape of absences that has been running since the Pleistocene.

And there are the mute beetles, which carry a cancellation field on their backs and get eaten constantly and persist. Nobody has settled it. The current best guess is not a defense at all: the beetles walk long straight transects, wall to wall, cutting and reading, cutting and reading, and what comes back to them is the chamber's own state — the mode, the drift, the slow wander of the attractor — sampled at a thousand points a night by ten thousand beetles. If the storm is doing something in parallel, this is how it finds out what it just did. The beetles are not signaling. They are *measuring*. They are eaten constantly because measurement is expensive, and they persist because the thing that needs the measurement is very large and does not mind paying.

## The masons, and the rim-kin

At the antinodes, where the pressure piles matter against stone and welds it there, there are sessile things the color of wet slate that pack grit into the hollows and catalyze the sinter. Slow, mineral, patient, more coral than animal. They are the reason loss and repair match to within a percent over centuries. The walls are not merely eroded into their vocabulary of curves; they are *maintained* into it, by organisms whose bodies become wall, generation on generation, and whose entire evolutionary interest lies in the running average of a million gales.

The flutes are composed. They are also, plausibly, authored. This is the point at which the question of who built the machine stops being clean.

In the Throat there are strainers: fan-shaped, anchored, filtering the outflow, catching the one gram a year that tries to leave. They are the customs service. Nothing gets out of the Bell without passing through something's mouth.

And at the Eye — the cartwheel aperture at the crown, the only opening onto the sky — there is a ring of tissue that is not quite the coat and not quite anything else. It is the only life in the chamber that has ever met the outside. It meets frost. It meets water. Water freezes here, sinks, dissolves what must not dissolve; it is the alien solvent, the poison, the thing that could not possibly support life. The rim-kin dies of the sky continuously, in a thin necrotic band, and is continuously replaced from below, and has been dying of the sky at the same rate for two hundred millennia so that everything beneath it can stay dry.

Its spores go out. They fall onto the salt pan and crackle and give nothing back. In the draws below the mountain, among the wax-frost, there is an ester-sweet fraction that no mineral process makes. The Bell has been seeding the desert, and failing, for a very long time. The word *failing* is doing a lot of work in that sentence and should probably be held loosely.

## The interval animals

At night the crown cools, the gradient steepens, the fundamental drops a fifth — eleven hertz to something near seven — and you feel it happen in the pelvis. Every Still in the volume unravels at once: thirty hanging orchards coming apart into glitter in ninety seconds.

There are things that live in that ninety seconds.

They condense out of the collapse itself, out of the released seed and shredded chitin and the sudden surplus of everything a dying garden was holding. Small, fast, iridescent, gorging, mating, laying, done. Their entire adult existence is a minute and a half wide and occurs once per night, and from inside their lives the storm is a geological epoch and the collapse is spring. They do not experience the Bell as continuous. They experience it as a series of nights, each one a world, separated by an abyss of daylight in which they are a dust grain waiting.

And there is a second forest.

Some species' seeds are tuned to partials that only exist in the low night mode. They gather, green over, fruit, and shed in the hour before dawn, in the same Stills, on the same dust, using the same real estate as the day breeders and never meeting them. Two biotas interleaved in one skin, separated not by space but by pitch. The Bell has a diurnal fauna and a nocturnal fauna and they share a body the way two languages share an alphabet.

## The wound, and the fence

Around the cracked peg the chamber screams a wrong note, and the rind will not grow into it, and the fliers avoid it the way birds avoid a window. Two hundred meters of bare scalloped rock, the only baldness in all that lushness.

Something lives there. A pale mat, slow, a centimeter a year, chemically not quite like the rest — the only organism in the Bell that is not tuned to anything, because the note it lives in does not belong to the attractor and never resolves. It cannot breed by resonance; there is no node for it. It reproduces by fission, alone, in the error.

Which suggests the thing that is hardest to say plainly: everywhere else in this chamber, to be a self is to be a function of the whole. In the wound, for the first time, something is merely itself. It may be the only individual in the mountain.

Across the floor of the northeast quarter runs the fence that is not wire: four strands of a held chord, humming in the fillings of your teeth, strung between two posts of matte black. The rind stops at it in a clean line. On the far side the dust is a different color, and the coat is a different green, and the cutters over there shape their holes differently.

They come to the chord. Both sides do. They stand a body-length apart and cut notches at each other, and the notches do not parse, and they hold them a long time, and then they go away. Two grammars of silence that cannot read one another, kept apart by a sentence that has been being said for two hundred thousand years. Whether the chord is a quarantine, a border, a translation, or simply the noise a thought makes at the place where it divides into two thoughts, is not settled and may not be settleable from inside.

## You

You are the newest inhabitant and the worst one.

You went deaf in the first four seconds — a small distinct sensation of something giving way — and now you read the world through your boots and the bones of your face, and at eighteen hertz the landscape smears vertically like wet paint and you wait it out with your hands on the wall. You are a sack of the poison solvent, breathing. Where you stand, the felt pales. Where you set a hand, a small crisp death the shape of a palm. You leave a trail of tiny catastrophes through a forest that has never in its history had to survive a wet thing.

Something lands on your shoulder while you are in there. It is very small and it is tuned to a partial up in the bright tactile buzzing, above hearing, above pain, in the register you have been feeling on your skin this whole time and calling *the noise*. It reads you. It finds you enormous, and warm, and fatally damp, and it dies before you reach the slot.

You are the only animal here that ever leaves.

## The room

The Mute Room is hexagonal, four meters across, half-swallowed by the rind on its windward side, and its walls subtract the wave *before it arrives*, which is not a thing walls do. Absorption is a lag; this is a prediction. Something in that hexagon holds a model of the storm accurate to a fraction of a cycle, and has held it, without power source or seam or decay, since before there were eyes.

Inside, the roar is gone. You hear your blood, your joints, the wet click of your own eyelids, and after ten minutes a high thin tone that is probably your auditory nerve reporting for duty in a world with nothing to report.

Cutters come in. They crawl through the low gap on the leeward side and arrive in the one place where there is nothing to subtract, and are therefore, for the first time in their lives, unable to speak — mute in the only room where speech is possible. Some of them stay. They die on the bench, small and armored and perfectly silent, and the room does not decay them because the room does not do anything to anything. There are eleven of them in there now, arranged the way things arrange themselves when they have come a long way to sit down.

The stair on the western wall goes up sixty meters and ends. The top eleven steps are worn away, which means the stair was longer, or that they climbed past it. There is a polished groove where a rope ran. There are handprints in the sinter near the Throat: five-fingered, small.

Whoever they were, they went deaf in four seconds like everybody else. They were the only things in the Bell that had ever had ears, and they built a room where a word could be said aloud, and they faced it at the exact center of the volume, and it has been facing it, empty, for a very long time.

There is a bench. There is a mark at shoulder height that could be a signature or a tally or a warning.

And there is a cup, unbroken, dry — a water-object, a thing shaped for the poison, left by something thirsty in a place where thirst is not a category.

Look in it.

There is a clutch of seeds in the bottom. Tuned seeds, each with its little resonant bladder, each waiting for a partial to gather it up and carry it to a node and introduce it to everything else that shares its note. No sound reaches the cup. No sound will reach the cup. They are dry and viable and they have been in there, out of the weather, out of the computation, unreckoned and unchanged, for longer than the coat has had the shape it has now.

That is what lives in the Mute Room: the only things in the mountain that are not currently being thought. Everything else here is a verb — a stroke of sap, a notch, a mason's grain, a kite's turn, a storm computing itself at enormous speed with no place to put a result. The room is where you set something down so that it will still be there tomorrow, unaltered, whether or not the weather agrees.

Somebody built the only quiet in the world, and sat in it, and used it to keep a few seeds out of the argument.

Then they went up the stair, past the eleventh step, and left the cup where the silence would find it.
