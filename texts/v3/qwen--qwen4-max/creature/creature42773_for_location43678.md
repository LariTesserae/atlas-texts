## The Residents

The oldest thing alive in the Cup is a tree, and you can step over it.

It runs from the second seep along the wet black band for thirty-one feet, and its trunk is no thicker than your finger, and it has been doing this for two hundred years. Every summer it puts up a fringe of leaves the size of thumbnails and a haze of catkins the color of old butter, and the midges fertilize it, and the wind that cannot find it takes the pollen anyway in the two inches of air where the wind actually lives. It has never once stood up. Its whole architecture is a decision about height. Under its bark there is a beetle larva that has been eating a single gallery for four years, unhurried, in the dark, at the temperature of a mild spring, because the willow holds the sun and the moss holds the willow.

The moss domes are older than the willow, some of them. Not the shoots — the shoots turn over — but the body, the continuous green flesh of it, which has been extending itself outward a hair's breadth a year since the ice let go of the floor. A cushion the size of a bed is four centuries of one organism's patience. It has a shape because shape is the whole trick: no gaps, no chimneys, nothing for the wind to get its fingers into. Cut one open with a knife and it is layered like a peat, warm and wet at the center, peppered with the droppings of ten thousand generations, and it smells like the inside of a mouth. It is not a plant so much as a stove that reproduces.

And inside it: springtails in numbers that make a handful of moss look like a handful of moving pepper. Mites. A white worm you can barely see, that eats the fungi that eat the willow. Voles, in tunnels they have chewed through the moss itself, so that the tunnels are roofed with living green and stay at blood heat all night while the air outside goes to twenty below. The vole in the cushion does not know it is on a mountain. The vole in the cushion lives in a warm wet corridor with food in the walls and has no reason to think there is anything else.

There is a great deal else. It is two hundred yards away and might as well be on the moon.

---

Because the nine gardens are nine worlds, and almost nothing crosses between them.

The voles cannot. Two hundred yards of lit stone at noon is a surface hot enough to blister a foot, and there is no cover, and a finch is watching. A vole that leaves its cushion dies in the open within minutes, and so voles do not leave, and have not left, for as long as there have been cushions here — which at their rate of breeding is something like eight hundred generations of never leaving.

The result is visible from a distance of one foot, if you know to look. The Bath voles are gray with a wash of ochre along the flank. The Kettle voles are dark red-brown, almost the color of the wet band, and smaller, and their tails are shorter. In the Two Pools there are two kinds and they do not interbreed and nobody knows why. The people know all of this perfectly well and have names for it, and use the names the way you would use a family name: *a Kettle vole*, meaning a small dark short-tailed thing that belongs to the Kettle and would be wrong anywhere else.

Once, four generations back, a boy carried a vole from one garden to another in his mitten. He was six. He wanted the gray ones to have a red friend. What he also carried, in the fur of the animal, was something that killed the cushions from the inside — a rot that turned the green flesh to a brown skin over a hollow, and the hollow tore in the first gale of autumn and blew off the mountain as dust.

That garden is not on the tally anymore. Nobody says which one it was. The rule that came out of it is taught to children before they are taught to walk the stepping stones: *a garden keeps its own*. You do not carry a living thing across the stone. You do not move a moss. You do not put your hand into one cushion and then into another. The people are the only ferryman in the Cup who has ever been forbidden to ferry.

Everything else crosses freely, and everything else is therefore a boat.

The hare is the plants' boat. It sits out the noon in boulder shade and moves at dusk between the gardens, and in its fur and on its feet it carries moss fragments and saxifrage seed and the eggs of things too small to name, and it deposits these, along with a pellet of dung, wherever it happens to sit down. Every garden in the Cup was probably started by a hare sitting in a wet place. The tenth will be too.

The finch crosses in passes, thirty feet up, drinking the columns. It has learned the shadow line better than the people have. It knows which garden is smoking now and which will be smoking in an hour and it works them in order, west to east, all day, like a man reading a line of print.

The fox comes up through the throat at dusk and makes a circuit — the tarn, the two low gardens, the elbow of the headwall, the high seeps, back down — and the circuit takes most of the night, and it is the same circuit every night, worn into the scree as a faint dotted line of paw prints that the people call the count, because it is the route they walk themselves.

The spiders cross on the snow, black and furred, running on the surface with their legs spread to keep their bellies off the crystals, and they are the ferrymen of the dead. Because the columns leak. All day the wind shears midges off the shafts and drops them out over the glare, and they land on the snowfield and dry, and by afternoon there is a windrow of them along the drift edge, a black seam of small bodies a hundred feet long, and the spiders work that seam. Half the calories eaten in the Cup are eaten by things that never entered a garden.

And the two ravens hold the whole bowl. They are the only resident, other than the fox, for whom the Cup is one place instead of nine places plus waste. They nest in a fracture halfway up the plucking face, in the black band, out of the wind, and they have held that territory for nineteen years that anyone can prove and probably far longer, and the people do not think of them as two birds. They think of them as a fixture. The Cup has two ravens the way a house has two doorposts. When one dies another appears, with the same mannerisms, and nobody finds this remarkable.

They steal. They have a cache in a crack above the third seep that has been excavated once, out of curiosity, and contained: sinew, a strip of gut, three pebbles of ochre, a bone goggle with the slit worn wide, a child's milk tooth, and a piece of hair net. Some of it was older than the woman who found it.

---

There are nine gardens. The listener will tell you there are seven.

She is the counter's daughter, and she was taught by her grandfather, who wintered here, and what she does is walk the bowl in the early morning with a wooden mallet and a river-cobble and put her cheek against the black band and strike, and listen to what comes back through the bone of her skull.

The rock conducts. It conducts the frost ticking, and the slabs settling, and — under that, in the register that a cheek can hear and an ear cannot — the water. Every seep has a voice in the stone. The Bath says *tak, tak, tak*, irregular, like a fingernail on a comb. The Kettle swallows. The Long Wood hums, an actual hum, low, continuous, the sound of a lot of water moving through a lot of small fractures at once. The Little One is not yet a voice; it is a wetness in the sound, a thickness.

Two gardens have gone quiet. Not brown — green, fat, rioting, columns twenty feet high, voles breeding — but silent in the rock, because the water that feeds them has found a deeper route and no longer runs near enough to the surface to be heard. She gives them three years, five at the outside. She has not told her mother. Her mother pecks the tally, and the tally is a count of gardens, and a garden is a green thing with a column over it, and by that definition there are nine.

So there are nine on the cairns and seven in the stone, and the listener carries the difference around like a stone in her boot.

Her grandfather is the reason she has the job at all. He went up late, in a bad year, with his brother, to bring down a cache before the lip iced. The lip iced. The brother died in the eleventh week of it — there is a cairn on the scree above the throat with no tally on it, and it is the only cairn in the Cup that is not a count — and the man stayed, because there was nowhere to go, in the low house, on midge cake and meltwater, for a winter.

He said very little about it afterward, except two things.

The first was that the columns are not the point. He had watched nine gardens go dark for the season and had assumed the place was dead, and then in the black of a February afternoon he put his face against the wall to warm it and heard the whole mountain running. Full. Everywhere. Under everything. The water does not stop in winter; it only stops coming out. The Cup is not nine gardens. The Cup is one animal with nine mouths, and the animal is the fracture system, ten thousand cubic yards of cracked rock full of moving water, and the gardens are where it breathes.

The second thing he said was that you could talk through it. He had spent a winter with a dead brother forty feet away under stones and had discovered, by knocking, that the stone carried a blow clean across the bowl.

So the people knock now. It is not decoration. Air is useless here — a shout dies in six paces, and shouting is what children do before they learn better — but a cobble struck three times on the black band arrives in every cheek in the Cup. Two strikes and a pause: *come*. Three slow: *the rope is set*. A fast irregular run: *rockfall, get off the wall*. There is a rhythm nobody uses, a spaced triple tap that sounds too much like the Bath saying *tak*, and children who use it as a joke are slapped, because for one second it makes every adult in the bowl think the water has changed.

And in the evenings, in the low house, ten people on their bellies in a stone room with a hide roof and a hearth-floor made of eleven older hearths, they sing the only way singing works up here. They lie in a ring with their heads against the black band and hum, and it comes into each skull through the bone, and everyone hears everyone, dead center, inside the head, louder than any voice has ever been in that thin air. It is the only choir the Cup permits. The listener says the mountain hums along. It does. It is meltwater. She has known that since she was nine and she still lets herself believe it for the length of a song.

---

The people themselves: three families and sometimes a fourth, eleven days, and a set of arrangements so old nobody bothers to explain them.

One family owns the rope. Not the rope — the rope is renewed every summer, spliced, and the retired lengths go into the cache pit under the house, so the pit has a stratigraphy of rope as well as of hearths. What they own is the right to set it in the groove at the lip, a groove worn a hand deep by other people's ancestors, and the toll is one cake in twenty, and there has been a dispute about this within living memory that ended with a marriage.

The counter is old and half blind, which up here is simply what happens to eyes that have looked at that snowfield for sixty summers. She counts by walking the fox's route with her palms out. She can find a garden from thirty feet away by the warmth coming off it, the way you find a stove in a dark kitchen, and she can tell you its health by the smell — pepper and rot and green water is a good garden; a sour, faintly sweetish note is a garden whose pool has bloomed and gone bad, and that one gets a flat stone laid on top of its cairn, which means *do not sweep, do not drink, do not let the dog near it*.

The dog is tied at the throat. There is one dog, usually, and it is miserable — the altitude, the glare, the silence it cannot understand — and it has learned to watch hands instead of listening for its name, and it has learned to lie with its belly on stone and its nose in a cushion, quivering, because a cushion is full of voles and it is not allowed. A loose dog in a garden destroys four hundred years of moss in an afternoon. Everyone knows this the way everyone knows fire is hot.

The children wear goggles ground by a parent, and the slits on a child's first pair are always too wide, because a parent cannot bear to make the world that small for them, and so every child goes snowblind on the second day and is led by the hand for three days and learns. They are brought up specifically to see water boil cold, and they put their hands into the steaming rope of it and it is barely blood-warm, and they are always, always disappointed, and the adults always laugh, because that is the joke, that is the oldest joke in the Cup: the mountain looks like it is cooking and it is only just alive.

The children also hold the herd, some of them. The herders who use the high route cannot bring animals into the bowl — no forage, loose scree, nothing — so the stock stands on the rim and a child sits with them for eleven days and looks down. That child is the only human being who ever sees the Cup whole. Everyone else is inside it, in an island, kneeling. The child on the rim sees nine green dots and nine standing columns turning slowly in the light, all day, every day, and gets used to it, and thinks of it as the shape of the world, and is bored.

Nobody walks on the moss. There is a route across the bowl made of chosen slabs, one foot on this stone, next foot on that one, and it is memorized like a song, and a boot-print pressed into a cushion stays there for twenty years, a brown bruise, a hole in the stove. The stepping stones are the only road and the road is invisible and it is the first thing anyone learns and the last thing anyone forgives.

And there is a woman — there is always one, in every generation, it seems to pass from aunt to niece — who is careless in a particular way. She never plants anything. Planting is forbidden, or worse, it is *silly*; her grandmother tried it, transplanted cushions into a wet crack, and they died in one winter and everyone was embarrassed. So she does not plant. She simply walks the wet places with dirty boots. She goes out of her way, a little, to stand in new seeps. She kneels where there is nothing but wet black rock and a fringe of hare-dung, and she gets up and moves on, and what is stuck to her boot goes into the water.

She has never said a word about it. Two of the nine are hers.

---

The harvest is eleven days of a very specific kind of work.

They wait for the soot-wings to open flat. The butterflies bask on the hot bands with their wings spread like scraps of burnt paper, and when they go from half-closed to flat, the stone is hot enough that the column will be thick, and the sweepers pick up their nets.

The nets are hair and sinew on a hoop of antler, and the sweepers stand on the standing stone at each garden's edge — one slab per garden, worn concave by four centuries of the same feet — and reach into the shaft and swing, and turn, and swing. The midges come into their eyes and their nostrils and the corners of their mouths. They breathe through a fold of cloth. Their faces swell for the first three days and then stop swelling, and the swollen face is called cake-face and is a thing young men display proudly in the valley. The comb is not for hair. The antler comb is for stripping the catch out of the mesh, and it is the most valuable object in the Cup after the rope.

Rules: never sweep a column to nothing. Take the lower shaft, leave the crown standing, because a column that comes apart does not re-form until the next noon and because the finch has to eat. Never sweep one garden twice in a day. Never sweep the Little One. Never sweep a garden with a flat stone on its cairn, no matter how thick the column, no matter how hungry the winter looks.

What comes off the nets goes into a gut bag and then under a slab with a lever, and presses out into cakes the size of a hand, dark, dense, half fat, stamped with a thumbnail crescent so that families can tell their own. Forty cakes is a summer. Sixty is a good summer. They taste of pond and burnt blood and something faintly peppery that is the moss the midges came out of, and they keep for years, and down in the valley they are worth more than anything else that comes off that mountain, and they are the entire reason anyone goes up.

At the bottom of the cache pit there is a cake nobody eats. It is older than the house's current roof. Every year the newest cakes go on top and the oldest are taken off the top of the bottom layer, and the bottom one stays, and if you ask why, you are told: *so that there is always one*.

---

There are also residents who are not currently alive, and they may outnumber everybody.

The dry bank of the third seep is a clay crust that has not been wet in forty years, and it is full of eggs. Copepod cysts. Springtail eggs. Spores in a state of suspension so complete that they are, for practical purposes, minerals. Every wet decade some fraction of them wakes and the pool has animals in it that nobody has seen in a lifetime, and the finch does not notice, and the counter pecks nothing, because it is not a garden, it is a mood.

The resident population of the Cup, properly counted, includes the unborn and the paused. It includes the midge larvae under the ice of the tarn, overwintering in the black mud in their thousands, waiting for a slush morning. It includes the vole that is not yet conceived and will be, twice, before the shadow line moves. It includes four dead gardens as brown hollows in the moss that has grown over them, and a hundred small drying bodies impaled on the frost-shattered spines of the pinnacle the people call the needle, which the butcher-bird adds to daily and which has been in use so long that the crevice below it is packed with a felt of things too small to identify.

It includes the glacier, if you are willing to be generous. The last tongue lies dead under its quilt of rubble at the foot of the headwall, going gray and gritty, retreating a few paces a year. It made the room. It is now the tenant dying in the room it made, and there are bacteria in its basal ice that were alive when the ice was snow, and they are being released, this summer, into the fracture system, along with spores and seeds and grains of a world that ended a few centuries ago. Some of what is growing in the ninth garden has never grown there before. Some of it grew there before the ice and has been waiting under it, in the dark, at a temperature just barely above nothing, for the whole of human presence on this mountain.

---

And under everything, inside the rock, there is the thing that will still be here.

The black bands are not solid. Nothing here is solid. The mountain is a sponge of fractures, and the water in it is warm — blood-warm, tired-animal-warm — and it is full of life that never comes out. Films of bacteria on the fracture faces, eating the rock. Not the moss's kind of eating; a slower chemistry, iron and sulfur and the glassy feldspar snapped bright a few centuries ago, still fresh, still unweathered, still worth eating. Growths a molecule thick and acres wide. Things that divide once a decade. An entire population in the dark, in the wet, in the warm, whose only contact with the sun is that nine times it leaks out onto the surface and makes a green place and a column of midges and a fox's nightly circuit and eleven days of people with swollen faces, and then goes back down.

The gardens are what that thing looks like when it breathes out.

When the headwall breaches — a few thousand years, a hand's breadth a winter, the bowl eating its own rim — the tarn will go out through the gap in an afternoon and the water table will drop below the roots and all nine will die in a week. The columns will come apart for the last time. The stepping stones will lead to nothing. The tally will say a number that no longer means anything, and there will be no one to peck it, and the windbreaks will stand at nine sleeping places where nobody sleeps.

But the population will not die. It will move down. It will follow the water into the fractures, deeper, the way it has always gone, toward the valley four thousand feet below, at the speed of water through stone, which is to say slowly, which is to say it will take longer than everything above it has taken so far.

Until then there is a tenth garden starting on bare wet rock below the elbow of the headwall, where a hare sat down in June with moss on its belly. There is a column over it the height of a forearm. The child on the rim saw it three days ago and has told nobody, because from up there it is only a flaw in the rock, a tenth green dot, and she assumes it has always been there and that the adults have simply not mentioned it.

The listener has heard it. A thickness in the sound. Not a voice yet.

The counter has felt it — she found it with her palms out, from thirty feet, the small warmth of it, the beginning of a stove — and she has not pecked it, because the rule is that you do not count a garden until it has lived a winter.

So: nine on the cairns. Seven in the stone. Ten in the light.

And under all of it, the chewing.
