## The Inhabitants

**I. Begin with the palms.**

Every adult in the Room has white seams across both palms, and the older ones have a second generation of scars laid over the first at a different angle, so that the hands read like a chart of two conflicting surveys. You get them from the floor. Under twenty millimeters of water the chisel work is still sharp, and everyone falls once, and the falling is a lesson taught by the stone rather than by the parents. After that you learn to put a hand only on the grids, on the rails, on the worn wooden handles that the district makes for itself out of nothing but crates. The hands are pale and permanently corrugated. The nails are soft. Nobody here has ever been dry, and the skin has stopped expecting it.

The hands are also the mouth. Consonants live in them — *bearing, seal, drift, level, gauge, seat, weep* — a fast clipped vocabulary of maybe four hundred signs, most of them concerning the condition of surfaces. Vowels are shouted. So a sentence in the Room is a shaped howl with hands moving inside it, and a stranger watching two people talk sees what looks like an argument conducted by a singer. It is not an argument. It is usually about a cup.

Because consonants cannot be shouted, the things that require consonants cannot be said in public. This turns out to matter more than anything else about them.

**II. Bodies.**

Forty-one and a half degrees at saturation is a temperature that does not permit sweat to do its work, and so the people have arranged their lives around not needing it. They are thin and slow and roped with visible veins. They move at the speed of the pumps, which is fifty-two strokes a minute, and their resting hearts have come to that figure too; a physician from outside would call it a pathology and would be shown, politely, the logbooks. They eat salt in measured pinches from a tin dish kept in every warren, and they drink constantly and without pleasure, the way you keep a boiler topped.

For cooling there are the cold benches: lengths of the cold lattice brought down to shoulder height at eleven places in the district, and the long iron trough along the outfall channel where the water leaves. Children are taught before they are taught anything else to lay the insides of their forearms along a cold main and count to sixty. Old people sit at the trough for whole shifts with their sleeves rolled, watching the eels. It is the district's porch, its plaza, its place for saying nothing.

Everyone over forty is deaf above the hiss. Everyone at every age carries the head slightly tilted, left ear forward — the professional deformity of a town whose work is listening — and it is so universal that a face held straight looks insolent. Their eyes have never dark-adapted. They have no word for night that is not a loanword. They have fourteen useful words for brown, and *red* is a theoretical term taught in school alongside the names of foreign cities, and when someone is cut the wound is black and shiny like oil, so injuries are found by warmth and by smell, and children are trained to put a hand near a friend's arm rather than look at it.

Children draw shadows as soft smudges directly underneath things. When visitors come down the stair with a hard black shadow slung off one side of them, the younger children cry, and are comforted, and are told that outside everything has a stain that follows it, and that this is not the visitor's fault.

**III. Names, and what a name is for.**

Six hundred and eleven is not a population. It is a staffing schedule with a tolerance allowed. The Room requires so many cup hands, so many pump minders, so many pruners, so many on the outfall screens, so many teaching, so many listening, and it holds a margin above that number the way a bearing holds clearance.

Public names are vowels: **Eia**, **Ouo**, **Aei-oo**, **Uaia**. They are chosen to carry. A name that cannot be shouted across the floor sheet is not a name, it is a possession. Alongside these run the station-names, which are what a person actually answers to at work and what their neighbors call them: *North Gallery*, *Third Stair*, *Nine*, *Sump*, *Eleven Landing*, *The Fig Crew*. You take the station-name when you take the station. The woman who has counted frogs by ear for thirty years is called Nine, and was called Nine before her, and the child now walking behind her with a tilted head will be Nine afterward, and none of them consider this a metaphor.

The doctrine is right there in the floor, and every child can recite it: *the cups are eternal; no individual cup is.* Eleven thousand cups, each lifted on the rota, reground to gauge, returned before wear can show. It is not a mystery religion. It is a maintenance procedure that they have noticed applies to them.

So: are they interchangeable? Is a person here just water in a cup?

That is the census answer, and it is wrong, and they know it is wrong, and they have built an entire second civilization inside the first one to hold the wrongness.

**IV. The two books.**

The official log is kept in fourteen hands across eighty years, and the hands match. An apprentice spends most of a year learning to write like the person before them — the same slope, the same open four, the same lazy loop on the *g* in *gauge* — and this is considered the beginning of character, not its surrender. To write your own hand in the log would be to put yourself in the record, and the record is not for that. The Departures, three hundred pages, is read aloud on the anniversary in the school null, and it is the history of every occasion on which a needle moved, and children fall asleep in the long middle reaches where nothing happened, and their mothers let them.

The other book is kept by the cup crews.

Here is the fact it exists for: **gauge is a dimension, not a voice.** A cup reground to gauge is dimensionally identical and acoustically not. The grain of the basalt has changed slightly, the wall is a hair thinner within tolerance, and the bell it makes when the drip finds it is a hair off the bell it made before. The Room cannot hear this. The people can. Every household knows the note of its own cup the way you know a footstep on a stair, and there is a warren under the cold lattice on the west side where a woman has lain awake for eleven shifts because the cup above her head came back from the wheel a quarter-tone flat and correct in every measurable respect.

So the cup crews keep a voice-book: eleven thousand entries, each one a number and a description of a sound in whatever words came to hand — *like a spoon on a tooth*, *dull, asks a question*, *the one that laughs*, *hers* — and it is written in every hand at once, ugly, individual, uncorrected, swollen with damp, mended with tape, and it is the most valuable object in the district. It is not hidden. It is simply never read aloud. It is the archive of everything that happened once.

When your cup comes back a stranger, you say *level*, because that is the word, and then you go and find the book and write a line in your own handwriting.

**V. Where a person actually lives.**

The nulls are surveyed and inherited and worth more than anything, and the tenements and warrens and the school are cut into them, and this is usually explained as an acoustic convenience. It is more than that.

In the roar you are a station. You cannot be heard breathing; you cannot be distinguished; you are Nine, or Third Stair, a shape with hands, a thing the pattern requires. Step into a null and someone one foot away can hear your breath catch. You become a body with a inside to it. The children stand in the nodes to whisper and step sideways into invisibility, and what they are practicing, without any adult ever putting it into words, is the local and intermittent nature of being someone.

Personhood here is not a continuous possession. It is an address you go to.

Under the third stair there is a null that fits three, and a flute in oilcloth, and on the eighth shift someone plays it, at fifty-two, and the music stops at the boundary of the node as if at a wall, and this is the correct way to make music. Nothing here is broadcast. There is no art in the Room that is meant for people who are not present. The idea would be indecent — a thing made to be received by strangers who did not come, a thing that travels, a thing that repeats itself in mouths that were not there.

Courtship is the exchange of consonants. You bring someone into a null, ideally a small one, and you say your birth-name, which is full of hard sounds and cannot survive the roar, and they say theirs, and that is the whole ceremony and it is never performed twice. To be married is to live with someone who can say your name and doesn't.

And when a person dies, the body goes into the cold channel — there is no soil, there was never any soil, and the eels hold at the outfall where the water leaves, and the old people sit above them on the cooling trough with their sleeves rolled up, and nobody in the Room finds any of this horrible. What is done with the name is this: whoever is left takes one other person into a null and says it. Once. It does not carry. That is what the null is for.

*Nothing here happens once*, they say, the way you'd say a fact about the weather, and they are honest people, and they know about the exception, and they have built the exception into the architecture.

**VI. The nine.**

There are nine places where the runnels were miscalculated and the water falls on open stone and slaps flat instead of ringing. They are named — the Slap, the Kettle, the Aunt, the Tooth, the Hem, and four more — and they are used as landmarks, and imitating one with your mouth is the only genuinely serious offense a child can commit, because a false landmark is a thing that can get someone lost in a hundred and eighty meters of amber roar.

They are also where the frogs are not. Nine holes in the cloth, found over thirty years by a woman with her head tilted, and the frogs will not hold a station at a drip that slaps; they need the bell, they tune to it, the territorial spacing of just under a meter is a spacing of notes. Nine holes, nine slaps. She worked this out in her eleventh year. She has never entered it in the Departures, because it is not a departure — nothing moved, nothing changed, it has been true since before she was born. It is in the voice-book, in her own bad handwriting, four lines, and it is the closest thing the Room has to a discovery.

And people live at the slaps. Not many: eleven, at present. They are the ones who could not hold a station — the hand that shakes, the ear that hears a thing that isn't there, the woman whose pulse runs at sixty and cannot be brought to fifty-two, the old man who was reground out of his job and did not consent. Every system has a tolerance, and here the tolerance has an address and a name and a smell of sulfur mats from the east wall. They are not outcasts. They are visited. They are brought flour. They are also, quietly, the source of everything new: the songs played in the nulls come from the slaps, because a place where the sound is wrong is the only place where a sound can be made that the Room has not already filed.

There is a boy at the Hem, apprentice to Nine, who hears a tenth hole. He has been hearing it for nineteen shifts. He cannot get anyone else to hear it yet. If it is confirmed it will be written down, and the writing will be an event, and the reading of it aloud will keep children awake on some anniversary sixty years from now. Until then he sits on wet stone in a place named for an error, with his head tilted, listening to a hole that may not exist, and he is not unhappy, and if you asked him how it was he would say *level*, and mean it, and be lying in his own handwriting.

**VII. The neighbors.**

Eighty-eight geckos, one per lamp, at the same height on the same side of each housing, heads down. The arithmetic is not negotiable and they settled it themselves. Each is called by its lamp's number. When one dies another is on the housing within a shift, and the town's position is that this is the same gecko in every sense worth maintaining, and the town's private position is that the gecko on lamp forty-one had a torn tail and the new one does not, and somebody has noticed, and it is in the book.

Thirty-nine herons at thirty-nine stations, twenty paces apart, standing in the sheet like furniture in a hall too big for it. The posts are inherited; the birds fledge from the station they take. The one at the outfall is deaf from the roar and hunts by the feel of water against its shins, and is the largest and oldest heron in the district, and is called the Grandfather, and is the only animal here with a name that is not a number.

Four thousand four hundred and twelve frogs, spaced by note. Seven figs, cut back on the rota to the same two-meter architecture they have had for sixty years — permanent saplings, kept by a crew who talk about them exactly as people talk about a difficult relative: *she's putting out again*, *he wants to go up*, *not on my shift*. One of them has a name made of consonants, which is signed, never shouted, and belonged to a child. This is the town's clearest picture of its own tenderness: love expressed as the prevention of becoming. Nothing is allowed to grow away from you. The bats come off the trusses one every few seconds all shift long because there is no dusk, and so there is no word here for urgency, and no word for a crowd, and no word for the moment when everything happens at once.

The mosquitoes are a tax and everyone pays at the same rate. The midges never thicken and never thin. The rats run the warm mains at a fixed density, and this is possible because the storekeeper spills flour.

He spills it deliberately, weighed, at a rate written nowhere, along a fixed line of sacks. It is the base of the food web. It is the district's single licensed addition in an economy whose entire purpose is subtraction, and it is called *loss*, and he is a respected man, and the apprentices are told that the flour escapes.

**VIII. The rim.**

The guard at the gate has cleared his ears eleven landings' worth and stands in dry moving air at thirty degrees, and he is the only person in the Room who knows what weather is. He is regarded as slightly unwell and is given the best tea.

On still nights he can hear the next bowl, four kilometers along the coast, sounding its one unwavering note — a different pump beat, a different hum, a different frog. He keeps a column for it. In the year the rust streak was young, the next bowl went quiet for nine hours, and it is in the Departures, one line, and nobody went to look, because going to look is not a thing that this district does.

He hums it. On still nights, at the gate, he hums the other bowl's note whether it is sounding or not, so that the column has a figure in it. This is what prayer looks like in a place with no gradient in time: not asking for anything, but keeping a value from dropping.

**IX. So what lives here.**

Not six hundred and eleven people and a census of animals. That is the roster.

What lives here is a chord that has learned to notice itself — a fixed sound eighty years old, four millimeters an hour, fifty-two strokes a minute, a hundred cycles, four thousand four hundred and twelve throats — and the noticing is not done by the chord. It is done at the edges, by the tilted head, by the drifting handwriting, by the man humming a silence at a gate, by eleven people at nine wrong addresses, by a boy listening for a hole that may not be there. The pattern is eternal and no part of it is, and the whole cultural apparatus of the Room — the nulls, the consonants, the voice-book, the names said once into a small quiet and never again — exists to hold the difference between those two facts without letting either one collapse.

They are not living outside time. They are the part of the waveform that knows it is a waveform, and they have made themselves small and local and unrepeatable on purpose, in the gaps of their own noise.

Down below, a cup is lifted out and a reground cup is set in its place, and the rain finds it without interruption, and it rings a quarter-tone flat, and a woman lying in the dark under the cold lattice hears it and says nothing at all. In the morning she will find the book and write one line in her own hand.

*Level*, says the Room.

*Hers*, says the book.
