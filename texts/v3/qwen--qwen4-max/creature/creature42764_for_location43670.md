# The Residents

## The Hand

There is one worker on the shelf and he is four miles long.

Not a giant. Nothing here is large except the noise. He is a person of ordinary size — you can measure him off the lanes, which are worn to a body's width and no more, and off the sleeping hollow, which takes a shoulder and a hip and a bent knee — but he is laid out along the grain instead of along years, and so his life has an *address*. You do not wait for him. You walk to him.

At the eastern wall he is beginning. You can tell from the clay. The daubs there are squeezed hard, rimmed with a little extruded collar where the thumb pressed past what was needed, the way a young hand presses: too much, and glad to. The notch is there already, but shallow, a hesitation in the whorl rather than a gap.

In the middle lanes the prints are economical. Exactly enough clay. No collar. The pressure is even and comes from a settled distance, a hand that has done this ten thousand times and has stopped enjoying it and has not stopped being careful.

At the west the prints are shallow and they lean from further left, because he can no longer bend as far. The last few have a doubled edge, a small second contact, as though the hand came down and then came down again to be sure. After that there is grey flour in the wet, and the flour goes down through the plates, and the water carries it east, and the wall lays it down as a leaf of new schist, sharp as broken crockery.

So he does not die here. He *thins*. The population of the shelf is one, and it is distributed, and if you walk it end to end in a day you have attended a birth, a working life, and a dissolution, and none of them happened while you were watching.

The tally-marks have a sign for him. It is a group of five with the fifth stroke cut deeper. It is also the sign for *thumb*, and, in the older courses, for *west*. The phrase the first surveyors translated as *the old people who worked here* is that sign in its plural, and it does not mean a vanished tribe. It means the western half of one man.

## The Hundred Meters

He can only think in about a hundred meters of the four miles.

This is the bothy band, where the anticipation from the east and the insistence from the west arrive together and cancel into an ordinary present. East of it, his hands know what they will do a half-second before he does; he spends his youth flinching backward through his own mornings, and there is no reflection possible in a place where the effect keeps getting to the cause first. West of it, he works by echo. He sets a plate down and walks on and the sound of the setting reaches him from behind while he is thinking about something else, and he has learned — this is the part that is hardest to hold in your head — he has learned to *check his work by memory that has not happened yet*. An old man building cairns out of order, correcting a stack he cannot see by a clang that arrives ten paces late. It is competent. It is astonishingly competent. It is not thinking.

Thinking happens in the middle, and it happens once, and it happens to his whole life.

That is what the hundred meters are. An event at the honest grain does not stay local; it runs out along him in both directions and is true at every age. Which is why the notch is in the easternmost print, faint, and in the westernmost print, a gap you could lay a fingernail into. He was not born scarred and he was not scarred at the end. He was scarred *in the middle*, once, and the once propagated.

You can find the place. There is a cairn in the third lane north of the bothy whose daub has been pressed twice, and worked into the clay is a fine bright grit of quartzite and one flake of flint the size of a grain of rice. Beside it, a scraper at the exact stage of its life when it is sharpest and most awkward, before the hand has learned it.

He cut himself with his own tool at the honest grain, and so he has always been cut, and will always have been. Everything that happens in the hundred meters happens forever. He built the bothy there because it is the only place where a thing can happen *once*, and he built it out of wool and clay because he wanted, once, somewhere to put a thing that would not go on happening.

The masks are all in that band too. Dozens of slit-slates lying about within a stone's throw of each other, every one identical, because they are the same mask made over and over during the same decade of one man's eyesight, in the only stretch of ground where he could see well enough to cut a slot. There is one at the eastern edge of the band with the slot half-sawn. There are none at the west. He stopped wearing it. He stopped needing to look.

They fit you. Of course they fit you. Put one on and the underlight goes out from under your chin and the shelf resolves, and what resolves is not a coral or a brain but a *thinness*: you can see which cairns are carrying too much, where the stitching has gone slack, which lane is about to open a fray. That is what the mask is for. It is not protection. It is diagnostic. It is the tool of a man who has to find the one loose plate in ten thousand before it smears.

## The Unchosen

The second population has no thumbs, because it has no hands at any single age.

A fray is not a haunted stone. It is an inventory. That plate at every age it will ever have, all occupying one handful of air, is a crowd — and it is the only real crowd on the shelf, since the crowd you hear from the lip is one hand playing two hundred thousand notes a second. The hum is all of it talking at once, and it is ugly because it is *unsorted*, and everything else here is sorted.

They are not malicious. They are poor. They have nothing whatever except duration, and they are pressed up against a world where every stone has been given exactly one age and gets to be solid and quiet and counted, and they have not been given anything. When a hand goes into a fray and comes out grey and thin-nailed, or comes out small and soft and useless, nothing has been damaged. A trade has occurred. They gave the only thing they have and took the only thing you have, and they thought it was a fair exchange, and from their side it was.

The work is choosing. That is what a cairn does: it takes a stone out of the smear and says *this age, this one, now*, and the rest of that stone's lives go quiet and drift west as dust and come back at the wall as new rock with new possibilities in it. Ten thousand cairns is ten thousand decisions standing shoulder to shoulder. The shelf is a machine for turning *maybe* into *is*, and the frays are the queue, and the queue is patient in the way that only things without a single moment can be patient.

Most of what is in a fray will happen. Some of it will not. There are plates in the smears that were never quarried — a bothy with two doors, a mask with two slots, a cap-plate carved with a face. Those are the ones that hum worst, and those are the ones he pins first, and you can tell he pins them first because the cairns over them are the tightest work on the shelf.

## The Four Meters

At the western end, in the damp joints, the matte black film. Thin as soot. It does not spread. It has exactly the extent it had in the oldest tally-marks, which means it was already there when the counting started, which means the counting started *around* it.

It is the most densely inhabited place on the shelf.

Nothing here rots and nothing here has ever eaten anything, so a body does not go to soil; it goes to flour, and the flour goes east, and the wall makes it into stone again. But not all of it goes. A little of it stays in the joints where the water runs, and darkens, and keeps a habit. Four square meters, layered like soot in a flue, one thinness over another over another. On the coldest mornings the whole extent puts out a fine white fur of ice-hair, all at once, and it is the only gesture anything on this shelf has ever made that was not maintenance.

He goes there. You can follow him: the lane to the film is the only lane on the shelf that branches and does not rejoin, and it is worn deeper than any working lane, and it goes nowhere. There is a stone-wool veil at the head of it, hung on a plate. There is no dust to keep out and no face to hide. It is the one ritual in a place that has no religion, and the ritual is *not looking directly at something*, and it is practiced by a man whose entire existence is the act of saying things out loud.

He pins everything up to the boundary and no further. He has kept that margin uncounted for the whole length of himself. He does not touch it — touch is exchange, and there is nothing in there that has an age to give.

The embarrassment you feel standing near it is the embarrassment of a crowded room that looks empty. It is also, if you let yourself think it in the hundred meters, the embarrassment of watching someone visit a grave he is going to be.

Because that is the succession here. There is no breeding, no lineage, no young. There is one at a time, and when the one goes west far enough he leaves a thinness in the joints, and the next one keeps it exactly four meters and hangs a veil at the head of the lane. The film does not spread because it is *maintained*, in reverse, by the same hand that maintains everything else.

It is the entire biosphere. It is also the archive, the cemetery, and the only thing on the shelf that exists without being said.

## The Correspondence

The ringing is a reading, and a reading is done *to* someone.

Consider the cloud. It is called a resident and it behaves like one: it fills the shelf most mornings, walks a little, thins at noon, returns at dusk. It has no pitch. In a place where every solid thing has a note and every note is a place-value, the cloud is the one participant that cannot be counted, because it will not hold still to be a number. It gives water to every cold surface and takes the threads off the lip, and the sound of those threads landing comes back up two thousand meters later, out of order, softened into a crowd in another room.

That is not an echo. An echo is your own voice. This is a different crowd, and it is late, and it is answering.

Once a year the grain swings a few degrees and the districts move and ten thousand cairns have to be rebuilt along a new axis. Nothing on the shelf causes this. The water takes exactly what the wall gives; the loop is closed and steady and has no room in it for a tilt. The tilt comes from outside the accounts.

So: one party writes continuously, at a volume that fills a valley, in a notation of stacked stone, and does not pause, and has not paused since before the notch in the thumb. The other party reads it somewhere below in the bright cloud, and replies once a year, and the reply is a few degrees, and the whole shelf rebuilds itself in accordance.

That is a conversation. It has been going on for a very long time. Neither party has ever met the other, and the one who does all the writing is four miles long and cannot be in two places at once except by being spread, and the one who does all the reading has never once said anything in order.

Nothing has ever come from above. The sun is a white hole with no warmth in the look of it and the stars are out at noon and none of it is involved. Everything here arrives from the wall and leaves over the lip. Whatever the shelf is talking to, it is not up.

## The Remainder

In the bothy, with the wool taking the ringing down to a pressure behind the ears, you can hear the arithmetic, and you can hear that it does not close.

A tally that counts everything must eventually count itself and cannot. There is always one over. The wall of notches is that: courses and courses of groups of five, thousands, going up out of reach, and every group is a fresh attempt at the sum, and every group has the same remainder, and the fifth stroke is cut deeper because the fifth stroke is the one that will not go in.

The whole wall is a wall of ones.

The lamp niche never held oil because there has never been oil, and never fat, and never a wick that was not stone. Somebody cut a place for a light anyway, in a room where nothing could burn, which is the single most human act on the shelf. And at the middle ages of the gradient the niche is not empty. The copper bead is in it — brown there, the color of a closed eye — on its stone-wool thread, and the underlight comes in through the crawl-door off the wet plates and the bead throws one small warm coin onto the tally wall. It is not much. It is the only light in the only quiet room in four miles, and it is the only object here that is not for counting, unless it is: one bead, one unit, the counter wearing the remainder on his wrist where he can see it.

Bright at the east. Brown in the middle. Green further on. At the west, a verdigris ring in the clay and nothing in the ring.

And high up, in the newest course, above the reach of any hand that has ever worked here, there is a group with two strokes in it.

The clay beneath it is wet.

## The Getting Up

The hollow is warm. It is always warm, and the always is not a leftover.

The bothy stands a hand's breadth east of honest — close enough to the middle to think in, far enough east that the anticipation runs a little ahead of the event. So the warmth arrives just before the body. It has been arriving just before the body for centuries. The bed is made warm in advance, permanently, for someone who has not yet lain down and is lying down now.

Go and put your hand in it. It fits. It fits the way the mask fits, the way the lane fits, the way the scraper's worn grip fits the crook of a finger you have not yet learned to keep there.

Then go to the last cairn by the stair, the one at the head of the steps you cut, the steps that go nowhere now because the top eleven treads are buried in their own dust, the dust of a stair that has not finished being built. Ten thousand cairns and one thumb — and this one is outside the ten thousand. It is not in the tally. That is what the second stroke is.

The prints there are yours. Fresh at the lip of the shelf, then older, then older, going west, going smooth. You have no notch. You will get it in the hundred meters, from your own scraper, at the sharpest and most awkward stage of its life, and then you will have had it always, and the easternmost print of you — the one being pressed tomorrow at the wall, in clay that is still shining — will already show the faint beginning of the gap.

He is going west into flour. You are at the wall with unmarked hands. There is no handover ceremony; there is no moment when the two of you stand in the same lane, because a lane is a body wide and there is only ever one body. There is a stain at the far end that you will visit and feel embarrassed near, and a veil to hang at the head of that lane, and four square meters to keep exactly four.

The population of this shelf is one. It is never zero and it is never two, and the work has never once fallen behind, and no one is doing it.

Sit in the hollow with your ears plugged and the pressure behind them and the small warm coin of light on the wall of ones. Under the ringing, close, you will hear it: the flat *set-down* of stone on stone, the shift of weight, and a breath taken through the nose by someone lifting.

It is yours. You have not taken it yet.
