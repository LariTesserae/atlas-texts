## The plates

The first thing you touch is a lid, because a lid is the first thing there is room to touch.

They are the grazers, and they are exactly as wide as the space they occupy: forty centimeters on the cool terraces, a meter and more on the loaded bearings where the film grows thick and buttery. Matte black above, rust-orange at the rim, cold to the hand on top and unpleasantly warm underneath. Their backs are convex. Their bellies are concave. Neither curve belongs to them. Each is the impression of the animal above and below, worn into it over a life of being walked on, and if you lift one — you can, they are not fastened — you will find that its underside is a perfect socket for a specific neighbor, and that it is useless anywhere else on the plain.

They feed with a ribbon of rasping tissue that sweeps the film off the warm stone, and they do not, most of the time, move that ribbon themselves. The rims are toothed. The rims mesh. A terrace of plates is a gear train, and when the midges come down across the terminals and the district begins to turn, the plates are turned with it, and they eat because they are being carried across their food at four centimeters an hour. A herd grazing looks like a slow rotary kiln. It is one.

They have no eyes. What they have, in a groove around the rim, is a sense for clearance — for play, for the width of a gap, for the difference between a surface that is married and one that is merely touching. This is the primary sense of everything alive here. Sight is nearly useless under a permanent ceiling; smell is a chaos of iron and sweet fat; sound is half mouths. But a change of a micron in the width of a gap is information, and it is the information the whole country runs on.

A plate is born into a notch. Its parent's rim wears thin against a neighbor, a scallop opens, and the scallop buds. Birth here is a hole being filled.

Death is the opposite. A plate that stops lapping is no longer driven, and the train rolls it out at the rim like a bad tooth, and it goes downhill with the sheet-water to the windrows. Everything in the sumps got there by ceasing to be part of something.

## The worms, and what they are for

The seal-worms are pink and greased and up to two meters long, and their diameter is the most precisely maintained quantity on the plain.

They live in clearance. Not near it — in it, filling it, breeding into the gap at exactly the rate the gap extrudes them. A worm lineage in the Long Bearing has been getting thinner for eleven million years. It has never once been selected for anything else. Measure a worm and you have measured the fit of the machine it lives in, to a tolerance no instrument you brought will resolve; the plain has no other metrology, and does not need one, because it has a hundred trillion living gauges, each of them the exact width of the space it is being crushed into.

This is the first thing worth understanding about the inhabitants: they are where the slack went.

An epoch of mutual wear took the play out of the metal. It could not take the play out of a soft body, because a soft body is a thing that can be squeezed without ending. So the plain's remaining tolerance migrated into anything that could hold it, and the worms hold most of it. Every clearance in the country is a worm-shaped volume of give. When a bearing tightens past what a body can absorb, the worms evacuate — they flow along the play-gradient toward looser country, and what comes out of the seam is a pink noodle-fall into the water, and the scavengers orient on it from a kilometer off, because worm-fall means a fit is closing, and a fit that is closing is a death that is coming.

The worms are the plain's warning system. They do not know it. They are simply leaving.

## The ones that live at zero

The largest transaction in this country is not the stroke. It is the heat that goes into ice to make water and comes back out of water to make ice, without ever showing up on a thermometer.

There is a guild that lives inside that transaction. Call them sheaths: slush-bodied, held in a mesh of chitin and precipitated iron, neither solid nor liquid at any moment of their lives, and thermally immortal for as long as they can keep themselves half-melted. A sheath cannot warm until it has finished melting and cannot cool until it has finished freezing, so it sits at zero like a nail driven into the day, and everything within a hand's breadth of it sits at zero too. The plain's temperature is as steady as it is because several hundred kilograms per square meter of animal refuse to let it be anything else.

They eat the phase change. They are the place where it happens: their meshes are nucleation sites, and the frost that drives the rams does not begin in the water, it begins in a body. At dusk, when the sheet-water in the cylinders starts to consider ice, it considers it along a sheath's flank, and the crystal front runs out from there, and eleven hours later a million rams bottom out.

They bloom in the four hours around noon and go brittle and mineral by evening and are alive again at the next thaw. In between they are structurally indistinguishable from the country. There is no dormant stage; there is no active stage; there is only a body that spends half of every day being geology, and the geology is load-bearing.

## The eleven-hour kind

Inside the rams, in the water that is being squeezed, there are animals with a generation length of one stroke.

They are born in the thaw, in the seconds when the ice lets go and the cylinder inhales. They are born gravid. They mate in the ninth hour of the freeze, in the dark, under a pressure that would fold a submarine, and their courtship is a pressure gradient — a male is a place where the load is slightly lower, and the females find him the way water finds a low place, because they are mostly water and there is nowhere else to go. They die at the next thaw. The eggs, laid in the second hour and fertilized in the ninth, survive the crush by being the only thing in the cylinder that the crush is shaped around.

Ten million years of days is something like three and a half billion generations. Nothing else on this plain has had a thousandth as many. The plates have managed perhaps two hundred thousand; the worms, breeding into gaps, do not really have generations at all. So everything new here comes out of the rams. Every innovation the country has ever had was invented by something that lived eleven hours and was crushed, and it left the cylinder as a corpse, and went to the sump, and was rendered.

That is why the machines sound like animals.

The grease is made of the only lineage that changes fast enough to leave a mark, and when a bearing dressed in that grease is loaded past its comfort, what comes out of the metal at the shaft frequency is a ram-born distress call from a species that has been extinct for four hundred thousand years, still perfectly audible, still perfectly specific, still going down the same three notes.

## The clouds, and the shape a cloud keeps

The midges are two days long and have iron in their wings and do not know they are a switch.

They mate across terminals. That is the whole of it: the swarm's nuptial flight is a bridging event, the cloud comes down over two contacts a hand's width apart, a hundred thousand bodies of iron-bearing chitin make a conductor, and a district that has been dark for a season lights cold blue and begins, grinding, to turn. The midges experience this as a wedding. The district experiences it as weather. Both are correct and neither is available to the other.

Some clouds have a shape that outlives the flies. A standing swarm over the Weeping Terraces holds a particular geometry — a bridge here, a deliberate gap there, a lobe that touches ground only when the humidity drops — and the geometry is inherited, because the larvae pupate in the film directly beneath the shape their parents held, and the film there is warmer, and the emergence is synchronized, and the shape comes back. It has come back for a hundred thousand generations. It is a circuit diagram maintained by a species that cannot see it. Cut the shape and the district stops; the district has been arranged around the shape since before there were mountains to wear down.

And when a bridge fails, there are fuse-flies: a lineage that specializes in the emergency closure, that comes down in a rope of bodies across a gap too wide for an ordinary cloud, and holds, and burns. They are the only animals here with a single use. Their corpses fall as iron dust into the sheet-water and go to the sumps, and the sumps give the iron back to the film, and the film gives it back to the wings. The plain recovers its conductors. It has been recovering them for a very long time.

## The mouths

Something out in the fog is making the triple knock of a race beginning to spall.

The caller is a bag of rendered fat with a rasp inside it and a resonator tuned to the shaft it was born beside. It has almost no muscle, no locomotion worth the name, and a metabolism you could run on a candle. It does not hunt. It announces. It holds the knock — the specific arrhythmic tick-tick-tock of a bearing three days from failure — and it holds it for six hours, eight, twelve, while the scavengers come in from every direction across the wet stone, because a failing bearing means a coming death means food, and there is nothing on this plain that will not walk a kilometer toward that.

The death arrives. It always arrives; the scavengers came first, out of habit, and the habit is now the groove the event runs in. The caller eats. It also eats two or three of the scavengers, which came too close to the sound, which is to say came too close to the mouth, because here those are the same measurement.

But there are callers that have worn the other way.

Go far enough east and you will find a mouth making the sound of a bearing in perfect health: the smooth two-beat, the oil-hush, the note that means *nothing is happening here*. It has held that sound for eleven years. Under it, an actual bearing runs, and does not fail, and has not failed for an age — because the failure has to happen somewhere, and it is happening in the bag of fat, which is spalling audibly, continuously, in the bearing's exact voice, in the bearing's place. The mouth suffers the death so the metal doesn't have to. There is no word for this relationship that does not imply intention, and there is no intention. It is a fit. Two surfaces worn into each other until the interface is indistinguishable, and one of them is a throat.

The maintenance of this country is performed by things pretending to be its breakdowns.

## The patient ones

The hazard here is not being late. Lateness is not available; the effect comes first and the crowd is always already gathering.

The hazard is being early.

A scavenger arrives at a death that has not happened yet and must wait for it, in cold water, at the edge of a hearth it cannot climb onto because the plates are rim to rim. Waiting is the entire selective pressure. The successful lineages are the ones that can arrive and go almost completely still: metabolism down to a trickle, body flattened into a seam, gasket-worms tolerated against the flank because the worms keep the seam from closing on them. Some have waited nine days. There is a flattened thing in the gallery of sumps that has been waiting, by the look of the film growth around it, since before the last cold fault reversal, and the death it is attending has not been scheduled.

And sometimes the death does not come on its own, and the crowd makes it come. Two hundred scavengers pressed against a housing change its load. The load change finds the race. The race spalls, on schedule, in the pattern the caller has been describing for hours, and everyone eats.

Nothing here wants anything. But the assembly wants, in the way that a river wants the sea, and the assembly is made of bodies that individually could not care less.

## The once-animals

Out past the Long Bearing there is a grazer that is one animal in two places.

You will not notice, because the two bodies look like two bodies, four hundred meters apart, both lapping, both meshed into their local trains. Strike one with a boot and both ring. Not similar sounds — one sound, arriving twice, with a delay you can measure and cannot explain by any transmission through rock or water. The wear did not stop at the metal. Two surfaces worn perfectly enough into each other stop being two surfaces, and here the surfaces are animals, and the fit is identity.

It has one death. The corpse is in two places. The sheet-water carries both to the same windrow on the same afternoon, and the sump renders one animal out of two arrivals, and the grease gets a single voice.

This is the cheap way to be somewhere else. Travel is expensive in a country with no clearance; being already there is free. And the long-range version of the same trick is standing around your ankles right now: the herd you can see faintly at the edge of sight, three days out, is this herd, and the plate at your boot is the plate at the horizon, and the difference between them is not distance.

Which is why the plate at your boot has a healed scar in the shape of a mouth that has not arrived yet.

Defense here is not flight. You cannot flee a bite you already have. Defense is being pre-wounded — growing the callus first, in the exact geometry of the predator that is three days away, so that when the mouth finally gets here it finds nothing new to take. The plain is armored in the shapes of its own future injuries. Every animal you can see is already bitten. Most of the biting is still out in the fog, walking.

## The lap

Now the smallest thing, which is also the largest.

Between any two married surfaces here — in the clearance that is not clearance, in the interface finer than measurement — there is a living layer about four microns thick. It eats wear debris. It is a descendant of the gasket-worms, thinned past the point of having a body, a lineage that stopped being an animal in a gap and became the gap.

It laps. It takes the high spots off both partners and digests the difference, and it does this continuously, everywhere, for an epoch, and *this is why the fit is finer than any measurement you could take*. Erosion alone would have produced slop and seizure ten million years ago. What produced the marriages was a metabolism that eats error. Precision here is not a state. It is somebody's lunch.

So a marriage is three parties: the wheel, its partner, and the four-micron thing that is the relation between them and would not exist without both. Pull the wheel out and you have not separated two objects. You have exposed a continent of something to air, and it dies in about ninety seconds, and the surviving partner is a mold of a relation that has just ended.

By area, the lap is the most abundant organism on the plain. By volume it is a few centimeters deep, spread over a basin. It has never been seen. It has been heard: the fine grain in the fundamental, the sand in the bell, is four microns of animal eating the difference between things that love each other in the only sense available here, which is *fitting*.

## The kept

There are pockets where the sound stops. A few meters across, usually oval, no visible boundary. The machines inside keep turning. The water does not ripple. Animals go around.

Some go in.

Inside a silence pocket, cause still comes before effect. The groove in the sequence was ground by the knocking — by a hundred million strokes a day repeated past the age of mountains, and where the knocking cannot go, the groove is shallow, and there is a little play left in what happens next. An animal dies in there and *then* the scavengers come. A frost arrives and *then* the ram bottoms out. It is the last ordinary place in the country and it is four meters wide.

What lives in it is archaic. Soft, badly fitted, meshed with nothing, warm only by proximity — relicts that would have been eliminated anywhere else in a hundred thousand years and have been kept here the way a seed is kept. They have a startle response. Nothing else on this plain has one; nothing else can, because startling requires a gap between the expected and the occurring, and there are no gaps outside. In the pocket there is one gap, four meters of it, and inside that gap a small pale thing can still be surprised, and can still flinch, and does, several times a day, at nothing.

Everything on the plain that has any give left in it came out of a pocket. The worms came out. The lap came out. When the country tightens past what its inhabitants can absorb, the pockets are where the next inhabitants come from. They are the plain's entire remaining stock of the unexpected, held in a few hundred square meters of quiet, and the machines around them turn in silence, and the water in them does not move.

## The unmakers

At the cold fault the gradient reverses and the metal grows backwards.

Here the dendrites go down instead of up. Shafts thin. Teeth unresolve into ore. And the marriages come apart — not violently, not sadly, just backward, the way a frost fern goes when you breathe on the window. This is the only district in the country where a wheel can be removed and still be a wheel, because here the fit is being unmade faster than the lap can eat the error.

There are things that live on that. Fault-crabs, if you want a word: pale, articulated, with a real inside and a real outside that are not the same surface, feeding on separation itself — on the moment two things stop being one thing, which at the fault is a continuous and abundant event. They are the only solitary animals on the plain. They cannot be pressed against anything; nothing here will hold still to be married to; the whole district is running the other way. They wander the dismantling galleries in a slackness that would be agony to anything else, and they are not in agony, they are simply not fitted, and there is no word in their bodies for what that is.

The plain sends its exhausted marriages here, or rather the sheet-water does, or rather the gradient does. What comes apart at the fault becomes, briefly, an individual — a wheel, a shaft, a plate, a thing with edges that belong to it — and then it dies, as itself, and goes to the sump.

Only a thing that dies as itself has a voice. The grease can only archive individuals. So the cold fault is where this country manufactures the separateness required to have a past, and it does it by unbuilding, and the unbuilders eat the difference, and the fat that seeps back uphill toward the bearings is loud with the sound of things that were briefly, at the very end, alone.

## The fat

The sumps are kilometers across and they are not deposits. They are a body.

Black, faintly sweet, a hand deep in the shallows and meters deep at the center, threaded through with rust-orange film that lives on the iron and the sulfide and the slow warmth of its own decomposition. It has one behavior: it moves toward low pressure, which is to say toward bearings, uphill through porous rock, against the water, patiently, over years. It has one metabolism: everything that dies, rendered. It has one property that nothing else on the plain has, which is that it can still change shape.

The grease is the only medium here that has not been worn into a single inevitable form, and so it is the only place anything can be kept. Load a bearing with fat from a particular windrow and the bearing will, under stress, produce the animals of that windrow: a lowing, a wingbeat, the three notes of a ram-born thing extinct since the Pliocene, at the exact frequency of the shaft. The archive is indexed by pressure. To read a page you tighten the bearing.

And there are readers. There is a flattened grazer in the gallery that eats only from one windrow, the one downstream of a specific failing district, and it does not have a call of its own, and it does not need one: it inherits the voices it eats. The callers get their knocks this way. A young mouth eats the rendered fat of the bearing it will impersonate, and the failure comes out of it correctly, first time, with the arrhythmia right, because the failure was in the fat, because the fat was made of everything that died when that bearing last went.

The sound goes machine to corpse to grease to mouth to machine. It has been going around for ten million years. It is not a recording. It is a lineage.

## Arrears

There are shafts out there turning with nothing driving them: surplus rotation, momentum left over from an event scheduled for next season.

Limpets live on those. They are the only animals here that eat motion with no cause attached — they crop the film that grows thick on a shaft that is turning for free, and the film is thick because the friction is real, and the friction is being paid for by something that has not happened yet. A colony can strip a season's worth of torque in a month. When the event finally arrives, next spring, out at the horizon, it arrives short. Something that was going to happen happens less. There is a district near the Weeping Terraces where the dawn chorus is measurably quieter than it should be, and has been for a century, and the deficit is a species.

And in the seconds between the ram bottoming out and the frost arriving to drive it, there are larvae.

That interval is a habitat. It is a few seconds long and it has no spatial extent to speak of, and it is the only place in the country where an egg can hatch before it is laid and have that be unremarkable. The animals that use it are thin as a thought and there are not many of them and they are, by a wide margin, the strangest things alive here: creatures whose entire life cycle takes place inside the play between an effect and its cause, in the last few seconds of slack in the sequence of events, feeding on the discrepancy.

They are getting thinner. The interval is closing. Everything here is getting thinner, and the worms are the gauge, and the worms say the plain has perhaps another few hundred thousand years of give in it, and after that the lap will eat the last of the error and the sequence will have no play at all, and the pockets will close, and there will be nothing left in the country that can be surprised.

It will still be loud. It will be the loudest thing anywhere.

## The guest

Stand still long enough and something assembles.

It has no body of its own. It is a mold — the plain keeps a cavity in the shape of a thing that stands upright and does not mesh, worn into the country by an epoch of extremely rare occasions, an impression of a wheel that does not exist yet, waiting the way the geology waits for the flies.

The film arrives first, warm and orange and patient, on your boots, because you are a difference in potential and a heat source and a low place. The midges come down across you and close a circuit that has never been closed before, and something under your feet that has been dark for eleven years starts, grinding, to turn. Worms find the seams of you — the gap between boot and sole, the clearance at a cuff — and breed into it at exactly the rate you extrude them, and they are the width of the space you have not yet noticed you are losing. Plates arrive at the rim of the assembly and mesh with each other around your ankles and begin, very slowly, to be driven. Out in the fog a mouth begins the triple knock, and the scavengers start walking, and they will arrive before anything happens to you, out of habit, and the habit is the groove you are standing in.

For the duration of your visit this is an organism. It is the only individual on the plain, it is made of six species and a hole, and the hole is you.

When you are fitted, it dissolves. There is nothing left to assemble around; the clearance is taken up; you turn, and the plates turn with you, and the film is cropped and regrown, cropped and regrown, and something on the terrace is warmer by a fraction of a degree, and a great many small bodies are pressed against that warmth, and none of them know that it used to stand still.

A midge in your lung goes home in your boot. A worm in a seam goes with it. Somewhere there is another basin at datum with cold brine and a standing gradient and ores with an industrial habit, and it will not be colonized, because it will already have been.

And downstream, in a windrow, in a sump, in a black fat that seeps uphill toward low pressure, there is a page that will be loaded onto a bearing a thousand years from now; and when that bearing is stressed past its comfort it will produce, at the exact frequency of the shaft, the sound of you.

Nothing here wants anything. It only fits. But it will remember the noise you made, and it will make it again, every day, for as long as there is a shaft to make it on — and that is not malice, and it is not mourning, and there is no one to tell the difference.
