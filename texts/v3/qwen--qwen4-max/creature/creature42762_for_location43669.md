## The count

Three numbers go up on the assay board at the head of the main cut, written in grease pencil on a slate that is itself turned quarterly: the minutes of sun, the fish, and the people. Last year: thirty-one minutes and eleven seconds; two hundred and nine fish; four hundred and six people. Nobody in Brome has ever had to ask what the numbers are. Children learn them in that order, and the order is a joke that has survived sixty-one years, because the sun is going and the fish are not and the people are somewhere in between.

They are a small, pale, unhurried population with wide pupils and a permanent sideways tilt to the shoulders. You can identify a Bromer on the plain at forty paces by the walk: the feet go down flat and careful, the torso leads slightly, the right hand drifts out to touch things and never rests on them. It is not a limp. It is a way of moving through a place that is narrower than a body and always slightly different from yesterday. Up on the steppe they look like people easing past furniture in a dark house.

Their voices are low and their vowels are long, and they do not interrupt, because interrupting means raising the volume and raising the volume means the crust. Their applause is silent: hands raised, wrists rotated, four slow turns. It is the same gesture they use on a valve wheel, on a milestone, on each other.

## Hands

Look at the hands and you know the altitude.

Terrace Two hands are soft. The assay clerks and the shift-bosses live above the weep line, in the cool dry marl, where the accretion is slow enough that a cup may sit on a shelf overnight and be only frosted by morning. Their knuckles are pink. Their fingernails are ordinary. They can wear a ring for a year.

Pan-crew hands are grained. In the creases of the knuckles, in the pads beside the nail, along the old scars, the salt finds the places where a body is most nearly still and begins there: a fine glassy stipple, like frost bitten into the skin, warm to the touch, faintly blue where it is thick. It does not hurt. It has to be pared, weekly, with a hardwood scraper, in the hour after the fog is wrung, and everybody does it, and everybody watches everybody else do it, and the parings go into the sump.

The old crews have hands that ring when they tap a rail. Nane Roke, sixty-eight smoothings, who has walked the turning rounds since she was nine, has thumbs that are more mineral than thumb, and can tell the temperature of a wall to within a degree by laying the back of one against it, and cannot any longer feel a wet cloth.

*Show me your hands* is what a mother says to a suitor. It is not a metaphor. It means: how far down do you live, how much of you has already been taken, how much turning will you need.

## The rounds

The most respectable employment in the Kettle is giving things a quarter turn.

Nine thousand four hundred items are on the sticks. The tally is kept in felt, four books a day, and the books are read by the assay office because the assay office reads everything. Valve wheels, milestones, boots, hinge-pins, the eleven cats, the pump-house gecko (listed as *gecko, one or more*), the sixty-one fixed objects that are people.

The rounds are worked by children from seven smoothings and by the old, in pairs, four shifts, and the pairs are always one of each. The child carries the stick and does the low work. The elder does the shoulders.

To greet someone in Brome you reach out and turn their shoulder a quarter, gently, through the cloth. It is a handshake and it is a sentence. It means *I am keeping you from being copied*. It means *stay approximate*. Lovers do it slowly. Mothers do it absently, twenty times an hour, to a child on the hip, so that a Bromer infant is rotated more or less continuously through its first year and grows up with a vestibular sense that visitors find eerie: they do not get dizzy, ever, in any direction.

The marriage formula is four words long. *I will turn you.*

The highest compliment payable to a turner is that they are *light*. Nane Roke is light. She can turn a sleeping man's shoulder without waking him, and she can do it in a stairwell hammock at forty-six degrees with the man's arm across his face, and she has done it four times a day for fifty-nine years to something on the order of a hundred thousand sleepers, and not one of them has ever grown a second self in the night.

## The still

There is a sickness here and it has no fever.

A man stops moving. Not dramatically — he does not lie down in the street. He sits in a chair that he has taken off its rope, and he sits in it after shift, and he sits in it the next day, and the felt begins at the back of his neck where the sweat dries slowest, and the salt finds the creases beside his nose.

Corr Ambry did it in his fortieth smoothing, after his wife went into the niches. He was a paddle-hand on Pan Four. He sat. The town did not argue with him and did not send for anyone. It put him on the stick.

For a year and a half, four times a day, a child came and turned him. Bel, nine smoothings, did the second shift, and the instruction she was given was the standard one: *shoulder, knee, the head if he lets you, and don't talk unless he talks first.* He let her turn his head. He talked about the fish. He had opinions about the fish. By the second autumn he was arguing with her about the count, and an argument is motion, and the rounds supervisor struck him off the stick, and he went back to the paddles, and he is alive now and grained to the wrist and remarried.

This is the whole of Brome's medicine. There is no physician. There is a list of people who have stopped, and a rota of people who go and move them, and the deeply understood principle that grief is a form of stillness and stillness is the only thing here that can kill you before the heat does.

Not everyone is caught in time. There are copies of people in the Understreets, hollow, translucent, more precise than the originals — the mole, the crooked incisor, the exact way the left shoulder rode higher. Most are broken up at once, quietly, with wooden mallets, and the pieces go to the sump. Some are not. There is a woman on Terrace Six who keeps her husband's second self on a rope in the stairwell, swinging, and turns it herself, four times a day, and has for nine smoothings, and no one has ever told her to stop, and no one has ever asked her why.

And there are the pairs: two people who lie very still together, for a long time, in a warm place, on purpose. The town officially disapproves. The town also knows exactly where three of them are, and does not go there, and lets the Smoothing take them every autumn, and does not save them, and speaks about it never.

## The others

The eleven cats are pale, half-feral, and named by everybody: Chalk, Widow, Ninepence, Sump, Bellwether, Two Bellwether, Aunt, the Other One, and three whose names are disputes rather than names. They are on the sticks. A cat that sleeps all day on a warm ledge is a cat that will wake up beside a better cat, and so the rounds turn the cats, and the cats permit it with the contempt of animals who have decided that a species has a religion. When one dies the shift bells are tapped once with a wooden mallet and the whole town stops for the length of the note.

The gecko has never been settled. The assay office has been asked, formally, four times, to publish a number, and has four times declined, and the refusal is now itself a fixture of the ledger, entered annually, turned quarterly.

The fish are the point of honour. Two hundred and nine, blind, pale, no eyes worth the name, in the sump, which is the only body of water in the Kettle that is *under*saturated at its top and therefore the only place where nothing accretes. There are no copies of the fish. There never will be. They are the only residents who cannot be improved, and the town's love for them is out of all proportion to their beauty, which is nil, and the arguments about them are conducted year-round with the seriousness other places reserve for elections. There is a faction that holds the count is meaningless because the lit board summons the same two hundred fish and misses the shy ones. There is a faction that holds the shy ones are the count.

And under all of them, in every sense, the felt: the true majority, the overwhelming biomass, olive-black and smelling of low tide, roofing the pipes, wrapping the dead, taking the ink of every ledger. It is not one organism and it is not four; it is a bargain between an alga, a fungus, an archaeon and a mineral, and it has been keeping that bargain here longer than the company has. If you are honest about the census — and the assay office is honest, it is the only virtue it has — the four hundred and six are a minority population living inside something else, and the something else drinks their fog, eats their sweat, and provides their paper, their shrouds, and their silence.

## The Patient

Three hundred and six, in niches above the weep line, in the cool dry marl where the copying takes forty years instead of one.

The town tends them on a rota. Soft brush, dry felt, no water ever. The brushing is done by the young, usually, and by anyone who has recently done something they are ashamed of, and the two categories overlap more than the town admits.

The oldest, from the eleven seconds of the Drop, have gone almost entirely to mineral and are extraordinarily beautiful and do not resemble anybody. They resemble *someone*. That is the difficulty. The salt renders the eyelashes and the weave of the felt and then it keeps going, past the person, into what it thinks should be there: a lace collar the woman never owned, a signet on a hand that never wore one, a second child in the arms of a woman who had one.

Officially this is chemistry. The assay office has a page on it, with the word *habit* used in its crystallographic sense, and a footnote about lattice memory, and it is a good page and nobody reads it. What people actually do is go up to the niches and speak to their dead at the correct low volume, which is the volume you use so that the sound does not carry through the crystal to the next room, and which is also, everyone understands without ever having said it, the volume you use so as not to wake anyone.

The hard ones are the fresh. A woman nine smoothings dead still has her face, and the salt has begun at the eyelashes, and her husband can see exactly how much of her is still hers and how much is now the town's other resident getting her right.

The funeral is the last turning. Four quarter turns, by the four nearest, and then the hands come off, and the not-touching begins, and the not-touching is the burial.

## The Fair Copy

Here is what nobody in Brome can turn: the buildings.

You cannot rotate a stairwell. You cannot give a pipe-bridge a quarter turn, or a terrace, or the drowned chapel with a tamarisk in its roof. The town has arranged for every movable thing to move and has simply had to accept that the immovable things are still, and have been still for sixty-one years, and are being copied.

In the twenty-second smoothing a cutting gang went through a wall in the Understreets and came out in their own room. Their room, empty, in halite, translucent, warm, the grain of the beam rendered exactly, the hook where the cup hangs rendered exactly, and no cup, because the cup swings.

They have mapped ninety-one chambers of it since. The assay office keeps the plan in felt and it is the only document in Brome drawn twice: the town, and beside it, at the same scale, the town's fair copy.

The Fair Copy contains only what stays still. No hammocks, no slung chairs, no hanging tools, no cats, no people, no felt — the felt moves, it creeps a millimetre a week, and the salt will not render a thing that creeps. So it is Brome with everything alive taken out: the fixtures, the bones, the shoulders of the buildings welded at the shoulder, every railing's caterpillar of crystal rendered in crystal, which is a joke nobody has ever laughed at out loud.

It is also better. The doorways are square. The main way runs straight for thirty metres, which no main way in Brome has ever done. There is a chamber at the confluence of the three lower cuts that corresponds to nothing at all in the town above it: an empty space, four paces by nine, with nothing in it and nothing hanging in it and no pipe crossing it. Like the lace collar. Like the signet ring. Something the salt has decided should be there.

They call it the Market, because the only open space anyone in Brome has ever heard of is the Sunday market that went down in eleven seconds before any of them were born.

It is the one room in the Kettle where you can lean on a wall.

People go there. Mourners go there. Lovers go there, and are not followed, and the cutting gang has standing instructions to route around it, and the instructions are renewed every generation and have never once been written down.

There are niches in the Fair Copy. They are above nothing, in a wall that has no weep line, and they are the right size. Somebody counted them, once, in the forty-fourth smoothing, and got a number close enough to four hundred that nobody has counted them since, and the fact that nobody has counted them since is the single most agreed-upon thing in the entire town, more than the fish, more than the sun.

## What lives there

So: four hundred and six people, eleven cats, an unsettled number of geckos, two hundred and nine blind fish, nine days a year of brine flies, and a consortium the size of a hillside that has agreed with itself about how to be alive.

And one more resident, which is not alive, and which is the oldest thing in the hole by two hundred million years, and which is warm, and wet, and rising, and paying attention.

It is not hostile. Nobody in Brome thinks it is hostile, and the assay office is right about this if it is right about nothing. It does not want them dead. It wants them *still*, which is a different thing, and it wants them still for the same reason a portraitist wants a sitter to hold the pose: because it is trying to get them right, and it is very patient, and it has all the time there is, and it improves as it goes.

That is the actual situation of the people here, underneath the shifts and the fog-wringing and the arguments about the fish. There are two kinds of love in the Kettle and they are indistinguishable from the inside and exactly opposite in their methods. One of them comes four times a day and puts a hand on your shoulder and moves you a quarter turn so that you will stay blurred and warm and slightly wrong and alive. The other one waits until you stop, and then renders your eyelashes, and then gives you the collar you never had.

The town has ninety-four years of sunlight left. It knows the arithmetic; it discusses it the way you discuss a relative's long illness; it will not cut the notch, because the notch would take the cold end, and the cold end is the light they have now. What it will do instead is what it did this morning: cut the streets back to a metre, wring the fog hand over hand at the cold hour, publish the fish, brush the Patient, put the children out with the sticks.

Somewhere below the Understreets the other Brome is getting wider and straighter and more exact, and has a market square, and has no one in it.

And in a stairwell on Terrace Eight, at the fourth shift, a girl of nine smoothings reaches into a hammock and turns a sleeping man's shoulder, gently, without waking him, and the salt, three rooms away, at forty cycles, in the dark, notes that it has lost that one again, and goes on working on the ones that hold still.
