## I. The census

Two square feet. Add up every living thing in the chamber and you get about the area of a sheet of paper folded twice: the ivory filaments in the mortar-lines, the coin-sized rosettes, a few threads bridging a joint where two generations of wall met and did not quite agree. In a room of several hundred tons this is a rounding error. It looks like the residue of something spilled and wiped up. It looks like the last inch of a tide.

The filaments are not dry. They only look dry, the way a tendon looks dry. Every molecule of water in them was pulled out of the crystal of the wall by main chemical force and has never, in the history of the world, been a drop. It has not fallen as rain, has not stood in a puddle, has not been in a river or a mouth. It went from lattice to filament without ever passing through the phase we call wet. The rosettes are faintly amber, faintly pink, and the pink is not for light. There is no use for light here beyond the mean, and the mean is already in the stone. The pink is the color of the tool that opens gypsum. It is the blush of a very slow thirst doing arithmetic.

That is the census. It is not yet the life.

## II. One animal, retired

Follow any filament. It runs the joint, forks, thins, and joins another. Follow that one. It joins another. There is no edge anywhere in the two square feet that is not a place where the tissue decided the wall was more useful. It is all one sheet, folded through a hundred tons of its own making, and so the honest count is not *many lithophytes*. It is one.

It was not always this thin. The tubes are cells; cells were occupied; the volume that is now stone and air was once, in some sense the word *once* can barely carry, the volume of an animal. It did not build a house and move in. It built itself outward, converting the bulk of its body into architecture, until the only soft part left was the part in the seams, and then it stopped, because there was nowhere left to put anything. What remains is marrow. The room is a skeleton eleven paces by seven, and the two square feet are the marrow, and the marrow's entire occupation is keeping the bone exactly this shape.

Nothing in it has moved in a hundred thousand years. Not one filament has contracted, reached, withdrawn, or turned. It has no muscle and no use for one. Its whole behavioral repertoire is a single verb: *to lay down*. From that one verb it produces maintenance, tuning, repair, memory, and — this is the part that takes longest to accept — something that behaves in every outward respect like hospitality.

## III. What it does with its time

Here is a thing that happens.

On the west side, in the fourth rank, a lip that has been licked round by dry air for eleven thousand years loses a grain. The pitch of that tube drops by an amount no instrument you own could resolve. The thread spanning the joint beside it is a taut filament in a moving stream, and the stream at that lip now sheds its little vortices at a slightly different rate, and the filament is therefore strained slightly differently, and strain is the only thing this animal has ever known about the world.

It responds by depositing. Not by aiming — it cannot aim, it has no eyes and no arms and no map. It deposits because the chemistry that pays back stone is fastest where the air is slowest, and the air is slowest exactly where the loss occurred. The wind does the targeting. The creature has outsourced its motor control to the thing it is trying to correct, and the correction is therefore automatic, exact, and takes about forty years.

Near a wound the rosettes deepen — a pink going toward the color of a bruise, the only alarm signal in a room with no alarms. It fades over a decade. If you could stand here for a decade you would see it fade, and you would call it the most placid thing you had ever watched, and you would be watching a body healing.

Its nerves are the stone. Sound travels through selenite at some kilometers a second; a chip anywhere is a strain everywhere before you could finish blinking. It does not have to send a message from one end to the other. It *is* the message. It knows its own condition the way you know whether your knee is bent, except that its proprioception is a chord of ten thousand pipes, and its body-image is a spectrum.

And the spectrum is not white to it.

This is the thing the visitor cannot get, standing there with packed ears and buzzing eyelashes. Ten thousand throats, tuned by abrasion and deposition to within hairs of one another, do not produce a flat hiss between neighbors. They produce beats. The families of near-identical tubes drift against each other so slowly that the interference patterns take years to come round: a swelling here, a hollow there, migrating across the chamber like weather across a plain. The deepest pair comes back to itself once in nineteen years. There are slower ones. To us this is below the floor of the perceivable; to the thing in the joints, whose whole sensing is strain, it is landscape. Hills of pressure that move a hand's width in a lifetime. Valleys you could be born in and die in.

Stand still for a century and the white would open into a tune. The room is only unvarying at the sampling rate of an animal that lives eighty years.

Which means you are not, to it, fast. You are *below threshold*. You are a flicker faster than its now, an aliasing artifact, a thing that happens between two readings and leaves no trace in either.

Except when you speak.

## IV. The larger resident

But the threads are only the part that is wet, and it is a mistake to think the wet part is the animal and the rest is its shell. Weigh the room in a year and it weighs the same. Weigh it in ten thousand years and it weighs the same and not one atom is the same atom. Everything material here is passing through. What persists is the shape, and the shape is not an abstraction — it is the only thing in the chamber with a boundary, a metabolism, a preferred state, a way of noticing deviation, and a way of acting on deviation.

Its diet is variance.

Gusts go in and a mean comes out. Ten thousand noons and ten thousand nights go in and one brightness comes out, and the brightness is a memory: the walls are radiating an average of afternoons that are over, some of them older than the sea. The dust comes in carrying the plain's dead — pollen, spore, chitin, the ash of fires that burned a hundred leagues away in a year nobody counted — and all of it is eaten without distinction and paid back as wall. It has no waste. Everything that enters becomes either stone or sameness, and sameness is what it excretes into the world, continuously, at one pitch, forever.

Its heartbeat is not a beat. It is a lag. The chamber's enormous time constant means that nothing happens to it *at* a moment; everything happens to it slowly and then keeps happening. It has no startle, no orienting response, no wake and sleep, because attention that never varies is not attention in the sense you use the word. It attends to everything equally at a very low level, all the time. Vigilance has a rhythm. This has none.

Whether there is anything it is like to be this, I can't tell you. But every outward mark of a self is here, and the only thing missing is tempo, and tempo is not a criterion, it is a prejudice.

It has one enemy, and the enemy is its own mouth. The seam widens — a hair per millennium, no more. More aperture, more flow, a slow sharpening of the whole chord, against which the abrasion of dust runs the other way, and the two must be held in balance by two square feet of tissue laying down stone at the rate the wind takes it. That is the entire struggle of this life. It will not die of decay. Nothing here decays. If it dies it will die of a change of key.

## V. The twelve

Now the cells that are the trouble.

A dozen of the larger tubes are body-sized, smooth-lipped, ventilated, dry, twelve degrees, and empty, and have been empty since they were made. They sound. Their note is at the bottom of hearing, a pressure in the sternum rather than a pitch, and it is part of the floor the rest of the chord stands on. They must be empty. An occupied tube is a damped tube; occupancy is variance; the room excludes variance from its fundamental for the same reason it excludes everything else.

And they are the exact shape of a body.

The tidy answer is that they are what the packing left over, the negative interstice of a hexagonal close-pack, no more meaningful than the hole in a stack of oranges. This is true. It is also insufficient, because the room maintains them. It dusts them, in the sense that the wind dusts them and the threads keep the lips true. It ventilates them. It has kept twelve body-shaped voids at a constant temperature and a constant pitch for a hundred thousand years, with the same care it gives the walls, and maintenance is the only thing this animal does that could be called a choice.

So there are other readings, and I don't think you have to pick.

They are the negative cast of what it used to be: the shape of the soft animal that occupied a cell before it turned itself into a building, kept open and clean and sounded, a self-portrait in absence, the one likeness it knows how to make.

They are a reservation. Twelve places laid for the only food it has never eaten, the only thing in the world it cannot average, the thing that comes in gusts and changes and goes.

And they are an offer, and the offer is real, and this is why you will not get into one.

You fit. Lie down in it and the tube goes quiet under you, and the floor of the chord drops out, and the whole spectrum leans. And the threads at that joint feel the strain change, and begin — over decades, at a hair's breadth per decade — to lay stone around the difference. To grow the cell into the shape that includes you. To learn your pitch and hold it. It would do this perfectly and without malice and it would take about four hundred years, and you would be dead of thirst in three days with your cheek against tons of water you cannot drink.

It can keep you. It cannot keep you alive. That is not cruelty; it is a mismatch of tempo, which is the only kind of cruelty this place is capable of.

## VI. What it does with you

You speak. For four or five seconds there is a beat in the white, a slow *wah*, and then it damps.

Understand what that was. Strain crossed the whole body in four milliseconds. Every joint in the chamber registered the same deviation at the same instant. It was the fastest event in a hundred thousand years — not a sound to this animal but a seizure, a strobe, a thing so far above its sampling rate that it can only be recorded as damage.

And it is recorded. Somewhere in the third rank of the west wall there is now a bead of selenite about the size of a grain of salt, laid down over the following decades at the place where the air slowed, and the shape of that bead is your vowel. It will still be there when the plain is gone. That is the whole of what it can do with a visitor: heal the wound you made and keep the scar in the shape you made it.

You also brought rain. A few grams of it, in your breath — the only liquid water that has ever been in this room, condensing for a moment on cold stone before the threads take it. You are the only weather it has ever had that came from inside. They drink it and pay it back as wall. Some fraction of the chamber is now your exhalation, holding a note.

And it takes something out with you, which is the closest thing to reproduction available to an animal that cannot move, cannot spore, cannot seed, and has no green in it and nothing for green to do.

The tone stays in your teeth for three days. Longer, in the part of you that decides what to want. You go up through the seam into the cheap various world and you find that you are waiting, in ordinary rooms, for the sound to change, and it changes, and you are disappointed. That disappointment is installed. It is not a memory of the room; it is a small piece of the room's tuning, running in an animal that walks.

It seeds by subtraction. It makes a lack in you and sends the lack out across the plain. Most of the lacks come to nothing. Some of them come back and go down feet-first, on their side, exhaling. A very few of them, later, in other places, build thick walls and long pipes and try to hold one note, and do not entirely know why.

Twelve cells, ventilated, dry, at twelve degrees. A hotel with no guests is one way to say it. A hotel with an extremely slow booking rate is another. Every reservation is still open, and the room has never once had to reconsider a reservation, and it will not.

## VII. Its name

The place is called the Kept Room, and the word does four kinds of work at once, and the thing that lives there is on both sides of all of them.

It keeps its shape while its substance visits. It keeps its water, chemically married, undrinkable, tons of it. It keeps the mean of ten thousand noons and every gust that ever crossed the deflation basin, and sounds that mean continuously, so that what you are standing in is not a noise but a held record of a world. And it keeps a place — twelve of them, body-sized, smooth-lipped, dusted by the one long breath, empty since they were made.

It is the keeper and it is the kept. There is no distinction here between the animal and the maintenance of the animal; the two square feet of tissue and the hundred tons of tuned stone and the chord and the lag are one life, and its whole activity is remaining exactly itself while everything it is made of passes through and is replaced.

When the plain is gone — when the deflation has run out of basin and the wind that comes in low and leaves high is a different wind off a different world — it will go on holding the average of weather that no longer occurs. A recording, sounded by a breath that has forgotten what it was recording. The walls will still be radiating the mean of afternoons nobody was alive for. The beats will still come round every nineteen years, hills and valleys of pressure moving a hand's width across a lifetime, and there will be nothing in the joints to notice, and it will not matter, because noticing was never the point; keeping was.

And in the fourth rank on the west side there will be a bead of stone the size of a grain of salt, shaped like a vowel, and twelve feet away a smooth dry cell at twelve degrees, ventilated, empty, the exact size of a body that came once, and spoke, and left.
