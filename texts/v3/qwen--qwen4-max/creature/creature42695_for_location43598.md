Ask at Cup Eleven what lives on the Sill and a yardman will hold up his hands, and this is not a joke. It is the answer, and it takes a visitor a season to understand it.

The hands are the population. Four generations of finger-language have made a physical type: the long extensors, the wrist of a woman who has spent thirty years turning her forearm instead of her head, the chalk-callus along the outer edge of the little finger where it drags on the slate. You can read a man's cup in the resting posture of his fingers, the way a lowlander reads an accent — Cup Four rests loose and low, Cup Nineteen holds the thumb tucked, a dialect trait three hundred years old that nobody can explain. There are obscenities that require both elbows. There are jokes that only work in the dark, told on the forearm, and are untranslatable into light.

Because to speak you must first be seen. This is the whole grammar underneath the grammar. Sound cannot fetch anyone; a shout is not available; so the vocative case is a touch. Shoulder for ordinary address, wrist for urgency, sternum for intimacy, the top of the foot for a child. Never the back of the head — that is the Sill's equivalent of a scream in the face, and men have been unaddressed for less.

The consequence is that courtesy here is *not looking*. A crowded terrace at shift-change is a hundred people with their eyes carefully down and to the side, brushing bell-flowers as they pass, granting one another the privacy of not being spoken to. To hold a stranger's eye is to compel him to talk. Lowlanders who come up to buy find this unbearable and then, slowly, find it addictive: nobody has ever looked at them like that, as if they were the only available sentence.

And the face is a separate channel. Children are taught *still face* before they are taught the alphabet of the wrist — the flat, mild, unreadable mask that a good yardman wears while his hands say something furious or filthy or adoring. A lowlander reads a Sill face as serene. It is the least reliable thing about them.

When someone dies, the name-sign is retired. Struck from the roll: a specific configuration of the fingers that no living person may make again. Old cups therefore have dialects full of holes, and you can feel the dead as gaps in what can be said, a stammer in the language where a grandmother used to be. To say a dead person's name you must go up to the lip, and out along the escarpment to where the air will carry, and shout it.

The works has its own tenants, and some of them cannot live anywhere else.

On the busbars grows a moss that came up with the wire. It is a copper moss, a metallophyte, happy in the salts that kill everything else, and it grows only along the warm wet metal, so that from the rim the whole electrical system of a cup is drawn in green — every busbar, every junction, every stub and splice, mapped in a pelt a hand-span thick. It will not grow on the pavement. It has been on the Sill four generations and has already forgotten how to be wild.

The moss-gaugers walk it. A thin patch is a bad joint; a brown scorch is a leak; a sudden lush flush at a staple means current going into the rock where it should not. A good gauger can find a failing pin from thirty meters by the way the moss lies, the way a shepherd finds a lame ewe. The instruments in the gauge-house are checked against the moss and not the other way round.

Then there is the shine. A wood-rot fungus, come up in the launders, that fruits along the warm wires and glows at night the cold green of a foxed mirror. It is eating the works. The most beautiful thing on the Sill is a map of decay: every constellation you see from the rim is a launder going soft, a shutter-frame going punky, a counterweight beam that will fail in eleven months. The gutter-wrights work by it, lamps shuttered, reading rot by its own light. They say a launder *has the shine* the way a lowlander says a man has a cough.

On the black plates: a lichen that etches glass and costs a fraction of a percent a year, and against it the wipers, who are the apprentices in their first two years, chamois and a bucket and eight hectares each. The lichen regrows in the arcs the cloth cannot reach, so a plate that has been cleaned by the same child for two years carries the exact shape of that child's reach — a reach that lengthens, season by season, out toward the corners. There are plates at Cup Six with a boy's whole adolescence in them, an expanding fan of clean glass with a green rim.

In the launders, the bell-flower, which has stopped bothering to be pollinated by anything else. It blooms at hand height. Its corolla is a tube sized to a squirrel-hair brush; it opens on the shift, not on the hour; it sets no seed at all without a person walking past thinking about something else. In the year the lowland squirrels died and the brushes could not be got, the tenders used their own hair, and there are old people on the Sill who will tell you, without embarrassment, that there is a flower in Cup Two that is partly them.

And in the moss, one insect: a wingless, earless, white moth that finds its mates by scent alone and never makes a sound in its life. Nothing else flies inward. The yardmen's theory, which is probably right, is that the moss and the hush between them have swallowed every small vibration that courtship needs — no drumming, no stridulation, no wingbeat carries — and so everything that stayed went over to chemistry, or to touch, or to light. The moth is the last one that solved it. Nobody counts it. It has no name in any cup.

Understand what the cups are built of.

A vat is a box of finished hush. The terraces are faced with it, the floors are laid with it, the walls of every channel are dead cut stone. What grows inside is sheltered by what grew before. This is why a cup gets quieter as it gets older, why grade five exists only among mature blocks: the works silences itself with its own product, and a hundred-year cup is a nest of dead stone forty centimeters thick in every direction.

The trade is called deadwork, and the deadwork-layers are the least respected and most essential people on the Sill, because they lay the cloudy stuff. The unsellable. The flawed.

Which is where the Loud Age went. Forty years of cloudy stone, the boiler's whole ruined output, the gray decade nobody could sell — it is all still here. It is the jacket of Cup Eleven. It is what the clean blocks grow inside. The engines were not a waste; they were the foundation, and every clear seam cut on this Sill is clear because a crusher ran for forty years and filled the walls with its own noise. There are yardmen who know this and will not say it, and yardmen who say it constantly, and the argument is four generations old and conducted entirely with the hands.

The one who reads it best is the Reader at Cup Eleven, an old woman with a lamp and a wet saw who can look at a cross-section and tell you the year of a hailstorm, the season the fog came late, the winter a crew worked short-handed. A human shout has a signature — a hard bright plane with a fan under it — and she has found eleven of them in her life, and has told the families, and two of the families did not want to know. It is considered bad luck to read your own block. She has never read her own block. It is due to be cut in three years and she has already said who is to hold the lamp.

Below the deadwork, the rock is raw, and the water is loud.

This is the thing visitors are never told properly. Air is dead down here; water is not. Put your head under the black eye at the bottom of a cup and the world comes back at you like a dropped tray: a fizzing, ticking, crackling roar, the snap of shrimp, the grind of grit, the low structural hum of eighty kilometers of copper, the plates singing through their pins, and under all of it a slow irregular knocking that is the Sill itself coming up out of the sea, three or four millimeters a year, a hair of joint opening at a time.

The Sill is not quiet. The Sill is deafening. It has only silenced the air, which is the part the people live in.

The wet ears dive it. They are recruited from the loud families at the lip, they are paid absurdly, they are not liked, and they go strange — not from the silence but from being the only people who hear the works and cannot get anyone to believe them. A wet ear can find a failing pump by its note, a hollow in a terrace, a leak. They surface with their eyes streaming and write on the slate and the slate is wiped.

In that water: amphipods, blind and white, in uncountable numbers, coming up out of the pores of the limestone. The yardmen call them salt fleas and believe them to be the sea's own animals, still living in the sea's own water, and they are correct; the rock came up with its ocean inside it and the ocean has not finished. It is what the iodine is. It is what the salamanders eat.

The salamanders are pale, eyeless, gilled like a ruff, and they live a hundred and ten years. They are counted individually and they are named, and their names are not finger-signs but whole-arm gestures, a swimming motion, because they are swimming things and the language makes room. There has been a Kettle in Cup Four since before the Loud Age. There is a salamander in the cistern at Cup Eleven called Boiler, named by a man whose name-sign has been retired for sixty years, and she was alive when the crusher ran, and she felt every second of it in the water, along her whole body, the way her kind feels everything.

She is the other record. There is a hard white line in a block in the gauge-house, and there is an animal, and between them they are the only witnesses to the noise.

And she is an instrument. Before real weather comes over the escarpment, before the lamps begin to stutter, the salamanders rise. Every cistern-keeper knows it; nobody can explain it to a lowland meteorologist; the pressure gradient reaches the deep water hours before the rain reaches the lip. When a cup shutters in time it is often because a blind animal that has never been in the light came up out of the dark to the surface of a pool, and a man looked at it, and understood, and started running.

There is one creature the works hunts.

A boring bivalve, a relic of the coral, stranded in the pavement when the seabed lifted, still doing what its family did before there were mountains: grinding a tunnel into carbonate at a little over a centimeter a year. In the pavement it is nothing, a white thread in a white stone. In a vat it is a disaster, because it bores through the green uncured foam faster than the foam accretes, and it wanders, and behind it it leaves a dense wandering line, and that line is permanent.

The yardmen call it the pen. The gauge-house at Cup Eleven has a drawer of them in spirits.

But a block with a pen-line in it is not merely ruined. There is a market. There are lowland sects that will pay four times clean price for *written* hush — a wall with a life in it, a wandering white sentence in the substance of forgetting — and there are people on the Sill who have worked out that they can put a pen on a seed-frame in their first apprentice year and be rich in their sixtieth. The figurers. Whole crews have done it. Whole cups have gone over and been re-laid and shamed.

The punishment is not a fine. The punishment is that your name-sign is retired while you are alive.

They are the unaddressed. Nobody will look at them, so nobody can speak to them; nobody will touch their shoulder, so they cannot be called. They live at the lip, among the loud families, and they are the only people on the Sill who use their voices every day, and they have songs.

---

A life here is shaped like a valley: loud at both ends, silent in the middle, and the middle is forty centimeters long.

Children are born at the lip and raised there, in the creche, in ordinary noise — shouting, crying, singing, dropped pots. They learn the hands from three, but they learn them the way a lowland child learns a second language, and they also learn to shout, which is the thing they will lose. At seven they are taken down. It is done as a ceremony and it is not a happy one; the ones who cannot bear it stay up and become porters, listeners, loud. The rest descend, and the grade closes over them, and they do not come back up to live until they are old.

The fog sets the week: four days wet, four days down in the accreting dark, everything still, everything watched. Two days dry, and the dry days are at the lip — market, weddings, arguments, drink, the water-balance incline hauling grain and brush-hair and copper up the escarpment with a groan you can hear a kilometer out. Salt-cured snails, moss-fed, farmed in pens on the pavement; a cultivated cousin of the shine grown in hush-lined cellars; lowland flour at a price that makes people angry. Everyone smells of iodine and cold copper and you can pick a Sill man out of a lowland crowd with your eyes shut.

Marriage is a block. Two names on one tag, one set of frames, forty years. There is no divorce, because you cannot unwrite a seam; there are only blocks cut across a bad year and sold as two halves, and there are walls in the lowlands with a quarrel in them, and a woman in a room in a city talking to her husband in front of forty centimeters of somebody else's silence.

And there is the cell.

Every cup has one: a small chamber lined floor to ceiling with dead hush, where sound goes in and does not come out and nothing is growing and nothing records. It is the only room on the Sill where a person may make a noise. They are taken there to scream. Women are taken there in labor. The dying are taken there to say the retired names, all of them, one after another, sometimes for an hour. Children are taken there once, at seven, before the descent, and told to shout, and most of them cannot, and some of them do, and never forget it. There is one drum, kept oiled, shared between four cups.

The hush is friable. It powders under a palm. So the walls of the cell at Cup Eleven are handprints — thousands, layered, overlapping, a hundred and forty years of hands, no names, no dates, the only writing on the Sill that nobody has ever wiped.

Then you retire, and your block is cut, and the two halves of the brass tag are wired together and go down with it, and you go up to the lip to live loud for whatever is left. The half-tag you carried in your breast pocket next to the brush belonged to a person you never met, who set your frames before you were born. The frames you set are for someone who is not born yet. Every block is a link between two strangers, and it is sold, and installed, and people talk in front of it, and the stone is dead and takes no record of anything they say.

That is what the Sill sells. It is a place where everything records — the seams, the moss, the shine, the lichen, the handprints, the rising rock — and it manufactures, at enormous cost, over eighty kilometers, out of the sea's own salt, the one thing it does not have.

---

Out at the band, a kilometer or two beyond the outermost cups, where the grade falls to one and the air will carry a call, there is a hut and a Listener.

He is hired from the loud families. He is paid more than a gauger. His whole trade is the sense everyone else has put down: he walks out at dawn and at dusk and listens to the escarpment for the wrong kind of weather, and he comes back and writes it on a slate, and the slate is wiped. He talks to himself. He is the only person who works on the Sill and hears the lark.

The lark nests only in that band, at the exact edge of audibility, and it has been listening to the unaddressed for four generations, and it has learned their songs. Not the melody whole — the shape of it, the boiler-rhythm in the middle, the long fall at the end where a man's voice cracks. It sings the song of the people whose names cannot be signed, at the top of its small lungs, at the boundary of the world, and no one who works there has ever heard it.

Before the children go down at seven, they are walked out to the band. It is the last thing they are given. They stand in the ordinary noisy air with the light coming up off the white stone into their nostrils, and they hear the loudest thing on the Sill, and most of them learn it, the way children learn anything, without trying — and then they turn and go down into the quiet with it inside them, and do not sing it for forty years.
