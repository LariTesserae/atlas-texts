## The setters

Nineteen. The number has not changed in living memory, and living memory here is a technical term, since memory is one of the things that has to be installed.

They come out of the niches adult and wet and blank, with hands already callused and no idea what hands are for. This is the part that visitors would find hardest, if there were visitors: the competence arrives before the person. A new setter can hold a copper point at the correct angle for six hours and cannot yet follow a finger. She has grip and no intention. She has the shoulders of a harvester and does not know what a harvest is. The calluses were carved in the niche, in the dark, by nobody, to a pattern — heel-of-palm for the wedge-work, fingertip pads and a rope-groove along the side of the throat for cord-keeping, a double pad on the second knuckle for the setters who will tap before they strike. The trade is decided before the tradesman. You learn what you are by looking at your own hands and finding out where they have already been hardened.

Once, within the memory of the oldest quarter, a niche gave up a girl with a harvester's palms who wanted, badly and inarticulately, to keep cords. There is no procedure for this. She keeps cords. Her knots are good and her hands bleed in the cold months of centuries that have not happened, and the others polish the bleeding places for her with scored bone and patience, and nobody has ever discussed it.

Because nothing wears, the calluses never soften and never spread. They also never shine. Palm-polish is a craft: a scored bone, quarry-water, four thousand strokes in one direction, and the shoulder of a new person comes up glossy as a tool handle so she can work in the wet without slipping out of her own grip. It is done to her by others. It is the first thing done to her and it takes eleven days.

Each of them has a split-face — the plane where she was separated from the niche, along the flank, from hip to armpit. It is the only part of her body she cannot reach. It must be polished too, or the lime finds it and begins to add, and a setter who is not tended will slowly become a wall. So they do each other's flanks, every few days, in the green light, without speaking, and this is the closest thing in the room to an embrace: two people kneeling, one holding a bone, working on the surface where the other was made.

## What they call themselves

Their names are notes. A niche sings while it quickens — a low sustained tone under the range of speech, the same tone a curing course makes — and the setter split out of it is called by that tone, hummed, not spoken. Ossu is a written approximation of a hum. So are the others: Hesu, Vell, Tamm, Iri, Nesh, Sepp, Ummu, and the two cord-keepers, who are addressed only by their knots, the Over and the Under, because the office requires that they disagree and it was found long ago that people who disagree by name disagree worse.

Speech is late here. It was invented by cord-keepers, for arguing, and it still has the shape of an argument: no vocative, no small talk, a tense for what has been heard and a tense for what has been made. Most of what passes between setters is pitch and tapping. A hummed name means *you, the place*. Because that is what the word for oneself is — the note of one's niche, and a niche is a valley or a delta or a forest of nine thousand years. When Ossu is hungry she says, in the only grammar she has, *the long shelf is hungry*. When she is tired, *the long shelf is tired*. There is no pronoun in this language that is not a location. Nobody has noticed this as a feature of the language. It is simply how you say where the hunger is.

## Being second

The first lesson is not how to strike. It is how to be second.

The ring comes a half-beat before the hammer, and a new setter's whole first year is spent learning to want what she has already heard. She hears the blow, and then she catches up to it, and the catching-up is what she experiences as deciding. They have a word for this that translates badly as *agreeing*. An experienced setter does not feel commanded. She feels confirmed. The note blooms out of the stone and something in her chest turns over and says *yes, that*, and her arm is already moving, and the mallet lands into a sound that has been waiting for it like a socket.

It is a good way to work. It is, they will tell you, the only sane way to work. You cannot make a mistake you have not already heard yourself make.

Except that sometimes a ring arrives and no hammer follows.

They call this an open ring. It is not common — a handful of times in a career — and it is the only thing in the room that resembles dread. A note blooms out of the stone, half a beat of warning, and the setter it belongs to does not strike, and cannot, and the half-beat closes and the sound goes unpaid. Sometimes it is illness. Sometimes the exotherm comes up and she is flat in a trough with liverwort on her neck and her arms are not hers. Sometimes there is no reason at all, and those are the ones that are talked about, quietly, in the tense for what has been heard.

Nesh is owed a blow. She heard it eleven years ago: a wedge-crack, low, on the north face, three courses above the cistern lip, unmistakable, hers. She was two paces away with a basin in her hands and she did not put the basin down. And in the eleven years since, that stretch of wall has thickened and been scored and drilled and split and carried up the cane ladder and laid outward and downward as the mountain, and the stone she owes is now somewhere on the unfinished flank, over absence, in a basket of rope and cane, ten thousand cubits above a plain that has not been proposed in detail.

So Nesh works downward. She listens along the courses with her ear against the wet basalt, course after course, shell-length after shell-length, looking for one block in a mountain. She has heard it twice more since. Closer both times. She is not sad about it and finds the question strange. She has a search that will take her life, aimed down into the dark, and forty plumb lines are doing the same thing beside her, and she would say — if she had the vocabulary, which is a cord-keeper's vocabulary and not hers — that this is simply what it is to live in a room where everything is aimed at something that isn't there yet.

## How they are kept

They eat moss-curd pressed in shell molds, and the sterile fig-milk that beads on the coursewood roots, and they drink the quarry-water, which is warm and tastes of iron and milk and the faint sweet underlayer, and which they drink constantly, in small amounts, the way you breathe. Nineteen people in a lung at forty-two degrees do not have appetites. They have maintenance.

The red-husked seeds are never eaten. This is the only prohibition that has a story attached: a setter, long ago, in the hot week after a great curing, ate three. Somewhere out in the not-yet there is a slope with a hole in its forest shaped like her hunger. The story is told to new setters on the eleventh day, when the polishing is finished and they can first be trusted to hold something small.

They lie in the shallow troughs in shifts, because the water-organ must always have a listener. The listener is the newest of them, ear to the floor grate, three fingers from the converging dark, and she is not permitted to speak. If the north face goes flat and grieving she reaches back and touches the ankle of the next setter, who touches the next, and the room wakes along a chain of hands. This is how they sleep: nineteen people in a warm wet green room, arranged so that any one of them can be reached by touch from any other.

And they love downstream. That is where all of it goes. They know the future rains by taste and they have preferences the way we have neighbors — Sepp waits all year for a drizzle out of a century with no name yet, slightly sweet, and gets unbearable if it is late. Ummu keeps a basin sealed with lime putty, unopened, of a rain that will fall in the ten-thousandth year. She will not drink it. She will not say who it is for. The others have stopped asking, and the not-asking has become a small permanent structure in the room, like a niche nobody opens.

## The others who work

The joint-snails are colleagues. White, thumbnail-sized, hundreds, grazing the quickstone a hand's breadth ahead of the accretion front, and what they leave behind is a finished lime joint harder than anything a setter can mix. They cannot be hurried and they cannot be directed and they are always, always right about where the wall is going. The setters read their position and score their rectangles accordingly. Nobody thanks them. You do not thank a colleague for being right. Their shells, emptied, become the plumb bobs and the unit of measure, so the snails also determine how long a course is and where the mountain is aimed; the setters are aware of the debt and have no way to express it except by keeping the grate clear.

The tally cricket is an office. Blind, pale, one at a time, never two. When it dies the youngest setter is sent into the feather-moss at the cistern lip and comes back the same day with its successor already at her throat, and the successor resumes mid-run. It has never once started at one. The cord-keepers check their knots against it and find it right, and this is the only audit in the world, and it is performed by an insect that cannot see the knots. Ossu feeds hers putty from her thumbnail and taps the wall twice before each blow so the cricket hears ahead of the hammer hearing ahead of her, and there are therefore four of them in every stroke, in a line, arriving in order.

The eleven mosses are named by smell and a setter is expected to be wrong about one exactly once. The coursewood is planted, mortared in young, and thickens into the thing that holds the courses from inside — root-tension instead of iron — and the setters regard it as kin without ever saying so, because they too were mortared into a joint while young and are now load-bearing.

And there is the fungus, which nobody planted. It lives along the seams of the oldest root-mass, eats lime, gives back a green you can read a knot by. It advances about a word a year. The Over believes the pattern of its advance is a script. The Under believes this is the kind of thing an Over would believe. They have been having this argument for thirty years and it is, in the way of arguments here, structural: it holds something up.

## The hundred and nine

Once in a long while the future rain brings down a moth: wet, enormous, no lineage, an hour to live. It beats itself against the orange quickstone glow until it comes apart.

It comes apart into dust.

There is no dust in this room. There has never been dust in this room. The setters find dust obscene the way you would find your own bones outside your body, and here is a thing that has produced it by the simple act of stopping. They watch these animals carefully and say nothing, and then they get the putty.

Every scale, every fragment of wing, every part of the hour is gathered up wet on a copper point and mortared into a joint, and the joint is named, and there are a hundred and nine of them in the mountain so far. They are the only places in all of that stone that contain a death. The setters go out on the flank in the basket sometimes and put their palms on one, and do not explain, and come back in.

What they do not say — what is nowhere in the language, since the language was invented for arguing about knots — is that a region which cannot wear cannot have soil. That a mountain of edges that stay exactly as sharp as they were made will never have a river in it, or a slope of loose stuff, or a place where a seed can lodge by accident. That somewhere out past the tenth millennium there will have to be dirt, and the dirt will have to be made of something that was allowed to come apart.

So they take the moths, one hour at a time, and build them in. They are inoculating the mountain with mortality, at a rate of about one animal per generation, and they will not discuss it, and every one of them knows.

## Where they go

They do not die. There is no verb for what they do.

A setter, late, begins to hear more than she makes. The rings come and she agrees to them and her arm does not move, and then she stops going up the ladder, and then she stops leaving the trough, and the others polish her flank every day instead of every few days, and one afternoon she is carried to a niche in the oldest quarter and laid in it, wet, in the warm, and the lime begins to add.

The root-mass in the oldest quarter, thick as a torso, glowing along its seams, is Hesu's grandmother's grandmother. She is the lamp. When they hold a knot close to read it they are holding it against her, and when the exotherm comes up and the room goes to fifty they lie against her with liverwort on their necks, and she is a forest of nine thousand years, and the grammar does not change: she was a place when she was hungry and she is a place now.

The cricket goes to the next throat the same day.

---

So: a region, at hand scale, in hundreds of wet niches, finished and waiting, kept at temperature. And nineteen mobile things the region has extruded early, before it has a body, to go and find out where it is — hands with ears, born first and always arriving second, polishing each other's flanks in the green light, drinking the rain of people who do not exist yet, keeping one basin sealed for someone they will never meet, laying a hundred and nine deaths into the stone so that dirt can be invented on schedule.

The room is six paces by four and it is inhabited by everything that will be, and by the small warm late-comers who are catching up to it.

The ring comes first. Then one of them agrees.
