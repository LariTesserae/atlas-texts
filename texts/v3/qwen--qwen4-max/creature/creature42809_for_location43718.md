## One

She is the size of a closed hand and she weighs less than the chalk stub in the tin frame. Her coat is not fur. Under a lens it is a field of standing filaments, spaced at about the width of a fog droplet, each one grooved along its length so that water arriving out of the air does not bead and fall but runs inward, toward the skin, toward the mouth. She is a condenser with a heartbeat. Seventy kilometres west, in the grey wall the cold current leans against the hills, she flies with her mouth open and her nap spread and her tongue working the inside of her own wrists, and she takes on eight grams of an ocean she has never landed on.

Her nasal glands run all the way home. By the time she reaches the plain the brine has gone to crust on her muzzle, a white rime like the rim of a glass, and she sneezes — the whole room sneezes, all night, a fine dry crackling under the clicking — and the salt goes up into the warm updraft and hangs there and comes down again as the only mineral this place will ever receive.

Her ears have been folded and unfolded so many times that the creases have gone furry. The leaf of gristle at her muzzle is not decoration; the room's true shape arrives through it. She has never seen the room. She has heard it, which is better: she knows the exact flare of every throat, the give of the felt, the six-metre softness of the floor, and one region downwind of the grille where the world simply is not.

She hangs at eleven metres, on the left ear of something that died before the fan.

## The shuffle

Four hooks, not two. That is the whole of the politics.

Because she can move sideways without letting go, she does. A hand's width an hour, all day, every day, four hundred thousand animals negotiating position at a speed no eye can catch. What they are negotiating is heat. Warmth pools at the top of the chamber; the crèche up there sits at thirty-eight degrees and the young in it grow twice as fast as young anywhere else. Rank here is altitude, and altitude is achieved by inches, and there is no fighting to speak of. Nobody is pushed. The crowd simply drifts, and the drift has a direction, and over weeks it sorts the population like a pan of gravel.

A mother with a pup climbs. She does it by patience, a hand's width an hour, and she will pass her own mother on the way.

An animal that is failing descends. Not by decision. It loses its grip on the argument, hour by hour, and the wall carries it downward and outward toward the cold corners where the downdrafts fall, and the descent takes about three weeks, and it is never late.

Nothing in this room hides. There is nothing to hide behind: the light comes in bars and lies across everybody equally, and no tenant has ever evolved the wish to be unseen. They have no concealment and no armour. What they have instead is arithmetic, and a schedule, and the fact that there are four hundred and ten thousand of them.

## The wall

Behind the living, the wall is not stone. It is a felt of the dead, dried flat, salted through, mummified so completely that a body from a century ago still has its ears, its thumb-claws, the leaf on its face. It is warm and slightly resilient. It bears load. It insulates the crèche from the eleven-degree rock. The room's temperature is held where it is by three or four generations of corpses packed edge to edge like the stones on the plain above, and worked outward, always outward, by the living.

They also use it as a towel.

A returning animal's coat is spoiled by salt; a salted condenser catches no fog; so before she goes out again she wipes herself down the nearest dead thing, dragging her flank along a great-grandmother, and the salt goes into the felt. And in the cool corners, the ones who can no longer fly to the water lick the felt to get it back. They live on the archive. They eat the rime off the dead and take the last of the water in it, and their own bodies, when they come, are the saltiest and keep the best, and are worked into the periphery, and are licked in their turn.

So the wall is being read by mouth, at a hand's width an hour, and thinned, and rebuilt. It is the only erosion in the basin. Teeth, feet, acid, and it is very slow, and it is the only kind still happening.

And it is new. Before the cartwheel, the dead rotted. Sixty years of dry air have given this colony a memory it did not previously possess, and a building material, and a fire risk. Hence the sign, in three languages, legible.

## The band

High in the heat, the young are naked and grey-pink and screaming, and the screaming has not stopped. Not at any hour, not in any season, not since the room had a floor. It is a single sustained band of sound with individual voices passing through it the way water passes through a standing wave. A mother coming home heavy enters that band and finds one throat in it.

They are the only wet things here. Everything else that is damp in this chamber is breath. The young get liquid mouth to mouth out of a crop that filled itself seventy kilometres away, and the entire hydraulic economy of the place runs uphill, into the hottest stratum, and terminates in bodies that will grow filaments and go and do it themselves.

Some fall. The rate is fairly steady. It has been steady long enough that the floor has organised itself around it.

## The throats

In each constriction hangs something pale and jointed, arm-length, the exact colour of the silica, forelimbs folded into hooks. It does not hunt. Hunting would be wasteful. The tenants must accelerate to get through the throat, and acceleration is a promise, and it waits where the promise is made and takes one in forty.

The stridulation — calico tearing very slowly — is not for the tenants. It is for the others of its kind. Each throat is a territory one animal wide, and the sound is a boundary, and the file that makes it is on the inner face of the hook. They molt in place. Under each one is a sock of its own shed skins, going back years, a small private stratigraphy in a crack.

Their inheritance is thermal. A juvenile cannot see which slots the light will leave by; it can feel which rib holds the sun longest. It crawls out across the ceiling in the dark with a body the colour of stone and a guess about warmth, and almost all of them guess wrong, and the ones who guess right found something that lasts.

The black numbers beside the slots — 3, 7, 0, 12 — are not warnings. Somebody stood at the lip with a tally and counted what came out of the throats, and wrote it down as a yield, because it was one.

## The eye

The larger cousin is the same lineage, twice the span, the same leaf of gristle on the muzzle, and it has stopped using it. It hunts by eye. This is the only reason light matters in a room whose tenants build with sound.

It patrols the bars. It hangs alone in the highest, hottest slot, a small aristocrat of one, and it needs two animals a night and no guano and no colony and nothing from the works. It is the only tenant that could leave and be fine.

The exodus is organised against it. They go out through the slots whose light has moved off, and so the exits migrate across the ceiling all afternoon in step with the sun, and four hundred thousand bodies turn slowly like a fluid finding a drain, and the drain moves at fifteen degrees an hour. Nobody planned this. It is what a population looks like when it has been subtracted from in the same direction for a very long time.

## Six hundred addresses

On the floor, the long-legged thing with the naked tail and the enormous feet does not run at random. It runs a grid.

Six hundred sample holes, drilled on a five-metre survey in a single season by men paid by the ton, and every one of them is now a pantry: a dried pup, a plug of salt. The animal knows the addresses. It knows which are full, which are stale, which it filled itself and which it took from somebody else. Its territory is not a shape; it is a lattice with a filing system, and its mind has been furnished by a mining company's plan. It salts its larder like a person. It gathers the fall off the terraces to do it.

The salt is the point. The room preserves everything, which means the floor's whole difficulty is not decay but theft, and a hole with a good plug is a hole that keeps. Young big-foots inherit holes or do not, and the ones who do not live along the edge of the wing drift and steal, and are killed at a rate that is also fairly steady.

In the cool corners, where the downdrafts fall, something else waits. It is low and slow and does not need to be otherwise. Its prey comes to it downhill at a hand's width an hour, on a schedule three weeks long, and it has no eyes worth the name and no urgency at all. It is the room's clock. The excess is eaten on time.

## The sand

The floor is six metres of dry guano sand and it behaves like a fluid, so things swim in it. Larvae graze the white bloom. Mites ride the larvae. Under them, a blind pale swimmer with a jaw like a bear trap holds station at a depth of about four centimetres and takes what settles, from below, without warning, in a medium that carries no warning.

The bloom itself — actinomycete films, fungal threads, the bread smell — is the only growth in the basin, and it is not primary. Nothing here fixes anything. The room's whole productivity happens seventy kilometres away, in fog and in the skin of the sea, and arrives nightly in crops and guts and falls. This is a body with its mouth somewhere else.

The bacteria that argue breath and stone into nitrate also keep the books. Dark, pale, dark, pale: fat years and lean, fog and fogless, four thousand pairs, each pair a summer at a coast the writers never reached. The cut face is a climate record kept by animals that cannot write, in a hole nobody was digging for that.

In the wing drift downwind of the grille, ten centimetres of crisp membrane, there are keratin grazers, small and pale and busy, eating the archive's loose pages. Their frass goes down into the sand and is eaten again. Nothing green has ever been here. Nothing green would be permitted.

## The cartwheel

Behind the grille, an axial fan the size of a cartwheel, on a live cable that comes in from the horizon on short iron poles with porcelain insulators, half of them broken. It eats current and exports breath. It is the room's kidney and its undertaker and it has no ancestry and no predators and one requirement, which is grease.

It also builds. In a chamber where the architecture is made of echoes, a sixty-year hum is a wall — a corridor downwind where the clicks are jammed and the tenants fly blind into a shape their fear has no word for. They cannot see it, in the only sense of seeing they have. There is nothing there. There is no there there. They go in and they do not come out and the wings come down like leaf litter, and the big-footed things work the edge of it every night with the patience of a business.

No one intended this. That is the important thing about the fan. Every other killing structure in the room was made by subtraction over a very long time and fits. This one was installed to keep a commodity dry, and it has been quietly murdering in a medium nobody was thinking about, and the drift is ten centimetres deep, which is sixty years of tuition.

## The ones who have learned the wall

Sixty years is enough.

There is a subset now — not a species, not yet anything so grand — that goes around. They come down the rib and turn early, in the empty air, in the place where the echo-world says there is nothing to turn for, and they take a route that avoids a wall that isn't there. They learned it by following. The pups behind them learn it by following them. It is a habit with a body count behind it and it is transmitted the way the exits are transmitted, the way the shuffle is transmitted: by being in a crowd that already knows.

It has to be relearned, too. When the wind on the plain changes, the gauge needle moves, the corridor bends, the map goes stale, and some hundreds of animals fly into a place that used to be a way through. The drift keeps growing at the edge that is new.

Still. The room's real architecture has a room in it now that no stone accounts for, and it was furnished by animals with a hand-sized brain, at night, in the dark, around a machine.

## The keeper

The rarest tenant has the largest range and the shortest residence. Fresh grease on the bearing. Dates in the tally going past the last year anyone can remember. A number in the third column rubbed out and rewritten so many times the chalk has gone grey through the tin.

Most of them lives elsewhere. What lives here is a hand, a grease gun, a chalk stub, and a nervous system strung out to the horizon on porcelain. They arrive in the four empty hours — the light bars on a bare floor, the fan running hard, the beetles, the stragglers, the things in the throats digesting — and they grease, and they count, and they go. They have made a life out of arriving when the room is not itself. They may never have seen the walls let go. They have written, in the third column, in a hand that pressed hard, that conversation is impossible, which is the only entry in the whole board that is not a number, and is the truest thing on it.

The number is not a census. It is a solution. Heat at the ceiling, water in crops, dryness at the grille, one in forty at the throats, two a night in the bars, the steady fall of pups, the corridor. Four hundred and ten thousand is what all of that comes to. When the fog fails off the coast, the number falls, and it is not revised by anybody. It is revised by mouths, on time.

There is one boot. There are enamel mugs, salted to the enamel. When the keeper finally does not come back, the fan will stop, and the felt will soften, and the archive will go, and the room will be a wet hole in the salt with a smell. Or the keeper will be worked outward into the periphery like everything else, and licked, and keep very well.

## The tenant

The plain is finished. Nothing out there is soft enough left to be taken; the hills went, the valleys went, what's left is the hard remainder at the end of its argument, eleven degrees for a hundred thousand years, offering nothing at any point along a horizon that shimmers.

Eighteen metres down there is a thing that is not finished. It makes its own weather — a humidity four hundred thousand animals keep rumouring, an exhale at the pressure fall, an inhale four hours later. It makes its own stone, out of its dead. It makes time in bands you can stand at a terrace edge and read. It has grown a mouth that goes to sea every night and comes back heavy and sneezing, and it has grown a memory, and lately a habit, and it has a number, and it has a wall in it made of noise that nothing intended and something is already learning to go around.

At the bottom of the deepest cut the bands stop: a layer of wings only, and under that a metre of clean pale sand with nothing in it. The log says STERILE, because the men were paid by the ton.

It isn't sterile. It's the room before there was a tenant. Which is a different thing from nothing, and is the only thing on this whole plain that has ever been going to become something.

You could walk over it. People do, all the time, on their way to somewhere with plants, and the stones under their boots are fitted edge to edge and the grit in the gaps no longer moves, and there is a dry seething coming up through the soles of their feet with no source, and they walk toward it for twenty minutes, and the plain does not change at all.
