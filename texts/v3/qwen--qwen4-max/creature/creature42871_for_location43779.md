## The hands

Ask a stranger to describe them and they will say *pale*, which is wrong, or nearly. The milk-light does not bleach. It saturates. Four generations under a lid of selenite and the skin has gone faintly translucent at the margins — the ear-rims, the webbing between the fingers, the thin place inside the wrist — so that a person's pulse is visible across a table. They read each other's blood the way we read a face. A woman lying about her calm is caught by her own hand.

Their eyes are unremarkable and their faces are strange to us, because nobody here has ever been lit from one side. There is no shadow under the brow, no dark socket, no sculpted cheek. A face in this light is a flat pale field with a mouth in it, and so the mouth does most of the work, and the throat, and the hands. They think we stare at foreheads. They think our portraits are pictures of skulls.

The hands are the people. Everything else is carriage.

The pad of the fingertip is the organ by which this civilization knows itself, and it is kept the way a singer keeps a throat: warm, oiled, wrapped at night in hush-cloth greased with fungus fat from a horn pot, and never, ever allowed to callus. A callus is a small death. Apprentices are tested by reading the tension of a sinew cable through two layers of felt; the gifted ones can tell you the age of a wall from the tremor alone, and the very gifted can tell you which crew tensioned it last, and whether that crew was quarrelling.

So they are named for their hands. A spoken name exists — a soft thing, mostly fricatives, used at a sickbed or in a cellar — but the name everyone actually uses is a small motion made at waist height, below the herd's notice, and it describes a habit. *Sets-It-Down-Without-Looking. Twice-Through-The-Channel. Comes-Back-To-Check.* A child runs unnamed for six or seven years until its hands do something recognizably its own, and then the quarter agrees, and that is the naming, and there is no ceremony beyond everybody beginning to use it.

## What a household is

Nobody owns a building. You cannot own a held breath. What a person owns is a set of obligations to certain joints — forty cables, say, in three walls and a drying frame — and a household is the group that shares them.

Two people marry by putting their hands on the same cable and drawing it up together until the note is right. The vow *is* the note; there are no words, and the witnesses memorize the pitch. When such a marriage ends, the cable is cut and spliced into two shorter lengths, and both go sharp, and this is what everyone says about the couple afterward: *they went sharp.* Children inherit tensions the way elsewhere they inherit land, and quarrel over them, and the great inheritance disputes of this city concern who is responsible for a dovetail in an empty avenue that no living person has ever slept behind.

The census is not counted in souls. It is counted in cables under tension, and the number is posted on the plaza plate where anyone can feel it, and it has been falling for four hundred years.

And every adult has a far wall.

At coming of age you draw a lot, and the lot names a wall in one of the abandoned quarters — sometimes an hour's walk, sometimes half a day — and that wall is yours to keep for the rest of your life. Four times a year you go out with a barrow, a scraper, a spanner, and a flask of sweet Fall water, and you sweep the bloom off it and ease the joints and bring the cables back to true, and you come home. Nobody lives behind it. Nobody ever will. No one follows you out there; no one checks; the whole institution rests on the fact that no one checks. People neglect their households and confess it lightly. To let your far wall go is the shame they do not have a comfortable word for, and it is the commonest private grief in the basin, and there are people who have not been out in nine years and who lie about it beautifully, with steady hands and a visible pulse that gives them away to anyone who cares to look.

The only crime is abandonment, and the punishment is that your hand-name is retired. Nobody uses it again. They feed you, they work beside them, they pass you the spanner. They simply have no way to address you. Some of the unaddressed live thirty years.

## The trades, and the ones who speak

At first light the tensioners go round with bone spanners and the scrapers go behind them with barrows, and the bloom is wheeled out to the salt-drifts, and the roofers walk the frames testing hush-cloth for the sag that means a full belly of water, and the children wring it into the cisterns with their red cracked hands and are proud of the redness. Down at the sink-pools the fishers lie flat on the cold pavement with an arm in the water to the shoulder, waiting for a paving stone to have an opinion, and they are the only people here with scars, and they are unbearable company.

Below the floor is another trade entirely: brine-work, salt-cutting, the sour half of the economy, done by families who smell of it and marry each other and are, in a manner nobody states, a separate people. Above: drink. Below: what brine does. The two halves meet at the Fall and nowhere else.

The finest tuners are blind. This has been true so long that it is not considered a paradox. Sight is a coarse sense for this work; the best of the blind lineages can hold a whole wall in their hands at once, all forty cables speaking together, and hear the one that is lying. They are courted, flattered, and paid in hand-fat.

And when a hand goes numb — age, cold, a lifetime of tremor — the person is released from work and becomes *voiced*. Speech here is the profession of those whose fingers have failed. The voiced do the talking, the teaching, the history-keeping, the long recitations of which quarter fell in which year and whose grandmother predicted it. They go out into the empty avenues where no herd passes, because that is where sound is affordable, and they talk to nobody, at length, in the loveliest light in the world. From a distance a voiced elder looks like a person sitting on a step doing something small with their hands. Often they are. Often the hands are just resting.

## Children, and the dead

Children are taught to set things down instead of putting them down, and they learn it as a game before they learn it as a law: the longest touch, the slowest bowl, who can lay a flint blade on gypsum without the plate feeling it. They build little keyed towers in the seams at dusk, dovetailed, wedged, tensioned with thread, and come back at dawn to see what the bloom has done to them. The game is called *tomorrow*. Nobody has explained to them that it is a rehearsal; nobody had to.

They keep day-lived motes in felt cages and name them and bury them by evening, and this is thought good for a child.

The dead go into the flour, on the fungus beds, and become hush-cloth, which is to say that the dead are what the roofs are strung with, and everyone knows this, and when the Fall comes down and the cloth bellies and the children wring it out, no one makes anything of it, because it is simply how water arrives.

Their portraits are relief carvings, meant to be read with the palms in the dark. And they are gypsum, and the bloom eats gypsum, so a face left alone for two generations rounds off into politeness — the nose gone, the mouth a suggestion, the whole ancestor smoothed toward the shape of a knuckle. To remember someone here you must recut them. There are families who have kept a great-grandmother's face sharp for a hundred and eighty years by taking turns, and there are shelves in the cellars full of soft blank ovals that nobody has tended since the census fell, and the cellar-keepers will tell you, if you ask, which is which, and then they will not tell you anything else that day.

When someone dies, their far wall is released. The household walks out, cuts the cables, and sits at a distance through the night while the salt takes it. It goes slowly out of true. Nobody helps it. In the morning they walk home, and that is the funeral, and it is considered a good death if the wall was still true when they arrived.

## The other citizens

The pallids are older here than the people, and everyone knows it, and the street plan is not a human artifact. It is a blind animal's memory of a river that mostly isn't there. The water wrote it, the herds keep it, the crews pave it, and the whole city is a stone transcription of something ten thousand hairless bodies remember about where the wet used to be.

They speak in pulses below hearing, and the grammar is not deciphered and probably is not decipherable by a creature with a ribcage that resonates at the wrong rate. But it has moods everyone can read. When a herd mourns — and they mourn, they do it for their dead and sometimes for no reason anyone can find — the drumming stops, and the soles of your feet go empty, and every crew in the quarter stands still in the street with a barrow in its hands until it starts again. It can last an hour. No one has ever timed it out loud.

There are people called waiters whose entire skill is standing in an avenue with a board across the chest, being read by a hundred thousand blind animals, and being uninteresting. That is the art. Not command, not calming: irrelevance. The greatest waiter of the last generation was famous for how little the herds noticed him, and he was insufferable about it, in whispers.

The jointers are not vermin. They are the oldest guild, and the phrase is *thank the crab* — meaning: credit the thing that ruined you with the skill you now have. Every keyed block, every dovetail, every horn peg and sinew channel exists because a small pale animal ate the lime out of nine hundred years of mortar. Children keep them in jars. A jarred crab is the standard gift between adolescents and the standard threat between neighbours. Their lime-drifts are swept for gravel; their shells make pins; they eat the shells of their own dead, which the crews find entirely reasonable.

The vein is called the Guest. It has no mouth and no face and it arrives about once in eleven days, and households set out milk and carved pegs and locks of hair that it does not want, and read its branching for the week's routes. It has never been wrong about water. It has never been right about anything else. Everyone asks it about love anyway, and everyone knows better, and the readings are argued over in hand-sign at waist height with great seriousness.

The chalk owls nest in the dissolution pockets, which means an owl is a living mark on the map of where the roof is thinnest, and the owl-watchers keep the chart, and the chart is the most valuable document in the basin. When an owl's shadow crosses your hands you stop and count ten. It is not superstition. It has simply never once been worth continuing.

## What they are afraid of

The Brighteners do the arithmetic out loud, in the empty plazas, and the numbers are not disputed. The lid thins. The Glare brightens. One day the roof goes and the basin becomes a valley and the salt washes out and the bloom stops, and a wall, at last, will stand.

The weeders walk the wrack-line after every flood and pull the seed, and the law is older than the reason, and the reason is not the one they give.

They say green will ruin the flour. They say green will turn the herds. They say a great many practical things. But high in the eastern swallet there is a stain the size of a cloak in raw unfiltered light, and twice in a generation crews have gone up on rope to take it out, and both times they came down without it, and the story is that the sight of it turned them around.

That is probably not true. But there is a version of it that the voiced elders will give you, out in the empty avenues, talking to nobody, if you have been kind and you have brought fat for their hands.

What stopped them, in that version, was not the colour. It was that it was growing without anyone.

Nobody was tensioning it. Nobody had swept it at first light. It had not been put back, because it had never come apart, and it did not need a single one of them, and it was going to go on doing that forever.

That is the thing in the crack. That is what they are waiting for and cannot look at: a world in which the whole of their skill — the fingertips, the notes, the names made of small motions, the four walks a year out to a wall that nobody will ever live behind — is simply not required.

They have no word for *finished* that is not also a word for *finished with*.

---

Late afternoon, then, and the Glare gone the colour of weak tea on the western wall.

Out past the last kept quarter, where the light is loveliest and only fools go, an old woman is kneeling at a wall with nothing behind it. Her hands are shining with fat. She has walked since the herd-turn to be here. She sets the spanner down in the flour without a sound, and puts two fingers on the cable, and reads the tremor, and finds it a little flat, and draws it up until it is right.

She is being a fool on purpose. She has been one for fifty-one years.

At her feet, in the seam, the white fur begins.
