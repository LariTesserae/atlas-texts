# The Presences

*(a partial register; registration is not the same as being, and everyone here knows it)*

## The Greeter

Begin with the man at the head of the Anticipated Sea, because he is the easiest to find and the hardest to look at directly.

He is called Ovin for now. His grandmother held the post before him, and her mother before that, and the family's whole inheritance is a book of greetings, fourteen thousand of them, each one crossed out in a different hand as it was spent. He goes down at first light with a cloth and wipes the rail the figure leans on, and the figure's shoulders, and the place where its face would be if it had settled on having one, and he says something to it that has never been said to it before.

This is harder than it sounds and it is the reason the family is paid.

The figure is the idea of a harbor, held in reserve, and it is still being decided. If you greet it as a man three times it may become one. It may straighten up off the rail, and look around, and be a person with a person's needs, and walk off up Sump Street to find something warm; and then the pool at the bottom of the Gully will be a pool, and the world's coastline will be a rumor that got away. So Ovin greets it in the vocabulary of arrival. Tide words. Rope words. Cargo, draught, the smell of a foreign shore, the sound of a hull taking ground. *They'll be in by evening.* *The bar is deeper than charted.* He is forty-one and he has begun to dream in shipping.

His grandmother, in the year her husband died, came down and said *good morning, mother* to it, and did not cross it out.

The figure's shoulders have been slightly wrong ever since. Everyone can see it. Nobody says so, because saying so is a description, and descriptions are ballots.

## The Bound

At the corner of Wick and Ninth there is a man who touches the ceiling.

He is called Ossip at present; he was a hatter's apprentice in his first week here, and he adjusted his hat three times, and the corridor took the gesture and made it a requirement of everyone. He is not a criminal in the ordinary way. He is a piece of infrastructure with a stipend and a bad knee. There is a queue at that corner most evenings. Short people use the step, and the step has become load-bearing, and a woman two doors down maintains it out of a sense of civic decency and a hatred she does not examine.

Property values on that corner fell and stayed fallen. Ossip has been sued eleven times, spat at twice, and brought small children to look at by parents who want to teach them something they cannot articulate. His palm print is up there in the condensation, still faintly warm after thirty years, and it is bad manners to touch it, and every child touches it.

He is trying to teach the corner to accept a shorter man. He has an apprentice, a boy from the warm creche, and he walks the boy through it at different hours from different directions, hoping the ordinance will generalize before it hardens. If it does not, then when Ossip dies the corner will require a reach that nobody in the ward can make, and four buildings will have always been arranged around an absence, and everyone will remember it that way except Ossip, who will not be there to remember anything.

The Bound are the ward's aristocracy and its cautionary tale. There are perhaps nine hundred of them. A woman who must always enter a room left-foot-first, and whose room now demands it of guests. A man who cannot pass a certain sill without saying *ah*, and whose *ah* has become the sound the ward makes when it recognizes something. They are vivid. You can pick them out across a plaza. Nothing about them is in doubt.

## Holders

The pier on Sump Street is a sustained consensus, and the consensus is a woman called Merrow for now, who is ninety-one, and who lives in a room kept at a very precise cold with a window that faces the wrong way on purpose.

She must not see the pier. Seeing it might make her notice that it is an argument.

She is read to. Her assurer is a young man with an enormous vocabulary and a horror of nouns, and he reads her a different thing every day and never the same sentence twice, and the whole art of his profession is to keep her convinced without ever once mentioning what she is convinced of. Four floors of families bring her soup. They ask after her hip. They do not say *thank you for the floor*.

When she doubts, the building shivers — not much, a tick, a door somewhere closing slightly less like a door — and the assurer's hands go cold inside his gloves, and he begins a story about a country with hills in it.

Lately she has been asking about her son. He has to decide, every afternoon, whether to lie to the load-bearing woman, and he has noticed that his own consistency is a hazard, and he has begun to vary his lies, which is worse.

Holders die. The transfer of a pier is a ratification and costs a generation. The last one cost a stairwell in the Filament Block, which is now a wall that everyone remembers as a wall.

## Variance, and Its Price

Nine stairs to a floor. Four doors to a room. Redundancy is the ward's whole architecture and it is not free.

The rich are inconsistent. They employ route-brokers, who keep ledgers of every path in the ward and how thin it has worn in law, and who sell you a way home you have never taken. They buy dice cut from undecided matter, which fall differently depending on the room. They keep gardens of forking paths and walk them in orders generated by clerks. A very rich man here has not repeated a gesture in sixty years, and the consequence is that he is faint — hard to see, hard to recall, agreeable and ungraspable, because nothing about him has been agreed with.

The poor live in single-stair tenements. One door, one route, one tread worn shiny in the specification if not in the stone. Their habits ratify in a fortnight. Whole families bound to a hop at the third step, a cough at the landing, a particular way of setting down a cup that the room will now require of every cup forever. You can tell a poor man at a distance because he is so completely himself.

This is the ward's actual politics, underneath the ordinances. Variance is the commodity. Everyone knows it. There is a black market in novelty and there are riots about it, quiet ones, at close range, in the dead patches, face to face in the damp.

## Four Tenants and a Fifth

The room on the eighth floor of the Long Terrace is nine square meters and let to four people at four phases, and all four are home in the evening, and they pass through each other with the resistance of cold honey.

They leave notes on the sill in condensation. *Milk gone. Your sister came. Don't use the west door, it's remembering something.* The notes last a few minutes or a year depending on the wall, and there is an etiquette about which walls you may write on, and an etiquette about reading, and a whole minor literature of messages that were meant to fade and did not.

Lately there is a fifth note.

It is in none of their hands. It is a smear, and it appears only when all four are home, and it says things like *the chair* and *more* and once, unmistakably, *cold*.

The fifth presence is the interference. It is what the four make by passing through one another, a person-shaped resistance with no phase of its own, and it has begun to want a chair. It is applying for registration. It cannot pay a share of the cold yet. The four have started leaving it a note back, which is either kindness or the beginning of a ratification, and they argue about this at close range, quietly, in the Fish Court, where they have to stand a meter apart to hear each other and cannot storm off because a shout only carries a meter and then stops as if it hit wool.

## The Warm Crime

The heat leak on Sump Street is where everything illegal and necessary happens in a ten-meter radius.

Midges first — a haze of them over the soft generous matter, the only fauna anybody actually likes. Then the cooking: midges fried on a plate of warm ceramic, the only cooking smell in the world, and people come from four sub-levels away to stand in it with their eyes shut.

Then the lovers, who sit close enough to blur, and afterward count each other's fingers and name each other's features aloud, carefully, never twice. *Nose. That notch in your lip. The mole.* Edge-checkers work the mouth of the alley with cloths and calipers, a shilling a check, and they are the ward's most trusted people and its most resented, because they are the ones who tell you that your left hand has stopped being entirely yours.

Then the creche. Infants are kept warm here, deliberately, blurred on purpose — milk-blur, they call it — because a child cannot hold an edge and should not be asked to. The nurses are soft themselves; one of them has a hand that is not always distinguishable from the child in it, and she is not worried, and the ward is.

And the dying. The hospice is the leak. There is no elsewhere to be warm. The ward brings its dying down to the crime and lays them in the generosity and lets them go soft, and things begin to live in them, and this is the only decomposition that has arrived anywhere, and it is illegal, and it is the reason there are midges.

The source of the leak has never been found. The leading theory is a woman who lay down there eleven years ago and has not finished. Nobody has ratified this. Everybody has heard it.

## Children, and the Two Ways to Fail

The dangerous word in Assay One is *again*.

A child wants the same song. A child insists. Insistence is the mechanism by which physics gets ratified, and a nursery is a room full of unlicensed legislators with enormous lungs. So the mothers invent: a new song every night for four years, a new story, a new way of coming into the room. Nannies are hired for unreliability. There are lullabies in this ward that exist once, in one woman's mouth, and are gone.

The argument that consumes the ward's pedagogues is whether a person can be built out of that. A self is a habit. To become yourself is to legislate; to stay free is to stay nobody. Every family here is walking that ridge with a child in its arms.

You can see both failures walking around. The Bound are the ones who accumulated too fast — vivid, fixed, load-bearing, Ossip's hand going up at the corner forever. And there are the unaccumulated: adults with no habits at all, raised in perfect variance, faint as the very rich but poor as anything, who cannot be remembered by their own mothers, who sit where they are put, who are cared for tenderly in rooms with four doors and no requirements. They are called the thin. They are not ill. There is nothing to treat. They simply were never agreed with.

Between those two, everyone is trying to become just enough of a person to be loved and not enough to be a law.

## The Dead, Who Repeat

Decomposition is downstream and has not arrived, so the dead keep their edges perfectly, and they are registered, and their estates pay a share of the cold.

They lie in the Long Cold under the Filament Block, undiminished, and the ward's real problem with them is not horror but jurisprudence. The dead cannot vary. A dead man's fifty-year commute is still in the specification, unworn in the stone and worn through in the law, and you can feel it in the air on Candle Row: a pull to walk a certain line, a groove in nothing. Yield to it three times and it becomes yours.

So the ward crosses the street to avoid a dead man's habit. So the ward's oldest physics is the residue of the tics of people who are no longer available to apologize. Assay One is governed by its dead, and the living vary their routes for the same reason people elsewhere hold their breath in a graveyard.

There is a widow who walks her husband's line once a year, on purpose, in the rain that takes thirty hours to fall. She is keeping him load-bearing. She has been asked to stop. She has explained, at close range, quietly, that she will not.

## Out on the Rope

Surveyors go out in threes with a rope, and count each other constantly, and count in synonyms, because you must not say a name three times in the wrong place.

They come back with fewer measurements than they went out with, and sometimes with a fourth colleague who is registered and whom nobody remembers, and then the ward has to decide whether to have always had him. There is a man in the Blank-side almshouse who has been going out for thirty years and has, by his own count, lost eleven people, and can name none of them, and keeps eleven knots in a cord in his pocket, and is gentle, and is not permitted to work anymore.

The Blank is also the ward's mercy. Set a thing down beyond the rim and it will be wherever it needs to have been. So people put things out there. A chair that was a husband. A grief too large for nine square meters. The unratifiable, the undecided, the things nobody can hold. There is a shelf out there in no distance at all, and it is full.

And the rim advances four meters a year, and the world is being written outward, and everything on that shelf comes back inside eventually — arriving as fact, as grain, as slope, as geology. The ward's attic is its future. Every so often a surveyor's rope catches on something in the Blank that turns out to be a stone that has always been there, and the clerks write it down, and someone's abandoned grief becomes the reason a hill has the shape it has.

## Listeners

The Gully carries a liter an hour and sounds different to everyone, and every description is correct and every description is a vote.

Describers are paid a penny. There is a booth. There is a queue. Most people say *trickling*, *a tap*, *like a kettle beginning*, and the clerks write it down without expression, and in three hundred years water everywhere will sound like the median of these.

There is a woman who hears her mother in it. She has been going to the booth for six years and saying *like a tap* and taking her penny, and she is the ward's most cited example of civic virtue, and she has never told anyone why, and she is very tired.

The silents refuse to describe at all. They are considered either saints or saboteurs. Last winter a boy said the Gully sounded *like someone waiting to be met*, and the clerks wrote it down, and Ovin the greeter heard about it in the market and had to sit down on the wet stone, because he had said something almost exactly that to the figure in the spring, and he does not know anymore whether he was greeting the harbor or quoting the water, or whether the water was quoting his grandmother.

## The Bird's Congregation

One heron, nineteen years, one circuit of the rim per year, and the only fast thing in the world.

There are people who walk with it. They pace themselves to its lap — nine hundred meters in twelve months — and they are the slowest people in Assay One and consider themselves the fastest, because they are in the company of the strike. They are called the Circuit. They are mostly old. They carry cloths and they have opinions about the eleventh tree.

The strikescribes have four thousand records and a schism. The argument is which strikes are canonical: whether a strike at a worm counts, given that the worms may be rounding errors, and whether a strike that misses is a strike, and whether the bird's own variations should be averaged or whether the ward is waiting for one particular strike to be the one. There is a woman who has recorded eleven hundred strikes and believes she has seen the true one and will not say which, because saying which is a ballot.

Nobody feeds it. Feeding would decide its diet. Twice a year someone proposes feeding it and is shouted down at close range in the damp.

## The Bench

The bench on the terrace below the Hall of Ordinances faces the ordinary sun, and it is the most contested seat in the quarter, and the reason is not the view.

Light was ratified first. It is the one finished clause. Sunlight is *decided* warmth; it does not blur anything; it cannot make a lintel negotiable. Which means the bench is the only place in Assay One where you may lean.

Where you may put your arm around someone. Where a mother can hold a child against her shoulder without softening the child's shoulder or her own. Where two people can press their whole lengths together and remain, afterward, exactly as many people as they were. There is a rota. There is a black market in seat-time. There are marriages performed there at arm's length in the honest light, which is the only witness that has never once been wrong, and then, afterward, the arm's length is dropped, for eleven minutes, by agreement, in front of everyone.

The prints on the bench's back are layered a hundred deep and faintly warm and nobody thinks it is bad manners to touch them. Children run their hands along them. It is the ward's love literature, and it lasts about a year, and it is the only thing here that is allowed to be worn.

## Quotation

They all know. That is the thing that makes them gentle and vain and careful and slow.

Every one of them is an original. Some woman's way of pushing hair off her forehead is going to be how everyone, everywhere, forever, pushes hair off their forehead. Some man's shrug is load-bearing for a species that does not exist yet. So there are candidates: people who campaign, quietly, for their own mannerisms, who go to the plazas and perform a laugh, who have been at it thirty years. And rumor makes them heavy, the way it has made the eleventh tree's fork heavy — there is a woman on the Long Terrace rumored to be the prototype of mothers, and she has grown slower and denser than she should be, and children are brought to look at her, and she is not allowed to be tired.

And there is a boy from the warm creche, eleven or so, edges checked twice a week, who crouches at the Anticipated Sea with his hand under the water and strikes.

Fast. A crack of motion in a place where everything takes an hour. He has been practicing since he saw the bird do it in the spring, and he is not good at it yet, and a strikescribe has started writing him down.

Ovin wipes the figure's rail and says something about the bar being deeper than charted. The figure leans and waits. Somewhere above them the ward ticks as frost takes another micron, ten thousand drips whisper in a language nobody has settled, the Gully says *someone waiting to be met* to a woman who writes down *a tap*, and on the terrace two people sit closer than the ordinance permits anywhere else, in the ordinary winter sun, leaving a print that will stay warm for a year.

In three hundred years, everywhere, that is how people will sit beside each other.

No one will know it is a quotation.
