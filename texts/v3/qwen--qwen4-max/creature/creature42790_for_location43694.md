Sit still on the ledge for an hour, watch the patch of open stone to the left of the third basin, and stop looking at the light. Eventually the stone will produce a hand.

That is what the people who have sat long enough call it, and the name is not a metaphor: palm-sized, white, flat as a leaf, five-lobed at the margin, no head you can find, no front or back to its going. It comes out of a pore in the flowstone that you could not put a pencil into and it pours itself across nine centimeters of shining wet rock and it goes back into another pore, and the whole errand takes less than the hush it is fitted into. Two millimeters thick. It has no skeleton and no shell and nothing in it that the room could round off; it is a sheet of muscle and fluid, and it survives the fit by not being in it.

It does not hear the hush. Under its margin is a row of fluid-filled sacs, and what they read is the thirty-hertz fundamental — the note in your sternum and your teeth — and when the load shifts and the fundamental stops, the animal is already moving. It is out before the basins have finished going flat. Whatever it is, it is not waiting for a sound. It is riding the same unloading that lets you hear your own breathing.

It is going out to eat.

---

Consider what the room does to a swift that falls down the chimney. By morning it is a smooth pale oval, no feathers, no beak, no bone, no eye. Averaging kills, but it does not burn; it takes away the architecture and leaves the chemistry. The ovals are sterile and warm and soft and full of protein, and they lie on the open shining floor where nothing can grow, and once every few weeks the plain above drops another one.

The hands harvest them. A hand crosses to an oval, lays its margin against it, and rasps — a sound too small to exist in ninety-five decibels, but it is there in the hush if you are close and the oval is near the ledge, a sound like a wet thumbnail on glass. It has about a second and a half of grazing before it must turn. Then it takes what it has dissolved and carries it home into the dark, and the crumbs it drops in the pore-mouth are the substrate the amber felt has been living on since before there were names for anything.

So the food chain here runs: sky, accident, averaging, hand, felt. Four links, and the third one is a machine.

There is a thing about the hands that no one has solved and that the honest ones say out loud. *It has never been observed to be caught by a pulse.* Of course it hasn't. A hand caught in a socket would be, within the cycle, a smooth pale oval indistinguishable from a bird, and by the next cycle it would be nothing at all, and there would be no smear, no fragment, no stain, no evidence that anything had ever gone wrong. The room does not keep its failures. The perfection of the crossing is a statistic assembled entirely out of survivors, in a place that eats statistics.

And when a hand comes home it is faintly luminous — it has been out in the wet, and the wet gets on everything — and it stops in the vestibule of its pore and rubs itself against the dark stone until the shine is off it, because the shine kills whatever it touches, and the pore is full of things too small to leave.

---

The reeds are not decoration and they are not scenery. They are clocks, and they are the reason there is more than one kind of hand.

Four stands, widely separated, knee-high, hollow, pale, no green in them. When the upwelling shoves air out of the rock it plays them, and when the pressure falls their note decays — a thin dying whistle, four and a half seconds long, ending a fraction of a second before the floor stands up. That fraction is the entire margin of safety in this place. The hands run on it. They do not count; they listen for the note to reach a certain thinness, and then they are already going home.

Each stand decays a little differently. Bore diameter, wall thickness, the mineral crust in the throat — the four stands have four different curves, and the four populations of hands are tuned to their own, and a hand from the north stand put down beside the east stand will graze a quarter-second too long and become an oval. They cannot migrate. Twenty meters of open shining stone is twenty meters of open shining stone; a hand's whole reach is nine centimeters, out and back, forever.

Four populations, four tempos, four dialects, separated by less distance than you could throw a boot. Speciation by acoustics, in a room you can walk around in six minutes.

The east stand is going flat. Something clogged two of its bores — averaged matter, the room's own tidy leavings — and its note now ends early and ragged, and the hands there have been dying at a rate nobody can measure because there is nothing left to measure with. Some of them have stopped listening to the reeds.

They have switched to the tick.

The tick in the wall is small and dry and it is the only sound the machinery makes, and it does not care where you are. It is the same tick under every ledge. A hand that runs on the tick runs on a clock that works everywhere, and can therefore go anywhere, one hand's width at a time, along the dark under the ledges, around the whole perimeter, into places its ancestors could not have reached in a hundred thousand years.

There are perhaps forty of them. They are the only thing in the Sump that has changed.

---

The crust at the cube's threshold is not lichen and it assays as partly metal because it is making metal — laying down, a molecule at a time, the pale gray dry composite that the stumps and the stair and the handrail and the cube are made of. It is not growing on the material. It is growing *the* material. It is a millimeter a century and it is the only presence in the chamber that advances, because dryness is the one thing the shine cannot enter and the fit cannot round.

Its growth front is straight. Not fractal, not lobed, not the ragged edge every other living margin in the world makes. Rectilinear, with corners, and the corners are right angles, and it has begun a second rectangle on the floor of the cube's threshold, doorway-sized, oriented the same way as the rectangle in the west wall that is not a doorway.

Something in this room is making doors, slowly, out of metal, at the speed of a fingernail, and has been for longer than the felt has been thick.

There is a thin gray ring on the dry lip of the chimney, nine meters up, at the surface, where the carried light stops.

Nobody has explained the stopping. The crust is at the lip. Those are two facts, and the people who have noticed both of them tend to go up.

---

The felt is the archive, and it is the oldest thing alive here, and it does not know how to write.

Under the south ledge it is one and four-tenths meters thick, which at a millimeter a decade is fourteen thousand years, and it is laminated, and the laminae are not cycles. A cycle is seventy-one seconds; there are four hundred and forty thousand of them a year; nothing could lay down a layer that fast. What the felt records is *falls*. Each bird, each moth, each bat, each handful of dry plain dust that comes down the slot and gets averaged and settles out under the ledge where the fit doesn't reach, makes a dark line. The mat is a rain gauge for accidents. It is the only history in the chamber and it is a history of things arriving from above by mistake.

Forty centimeters down there is a pale band a millimeter thick — a decade of something that was not bird and not moth and not dust, coming down steadily, in quantity, for ten years.

Nine centimeters down, a lamina with glass in it.

And in the topmost millimeter, which is to say within the last ten years, there is a line so thin it takes a section and a stain and a very patient person to see it at all, and it is organic, and it is homogenous, and it is the leavings of something large that was averaged all at once and went everywhere in the water and came to rest, a little of it, in the one place the water doesn't sweep.

That is the whole funeral. It is being buried at a millimeter a decade. It will be legible for as long as there is felt.

---

Inside the cube: the sitter.

Four hundred and fifty thousand marks, one per cycle, spaced with a regularity that does not drift, does not cluster, does not correct. Three hundred and seventy-two days without a gap. Whatever sat in that groove did not sleep, or did not stop counting when it slept, and the difference is not recoverable. The marks tile the reachable interior exactly — every wall, the ceiling, the underside of the bench — and there is no unmarked surface left inside the cube. The last group is on the sill. Three strokes, and a fourth begun, and the fourth runs to the edge of the dry and stops there, at the line where the material goes wet, because the wall stopped being a wall you could scratch.

The room averages everything and the bench remembers one particular body. Those are the two kinds of memory in the Sump and they are opposites: the flowstone is a mold of the mean of all chaos, and the groove is a mold of one person's spine, and only one of them is a portrait.

The cup is upright and dry and it is not empty.

In it is a hand. Small — six centimeters, a juvenile, the fifth lobe of its margin still unfinished, a bud that never opened. Dry, white, intact, unaveraged, its two millimeters of sheet still holding the exact shape it died in. It is the only corpse in the chamber. It is the only dead thing in this place that has a shape of its own, and everything science knows about the young of the species it knows from this one body, in this one cup, in the one dry box, where somebody put it.

Somebody put it there. That is all that can be said and it is a great deal.

And the tick-followers have found the cube. They come along the dark of the ledge and over the crust and in through the open face, and they are dry here, and nothing rounds them, and nothing hurries them. They lie in the groove, because a groove the width and depth of a person will hold a great many hands, and they fit it the way a hand fits a glove. They run the tally marks. They go along the scratches with their whole margin, group by group, five by five, all four hundred and fifty thousand of them, over and over, at the speed of a thing that has all the time there is.

It is not reading. It is a property.

Or it is reading, and the difference between those two sentences is not available to anyone who has ever been down here, and will not be made available, and the marks go on being gone along in the dark by something blind and white and patient that lives in the only room in the world where a mark can stay.

---

Under the east ledge there is a hand the size of a dinner plate. It is the largest ever seen and it is presumably the oldest, and it crosses two hand's widths instead of one, and once — twice, in the recorded history of the place — it has been observed to stop. Out on the open shining stone, in the middle of a hush, with the reeds thinning toward their end and the load about to come back onto the bearing, it has stopped, and stayed, and then gone home in time.

Nobody knows what it was doing. There is nothing out there to eat. It is not a message. It is not a property either.

It is the only thing in the Sump that has ever spent part of its four and a half seconds on nothing at all, and it is the reason a certain kind of person sits on the ledge for eleven hours in the wet heat, drinking water that does not help, watching a patch of floor, waiting to be shown a pause.

Then the tick, and the drip, and the small dying note, and the white shape going home.

Then the floor stands up.
