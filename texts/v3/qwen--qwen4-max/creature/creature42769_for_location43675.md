## The Tenants

Begin with the feet.

Everything else about them is negotiable — the brass-button heads, the seams of gold, the shortened antennae, the thorax worn concave on the left side where four hundred years of leaning into a rib has pressed the whole lineage into a shape — but the feet are the truth of them. Six pads, softened, spread, gone slightly translucent, like the hands of women who have spent their lives in water. They do not grip. They *listen*. A tenant of the Hold stands the way you stand with your ear to a door, all day, in every posture, including sleep. Their feet are their ears and their mouth and their newspaper and their family, and a tenant who loses a foot does not limp; she goes mute and half-deaf, and is given work in the drifts, where the wings are soft and nothing needs to be heard.

They are small. They are warm. They are, by any measure you would apply from outside, extremely ordinary insects, and they have been ordinary for four hundred and eleven years in a way that requires more discipline than most orders of monks have ever managed.

They cannot hear the thing they spend their lives making.

---

The Holders are the largest guild and the least interesting to talk to, because their conversation is entirely about the shift. Two hundred to a gallery, eight galleries, and the fundamental below all of them, which nobody holds because everybody holds it: it comes up through the floor and the wing-drifts and the water in the cisterns and the bones of the frogs, and it is what a child knows before it knows its own name.

A Holder's name is a rhythm. You cannot say it in air. You put your body on a rib and knock it, four taps and a drag, and three terraces away someone feels *Four-and-a-Drag* arrive in the soles of her feet and knows who is being spoken to, or spoken of, or summoned. Names are short, because long names take time to knock and the ribs are public. Everything said on a rib is said to everyone on that rib. There is no whispering in the Hold. There is only the choice of which cable to stand on, and everybody knows which cables the gossips use, and the rent is low there.

Four-and-a-Drag has been on the second gallery for nineteen cycles. Her left side is dished. Her jaws are ground to a bevel and she has a callus on her sternum like a walnut, and when she sleeps she keeps sawing, faintly, out of time, and this is not a pathology; it is a contribution. The old are moved into rooms on the resonant ribs, the ones that carry best, and there they twitch through their last weeks and add their small ragged partial to the tone. The Hold does not have a hospice. It has a section of the orchestra where the dying sit. Their families visit and put a foot on the wall and feel the mother's arrhythmia going out into the world's structure, holding the crown up, and this is considered a good death and a good address.

They tune by falling wings. There is no other instrument. A wing dropped from the crown takes a certain time to reach the floor, and that time depends on the air, and the air depends on the volume, and the volume is the thing being defended. So the Sweepers keep a dropping-post at the crown and a girl with good feet at the bottom, and every dimming they let go a wing and count, and if the count is long the walls have come in and somebody is flat. The whole acoustic apparatus of a civilization is calibrated against a piece of shed membrane fluttering down through the dark. It works. It has worked for four centuries. The girl at the bottom is always the same girl for about six cycles and then she is somebody else, and the count goes on.

---

The Sweepers are the largest guild that is not the Holders, and they are the ones with dirt under their nails, and they are the only tenants who routinely handle the past.

The drifts at the wall's foot are the city's sediment and its larder and its quarry. Wings come down for hours after any arrival, and they lie in bands, amber and smoke, fat years and lean, and the white seams where a great many came in at once for reasons nobody recorded. Sweepers cut sections with their hands the way you'd cut a peat bank. They can tell you which band was the nematode summer. They can tell you the white seam ninety bodies deep that everyone calls the Wedding, though nobody knows what a wedding is; the word came up through the Weep with something that had ears, once, and died, and left the word behind as a twitch in a rib on the third terrace, and the tenants use it politely, the way you'd use a dead neighbor's name for a street.

Gleaners work the top of the drifts: springtails, in numbers that make the surface move like a pelt breathing. Below them the Sweepers chew pulp, and the pulp goes into the wall as request, and the wall answers with a room. So the houses are made of the wings of everyone who ever arrived. You live inside the shed flight of your great-grandmother's immigration. When a tenant says *my family has been here since the Wedding* she means that her family is, chemically, part of the load-bearing structure of the Wedding district, and that if you cut her wall you would find her ancestors' iridescence in the grain.

---

The Tasters are small, respected, and lying.

There is a reference in a sealed gall, and there is a sample from the current flesh, and the guild's whole function is to lay the two against a tongue and report the difference. The reports are scars: a wound in a chosen rib, in a chosen season, with the correct saliva, and the wall heals into a raised character that can be read with the feet. This is the only writing in the Hold. To read the archive you walk it. The oldest reports are in the Pricked Quarter, which means that the earliest and most alarming findings are in rooms nobody may enter, and the history of the sweetness is therefore, physically, a district that has been shrinking for ninety years.

Here is what the Tasters do not put in the scars. The reference is refreshed. It has always been refreshed, once a generation, from the flesh of the day, because a reference must be *sound*, and an old sample would sour, and a sour standard is worse than none. So the standard is always now. So the report has said *no change* for four hundred years, and the sweetness has climbed the entire time, and every tenant's teeth ache in the warm part of the cycle and nobody's report says why.

One Taster in nine keeps a private reference. She takes a drop of the true old sample and she wounds her own thorax and lets the gall close over it, and thereafter she carries the year 0 inside her body, and once a cycle she opens herself and tastes and knows exactly how fast the world is ending. It is not a secret society. It is worse: it is a habit, uncoordinated, passed from mistress to apprentice with no name. They tell each other in the twelve minutes.

---

Because there is one window in which the Hold is private, and it is made of noise.

Twelve minutes after the dimming the crown sweats hardest and the ceiling lets go all at once, and sixty feet of falling water hits a tuned membrane, and the floor becomes a solid roar, and no rib carries anything at all. The city clings. The frogs spawn. And for twelve minutes you can put your mouth against your daughter's antenna and say anything.

Everything true in the Hold is said in those twelve minutes. Confessions, treasons, the Tasters' private numbers, the Reconcilers' methods, love, the names of the dead spoken badly, the actual opinion of the ants. Then the rain thins and the ribs come back and everyone climbs to their shift with their feet full of a silence they cannot break, because to record a thing you must wound a wall and wait a season, and by then you have decided not to.

The Hold has no memory of any of its honest speech. It has an extremely good memory of its rents.

---

The frogs own the water and have owned it since before the Long Gallery, and they are the only tenants who do not hold, do not sweep, do not count, and do not pretend to. Blunt, patient, earless — their lineages gave up hearing a hundred generations ago and got the cisterns in exchange, or that is how the frogs tell it. They sit in bract-cups the size of bathtubs with their hands in the algae and they spawn in the roar, and their tadpoles are the city's water quality, and if a cistern goes bad the district moves. Rent on the second terrace is water rent. It is paid in pulp and in defense and in a deference so old it has become a posture.

The ants are foreigners and always will be. They came in on somebody's legs, they farm scale insects along the ribs, they drive and milk and defend with a discipline the tenants find frankly foreign, and they have something no wasp in the Hold has: a living mother, in a gall on the ninth cable, fed and turned and carried. The wasps' foundress died four hundred years ago. Every wasp here is the orphan of one woman. The ants still have theirs, and when the tone fails they will take her out through the Eye in six pieces and be the only survivors of the end of the world, and they will not remember it, because they were never listening.

The Verger is a blind centipede as long as your forearm and he is the calendar. One terrace a day, Ladder Nine, announced and never fought; the announcement goes down the rib ahead of him and the district clears its washing and its children. He has been round more times than the pollen record has years. He is the only creature who has walked the widdershins circuit enough times to be bored by passing the Weep four times in a loop that contains it once, and the Sweepers, who use that fact to move drifts, consider his knowledge uninteresting, and are right, and are also the reason the city eats.

---

The poor drink the latex.

You choose your spot. You drink. It is bitter and it sets, and you stiffen where you stand, over about a day, and the wall digests you slowly and grows a room around you, and in a season your grandchildren are living in your chest. Most of the older tenements are people. This is not a scandal; it is a housing policy, and there is a waiting list, and the good spots go to the old and the well-liked, and there is a form of words knocked on the rib when a family moves into a grandmother.

And there are the leftovers, which are different. Anything that arrived with ears went out into the void and died of the noise, and the wall took it, and thereafter the wall has a habit: a rib that twitches at a certain hour, a shudder that runs the Long Gallery every ninth dimming. Those are the ancestors. They are addressed politely. Children knock *good morning* on the shudder and feel nothing back and are told that this is what respect feels like. Somewhere in the fourth terrace there is a bird's hearing, four hundred years dead, still going off at dusk, and the district sets its shift change by it, and calls it the Bell, and does not know what a bird is.

---

On the fifth partial there is a tax district that does not exist anywhere else, and it is full.

When a shift fails and the walls come in a hand's breadth, everything becomes nearer, and there are two of some things. Two Vergers. Two of a courier named Halla, both carrying the same pollen, both certain, both correct. The reserve gallery comes in, the walls go back out, and the Reconcilers do their work, which is discussed only in the twelve minutes and remembered by nobody.

What they do is assess.

The second Halla is not killed. She is entered on the roll of the fifth-partial district, where she pays what she would owe if she were spatially obliged, and where she lives with the other twice-born, in a Long Gallery of their own whose scoring nobody can read, and where the same year appears forty-one times in a pollen record worn smooth, with grooves in it. They are not ghosts. They have calluses. They come on shift. They are the only citizens who have felt the wall stop answering, and they describe it, in the twelve minutes, over and over, to people who will not carry it out of the rain: *a pressure drop in the chest, and then everything was close, and then I was not the only one who was right.*

---

And once or twice a cycle a foundress goes up.

She crawls the Bitter Reach, dusts herself with pollen so the florets stay half-made and the fruit stays undecided, squeezes through the scales, and enters a world she will experience for eleven hours. Eleven hours: that is the entire outside, for her species. Mud, wind, the flank of another low tree, another needle-hole. Everything you are standing in as you read this is, to her, a birth canal between two rooms.

At the threshold she sheds her wings. She also sheds her antennae.

Which means she arrives in the new chamber unable to speak, unable to feel a rib, unable to knock her own name. She has eleven hours of memory and no organ for any of it. So she does the only thing left: she wounds a wall in a pattern her body remembers, in the correct season, with the correct saliva, and waits, and the wall answers with a colonnade.

Nobody in the new Hold will ever be able to read it. It will be a district. It will be called something short. The rent will be high.

Every Hold has one. Every Hold has a Long Gallery whose scoring means nothing, and it means *my mother's name was four taps and a drag, and we held the note, and I am sorry, and it is still green.*

---

So: what lives here.

Four thousand small warm deaf bodies with listening feet, and eight galleries of them leaning left, and a woman with a walnut on her sternum, and a girl counting a falling wing, and a Taster with the year 0 sealed in her chest, and a blind centipede who is Tuesday, and blunt frogs with water rights older than the architecture, and foreigners with a mother in six pieces waiting to be carried, and a district of second copies who are correct, and ten thousand rooms that used to be grandmothers, and a shudder that used to be a bird.

And in the middle, ninety-eight parts in a hundred of everything, the void: the great warm volume of sweet air with the tone standing in it, which none of them can hear, and all of them are holding open. They are not living in a fruit. They are living in a refusal, four centuries thick, and the refusal has stairs, and gutters, and a housing list, and a district where the rent is high.

Put your palm flat against the skin. That small change under your hand, that hitch and settle — that is Four-and-a-Drag coming on shift, and her mother before her, and the note not moving.

She cannot hear it. She is very good at it.
