Start with the skin, because it is the only thing in the valley that wears.

Everything else thickens. The lips of the basins thicken, the jambs thicken, the chisel handles thicken under the hand that holds them. A person goes the other way. A person is a soft subtraction in a hardening world, and the water is always trying to close them, and their whole life is the argument.

You can read a stranger's history off their forearms. Speech here is scraping, and to be spoken to is therefore to be abraded, so the much-addressed develop a reading surface: a yellow, glassy, faintly translucent patch along the outer forearm, thin as a fingernail, warm to the touch, worn *down* while the city around it builds up. A person nobody talks to has thick arms, coarse and unreadable, and to speak to them at all is to hurt them. Foremen have arms like the flat of a blade. Hermits have arms like the wall.

The back of the left hand carries the name-pad. Names are tapped there, always there, by everyone who addresses you, and a well-known person's pad rises into a callus the size of a coin — the only writing that ever occurs on a body. Magistrates have pads you can feel across a room. A child's pad is a suggestion. When a name is struck from the archive the pad is shaved flat by a guild officer with a wetted stone, and this takes an afternoon, and the person is afterward Smooth: alive, unaddressable, and touched only with tools. There are perhaps two hundred of them. They clear the hollows and carry the sealed dead. They go unoiled, and the lace comes up over their knuckles and their eyelids and their shoulders, and by forty they are partly statue, and nobody has ever thought this cruel, exactly, because they are already counted among the walls.

Teeth go flat by thirty from the grit in the mat-cake. Eyes go late, and not from age: the lid rims plate, the lashes cable together, and a person's sight closes like a door closing, a millimeter a year, until they are led. Hearing goes on schedule, the top of it, somewhere between ten and twenty. What is left is the low end, the grain in the sternum, and this is not deafness as the dry world understands it. They feel the valley's state as a pressure with texture. A scraper knows a chute has silted because the floor of their chest has changed pitch. They cannot hear their own child. They can hear the mountain arriving.

Distance, for them, is wetness. Near the spray face you are wetter; deep in the mass you are merely damp. A body navigates by the gradient on its cheeks. Faces are not the site of identity here — nobody looks at faces much, and nobody is good at it. You know a person by the topography of their nape, the specific ridge and hollow of it, the smell of their rime, the rhythm they tap. Lovers are cartographers of one shoulder blade. When they want a face, they cast it.

A woman on the ninth terrace, whose name is three quick taps and a drag back, which glosses roughly as *Wide-Enough*. She is fifty-one, which is to say six fingers and a knuckle. She is a separation-mason, guild of doorways, and her working life is four doorways and a stair.

She rises in the bruise-dusk before the ceiling brightens, because the cut is best done while the stone is still soft from the night's drizzle. She oils her hands from a thimble of fish fat — oil is the resist; oiled things do not plate; this is the whole of their chemistry and the whole of their economy — and she goes to the first jamb and cuts back the day's millimeter, and then the next day's worth in advance where the spray is heaviest, and she checks against the copper gauge, which she also oils, which she has oiled every morning for thirty-five years, and which is therefore the second-most-worn object she owns.

The doorways of her beat have widths in the archive. The widths are grooves, and the grooves are maintained, and the lawful width of a door is not a measurement but a maintained depth, and to widen a door without authority is not vandalism but perjury.

At midmorning she is read eleven or twelve times: a neighbor asking about a cup, her sister's boy, a foreman, a stranger wanting the outflow road. Each one takes her forearm and draws along it, cleaning as they speak, and she answers on their shoulder, and both of them are a little more worn afterward than before. She has been read perhaps four hundred thousand times. Her arms shine. When she is very old, being spoken to will feel like being sanded, and people will come anyway and speak to her with a soft cloth over their fingers, slowly, which is what love looks like here, and she will let them, because the alternative is to go unread and thicken.

Her house has three rooms and a cup. The cup is a hollow in the wall of the inner room where the ceiling's drizzle is caught and led, and it is a hand deep, and it took her grandfather forty years to keep it a hand deep, and this is the family's entire wealth: not the stone, which is free and arriving, but the forty years of not letting a hole close.

The trades sort themselves by what they resist.

Ten thousand scrapers, of whom perhaps six thousand are separation-masons and the rest channel-cutters, basin-dressers, cup-keepers, chute-clearers. They cut to a groove. Their craft is entirely negative and entirely daily; there is no such thing as a finished door, only a door that was opened this morning.

The re-cutters keep the archive, which is a wall of ten thousand grooves in the deepest and driest chamber of the mass, reached by a tunnel four hundred paces long, and even there a line fills in five years. They work in rotation, a groove each day, a lifetime of grooves. Depth is truth. A thing said once is a scratch; a thing said every year for two hundred years is a trench you can lay a finger in, and it is *true*, and there is no other criterion. They can date a groove by the shape of its mouth: honest repetition wears a groove wide and rounded, generations of hands going in and out at slightly different angles, a mouth like a river valley. A fraud is deep and narrow. A fraud is *sharp*. This is the only forensic science they have and it is infallible, and it means that in a place where nothing is old, they can tell truth from lies by wear.

The cone-cutters go out along the rimstone dams into the wader colonies with hooks and gut plugs in their ears and come back with blood in the plugs. The birds build faster than the water does. Somebody has to cut the birds. Two hundred thousand nesting cones rebuilt daily, calcite grit and guano, raising the dams, shoving the terraces upvalley and uphill — the colonies are a city with no scrapers, a city that is winning, and the noise off them is brass at a hundred and ten decibels and it is the only sound in the valley that anyone is afraid of. Cone-cutters retire at thirty-five with their chests ruined. Their name-pads are enormous.

The listeners are children, pressed to partitions to hear hollows and cracks and the change in a channel, and they are retired at ten, when the instrument begins to fail, and they are given a second name at retirement: the last sound they could still swear to, rendered as a tap-rhythm. There is a woman on the fourth terrace called *The-Crack-in-the-Sixth-Partition*. There is a man of seventy called *His-Mother-Saying-His-Name*. They live out their lives as the only people in the city who have heard anything, and they are hired — this is their trade, this is what the Retired do — to sit with the bereaved and tap, on the back of the hand, the rhythm of a dead person's voice.

They were nine. They are remembering across sixty years. They are, all of them, unreliable, and everyone knows it, and everyone pays.

And the oil-pressers, and the cord-braiders, and the hair-farmers — hair is crop here, grown long, harvested at the waist, and it plates as it grows, so a woman's dowry is a white cable she has to have cut off her with a chisel — and the natron-walkers out at the lagoon, and the cast-makers, and the cave-carriers who go up for dryness with a body packed in natron on their back.

---

The other citizens are not people, and it is not clear that the people are the majority.

The mats are the population. Everything else is a complication of the mats. A felted mat in a tepid basin is ankle-deep and layered like the pages of a wet book, and the layers are annual, and a mat-eater of any refinement can taste a decade in a mouthful and tell you which year the iron ran high. The archive is a wall of maintained grooves; a mat is a wall of unmaintained ones. The mats keep better records than the re-cutters do. Nobody has thought about this very hard, because thinking about it leads somewhere the city does not want to go.

The snails are gravel that walks, ten thousand to the square meter, and they are the poor's protein and the poor's paving and, eventually, the poor's walls. The flies stand in clouds and make the wire-tone over the drum. The fish are thick and stupid and heat-hardened and there are so many of them in the tepid channels that a basket lowered blind comes up full.

And in the sealed houses — the hills, the ones left three generations — there is fauna.

When a house closes, the mats and the beetles get in before the stone finishes, and they eat everything soft, and then the stone finishes, and there is a void left in the exact shape of a basket, a mat, a curled body. Sometimes the beetles do not die. Sometimes a lineage goes on in there, in the old air, in a hollow the shape of a kitchen, eating the last of the mat and then eating each other and then eating whatever the mats send in through the hairline, for eighty years, a hundred, blind, pale, small, speciated. Every hill has its own. No two are the same. When a mason's chisel breaks into a hollow they come out over her hands in a warm rush, dozens of species that no living person has seen, and the Smooth are called to clear them, and the hollow is plugged.

They are the only new things that ever happen in the valley. The word for change is the word for error. The guild has a suppressed catalogue of them, cut very deep.

What they do with each other, in the hours not spent cutting:

They sing. All of them, constantly, inaudibly, the sound coming up through the jaw from somewhere behind the sternum, and since the only thing anyone has ever heard is the roar, the eleven thousand private operas are eleven thousand variations on one drone. A person's inner song is the only private thing in the world and it is absolutely private, and it is the self, and there is exactly one way to share it: press your skull to another skull. Temple to temple, or jaw to jaw, or forehead to forehead, and bone conduction crosses, and you are inside someone else's opera and they are inside yours.

This is the intimacy. Mothers do it to infants for hours. Lovers do it until their temples ache. It is the worst obscenity to press your head to a stranger, worse than striking them, and there is a word for the people who do it. A marriage is a two-part song worked out over decades, and when one of them dies the survivor goes on singing both parts, and everyone who touches them can feel the gap where the harmony was, because a shudder travels a hundred meters through the one stone, and the whole terrace knows.

They laugh, and laughter is seismic. A joke is a tickle in the ribs, delivered fast, and the answer is a body-shake, and the floor carries it, and you feel a hundred people laugh at once through the soles of your feet and never learn what the joke was. There are professional ticklers. There is a comic tradition entirely about doorways, and another entirely about the Smooth, which is not funny to the Smooth.

They eat boiled things. Everything is boiled, in the Kettles, in baskets on cords, at ninety-six degrees, and nothing has ever been roasted or smoked or charred, and so the whole cuisine is texture: the specific resistance of a thing to the tooth. A great cook is a person who can lower a basket for a count of eleven and bring up something that pushes back correctly. Everything tastes faintly of stone. Everyone's teeth are flat.

And they cast. To sculpt here is to make an absence: form a thing in mat and gut and hair, let the water plate it, let the beetles eat the inside, and what remains is a perfect white hollow in the exact shape of the thing. They export these. They also cast hands, and feet, and the faces of the living, pressed into the setting gel at the downvalley end where the rims are still soft, and this is what a portrait is: a hole with your face in it.

The shrines are the hollows they did not plug. A mason breaks into a hill and finds old air and the shape of a bowl and a handprint with the ridges still in it, and instead of plugging it she sets a fish-oil lamp at the mouth.

And here is the thing the masons know and do not advertise: outside, nothing has a shadow, because the light comes from everywhere. Inside a hollow, with a lamp at its mouth, there is a shadow. There is one. It moves when the flame moves. It falls across the inside of a dead woman's ribcage, in the exact shape of her ribs, and it is the only shadow in the world, and children are taken in once, at seven or eight, and made to put a hand into it, and they do not have language for what they feel and they never entirely recover.

The religion, when you get down to it, is not about the water.

The water is the creditor. The water is level, and justice is level, and the name of the city is two flat hands sliding over one another and means *held*, *even*, *the debt is paid for another day*. You do not worship your creditor. You pay him.

What they revere is wear.

It comes out slowly, in the way they handle things. The archive's honest grooves, worn wide by two centuries of hands. The copper gauge, thinned by thirty-five years of oiling. The forearm, polished translucent by four hundred thousand conversations. And at the center, passed hand to hand in the dark of the deepest room, an imported chert blade from the dry world, rounded, scratched, thin from a century of sharpening — a thing that has been *used* and has not been renewed, the only object anyone has ever touched that is less than it was, and they weep and cannot say why.

Not damage. Contempt is a coarse grit, fast, and that wears a person too, and it leaves them raw. The distinction they cannot articulate but never get wrong is *use attended to*. A wound is wear without care. A holy thing is wear that someone polished. This is why affection is a slow cloth, why the old are addressed through fabric, why the guild officer who shaves a name-pad is watched with a horror nobody can justify: he is not damaging the hand. He is erasing the use.

Against this there is the other party, and the argument is old and cut very deep on both sides.

The buds. Small white calcite buds with a green ghost at the center, seeds that arrived on a bird's foot and were sheathed in a fortnight, kept in the dry reliquaries, never used, never worn, perfect. These are the most valuable objects in the valley and they are not money. The Dry hold that holiness is what has been *kept* — the natron-packed ancestors up in the caves, brown and light and whole, in the only air in the world that will not add anything to them; the bud with its ghost; the groove that would not need re-cutting if only you could get it dry enough. Their entire program is a room in which a fact stays true without labor. Their priests travel up for dryness and come back with a pinch of cave dust, which is the rarest substance in the valley, and which, dropped into saturated water, immediately begins to grow a pearl.

The Worn hold that holiness is what has been *used*, and that a thing which does not need maintaining is not holy but merely dead, and that the Dry are proposing to move the archive into a cave full of bats and call that truth.

Both parties handle the blade. Neither can explain the weeping. The Dry say it is recognition of the sun. The Worn say it is recognition of work.

And when traders come down the stair from the rim, and one of them goes up and sees the sun and comes back sun-blind and strange, he tries to render it in touch, and he puts a warm palm on your face and holds it there and takes it away and puts it back, and this is not it, and he knows it is not it. There is a man on the sixth terrace who has lived here forty years whose name is that gesture. His name is a failed description of the sun. Everyone taps it kindly.

---

Wide-Enough will die at about seven fingers, which is old. Her arms will be too thin to read by then, and her sister's granddaughter — Retired, called *A-Crack-Under-the-Ninth* — will come and speak to her through a cloth, slowly, an hour a day, and it will still hurt, and she will let her.

When she goes into the pool she will become the wall, and the wall will be cut back and re-cut, and she will circulate; her name will be tapped on her descendants' hands for three or four generations and then less, and the pad on the last hand that carries it will soften, and that is the second death and it is not marked. Her cup will be kept open by someone or it will not. Her four doorways will be exactly as wide tomorrow as they are today, because six thousand people will make sure of it, and this is not a metaphor for anything, it is the literal achievement of the city, renewed each morning, and it is the only kind of eternity they have.

Except.

In her fifty-first year she cut one jamb a hair wide. Not much — a fingernail's thickness over the gauge, on a door in a poor court where the spray is heavy and the family cannot pay a cup-keeper. She did it because the woman there had a bad shoulder and could not turn sideways anymore. Then she did it the next day, and the next, and she went on doing it for eleven years, and by the time she stopped, the door was a finger and a half wider than the groove in the archive said it was.

The guild found it. They measured it against the record and the record was shallower than the door, and by their own law a thing repeated often enough becomes deep enough to be true, and eleven years of daily cutting is a great deal of repetition, and the mouth of that particular groove had gone wide and rounded in the way that only honest wear makes.

They argued for a season. Nobody had a word for what had happened. *Error* was available. *Fact* was available. The re-cutters were sent for and they put their fingers in it and stood there a long time in the shell-colored light with the roar coming through the stone into their chests, feeling the shape of the wear, which was the shape of the truth, which was the shape of a woman's hand going in and out at slightly different angles for eleven years.

They deepened the groove.

Then they went out and the water was still arriving, and the mouths were still pouring, and the doors were exactly as wide as they had been, and one door was a finger and a half wider, and the ceiling dimmed by half at an hour nobody could point to, and eleven thousand people sang their private operas into their own skulls, and somewhere in a plugged hill a blind beetle that no one will ever name went on eating the last of a sleeping mat.
