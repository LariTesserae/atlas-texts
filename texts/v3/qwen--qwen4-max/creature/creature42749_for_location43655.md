## The count

Count the residents and you get a number small enough to write on your hand: eleven people from thaw to first snow, two after; one tree; one mouse lineage; nine populations of a blind amphipod; a lark; a beetle; a flatworm; a springtail population that cannot be counted and does not need to be; and one plant, which is also the floor, the roof, the water supply, the weather, and the noise.

Everything else is visiting. The harrier is visiting. The rain is visiting. You are visiting, and the mat has already noticed.

## The crew

They are known by their stretches. Three-and-Four. Six-to-Eight. The Weeps, which is the worst job and the one everyone wants, because the weeps are where the shelf is doing something and a person can stand there and watch water leave. The oldest trimmer has held Seven-to-Eleven for thirty-eight summers and has been called Eleven for so long that his sister, in the valley, has to think for a second when someone asks her brother's name.

There is no talking up here. Ninety-four decibels of broadband hiss removes speech the way a river removes a footprint, so the crew has gone where everything else on the plateau has gone: visual, and under. They sign — a small vocabulary, forty or fifty signs, most of them about knives, water, and the condition of a lip — and they chalk. Every intake carries a slate of painted dates and, beside them, a chalk panel that is wiped and rewritten all summer: *SEVEN WEEPING SOUTH. RACK BENT. TURF STACK AT 41.* Chalk lasts about four days in that humidity. The whole maintenance history of the scheme is written in a medium that cannot survive a week, on top of a record chiselled into concrete by men who wanted it to last forever.

And they thump. This is the part a new hand learns in the first fortnight, without being taught, the way you learn to lean in a doorway on a moving train. You cannot hear a man ten meters away, but you can feel him: the mat carries a heel-drop beautifully, thirty centimeters of felt over gravel, a drum skin four hundred square kilometers wide. Two thumps: *here.* Three, slow: *come.* A long roll made by shifting weight from heel to toe: *look down, look what I've found.* The shear handle is used for this too, butt-end into a cushion, and a trimmer who has been up here long enough will hold conversations of considerable subtlety through the soles of his boots while facing the other way.

They wear ear defenders, which cut the hiss and do nothing at all to the flush. The forty-one-minute cycle arrives in the sternum and the ankles: the thickening, the drop, the return. Nobody up here needs a watch. The gates flush, and eleven people straighten up at the same moment across thirty kilometers of empty floor, and then bend back down.

Their bodies are the only place on the plateau where anything accumulates quickly. Sunburn on the nose, cold in the wrists, a permanent squint, hands like tool handles, and — inside, where it is warm — the note.

## Under the floor

Put your arm in up to the shoulder and you are in the inhabited part.

It is not dark. Light comes down through waxed leaves and through the water film itself, which hangs in ten million apertures a hectare and acts as ten million small lenses, so the gravel below is covered in a slow-moving lace of caustics, bright rings sliding and merging, the same pattern you get on the bottom of a swimming pool. Green-gold, moving, warmer than the air by a degree or two, humid beyond humidity, and loud in a way that is different from above: up top the roar is a substance, down here it is *events*, the individual plinks and knocks separated out by the felt, each one arriving from a specific direction. Under the canopy you can hear that the noise is made of things.

Springtails live on the underside of the ceiling, on the meniscus, in their millions, grazing the film. They are the pasture. The tea-colored flatworm moves through them like a spill. There is a mite nobody has named that eats the flatworm's eggs, and a flightless black beetle that carries a bubble of air against its abdomen like a mercury bead, replenishes it at the weeps, and lives two years down there, which is a long life for a beetle and an eyeblink for the floor it lives in.

The beetle's wings are gone. Not reduced — gone, the elytra fused into a smooth black shell, because there is nowhere to fly to that is not the same place, and the air above is a wall of falling water. Half the invertebrates under the mat have done this. The plateau's animals are steadily resigning from the sky.

None of them use sound. They tap. The beetle drums on a sedge stem with its head, a stuttering pulse train that travels the felt for meters; the flatworm answers by pressing; there are rhythms recorded down there that repeat across seasons and have no assigned function, and a paper about them, and an argument. It is a private network in a public roar, and it has been running since before the valley had a church, and it does not care in the slightest that the air is unusable, because it never used the air.

Their clock is forty-one minutes. The flush is the only event on the shelf with a shape, and underneath it is a tide: the sheet thickens, the film rises a millimeter, the strand line of dead springtails moves up the gravel, the flatworm follows it, the beetle vents and refills. Everything down there breeds, feeds, and hides on a forty-one-minute calendar. Twelve to fifteen thousand water-events a second is weather. The flush is history.

And they get warnings. Step down hard anywhere on the shelf and the pressure pulse goes out through the continuous water in the felt, and thirty meters off the cushion pores begin to close, minutes later, in a slow ripple. Downstairs that pulse is unmistakable. Long before a trimmer arrives, the ceiling above him has begun to shut, the pasture has withdrawn, the beetle has stopped drumming. The mat announces you. It has no hurry about it — it is not afraid, it is not anything — but it tells the whole neighborhood, and the neighborhood is already gone by the time your boot lands.

The crew knows this. Eleven will tell you, if you get him in the bothy with a thermos, that the shelf is polite. It clears a path.

## The nine dark rivers

Inside the sumps there is current, which is to say there is a thing that does not exist anywhere else on the plateau: a flow with a direction, gathered, strong enough to push against.

The amphipods live there. Pale, blind, a centimeter long, in the gravel at the bottom of nine concrete pits, filtering the concentrated sheet. They are relicts — outwash animals, left over from the meltwater that ran here for a few decades after the ice let go, and they should have died out nine hundred years ago when the mat closed the drainage down. They didn't, because the scheme punched nine holes in the floor and made nine small permanent streams, and the last of the glacier's river fauna crawled into them and has been waiting there ever since, in the dark, under a steel trash rack, in a habitat that only exists because a company in the valley wanted load that never varies.

Nine populations, isolated, drifting apart. Six-to-Eight's amphipods have a longer antenna than the Weeps'. Gene flow between them is entirely by human hand: a knife blade, a boot sole, a lifted turf carried forty meters and re-laid. The crew is the river system. They are the only means these animals have of meeting.

If you want to see the future of the plateau, don't look at the trench gully. Look in the sump. Everything that will live in the rivers of four thousand years' time is already alive up here, in nine concrete boxes, being maintained.

## The lark

The mat-lark is the only bird that stays, and it does not live on the mat. It lives *in* it.

The nest is a chamber under the canopy, reached through a beetle hole or a broken edge, a warm wet room in the dead felt with a roof of living leaves and a floor of gravel and a ceiling that glows. Two or three eggs. The chicks are raised in the only warm place on the shelf, in the compost heat of nine centuries of ancestors, and they are fed springtails scraped off the meniscus and amphipods robbed from the weeps, which is why the lark's territories are not spread evenly across four hundred square kilometers but strung along the contours near the intake mouths, one pair per two or three mouths, a distribution that looks, on a map, exactly like a subsidy.

Above ground the cock courts with a shiver and a flash of white underwing, because a song here would be a mouth moving in a roar. Everyone knows this about the lark. What is less known, and what Eleven has been writing on the back of chalk slates for eleven years, is that the cock also drums. He lands, he stands on a cushion, and he beats it with his feet — a fast triple, a pause, a fast triple — and thirty meters away, under the floor, another bird answers.

They are moving channels. In front of us. The one bird that stayed is teaching itself the private language of the beetles, going under with everything else, and in some number of centuries there will be a flightless thing down there with a white patch on its wrist that no longer knows it was ever a bird.

## The tree, the mouse, the moth

The spruce was planted in 1911 by a man whose name is in the scheme's ledger and who is otherwise gone. Four meters, wind-flagged, all its needles on the lee side, kept alive by sentiment: it has been guyed twice, mulched with imported soil (the only soil on the plateau, eleven bags of it, carried up the incline), and pruned by people who did not know how to prune a spruce. It is the only resident that stands up and is not steel. It has its own tenants — a lichen that grows nowhere else on the shelf, a spider that rebuilds the same web every night in the lee, and, in a good October, a wren that has come three thousand meters up to be somewhere quiet.

The mouse lives in the head-house and is descended, presumably, from a mouse that came up in a crate with the incline. It is the only mammal born on the plateau other than the crew's children, who are born in the valley. It eats crumbs, and it does not go outside, and if you set it on the mat it will run in a straight line for the nearest wall.

The transformer shed is the warmest dry air for thirty kilometers and has a moth in it. Not a species — a *population*, of a valley moth, established up here on the strength of a chapel-sized box that has been at forty degrees since before the war. Its wings work fine. There is nowhere to fly.

## The one plant

Now the resident that is hard to write about without sounding like you are claiming something.

The mat is a single continuous fused thing, thirty to fifty centimeters thick, four hundred square kilometers, and the bottom two-thirds of it is dead. It stands on its own dead. The brown felt beneath is nine hundred years of previous surfaces, perfectly preserved because it is cold and wet and the fungi down there work at the pace of geology, and it is holding the living skin up off the rock like a floor on joists. The plant is living on the accumulated bodies of every version of itself since the ice. That is not a metaphor. It is the load path.

Cores through it read like an archive. Ash layers. A copper band from the insulators. A thin gray line of concrete dust that dates to the year the sumps were cut. And, at every intake, folds — inverted stratigraphy, doubled bands, a place where the sequence runs backward — because a turf was lifted, stacked, and re-laid by a man with a hook knife. You can count the maintenance. Six or seven hands of paint on the lip; six or seven folds in the felt beneath it. The mat's body is a record of everyone who has ever cut it.

It signals hydraulically because that is the only medium it has that is continuous. It has no center, no season of decision, no speed. Its response time to a footstep is minutes; its response time to a cable trench is four thousand years. It is not aware of you. It is *informed* of you, in the way a lake is informed of a stone.

And here is the thing that takes longer than a day to accept. The mat cannot heal a wound. Break it and you get lichen scab and then, if the water finds the till, a gully inventing itself headward at eleven meters a century — the only bare mineral ground for thirty kilometers, nine meters long, the youngest landscape in the district. The mat's rule is absolute: *no ground exposed, ever.* It cannot enforce that rule itself. It grows two to four millimeters a year.

So it has hands. Eleven of them, from thaw to first snow. They arrive on an incline paid for by nine turbine houses that exist only because this plant regulates water better than any dam in the range, and their entire occupation is cutting the mouths open and carrying turves so that no ground is ever exposed. The crew's discipline is the mat's discipline. The scheme's business model is the mat's metabolism. The machines are here to sell constancy; the constancy is a living thing's habit; the living thing's habit requires open mouths; the mouths are kept open by animals that come up the headwall every May because a company sends them.

Nobody arranged this. It is not intention. It is a loop that closed, and it has been closed for a hundred years, and inside it four hundred square kilometers of plant has acquired a maintenance department.

## Winter, and the hush

For a few weeks in deep winter the snow seals the apertures.

The sheet keeps moving underneath — it never stops, the felt is still composting, still a degree or two warm — but the falling is muffled, the ten thousand threads at the rim are ice, and the roar goes out of the air. The valley's name for the shelf becomes true. Two people are up here to hear it, and both of them will tell you, separately, in the same words more or less, that it is the worst thing on the plateau.

Because in the silence you can hear the tapping. All of it. The whole understory, talking, at once, across a floor that has gone quiet: beetles drumming, stems ticking, the lark's triple under the snow, and things nobody has identified, kilometers of it, a conversation that has been going on the entire time underneath the noise and is only audible when the noise stops. Eleven has heard it thirty-eight times. He has never once gone out onto the mat to listen. He listens from the doorway with the light behind him.

## What leaves

The plateau exports one thing besides electricity, and it exports it inside people.

Every trimmer goes down the incline at the end of the season carrying the note. Not a memory of it — the note itself, a permanent high hiss laid in by the cochlea, which follows him into the valley, into church, into bed, and stays for the rest of his life. It is the only resident that emigrates. There are men in the town, seventy and eighty years old, who have not been up the headwall in decades and in whom the shelf is still roaring.

And they are archives. The plateau's voice has dropped a semitone in a century as the gullies open and the apertures widen, and the instruments agree, and there is a wax cylinder from the opening ceremony that agrees too. When you put a gauge on the shelf and then ask an old man to hum what he hears at night, he does not match the shelf. He matches the cylinder. He is carrying, in his head, a pitch the world no longer makes: the plateau as it sounded when he was twenty, held in a body that is failing, more accurate than any recording, unverifiable except against other old men.

There are four of them left who worked the Weeps before the flush cycle was re-timed. They meet in the valley, in a room with a carpet, and they hum, and they argue about a semitone.

Meanwhile the amphipods wait in nine concrete boxes for a river. The lark beats a cushion with its feet. The felt lays down three millimeters. And you walk east on the canopy with the sun coming up off the wax under your chin, and thirty meters ahead of you, in a slow ripple you would never notice unless you had been told, the pores are closing, and the pasture is withdrawing, and the drumming has stopped, because the floor knows you are coming and has all the time in the world.
