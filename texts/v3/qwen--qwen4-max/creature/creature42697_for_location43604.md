Most of what lives here has no face, and the place is not arranged to give it one.

The basin stock — the stamp calls them HX/4, and the stamp is the only thing that has ever called them anything — are kidney-shaped because a kidney is the shape that puts the most wet surface closest to the most blood. Four meters along the long axis. A hide like the inside of a wrist, hairless, faintly grained, sound-eating. Ribs that are not ribs but fins, opening and closing a millimeter, a millimeter, a millimeter, sixty thousand turns of it, which is a little over ten years, though there is no year here and nothing has ever needed one.

Where a face would be there is a seam. It runs from the high shoulder to the low flank and it is not a mouth; they do not eat with it; feed comes as a warm slurry through the floor channels and is taken up through the feet, which are pads, which are also roots. The seam is where the animal was folded before it was unfolded. It is the only asymmetry it has. If you put your hand along it the animal will not flinch, because flinching costs feed, but the rib cycle will change by a fraction of a millimeter for eleven or twelve breaths, and this is the entire available vocabulary of alarm, and it means something like *the temperature at my left side has become interesting.*

They have no eyes. They have no use for the cauls or the light or the enormous tilted bowl of the world. What they have instead is a field: a wide, low, unbounded sense of warmth and pressure and wetness, in which the neighbor eleven centimeters away is not an object but a condition, the way weather is a condition. Two hundred and forty thousand of them on a shelf, each one aware of perhaps nine others, none of them aware of a shelf.

An individual is an address. Shelf, tier, row, basin: 4/17/2210/0093. The animal at that address has been replaced six times. The address has never moved. When a reducer takes one away the number stays open for a turn and a half, and then a new adult is walked in on its own soft feet, and it settles into the exact pallor pattern of the thing before it, because the shadows have not moved either, and the pattern is not inherited, it is simply what happens to any hide left in that light for long enough.

---

Here is the thing about the sockets.

Every adult grows, on the crown of its shoulder, a cup. It is shallow, lipped, glazed, precisely dimensioned to the drip that falls from the trimmed stalactite of the gantry above. It grows the cup because a drip that lands flat makes a sound and noise costs feed. That is the reason. That is the whole reason, and it is sufficient, and it is stamped into the ceramic.

But the cup is grown in the shape of a cupped hand.

The adults were workers once. They were small and many-handed and they spent two thousand turns fitting grommets in the seams, and then a hormone came through the feed at the fixed rate and they stopped eating and went still, and the other workers moved around them without noticing, and they swelled, and their hands went soft and were taken back into the body over about nine days. The nerves are not taken back. The map remains in the cord, a hand that is nowhere, and when the adult grows the only thing it will ever grow — the one structure it makes rather than merely being — it grows it in the shape of the palm it no longer has, held up, slightly hollowed, waiting for water.

There is no memory in this world. There is no organ that keeps anything. But a hundred million animals are standing in warm water holding out their hands, and every ninety-one minutes the rain-circuit comes around again and fills them.

---

The workers are the only ones who travel, and they do not travel far.

A maintenance larva is the length of a forearm and has between nine and fourteen hands, and it knows a corridor. Not a route — a corridor, a specific two hundred meters of seam under a specific gantry, with its specific scale and its specific weep and its specific grommet that has always been slightly proud and always will be. It knows this corridor the way you know a face. It has trued this seam ten thousand times. It cannot remember doing so, but its hands have become exact for it, the way a key becomes exact for a lock, and when it fits a grommet into the proud place there is a small chemical completion in its body, a click of the interior, and this is the only satisfaction available anywhere in the Hold, and it is available in enormous quantities, and it is not recorded.

They are not unhappy. Unhappiness would require a comparison. They have never seen another corridor. When two workers meet at a boundary they touch hands, all of them, briefly, in a knot that lasts about four seconds, and exchange nothing, and go back. Nobody has ever established what this is for. It is not in the specification. It costs almost nothing, so it has been permitted.

At the end they stop eating. The others work around them. Their hands go first — softening, clouding, sinking into the swell of the flank like fingers into rising dough — and if you were to watch closely, which nobody does, you would see the hands go on making a small closing motion for two or three days after they are no longer hands.

---

Two castes live in an almost empty world.

The counters sit along the flume lips with their manipulators folded and their horny throats working, and they tally. Stock, drift, drip, larvae, flow. The tally never closes. It cannot: thirty thousand comma-sized bodies per cubic meter, replaced continuously, moving at the speed of a slow breath, and no count of them can be finished before it is wrong. So the counters live inside a permanent shortfall, an itch with a number in it. They have no word for the itch. They have no words. They have the click, which is the only consonant in the world, and they make it about four times a second forever, and it is not distress, and it is not language, and if you stand among them long enough you will begin to feel that something in your own chest is being enumerated and found slightly insufficient.

The reducers move through the basins on long low bodies with soft jaws, and they taste deviation. That is their entire sensorium. They cannot taste a healthy animal — a hide at spec is invisible to them, transparent, not there. What they perceive, in a world of a hundred million, is perhaps eleven or twelve bright points per turn: the hide half a millimeter too thin, the rib cycle four percent loud, the one that bred. Everything else is a blank plain they cross without sensation.

They are the only predators here and they have no appetite. They have tolerance bands. When they take the loud one there is no struggle, because struggling would be noise, and the loud one is already loud, and the reducer's jaw is soft, and the whole event lasts under a minute and is logged as a plumbing event and goes down the warm flume with everything else.

They are the loneliest things in the Hold, and loneliness is not a thing they can have, and they have it anyway, structurally, the way a socket has the shape of a hand.

---

The small trades:

Seam-lice live in a canyon two centimeters wide and never come out of it. They eat scale and lay mineral at exactly the rate the water takes it, and their world is a crack with a weather of iron-tasting condensate, and they have been in that crack, lineage after lineage, for as long as there has been a crack. They are the only makers of permanence here and what they make is the appearance of newness. At the fourth sump they maintain the edges of a stencil. They do not read. They know the letter **D** as a coastline: a long straight shore, a bay, a headland, a return. Generations have memorized that bay. It is the closest thing in this world to a tradition, and it is a tradition of keeping a word legible to no one.

Grazers crop the slick under the gantries with mouthparts like wet leather, and they are the only animals here with a direction of their own. Warmth requires a gradient and there are no gradients — but sugar is a gradient. The mats are thicker where the condensate runs and thinner at the margins, and a grazer can taste the difference across four centimeters, and it moves up the slope of sweetness, and it is the only creature in nine shelves that goes somewhere because it wants to. Four steps up from it everything is dissolved and returned. It does not know this. It knows bread and iodine and *more this way,* and it is, by a margin that cannot be measured, the happiest thing alive here.

---

The air has a population and the sky has a livestock.

The drift are comma-sized and sterile and do not hatch and do not feed. Mean residence forty minutes. They have one reflex, which is to cling to whatever they touch, and no behavior after that, and the specification lists them under consumables. When they land on your face it is the world's slowest snow, and it is also the only embrace available anywhere in the Hold, performed thirty thousand times per cubic meter by something too small to mean it.

The fog banks are herds, and a herd has a shepherd: a thin ribless thing, six meters, warm, charged, walking a fixed route with a province of saturated air held around it like a held breath. Drift enter and leave; the fog persists; the shepherd persists longer. Follow one and you will meet it again coming the other way with the same animals in it. It is the only bounded thing here that departs and returns. It is the only thing with a biography. It has no interior whatsoever, and if you were looking for a person in this place you would end up, reluctantly, following a cloud.

And the cauls — the ceiling, the daylight, the stretched translucent veined animals grown flat and lit from within by the mother-culture — the cauls are the only residents that touch the whole shelf at once. They have no eyes and no underside sense. What they have is a map of warmth: a hundred million breathing bodies below them registering as a slow continent of temperature, freckled here and there with cold.

A cold freckle is a death, or a failure, or a visitor. The caul's light dims by an amount too small to see across the freckle. The workers, who cannot see the map and do not know there is one, move toward dimness because their hands work better in even light. They arrive. They repack the gland, or they clear the flume, or they stand puzzled beside a warm empty basin with nothing wrong in it.

That is the only sentence anyone speaks here. The sky says *cold,* in brightness, to larvae who do not know they are being addressed, and the world repairs itself, and there is no foreman, and no one is above any of it.

---

Below the ninth shelf, in the jacketed sunk baffled dark where the presses run, there is a caste that has never been on a shelf and could not survive one.

They are grown without tympana. They sense through the bone. Their whole world is the held note — the load, the pressure, the floor's endless single tone — and they navigate by its interference patterns the way you navigate a room by its walls, and they are deaf in the way you are deaf to your own blood. If you brought one up to the fourth sump it would experience the quiet as a void, an absence of ground, a falling. It would try to hold on to something. There would be nothing there to hold.

They tend the wool-stock: animals that are mostly cavity, a labyrinth of drawn fiber grown in the body's hollow, harvested and re-grown, harvested and re-grown. The wool-stock eat nothing but feed-slurry and their entire function is to be full of a sound. They are the only hollow things in a world of solid wet bodies. Put your hand on one and you will feel the kiln through it, at load, holding, and the animal will not react, because the note is not an event to it. The note is what it is like to be alive.

They know there is something below them. Not as a fact. As a pressure that does not come from any direction. It is the only thing in the Hold that has an intuition, and the intuition is correct, and it is worth nothing.

---

Under the floor there is more floor.

The decommissioned basins are still warm and still wet and still occupied, because cooling them would cost more than keeping them. The stock down there have been reassigned to nothing in particular. They breathe. The heat goes nowhere useful. The pallor maps form anyway, because the shadows are still there. The sockets fill anyway, because the rain-circuit does not distinguish.

And no count is kept down there. There is nothing to be counted against. The reducers pass through and taste nothing, because deviation requires a specification and these animals have been released from theirs — not freed, released, the way a bolt is released into a bin.

So things drift. Not much. A hide half a millimeter thick with no one to skim it. A rib that opens two millimeters instead of one and has done so for four thousand turns. A socket cupped for a drip that was trimmed away and never came back, still waiting, still exactly the shape of a hand.

And in the margin of a flume on the fourth decommissioned tier, in eleven centimeters of standing water at thirty-nine degrees, there is a thing the size of a hand that has never been counted.

It is not a larva; it has too few hands and they are the wrong shape. It is not stock; it has a mouth, and a seam that is a seam and not a fold-line, and lungs it uses for something other than evaporation. It is warm. It is exactly as warm as everything. It is the descendants of one that bred, three or four generations of them, in a place where breeding is not detected because nothing down there is being asked any questions.

It has no function. It has no address. It has hands and lungs and a mouth and no caste, which means it is the whole animal, the undivided one, the thing the lineage was before it was split into fins and counters and lice and sky. It could become anything. There is nothing here for it to become and no gradient to climb except a very slight sweetness in the direction of the flume.

A reducer will pass within a meter of it next turn and taste nothing, because there is nothing there to be wrong.

---

And you.

You are the coldest thing on the shelf. Thirty-seven in a thirty-nine world: you are a small radiator running backward, a sink, a freckle. Above you a caul registers an area of dimness the size of a body. Somewhere under a gantry a larva's hands feel the light go uneven and it comes, without hurry, without curiosity, to true a seam that is not out of true.

The drift lands on your eyelashes and clings and is replaced. The swallow crosses you at the ninety-first minute and you feel it in the sternum and the sinuses, a sigh under hearing, and for eleven seconds you are part of a hundred million guts and then you are not. The water braids around your ankles and goes somewhere. You stop being a separate temperature. This takes about four minutes and it is not a metaphor; it is the loss of the last gradient you were carrying, and with it goes the only thing that was making you distinguishable from the floor.

A reducer comes. It moves along your leg with its soft jaw and finds no specification, and therefore no deviation, and therefore nothing at all, and it goes on. You and the hand-sized thing on the fourth decommissioned tier have the same condition. It is not safety. There is no such thing here as safety. It is only that the world has no instrument fine enough to notice a thing that has not been assigned.

The counters have already given you a number. It will not close. Long after you have gone up the ramp and out, a basin on the fourth shelf will stand empty and warm at thirty-nine degrees with the water exactly at the line, held at temperature because cooling it would cost more, and a horny throat will click your figure four times a second into a quiet that has no consonants, and the ledger will balance to the gram around a hole the shape of a visitor.

---

Because that is the last resident, and the largest, and the one nothing here can perceive.

The kiln is at load. The test is a test of holding. Everything above it — the fins, the breath, the sockets, the rain that walks a route, the lice keeping the letters crisp, the sky saying *cold* to hands that don't know they're listening — all of it is not the machine running. All of it is the machine *ready.* Two hundred and forty thousand basins on a shelf and nine shelves and more shelves under those, forever eleven days old, held at temperature, variance skimmed, ledger balanced, waiting for a use that has not been declared and may not be declared and is not anticipated by the specification.

What lives here is a standby. It has a hundred million bodies and no night and one sound and a font nobody reads. It has been ready for a very long time. It is extremely good at it.

And in the margin of a flume four floors down, uncounted, at exactly the temperature of your palm, something with the wrong number of hands goes on becoming, very slowly and without permission, a little more itself.
