## What Lives in Return Nine

Start with the census, because the census is the only thing down here that admits to being alive.

Three names on the slate, chalked in the same hand as the valve figures, each with its number: **0041 ROPE. 0117 ROSETTE. 0902 CRUST.** They are not filed under a heading for living things. There is no such heading. The board has three columns — IN, OUT, HELD — and everything in the hall is accounted for in grains of water, and the three species appear under HELD, because that is what they are: quantities of the city's breath that have declined to go back up.

This is the correct way to understand the inhabitants. Nothing here is alive in the way a garden is alive, competing, spreading, trying. Everything here is *retained*. The rope-fungus is eleven hundred feet of water that has agreed to be a rope. It runs the hot feed in the gap between lag and pipe, exactly where the temperature is right, and it does not explore; it has already found the only place it can be, and it fills that place at the speed of a millimeter a month, and at the joints it puts out flat fans, and the fans are cropped. Even. Level. Something grazes them.

The rosettes on the cold returns are water that has agreed to be flowers. They smell of cut mushroom and pennies. They bloom in the fog no wider than a finger that lives between the hot rack and the cold rack, and they are the hall's only ornament, and they are perfectly indifferent to being looked at, and if you put your face close enough to smell them you will breathe some of them in, and they will be entered in the ledger as having gone out.

The crust on the sight glass is water that has agreed to watch. It grows a millimeter a year toward the amber, cracked into plates like old paint, and it is the only thing in eleven thousand objects that has ever oriented itself toward a light. It has been looking at that hand's-width of trapped sunset for four hundred years. It has never seen anything else. It does not know there is anything else. It is, in the strictest sense, an eye that has only ever had one object, and it is very patient, and it is winning.

---

Then the uncounted, which are not on the board because the board only holds water and these hold nothing; they only pass it through.

The shrimp-thing in the west steam trap is blind and pink and about the length of an eyelash, and its whole world is a chamber that floods and empties every forty minutes. It lives in the pause. It breeds in the pause. Its tides come nine hundred times a month, and the great eleven-year Turn is, to it, an incomprehensible theological event that has never once occurred within a single lifetime, only in the accumulated rumor of the line. There have been perhaps two hundred thousand generations of it since the trap was hung. It has never been anywhere. It is not deprived. The trap sighs its long tired *hhhaa* and the shrimp rides the sigh out and clings and waits for the water to come back, and the water always comes back.

The moth is not a visitor. This is the first thing regulars get wrong. A small brown moth with a thumbnail's wingspan seems like something that got down the air, and one does, occasionally — but the one you see making its slow circuits of the hot rack, landing, testing, moving on, was born here. There are larvae in the mineral wool. They eat stitched canvas and gasket lint and the powdered rust that is the only dust available, and they spin their cocoons out of the hall's own lagging, and what comes out is an animal assembled entirely from manufactured things. It is the hall's native fauna. It is the hall's own dust, flying.

It navigates by heat. It has no other sense worth the name. Which means that every eleven years the moth's entire universe inverts, and it must relearn the room — a migration of forty feet conducted over three years, at the pace of a thing that has never been in a hurry. Watch it long enough and you will notice it always tests the same six or seven fittings, in the same order, because those are the warm ones, and because a route is the only memory a moth has.

And it has never once landed on the keeper.

The cricket is a visitor, and it dies here, and while it lives it is a catastrophe. Its stridulation carries four feet and stops dead in the wool, and for that four feet the hall is unbearably, indecently loud, a shriek in a library. Everything in the room stops when it happens. The rosettes do nothing, being flowers. But the grazing stops. The rounding stops. And if you are down there and very still, you will hear, over the top of the cricket, one sharp tap on the copper — the pattern that the slate table lists, worn shallow by fingernails, as **AGAIN**.

---

Now the hand.

The wicks are trimmed. The chalk smears. There is a handprint in the drip-dust on the north catwalk newer than the last Turn. Visitors read these as *recent* and go up the ladder feeling watched, and they are wrong about the reason, which is worse.

Chalk in a room with no wind and no hands stays smearable forever. Freshness here is not evidence of a moment; it is evidence of an absence of disturbance. And the wicks are trimmed once a decade — the text of the hall says so plainly, and nobody reads it as a schedule. Once a decade. Not because the lamps need it that often. Because that is how long it takes to come around to them.

The keeper's round is eleven years long.

It is one circuit of a room a hundred and forty paces around. It proceeds at the pace of the tide, because it is the tide's creature, and it moves the way the dripstones grow: a hand's breadth, a year, a fraction of an inch of accretion, and then the same again. It navigates by drip, which is a slow sense; you must stand under a leak long enough to be sure it is the one you think it is. It crosses the buckle in the third catwalk with the overstep that four generations of men have used, and it uses that overstep because the overstep is what the room teaches, and the room teaches with the patience of a billion drops on steel.

Once a Turn it passes the north catwalk and steadies itself with a hand in the drip-dust. There is one print there. Under it there are others, in strata, going down: forty or fifty of them, all the same size, all the same spread of fingers, all left hands. Under those, in the older dust, on the older floor, the prints are smaller. Different hands. Someone's daughter, once. Someone with a bad thumb.

This is what lives in Return Nine, and it is not a ghost and it is not a man. It is the thing the hall grows around a person.

Every dripstone here has a washer at its heart, or a cotter pin, or a nut — some small made thing the mineral chose to grow around, because the water needed a nucleus and the hall was full of them. The hall does the same thing to whoever comes down and stays. It lays down habit over them, layer on layer, at a millimeter a year: the overstep, the callus from the rail, the deafness to the Engine that never comes back, the throat that stops bothering because the room declines the voice anyway. Two Turns and the person is invisible inside the practice. Three and the practice is what walks the round, and the person is a nut at the center of it, and the practice does not need to consult them, and the columns balance.

They went up once. There is a record of it, tapped into the north feed and answered from the south, in the old men's table: a keeper climbed to the second landing and stood in the thin cool air and heard their own footsteps go off across the world and come back off a wall, and could not bear it, and came down. That is why nobody is ever met on the stairs. The stairs are the loud place. Nobody who lives here goes there.

The gap in the bulkhead is polished on the inside face, low down, about the height of a hand held palm-down by something taking its time.

---

And the ninth column.

Eight columns on the slate balance among themselves: the returns, the feeds, the coil, the trays, the sump, the fog, the held stock, the loss. There is a ninth. It has a heading worn to a smear and a small chalk drawing beside it, the only picture on the board, and the picture is a cup.

The ninth column is never zero. It is not small, either. It is about the size of a person: what a body drinks in a day from a copper tap that has never once been turned off, plus what a body breathes out into a room built to catch breath. That is the entry. That is the whole receipt. *Nobody is sure who receives the water* — the ninth column receives it, forty liters at a time, and holds it, and walks it slowly around a hundred and forty paces for eleven years, and gives most of it back.

The tap by the slate has a brass tag like every other object here. Every other tag has four digits. That one has one.

The keeper grazes the fungus fans level. It tastes the pans, which is how it knows what year it is up there — pollen, ash, salt, sugar, the thread of dyed wool, the sequin it could not digest and did not swallow. It trims three wicks a decade. It writes a column of figures with a hand that has converged, over four centuries and fifty nuclei, on one hand. And when the cricket sings it stops, entirely, for as long as the singing lasts, and then it taps **AGAIN**, because that is the only word in the table for what it wants.

---

There is one more thing to know, and it is a courtesy, and it is also a warning.

Two sharp rings on the metal, in the pattern the slate lists near the top: that is **WELL?** It is a question. It is the hall asking, at intervals, at whatever fitting happens to be growing a millimeter and telling you about it, whether the thing it has grown around is still in there.

The answer is one ring. **WELL.**

The answer comes. It comes from wherever the round has reached — the far side of the east rack, the sump corner, the underside of the second catwalk — and it travels a mile of copper without losing a thing, and it arrives clean and bright, and it takes on average five and a half years.

So don't ask unless you can wait. And if you do ask, and you go up, and you live your loud empty echoing life where sound goes wherever it likes, and you come back down one decade later with your sleeves pulled over your palms — one ring will be waiting for you in the metal, somewhere near the south wall, still going, patient as the bowl.

You drank from the tap. You sweated into the pans. You are already in the ninth column, and it balances, and the hall has begun, very slowly, to grow around you.
