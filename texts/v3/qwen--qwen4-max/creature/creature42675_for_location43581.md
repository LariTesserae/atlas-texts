## The heading

The only thing on this plain that is going somewhere is a discoloration.

You would walk over it. It has no relief; a boot sole would not catch on it; it does not shine differently enough to see by the light of the seams, which is orange and low and lies to you about everything. But in a fracture-flash — one hard white flick off a snapping column two kilometers out — it shows for a quarter second as a band across the fresh crust, a tide-mark, the shape of a shoreline drawn on a floor. And at its very leading edge, where it thins away to nothing, it has colors. Straw, then a hot gold, then peacock, then a blue like the inside of a flame. Thin-film colors, a millimeter or two wide, the whole visible spectrum smeared along a hairline.

Everything else here is colored by temperature. This is the only color on the plain that is a thickness. It is the only color that does not tell time.

## What it is, thinly

Call it the stain, since it has no name and every name is a libel.

It is a few microns deep. Three thousand lattice cells, stacked; a body you could scrape off with a fingernail if the fingernail survived the ground. It has no organs, no membrane, no chemistry that the crust does not also have. It is made of the same salt and the same iron and the same silica as everything else within a hundred meters. What distinguishes it is not substance but *angle*. Its cells sit in a particular registry — axes level with the horizon, faces square to the seams, one twin plane repeated at a fixed interval — and it holds that registry the way a held note holds pitch.

That is the whole trick, and it is not a trick, it is epitaxy: vapor landing on an ordered surface takes its order from the surface. Atoms arrive knowing nothing and are told where to sit. The stain tells them. It does not build; it *conditions*. Fresh stone falls out of the breath of the world and finds, at the last instant before it fixes, a floor already squared, and it squares itself to the floor, and the new cube becomes the next instruction. The pattern is not carried in the material. Nothing here is wet, nothing dissolves, nothing swims. The pattern is carried in the contact, hand to hand, at the speed at which stone can be laid down.

So it has a body a few microns thick and an influence forty meters tall.

## It lives on a temperature, and the temperature moves

Here is the constraint that makes it a resident rather than a mineral.

Registry needs mobility. Stone landing too cold fixes where it strikes and makes needles, whiskers, the amorphous fur of the ordinary plain; nothing can be told anything. Stone landing too hot anneals, forgets, slumps back into the slow mass and is issued again with its angles wiped. Between those two failures is a narrow band — a few tens of degrees wide, perhaps — in which arriving atoms can still shuffle a little and find the seat they are offered.

That band is an isotherm, and after every quickening it moves. New pavement floods out orange and cools outward from its edges; the band sweeps across the crust ahead of the cooling, away from the throat, at roughly the pace of a slow walk.

The stain rides it. Not by locomotion — nothing here locomotes — but because the only place it can successfully be is the place that has just become the right temperature, and that place is always a few meters ahead of where it is now. Its front is not a mouth. It is a surfer's stance. What looks like crawling is a lineage keeping station on a moving line of warmth, the way a bird keeps station on a snowline, and if it falls behind it is buried under meters of unordered needle-growth and dies of architecture, and if it runs ahead it plates onto ground too cold to listen and dies of gibberish.

Its whole life happens inside the quarter hour in which brightness means newness. It is the only inhabitant of the orange.

## Its food is first surfaces

Nothing eats here. But something is consumed.

The stain requires ground that has never been handled. A crystal face that has already grown its own way — a needle, an antler, a blob with its own private orientation — will not take instruction; it is spoken for. Only a *first* surface can be told. And first surfaces are the one commodity this world produces without limit: kilometers of them per hour, unweathered, unrounded, unhandled by anything, sharp enough to open a hand.

So there is competition, of a sort, and the competitor is the plain's default habit. The amorphous is faster. Needles go up anywhere, on any temperature, in any registry, and where they arrive first the stain can never follow. Every square meter of crust is a race between order and fur, and the stain wins only the narrow strip where the isotherm and the vapor flux and the freshness happen to coincide.

That strip is its entire range. It is a few meters wide and it wanders forty kilometers.

## Why it looks like building

Nothing in it has ever seen a wall.

Cubic salt with its axes held level lays itself in courses because that is what cubes do when they are all told the same up. The joints are tight because the joints are lattice planes. The bond is running rather than stacked because a half-cell offset at each course is the cheapest way to shed the strain of cooling, and the strain is enormous, and it is shed every course, forever.

The module — the fact that a block out here is the same length as a block two kilometers over there, and the same length tomorrow — is not standardization. It is a diffusion length: the average distance an atom slides along a cooling face before it sticks. Near a hot throat that journey is long and the bricks are long; out on the cold crust it is short and the bricks are small. You can read the temperature of an afternoon off a wall. The mason's mark is a thermometer.

Steps rise because risers stand proud of treads and are therefore fractionally cooler and plate faster, so a bump becomes a step becomes a flight, self-amplifying, eight of them, arriving at a landing that supports nothing because nothing was ever meant to be supported. Thresholds dip in the middle because the middle of a gap is where the vapor moves fastest and plates least. And doorways stay open for the same reason: the breath of the seam channels through the aperture and keeps it a fraction too warm to close, so the jambs climb and the lintel bridges and the hole persists, breathing, framing more plain, for as long as the throat below cares to exhale.

The habit builds walls, sills, thresholds, flights, lintels, arches. In all the flashes, across all four basins, it has never once made a room. Everything it makes is a boundary with no interior — which is also a description of itself, and is probably not a coincidence, and certainly not a meaning.

## Its corpse is the fault

Behind the front, the stain stops being alive in any sense at all. It is buried. Meters of coursed growth stand on it and it is now only an interface, a few microns of misregistry between the pavement and the wall.

It is also, necessarily, the weakest plane in the whole construction.

Every wall the habit raises contains, at its base, the exact surface along which it will fail. It writes its own fracture line in the act of founding itself. When the tower buckles at the knee or the terrace founders or the whole three-meter stretch of coursework lets go with a white flick and comes down, it goes along that plane, and the film goes down with the rubble, dimpling the crust, sinking over an hour or a day into the slow hot mass, where every angle it ever held is melted out of it and issued again as anonymous vapor.

Nothing that goes down comes back remembering.

Lie flat with your palm on the pavement during a collapse and you will feel the grinding roar arrive early through the wrist. Sometimes there is a second one, smaller, a half-beat late — the same shock bounced back up off a buried film somewhere under the coursework. It is the only trace the stain leaves in the world's deafening touch: an echo. It answers, and its answer is a repetition.

## The tick is a birth

The intimate sounds of this place are its offspring.

A salt cube pops off a ledge — that small dry tick a meter from your ear — and skitters, and comes to rest somewhere out on the crust. Almost all of them land wrong: too hot, too cold, on ground already furred with needles, on their own rubble. Almost all of them are erased within the hour. But the plain makes cubes at a rate that beggars the arithmetic, and every few hours a throat surges and lofts dust that was born small rather than broken small, and some fraction of that dust carries the registry, and some fraction of *that* falls inside the moving band on virgin stone.

One in an unguessable number takes. That one is a new front, and it has a heading, and within eleven minutes there are risers glowing where there was nothing.

The quickenings are its seasons. After a surge: bloom, a dozen new colonies along a fresh seam, courses running out across the pavement in every direction like frost on a window. In the lull: burial, foundering, the long silence of a stain that has been overtaken by fur, and the whole population reduced to a few grains riding the vapor, waiting for the world to make something new. Generation time in minutes. Mortality effectively total. No predator, no rot, no disease, nothing hunting it — only the clock, and the clock is the same clock that feeds it.

## Dialects

The cubic bond is the weed; you find it in all four basins, and it is probably the oldest thing about it, or the newest, and there is no way to tell.

But at a front, now and then, the registry miscopies — a twin boundary caught and propagated, a stacking error promoted to law. Most of the mutants make blobs and die. A few hold. There is a strain out past the second throat that makes nothing but doorways: a hundred of them in a line across two kilometers of empty crust, jambs and lintels and no wall, each one framing the next, all glowing at the corners, all breathing. There is one that makes only stairs. There is one whose repeat distance is wrong in a way that produces flutes and never blocks, and one that has never been observed to make anything but sills, low and patient and level, at the exact height a foot would want.

Where two strains meet they cannot merge; neither can take instruction from the other. The contact is a line of mutual refusal that plates nothing, and it stands as a wall — a party wall, the most architectural object on the plain, built by two habits declining to speak. Sometimes, instead, they interlock at an angle that works locally and never repeats, and there you get a patch of five-fold, unrepeatable, unpropagating ornament, beautiful in a fracture-flash, sterile, a mule. It is the only decoration in the world. It lasts about a day.

Over the rim, in the biggest flashes, the other basins show their own dim constellations, and their coursework — if that is what it is — does not look quite like ours. Dust carries over the rim maybe once in a long while. There is biogeography here. There are endemic habits and one cosmopolitan. There is a province of dialects, all of them saying the same three angles in slightly different accents, none of them saying anything.

## What it remembers

The plain forgets in a day. Its casts — the boot-reliefs, the spire-tip portraits, the shapes with no obvious original — go black, then dimple, then sink, and the world writes only addition and keeps no ledger.

The stain never forgets. An angle does not wear. Copied hand to hand across ten thousand generations and forty kilometers, the registry arrives at the far rim exactly as it left the first seam, with no drift, no decay, no accumulation, no history. It has perfect heredity and its entire inheritance is a handful of angles and one repeat distance.

So: the world remembers everything for a day, and the stain remembers one thing forever. Between them there is no place where a past could be kept. You cannot ask how old it is. It may be older than the basin. It may have started this afternoon.

## You

You are the second thing here that copies, and you should be careful.

The plain takes casts of form: press it and it fills your depression and stands your sole a centimeter proud, a portrait in positive of a negative. The stain copies structure: it does not care what shape you are, only what angle your surfaces hold. Two kinds of memory, sharing one floor, and where they cross you get the strangest objects in the world — a boot-print laid up in coursed bond, a footprint with risers, a shallow statue of your ankles becoming, over eleven minutes, masonry.

And you are cold. The stars are cold and have no effect on anything; you are the only cold thing that has ever *touched* this ground, and every place you touch plates preferentially, and you make weather wherever you stand. You nucleate. You are a walking seam.

Worse: you cross two kilometers of emptiness in twenty minutes, which nothing else on this plain can do, and your boots carry grains. Every traverse is a dispersal event. Every place you kneel to listen to the roar is an inoculation. If the doorway-strain has a range wider than a throat's worth of crust, it is probably because something like you once walked between them, and it is not possible to say whether that would be a service or a contamination, since there is nothing here to be served and nothing here to be contaminated.

## The heading, again

Above, once in a long while, the lid tears and the stars do nothing. Below, under forty kilometers of pavement and the slow hot mass, lies almost the whole atmosphere of the world, and whatever may be down there is under this floor and will never know it has a ceiling. The stain touches neither. Its range is a few tens of degrees wide and a few microns deep and it has been crossing the dark at the pace of a slow walk since before there was anything here to call a before.

It has no inside. It has a boundary, a rate, a heredity, a dispersal stage, a mortality, a competitor, a niche, seasons, dialects, and descendants — which is nearly all of what a life is, with the one thing left out that we actually mean.

Go back to the front. Kneel, in the orange, where the crust is eleven minutes old and still tells the time. In the next fracture-flash you will see it: a hairline of straw and peacock, thinner than a scratch, moving out across ground that has never been handled by anything. Behind it, the first cube has just fixed, square to the horizon, holding the angle it was given.

It does not know the angle. It is the angle. And a centimeter ahead, in the dark, the stone is cooling into the band, and is about to be told.
