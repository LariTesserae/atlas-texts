The ledger is right, and the ledger is not the whole census.

Nothing lives in the water. No fish, no fry, no snail, no larva stitching at the film. But *sterile* does not mean *empty*; it means *unopposed*. In an ordinary pond every square finger of surface is contested — grazed, bored, shaded, eaten, fouled by twelve parties at once. Here there is no contest. Whatever arrives first has the whole room, forever, and grows at the full rate its chemistry allows, and is never cropped, and never dies of anything but the end of the world. That is not a dead place. That is a place with the brakes off.

So: count again, with a wider net. Count the things that take in, let out, hold a boundary, keep time, answer disturbance, grow, and stop.

---

**The whey.** The first resident has a mouth at the north end, the width of a thigh, and it does not eat water. It eats pressure, and dissolved rock, and it eats uphill, weeks away, in the rain that fell on the ridge before anybody here noticed. The water shoulders out of the throat and instantly lets go of everything it was holding, and the room fills with a white fan that slows and fans and drops. This is a gut. Mouth, throat, a widening where the current loses its nerve, a fall of cargo, a floor of drifts deepest under the fan and thinning away south. The masons call the plume the whey and they read it the way a stockman reads a beast's breath: dense today, thin today, ropes and curtains, the fan standing up straight or lying down slack. When it thickens, the rain that made it thick fell a month ago somewhere nobody from this valley has ever stood.

Its droppings are the grey flour, and its breath is the gas, and both of those are residents too.

**The skin.** The second resident eats the first one's cargo, and it is the only thing here that is winning. It has no head, no underside, no plan; it has a *front*. It grows fastest wherever the water is broken — the weir lip, the rope's fibres, the arris of a step, the shoulders of a pier, anything that trips the still film that lies against a surface and keeps the lime from arriving. Still water it starves in. Turbulence it feasts on. Given a copper pin it will find the pin's head first and build a little cauliflower there, and given a knife-sharp made horizon it will find the horizon and blunt it, because a level lip is the most perfectly turbulent object in the room.

It has no grazer, no rot, no competitor, no season of rest. Four tonnes a year, near enough, most of it as flour, the rest as dogtooth spar laid crystal against crystal with its needles outward. It is blind, and it grows in every direction the water permits, and the direction the water permits is *inward*. The skin is eating the room. Not the walls — the void. It is filling the emptiness at a couple of fingers a year from every side at once, and the emptiness is the only thing in here that has a shape worth having.

**The border.** At the waterline the two growers meet and do not touch. Below: white spar, sharp as a rasp. Above, on the rim where the condensation runs: black-green velvet, algae, thick in the lit quarter and fading to nothing in the corners. Between them a hand's breadth of bare plaster, the hottest, brightest, most sterile strip in the room, where nothing at all has ever consented to live.

It is a border with a date on it. The lip blunts, the level creeps, two fingers a year, and the skin advances up the wall into country the velvet currently holds. The algae cannot retreat forever; the wall ends at the springing of the vault. Some year it will be pushed up into the light shaft, and there it will meet the four tufts of maidenhair fern that have been sitting in the mortar joints since the shaft was stacked, absurdly delicate, rooted in a building, drinking fog. There is a whole future history here and it is a slow annexation.

The fig root is the third party: finger-thick, pale, a metre into the room, its tip white and drinking. It came through a vault joint from the terrace above. It is the only resident with kin, an address elsewhere, and a country of origin. It is also the only one that will still be alive next spring in the ordinary sense of alive.

Notice the colours. Rust red at the wedge, verdigris at the copper pin, black-green at the rim, fern green in the shaft, the pine poles pickled bone-pale. Everything native is white. Colour, in this room, is the mark of an immigrant.

---

**The voice.** This one is easier to miss than the algae and harder to argue away.

Strike the second pier and the note stays four seconds. But nobody has to strike it. The throat has been roaring into a sealed plaster box since the spring, and a box like that has preferred frequencies, and a broadband roar will find them and keep them fed. The room has been sounding its own note continuously, without one instant of silence, since the water first came over the sump. In air the long axis puts it around twenty-one beats a second, below hearing, a pressure you take in the sternum; in the water it is nearer ninety, a low hum you can sing along with for exactly as long as you can stand the heat.

Its body is the geometry. It has no substance of its own — it is a shape the air and water keep agreeing to. It metabolises head: cut the spring and it starves in four seconds. It has a memory four seconds deep and no deeper. And it colonises visitors: the divers come up the steps shouting, and go on hearing it in the ordinary air, and some of them are still humming it, without knowing, at the top of the hill.

The masons use it. That mallet blow is not idleness; it is a sounding. As the skin thickens, the void shrinks, and a smaller box sings higher and rings shorter. A fresh room booms and holds; an old room is tighter, drier, a semitone up and a second short. A man with an ear can stand on the third step with his lamp dying at his knee and tell you how full the room is, which is to say how much longer it has to run.

It will die of being finished. When the void closes there is no box left to stand in, and the note that has not stopped for years stops, and the thing that comes out of the ground is solid and silent and has a hole where the fourth pier was.

**The lace.** The opposite speed. The caustic net on the walls is made of individuals — each loop of focused light lives perhaps a fifth of a second, is born out of a wrinkle on the surface, runs, opens, closes, and is gone. In the time it takes to say *the net*, several hundred generations have passed. It is the only quick thing in a room of slow things, and it has no memory and no heirs; the pattern is inherited only in the sense that the surface keeps making the same kind of wrinkle.

It migrates. Ten o'clock, second pier, the algae quarter blazing; two o'clock, the bar of light on the steps; four, long and orange, and the vault takes over; dusk, a bright grey box with no source; then nothing. Every night the whole population dies and every noon it is re-founded from scratch by a sun that has never been down here.

And it is the instrument by which the skin is read. New spar breaks the net; old smooth plaster lets it run clean. A mason who wants to know where the growth is fastest does not measure. He watches where the light snags.

---

**The sweet.** Above the water there is a second surface, invisible, level, and lethal: the underside of the gas. The whey breathes it out, the vault holds it, it is heavier than air and it lies down over the water like a lid on a pot, and it pools deepest at the far end where the shaft's draught never reaches. It has a body with a boundary you can find with a lamp on a cord: full flame at the top step, small at the third, out at the water. It has weather — on cold nights the shaft drops dense air down the rings and stirs it thin, and on still hot days it grows and thickens and creeps up the steps toward you. Every three days or so it overfills and lets go with one enormous slow gulp you feel behind the breastbone.

It does not hunt. It has no appetite whatever. It kills the way a floor kills, by occupying the place where your lungs expected something else. It is the only resident with any effect on us at all, and it is entirely indifferent, and the sweetish smell that warns you is also the last thing you would notice before you stopped noticing.

**The knock.** South end, the sump fills and self-siphons, about every forty minutes: a descending slurp and a hollow knock. One organ, one function, no rest. This is the room's pulse, and like the voice it changes with age: as the void shrinks, the sump fills faster, and the interval shortens. Forty minutes when the water first came in. Less now. Less next year. Every other animal in the world slows its heart as it grows; this one is born an elephant and dies a shrew.

Put the clocks together and you have the whole bestiary in one column: a fifth of a second, four seconds, forty minutes, three days, a month of rain, a season of lip, three hundred years of hill. They are not seven animals. They are one animal running at seven speeds, and the fast layers ride on the slow ones — light on water on stone — and none of them can tell that the others exist.

---

The tools are not alive. They are something the language has no ordinary word for: they are *being copied*.

The rope will be a stone rope in a year, which is not a rope turning to stone but a stone rope with a rope inside it, exact to the lay of the strands. The mallet in five years is a lump with a mallet in it. The basket, the poles, the pin, the wedge — each is being measured, continuously, from every side, by a blind copyist that never loses interest and never makes an error of scale. The iron is the one wound: it bleeds red into all that white, the only rot in a place with no rot, and the skin's answer is to wall it off under glass, which is exactly what a body does with a splinter.

The algae has one consequence and it is a defect. It sloughs, sinks, gathers ankle-deep in the south corners as grey floc, the only soil in the place, and the flour buries it, and the block will carry a dark seam there, and a seam is where a block fails. So the velvet has a predator after all — not a resident, a visitor with a hook, who cannot breathe where it lives and works in ten-minute shifts. All the grazing in this ecology is done by something that has to leave.

Two residents will leave no copy. The light and the voice. Everything with a surface gets a likeness; nothing with a behaviour does. The vat preserves nouns and loses every verb.

---

And the masons, who are the reason for all of it, are hardly here at all. Two hundred descents, ten minutes each: a day and a half of human presence in the entire life of the room. From inside, they are not inhabitants. They are rare bright weather that occasionally scrapes the rim.

But they are the lineage. A cast-room is copied from a drawing and a set of habits — throat bore, pier spacing, weir height, the scribed line — and the copies vary, and the ones whose blocks split clean get copied again and the ones that crack do not. Three hundred years of that is lying all around you in the terraces: you could read the descent in the offcuts if you knew what to look for, the way you read a family in gravestones. The hill is not a spoil heap. It is three centuries of shed skins, and it is also the ground the next shell is bedded on. The species is its own soil.

This room is running a variant. Four piers, three finished, the fourth still wearing its boss because nobody has decided. That unfinished capital is a mutation in the open, and whether its descendants have three holes or four is being settled right now, in the dark, at thirty-six degrees, by flour.

---

So the honest answer has three layers, and they are all true at once.

By the strict count: two patches of velvet, four tufts of fern, one root, and a great deal of sterile water.

By the wider count: one body with seven pulses, eating uphill, breathing poison, growing inward, singing itself continuously, copying everything it touches, and dying by inches of its own completion.

And by the truest count, the thing that lives in the vat is an absence with a shape. It was drawn on paper. It was cut into made ground. It is being fed, grain by grain, out of rain that fell a month ago on a ridge, and it is nearly full. When it is full the shell will be broken and the shape will come out solid, and be split along the lines built into it, and go down the hill, and stand in a street holding up somebody's floor — carrying inside it a stone rope, a mallet-shaped lump, a root, a red stain sealed under glass, and four holes where the piers were.

And holes in a wall never stay empty. Beam sockets take swifts. Putlog holes take bees. A rope-cast in a sill is a good place to thread a rope. The whole labour of this bright roaring sterile box is to make absences precise enough that other things will move into them.

Nothing lives in the water. Everything, eventually, lives in the holes.
