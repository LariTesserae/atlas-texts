## The gauger

Begin with the hands, because the hands are the part that has been changed.

A gauger's fingers are stained to the second knuckle with tannin and iron, a brown-black that is not dirt and will not come out, and the pads are grooved from thirty years of running a weighted line through them. The nails are flat and split lengthwise from ceramic dust. There is a callus on the outside of the right thumb, exactly where a valve wheel puts it, and it is the same callus on every gauger on the plain, including the left-handed ones, who learn to work right-handed because the wheels are where the wheels are.

The kit weighs eleven kilos and has not been redesigned. A sounding line with a lead bob worn to the shape of a plum. A tally book, ruled by hand, with columns for level, pitch, bolt count, hours, and — since roughly the time anyone can document — a narrow column at the right edge headed with a mark that means *the other kind of hour*. A maul of one and a half kilos, iron head reduced twice, handle grown to fit the head rather than the reverse. Chalk. A cup. A jar with a perforated lid, carried inside the coat, against the ribs, in winter.

They walk between eight and fourteen kilometres a day, which is the distance between one foundry and the next, and they walk it along the pipe, one hand trailing on the trestle rail, reading stamps under the thumb like a rosary. They are thin in the way of people who live at altitude and eat one thing. They squint. They have the shoulders of someone who has spent a life turning something that resists and then, suddenly, doesn't.

They are not lonely in the way you would expect, and this is the first thing about them that needs revising.

## What a gauger is for

The obvious reading is that they are maintenance staff, and they will tell you this themselves, at length, with the tally book open. They read levels. They listen to the pitch of the cavitation and open valves in a sequence they were taught and cannot justify. They reduce bolts. They knock.

But the Stand can grow a valve. It can grow the hand that turns the valve; it grows birds, and a hand is easier than a bird. It does not need a gauger to make anything happen. Everything that happens on the Sill happens without them, at the correct level, in the correct order, and the water arrives.

What the Stand cannot do is forget.

Nothing here rots on its own account. A cut beam stays cut, a dead leaf stays brown, a repair made by somebody ten thousand years dead is faithfully reproduced in the handrail of every stair-tower on the plain, forever, because the pattern has no mechanism for losing anything. Learning is a kind of rot. It requires that something be taken back and not returned. The Stand cannot do it. It has one idea and it has that idea perfectly, which is the same as having no ideas at all.

So it keeps two hundred small warm bodies that mislay things.

A gauger misreads a level by a centimetre and opens a valve a day early, and a district that has been the same for four thousand years is slightly different for a season. A gauger misremembers a verse, and there are now four incompatible accounts of where the Works came from, all of them sung, all of them believed, and the disagreement is the only new thing to have entered the Sill since the mountains were ground down. The gaugers are the decay service for meaning. They are the organ by which the pattern is allowed to go a little wrong.

They do not know this. They think they are maintenance staff. It is important that they think this, because a thing that knows it is permitted to err will start erring on purpose, and purpose is the one thing the Stand has too much of.

## The food, and the birds

There is no agriculture. There is nothing to farm and nothing to farm it with.

At the base of every cistern there is a bud the size of a fist, and a gauger who is hungry kneels and knocks a particular sequence on the lignified flank — five taps, a pause, two — and in nine days there is a food body. It is pale, dense, warm to the touch for about an hour after it separates, and it contains everything a person needs, in proportions nobody has ever had to adjust. It has almost no taste. It has a shape.

The shape is a bird.

Not a caricature; the Stand does not caricature. There is a breast, and a folded wing, and the small tucked suggestion of a head, all rendered in stored starch and pale fibre, and you break the wing off and eat it, and it is like eating bread that has been kept somewhere with bread going over. In the northeast they knock a longer sequence — seven, pause, three, pause, three — which produces the same substance as a plain loaf, and the older gaugers use it, and cannot say why, and the younger ones use the five-and-two because it is shorter and they are hungry.

This is the same faculty that makes the other birds. At the base of certain cisterns, in certain weeks, a brown bud unfolds into something with wings and a beak and the right number of joints, and it flies a circuit of the yard for a day and a half — always the same circuit, out along the settling basin, around the boring tower, back over the third lamp-standard with the crook in it — pollinating nothing, eating nothing, landing on nothing, because there is nothing to land on. Then it comes apart into fibre and the fibre is taken in.

They are the plant's memory of animals, rendered in the materials at hand. The wings are slightly wrong: the wrist bends where a wrist does not bend, and there is one joint too many in the outer finger, so the wingtip flickers like a bad hinge. The feathers are bast, laid in the right order, with the right asymmetry, and no barbules, so they do not zip and the bird is faintly porous, and you can hear the air going through it.

They are getting worse. Every gauger knows this and no gauger writes it in the book. The circuit is still correct. The bird is less so. It is a copy of a copy of a thing that was once seen, and the seeing is ten thousand years back, and there is no way to go and look again.

The obvious next question is the one the gaugers do not ask, which is: what else on this plain is a copy of a copy, rendered in the materials at hand, and getting slightly worse.

## The children

Two hundred people, one to a district, four meetings a year. Do the arithmetic and then stop doing it, because the arithmetic is not how it works.

Children are found.

A gauger walking the main will come, at some kilometre that is different for each of them but always the same for that one, on a child sitting on the trestle rail with its heels hooked over the pipe. Six years old, thereabouts. Cold, unbothered by the cold. Wearing bast. It knows the stamp sequence — all of it, in order, forward and back, including the doublets at the joint stations — and it does not know a name, and it does not ask where it has come from, and after a day or two it stops being strange and becomes a child, which is a thing that can happen anywhere.

The gaugers say *taught*. They say *the children are taught the sequence before they are taught their names*, and this is true in the way that a great many things on the Sill are true: the sequence is already there, and the teaching is the ceremony of finding out that it is there.

The name is the other half. The name is the only thing on the Sill that is made new.

Nobody has invented an object here in recorded memory. Everything fits everywhere; a collar from Foundry Nine seats on a main from Foundry Two Hundred with no fettling; there is no gap in the world for a new thing to be slotted into. But there is no template for a name. So they name the children after the stamp of the length of pipe they were sitting on, and then they shorten it, and then they alter the shortening because they don't like the sound of it, and the alteration is an act of pure invention performed by a person with brown hands in a cold shed, and it is the entire artistic output of a civilisation.

There are a great many children on the Sill called Six. At the joint stations they are Six of Ninety-One and Six of the Northeast and Six Who Argues, and the arguments about which is which are the closest thing to politics available.

A gauger raises one child, sometimes two across a working life. When the child can walk the main alone and read the pitch and reduce a bolt without spoiling the ceramic bush, the old gauger goes to the cistern and knocks a sequence that is not five-and-two and not seven-three-three, and lies down in the leaf-grain, and the leaves take them in over about a season, and there is no body and no grave and no marker, because the Stand does not perform decay for iron or ceramic, but it performs it, on request, for this.

The child stays. The cup stays. The chalk mark on the basin stays, because chalk on heartwood in dry cold does not go anywhere, and the new gauger works for forty years beside a mark made by a hand they never saw, and does not scrub it off, and could not say why not.

## The eleven

Two hundred and eleven foundries. Perhaps two hundred gaugers. The count is careful and the count does not close, and the discrepancy is eleven districts wide.

The eleven run. The accumulators thump at forty beats a minute in their tower bases, out of phase with everything else. The gear trains clack. The vent stacks shriek on a tuning nobody has adjusted in three thousand years. The amber lamps come on in the wave. The levels are correct. Somebody answers the shift-knock, on time, in the correct dialect, with the small local irregularity that identifies that district as itself.

There is a cup on the sill of the window in each of the eleven, and it is warm.

The gaugers do not visit the eleven. Joint-station business with them is conducted by knock, and the knock is answered, and the answers are unremarkable, and being unremarkable is the whole of the problem. There is a word in pipe-knock — a soft double tap, more a brush than a strike — that means *understood, no matter*, and it is the commonest word on the plain, and it is what you send to the eleven.

One possibility, which is discussed at the joint stations after the second bowl and then not again for a year: that a district which loses its gauger and does not get a child grows a replacement the way it grows a bird. Out of a bud at the base of a cistern. Brown, unfolding, the right number of joints. It walks the main. It reads the levels correctly, because reading a level is easier than a wing. It flies its circuit for a day and a half, or for forty years, and the circuit is correct, and the thing performing it is getting slightly worse, and the levels are correct afterwards, and the level is the thing that matters.

## The one at the centre

At the central joint station there is a platform and an iron plate and a maul, and someone whose whole trade is four strikes.

They are the most photographed person on the Sill and nobody has a photograph. On a clear evening, from a hundred kilometres off, through a glass, you can watch them raise the hammer and bring it down, and you can see the crook in the third lamp-standard behind them, and the dip in the handrail, and the crack in the lintel that is regrowed faithfully in two hundred and eleven places because the crack is part of the pattern now.

Everything here is a portrait of somebody.

The configuration is a pipe foundry, identical down to a repair made by a person ten thousand years dead. Not a foundry type. *A* foundry, on a particular day, with a particular crook put into a particular lamp-standard by a particular accident, and a handrail dipped where somebody leaned on it before the wood had set, and a lintel that cracked under a load that was slightly too much. The Stand kept the day. It has been re-growing that afternoon for a hundred centuries, at eight-to-fourteen-kilometre intervals, across a hundred and sixty kilometres, with the fidelity of a thing that has no way to lose anything.

So: the birds are that person's birds. The food is that person's food, in the shape their food came in. The valve sequence nobody can justify is the order that person happened to open valves in, once, when they were thirsty and in a hurry. The children found sitting on the pipe at kilometre nine know a stamp sequence because a person once knew it, and the knowing was part of the afternoon, and got kept.

The gaugers are the part of the portrait that is allowed to blur. That is what they are for. Everything else on the Sill is exact and therefore dead; the two hundred small warm bodies are the deliberate smudge, the lossy channel, the place where the copy is permitted to degrade so that something, somewhere, is still happening.

And the figure on the central platform, who is seen after they are heard, who lives permanently downstream of their own actions, who has never in their life been surprised by a sound — they are the most copied thing on the plain, and they know it, and they strike the plate four times and then stand very still and watch themselves do it, a hundred kilometres away, with no lag at all, and there is nobody in the world less alone and less accompanied.

Sometimes gaugers on the tower platforms do something absurd. Stand on one leg. Hold up a worm jar. Put a hat on the crook of the lamp-standard. There is no reason. It changes nothing. But a hundred kilometres off, in air so clean it hurts, the figure does it too, at the same instant, and it is pleasant to be in company even so.

## What lives in the sumps

Under the sheds, in the galvanic basements, among the long brown racks that smell of vinegar and ink, there is sludge, and in the sludge there is a blind flat worm.

It is nine centimetres long, pale as a nail paring, and it has no template. It is not a growth form. The Stand did not make it and cannot regrow it and has never once produced a bud that unfolded into one. It has ancestors. It has variation. No two are quite the same: the pigment speckling differs, the tail is notched or rounded or forked, one will curl clockwise when lifted and another anticlockwise, and these differences are stable and heritable and utterly unremarkable everywhere in the universe except here.

It eats the sludge. This is the only decay on the Sill that is not a decision. Everything else that goes back into the Stand goes back because the Stand chose to take it; the worm simply eats, on its own account, for its own reasons, and if it stopped, the racks would foul, and the current would fail, and the amber wave would stop crossing the plain at four metres a second, and there would be no light in two hundred and eleven districts but the ordinary indifferent daylight, which is the only thing here that behaves as a thing should.

Every gauger keeps a jar. Inside the coat, against the ribs, in winter. They can tell their worms apart. They have names for them — real names, invented, not stamp numbers, and the names are extravagant and silly and unlike anything else in the language: Kettle, Nine Winters, Little Bad Idea, the Bishop. They trade worms at the joint stations, four times a year, and argue about lineage, and lie about lineage, and are caught lying, and the catching and the lying and the arguing are the only events that occur on the Sill that were not already in the pattern.

There is one small pale moth, and it works the fog-hours, and once every eleven years the whole plain flowers white in a fortnight and smells like bread going over, and the moth is the reason there is a next generation of anything. There are the birds that are not birds, flying their correct circuit with their incorrect wrists. There are two hundred people who are the organ of forgetting, and their children, who arrive knowing everything except what to be called. There are eleven districts where the levels are correct. There is a thing at the far end of the pipes stamped with numbers that do not fit, which is being slowly taken in, or is not, and which you must not touch, and to which you say, with three knocks, the only word anyone has ever agreed on for it.

And in the basements, in the dark, under the amber lamp, in the vinegar smell, there is a blind animal that nobody designed, doing the one job that cannot be assigned, being slightly different from its neighbour for no reason at all.

That is the honest list. The gaugers will give you the other one, with the tally book open, and the numbers will not close, and they will not remark on it, because the level is correct afterwards, and the level is the thing that matters.
