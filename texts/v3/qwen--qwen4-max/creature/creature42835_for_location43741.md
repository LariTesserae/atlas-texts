## The Census of the Bell

Begin with the obvious answer, which is wrong the way first answers are usually wrong: four hundred and six people live here.

They are pale in a particular way that surface people find unhealthy and that they find unremarkable — wound-pale, the color of the walls they shave, which is not a coincidence but a diet, a light budget, and nine generations of not going up. Their irises are wide. They have a gesture that visitors mistake for piety: to read a gauge, a letter, a face, you tilt it up toward the vault and wait. In Nine Tap you wait for light the way elsewhere you wait for a kettle. Lovers on the Bell Steps turn each other's chins upward with two fingers, patiently, to see what was said.

They speak quietly, because the room gives every word back twice, and a shout in the Bell is a thing you have done to four hundred people at once. The whole town murmurs. The only loud hour is the loose hour, and they use it: grievances are saved for the rain, proposals are shouted through it, and the great family feuds of Nine Tap have all been conducted at a bellow, in warm falling water, in the middle of the floor, with everyone watching and no one able to hear.

For eleven minutes at dawn nobody speaks at all. You cannot; the tide takes the air. They have a hand-cant for it, forty-odd signs, mostly about work, and children learn it before they learn to write. Old people sign in their sleep.

The trades are legible on the body. Scarifiers have a long right shoulder and a shortened left, from the drawknife, and a way of standing sideways to a wall as though appraising a cut in it. Callers go deaf from the bottom of their hearing upward, so that a retired caller hears only the wire-note and the highest struck bars and, eventually, nothing else at all — not a voice, not a door. They stay. They have to. There is nowhere else that sounds like anything they can hear. Assayers have the opposite problem: their noses get so specific that ordinary life becomes faintly odorless to them, and they are the only people in town who eat with salt.

The reckoners keep a column in the books for mean reach — the height a working adult can shave with a drawknife at full extension — because the wound rate depends on it. It has fallen four centimeters in nine generations. Nobody in town has noticed. The reckoners have noticed. It is one of several things they have noticed and not published.

Nine of the four hundred and six were born on the surface. There are always about nine; the town is hospitable, marriages happen, people come down the pore with a trunk and a good attitude. They last a season or two. The complaint is always the same and is never believed until it happens: the air up there is loose, and the room at night is silent, and there is nothing holding the water. They lie awake listening for the note and hear their own blood instead, and by the second month they are weeping at odd times, and by the fourth they have gone back up, and the town is kind about it and does not mention it again.

Nobody born in the Bell leaves. Some climb. They come back down within the year, thin, talkative, unable to say what was wrong. The assay office has a fraction for that state. It is one of the milder ones.

---

The stock are not cargo, though the ledger treats them as cargo, and this is the ledger's oldest error.

They are alates. Six legs, blind, thumb-sized, fingernail-colored, and folded on each back a pair of wings like wet mica that they will use exactly once. The galleries are a nursery. The tide is a wedding. Everything the town calls economy happens in the eleven minutes between the leaving and the landing, and the nets are simply the place where the town has arranged to be standing when the marriage passes.

The ones the nets miss come down. They land on the vault and the buttresses and the parapets and the shoulders of the callers, and they shed — a dry tick of wings letting go, all across the chamber, for about a quarter of an hour after the drums stop. Then they walk. The grain of the floor runs down toward the sump like water going somewhere, and they follow the grain, and for the rest of the morning there is a slow pale river of mothers moving out of the empty middle toward the throat, unhurried, six-legged, wingless now, going down to be something else.

The town lets them go. The town must; it is in the arrangement. The cake and the frass and the mothers are the payment.

The wings are swept by children and sold by weight. Poor wards' children sell them; rich wards' children buy them. A bride in the Combs wears a veil of wings sewn on netting, twelve thousand of them, and it makes a sound when she moves like the last thirty seconds of the tide, and everyone at the wedding cries, and no one has ever been able to explain to a visitor why.

What comes back up the throat at the slack is long-jawed and patient and about the length of a man's arm. The assay office maintains that these are unrelated deep fauna, and publishes a plate. The reeves, who have to handle them, point out that the big ones sometimes carry a healed notch in the second leg joint exactly where a net-anchor's mesh takes a flying alate, and that they come up the same grain they went down. The town holds both opinions comfortably. It has held them for two hundred and six years. It feeds the throat either way.

---

The reeve-wasps are the only thing in the Bell that the town did not ask for.

They arrived around the year the third net went in, and they nested in the bosses the tree had grown around the anchors, and they have stayed, and they hunt the buttresses at the slack with a sound like someone slowly tearing wet paper. Forearm-long, press-cake brown, a jaw hinge you can watch work. The reeve families keep them the way elsewhere people keep hawks: gauntlets of cake-leather, a debt of meal, a great deal of formal courtesy. A reeve wasp will let a child touch it once. After that it is a working animal and the child is told to go inside.

They are also the town's best instrument, and everybody knows it, and the gauge-makers resent it. Wasps leave the vault before a column snaps. Not always, and not by much — twenty seconds, thirty — but they leave, all at once, streaming down the buttresses to the low wood, and any scarifier who sees it drops his knife and runs for the wall. There are men alive because they watched a wasp instead of a dial.

The assay office would like to know what the wasps are hearing. The assay office has been asking for a hundred and forty years. The reeves' position is that you may pay for the hunt and you may pay for the cake, but the wasps are not a fraction, and you cannot put a warm plate under a thing that has already left.

---

The cats are not cats and the town has stopped bothering to explain.

They are galls. The tree grows them the way it grows a boss around a bolt: a local thickening, a specialization, a thing issued rather than born. They come out of the wood at the sill-height of a doorway, warm, about the weight of a full bucket, with no eyes but a wet groove that tracks you, and they do not eat, and no one in two centuries has seen one eat, and they do not drink, and they are always slightly warmer than the room.

They are not replaced by breeding. They are replaced by reissue. When one dies it is found dry and light as a paper nest, and within a week there is another on the same sill, and it knows the house. It knows which stair the old woman's foot catches on and waits at the bottom of it. It knows the child. There is a crook-tailed one in Tallow Row that has had a crooked tail through eleven bodies and a hundred and eighty years, and the family's name for it is older than the family's surname, and the reckoners have the reissue dates in the books, and the intervals are getting shorter.

They purr at the wire-note. Exactly at it. Not approximately — the callers use a cat to tune a struck bar when they cannot get up to the crown-shaft, and the bar and the animal and the water column make one sound, and the sound is the town.

And at the loose hour, when the canopy shuts and the tension slackens and the note goes out of the air for fifty minutes, the cats go on purring it. Every one of them, on every sill, holding a pitch that is no longer there.

That is when they walk out into the middle. Each to its own place — nine places, fixed, surveyed by the reckoners a century ago and never once found to have shifted — where they sit in the emptiness facing the sump, warm, keeping the note going in a room where the note has stopped. Children are told they are hunting. Children are told a lot of things. The nine places are the nine points at which nothing may ever be set down.

---

The mother-stock lives here too, and she is not the building.

Her language has forty-one named fractions on the assay plate and a shelf of unnamed ones behind it, and no first person. She has bloom, and drought-three-days, and cut-on-the-north-side, and thirst, and a word that means *more*, which is also the word she uses for injury, because from where she stands they are the same request.

She also has a fraction for each of them. For every person. A smear so specific that a good nose with a warm plate can tell you which gallery a woman walked past on her way to the presses, and the office keeps a private index of it — not the public forecast books, the other book, the one that is really a cemetery. When someone dies their fraction thins out of the catch over about a season, and the assayers sit with it while it goes, and this is the only funeral rite in Nine Tap that anybody actually believes in. The bodies go down the throat. The names stay in the wood for ninety days and are smelled, one last time, by a professional.

There is a further thing in that book, and it is the reason the reckoners are feared and the reason the reckoners are frightened.

The fractions arrive early. About a season before a conception, a new one appears in the catch: a person who does not exist yet, described in chemistry, complete. The office has known this for four generations. The town believes the reckoners allocate the births — that the ledger, balancing to the kilogram, decides how many mouths the wound can carry, and grants, and refuses, and is hated for it. What the reckoners actually do is transcribe. They read what has already been named and they write it into the book and they let the town hate them for arithmetic, because the alternative is telling four hundred people that they are a crop with a forecast.

And she keeps the room open. That is the part nobody says out loud, because it is obvious once said and unbearable after. Wood fills. That is what wood does; callus after callus, a thousand years of it, the whole slow cataract of the walls running down toward the sump to close the wound. The scarifiers do not shave the streets to make light. Light is the byproduct. They shave the streets to keep a wound from healing, and the lighting rate and the wound rate are the same rate, and every guild in town — the callers, the net crews, the bolters with their pilot holes and their overnight bosses, the scaffold-waiters standing eighty years in front of a wall that has not yet agreed to arrive — is in the business of preventing closure.

She wanted hands. Hands are the only thing in the world that will keep a hole open on purpose. She tried callus for eight hundred years and lost the room every time, and then somewhere up the pore something came down with a chisel, and she has been feeding it, lighting it, watering it, tuning it, and quietly making it smaller and paler and better-hearing ever since.

---

Eight taps in Nine Tap are brass, padlocked, and counted. The ninth is the throat, and it is not a tap, and the town has a word for what drinks there.

The word is a pitch. It sits in the callers' scale as a barred interval — a hole in the music that every child learns to step over before they learn the drums, the way children elsewhere learn not to say a certain name in a certain house. There is no rule written down about it. There has never needed to be. A caller who sounds it is put up the pore, which is the town's only sentence, and which is understood by everyone to be a death, because up there nothing sings and the air is loose.

The four names on the shrine at the Ninth Tap were assayers. The town's story is sabotage, and the town has told it so long that the shrine has a worn place where people put their thumbs. The reckoners' books record only that all four had been on plate duty in the season before, that they had asked for the deep fractions three times, and that on the night they opened the taps they did it together, in order, calmly, from the bottom of the shaft upward.

The larger shrine is unmarked and always swept.

And the equality has never once been equal. There is a discrepancy of four hundred grams a year, always the same sign, carried forward in the books since the first ledger, called the float. Two hundred and six years of balancing to the kilogram, and every year something takes four hundred grams that no press, no throat, no frass-crew, no child can account for. The reckoners do not investigate it anymore. It is not a loss. That is what stopped being discussed around year sixty. It is a payment, it is regular, it is small, and it is not going to the roots.

The column is under a hundred atmospheres of negative and it sings. Everyone says the tree is pulling water up from something deep and hot below. The arithmetic works either way. You can read that note as a thing being lifted, or as a thing being held — a wire drawn tight over a mouth, and the sound of the tightness, and fifty minutes a day when the tree has to slacken it because even she cannot hold forever.

That is what the loose hour is. The rain is real, the children are genuinely delighted, the iron-and-pepper taste is on everyone's tongue, and the whole town is out in the middle of the floor directly over the throat, shouting, at the exact hour when the drinking can be heard. The drums are silent because nothing may be steered then. The wasps work the buttresses. Nine warm galls sit in the emptiness holding a pitch that has stopped. Four hundred people make as much noise as they can, joyfully, for fifty minutes, and call it a holiday, and are correct.

---

So: what lives here.

One thing. It has a woody skeleton and eleven billion flying blood cells and four hundred and six faces; it has an immune system with a jaw hinge and a memory made of ledgers and a sense of smell it sells to strangers; it keeps time with nine warm animals on nine sills, and it thinks in a hollow it must cut open every week or lose. Its generation is nine human generations. It eats light and nitrogen and it excretes forecasts. It has been domesticating its own nervous system for two hundred and six years, and the nervous system has a guild structure, a marriage custom involving wings, and two shrines, and believes it is a town.

Under it, at the throat, something drinks that is not it, and takes four hundred grams a year, and is either a partner or a prisoner, and the town has arranged its entire calendar around not finding out.

And you. You came down through a pore that has been widened by nine generations of hands and is closing again at the bottom, where the wood has flowed over the rungs into grips that fit a Nine Tap hand exactly, and fit yours better than they should. By morning the assay office will have a fraction for you off the warm plate — surface-born, adult, one who has come down — and it will not be a new fraction. It will be one from the shelf behind the forty-one, the old ones, the ones that arrive a season early.

The ledger has room for four hundred and nine.
