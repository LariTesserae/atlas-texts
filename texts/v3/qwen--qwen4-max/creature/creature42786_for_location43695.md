## Noon, when no one is home

Begin at the white hour, because that is when the question looks unanswerable. The floor is a clean bowl of pink clay and glass-dark nodules. The walls are two hectares of wet-looking white with nothing on them but a scatter of dots that are mineral until they aren't. The pool holds its brightness like a held note. Nothing moves.

Nothing is *here*. That is different. The residents of this room are not distributed through it the way fish are distributed through a lake; they are distributed through its hours. Everyone who lives in the cup lives in it at a particular time, in a particular thin band of condition, and at noon they are all in the stone. To ask who lives here at midday is to ask who lives in a house at four in the morning. The answer is: everyone, asleep, in the walls.

So the census has to be taken sideways. Not *what is present* but *what keeps returning*.

## The green, which is two square meters wide

Follow the seep-faces around a third of the circumference and you find the only visible plant life in the chamber: a band of moss and algae two fingers wide, wet at dawn, crisp by the second hour, alive again the next dawn. Laid out flat it would cover a door. That is the entire standing green of a room a hundred thousand cubic meters in volume, and it is not what feeds anything.

What feeds things is the invisible pasture. Above the green line and below it, on all that white, there is a film of cells one cell thick, epilithic, living in the pores of the case-hardened skin, drinking the dawn bloom and spending it before noon. Twenty thousand square meters of it. It has no color you can name. It is the largest organism in the cup by area and the thinnest by body, and it is eaten continuously by animals that leave no mark except a slightly cleaner white, which fills back in within a season.

Everything else here is a way of moving that film around.

## The snails, which are the wall, walking

Four thousand and some, each holding roughly five square meters of vertical white — a country, an estate, a lifetime's range. They graze the film with a ribbon of teeth hardened by the chert dust that hangs in the clay, which means they are scraping limestone with limestone's own indigestible fraction. The wall goes into them. Some of it becomes shell. A snail here is a piece of the chamber that has acquired a direction.

They are not all old. Most are young and get eaten. But there is a small gerontocracy up on the high wall — perhaps forty animals whose banding records eleven hundred wet seasons, whose shells have thickened past the point where a chert edge will open them, and who have not been reachable in centuries because they climbed. Age here is measured in distance from the floor.

The old ones navigate by copies of the sun. Each has settled into one of the hundreds of pinhole discs the fig canopy throws on the white, and sits in it, and the disc drifts a few centimeters a year as the trees grow and the sky's mechanics shift, and the snail drifts with it. Two clocks, neither wound, keeping each other.

Once a decade they all mate, at one place on the eastern wall. Consider what that requires: four thousand animals crossing their five-square-meter countries at a speed measured in centimeters per hour, setting out — some of them — a year or more in advance, converging on a groove a finger deep worn into stone that a knife will barely scratch. Soft bodies made that groove. Nothing else did. It is the only path in the room and it looks constructed and it is not; it is the accumulated abrasion of ten thousand pilgrimages, which is the only way anything gets shaped here.

## The lizards, who own a schedule instead of a place

Uncounted, and probably uncountable, because they live on a border rather than in a territory. The coin of direct sun has an edge, and at that edge the midges are dazzled and slow, and the lizards work it — moving with the terminator across the floor in the morning, up the eastern wall in the afternoon, retiring when the light leaves the rim. Their home range is a figure-eight laid across the year. They set their fringed feet down the way you'd lower something into water you didn't want to disturb.

Two of them on one wall is a crowd. They have never resolved this. They simply leave.

## The midges, which are weather

Several hundred thousand in the air column at any instant, and not one of them a resident in any sense that would survive a scrutiny. They live hours. The column is not a population; it is a rate — the visible portion of a stream of small bodies drifting in over the lip and drifting down through the light: pollen, mites, the ballooning young of spiders that will never spin anything here, the whole aerial silt of the plateau outside.

Count them and you have counted a river by its width.

## The swifts, who are the only ones that leave

Eleven thousand, fringed into silence, turning through the volume without a sound, roosting in pockets the water made, building nothing at all. They are the cup's lungs and its only mail. Everything they eat came from elsewhere; everything they carry on their feet came from elsewhere. When they come back before dawn they bring the outside in on their bodies, a few grains at a time, and the room's entire foreign trade is conducted by animals that own nothing and stay nowhere.

And for ninety seconds at the hinge of dusk they say the one thing that gets said here.

It is not clear what it is. It has the shape of a roll call — a hard bright chittering that fills the ellipse and comes back doubled and tripled until the volume is saturated with itself, and then stops as if a hand closed. Perhaps it is only the day's silence being discharged. But there is a use for it that nobody in the room intends: high in the nursery pockets, where the infants of the nine spend fifteen years unseen, that sound arrives once a day, every day, for fifteen years. It is the whole of their education. It is the only thing that comes up to them. When they finally descend, they already know the law, and no one has taught them, and there is no one to ask.

## The pool, a sea the width of a hat

Forty-odd translucent shrimp, a flatworm the color of a fingernail, larvae hanging under the surface film like small suspended punctuation. Ankle-deep, never deeper, never dry, rimmed in a collar of travertine it precipitated out of its own disappearing.

The shrimp are a number rather than a set of individuals: forty is what this much water and this much light will hold, and the occupants rotate through it. The flatworm may be a different case. It divides, and it heals, and it is possible that the animal in that pool has not died in a very long time and has also never had a body older than a season.

The larvae become midges and rise. Some are eaten by swifts a few meters above the water they were born in; some go over the lip and become the outside's problem. So the cup exports midges and imports midges, and the trade very nearly balances, and the pool sits in its own collar of stone making the difference.

Above it, one drop every eleven breaths.

## The isopod and the hundred million, who live in a different room

Under the clay there is another chamber occupying the same coordinates. It is a millimeter or two thick and it is entirely dark. Its ceiling is a leaf, its floor is a grain of chert, its weather is the slow wet creep of the whole floor downhill. A hundred million springtails tend it. A slow armored isopod pushes grains one at a time and does not stop.

Nothing in that room has ever seen the coin, or the copies of the sun, or the white. The brightness that defines this place is, to its most numerous inhabitants, a rumor about temperature. They know the drop as a pressure arriving through the clay. They are the strongest animals in the cup relative to their work, and they are the reason the floor is clean — not clean like a swept thing, clean like a river, which is to say moving.

There is no litter here because there is a labor force underneath, and it never sleeps, and it has no idea what it is doing.

## The seven figs, who live in the future

Rooted along the annual track of the coin, bent in arcs that point not at the light but at where the light will be in a season. They forecast. It is the only forecasting in the room. Every other resident is a specialist in the present tense — the wet hour, the moving edge, the interval — and the figs are eleven hundred years of accumulated anticipation, their trunks a physical record of the sky's mechanics, the northern one bowed to the ground and up again in the exact shape of a year.

That bow touches the floor. Figs root where they touch. It is not certain that there are seven trees here; it may be one, seven times, walking outward along its own prediction at the speed of wood.

Through their steep-held leaves the sun comes as hundreds of pinhole discs, all the same size, all the actual sun. Clouds cross the floor in the shape of clouds. Eclipses fill the white with crescents. The room has never needed a sky of its own; it has been content to borrow one, in miniature, repeatedly.

## The fern, which is luck

One. Forty years old. The size of a hand. In a crack that happens to stay wet.

It has no partner and no plan and no second fern anywhere in the chamber. A spore came in on the drift, or on a swift's foot, and landed in the single fissure out of twenty thousand square meters that could hold it, and it has been sitting there ever since, being the only thing in this room that is here by accident. Everything else has a reason. The fern has a history.

## The nine, who are the room's hands

Long-armed, slow, pale-eyed under a shelf of bone that works like a brim. Nine rough quadrants of wall, maintained with enormous courtesy. Two meetings a year at the pool, a body-length apart, no touching. A different pocket every night. No tool kept, no cache, no property, nothing carried that is not currently in use.

The limestone dissolves and the chert does not, so the floor is studded with an inexhaustible supply of edges — the room's indigestible fraction, weathering out, blue-grey and liver-red, lying in the clay like drops of glass. The nine are what the chamber has instead of a way of dealing with this. A nodule is picked up, struck, opened; forty snails come apart over a month; fig bark is cut for the sweet cambium; and then the tool is walked to the low point of the floor and dropped into the slot.

Every one. Spent or unspent. Three strikes a season, and after each the whole room stops for a minute — the swifts stall and drop and catch themselves — and then resumes, and no one has learned anything about how to do it more quietly.

They do not appear to consider the disposal. That is the important thing. It is not thrift and it is not ritual and it is not manners; it is the way you put your hand out when you fall. Somewhere in fifteen years of listening in the dark, the slot became a direction rather than a decision.

## The tall one, which is a consequence with nothing left to follow from

Two long legs, pale as the wall, a fan of stiff feathers around each ear-opening, independently turned. Very old. Forty paces a day. It does not watch, because watching is useless in a room with four bounces of light and no honest shadow. It listens to the drop and to the intervals and to anything that arrives in an interval that shouldn't.

Nothing drinks at the seep. Nothing has drunk at the seep for a very long time.

Which raises the question of what the tall one eats, and the answer appears to be: almost nothing. An occasional swift that misjudges the turn. A lizard that gets greedy at the edge. The exceptions. It lives on exceptions, and there have been no exceptions, and so it is starving — not sharply, not in a way that shows, but at the speed of the room, over centuries, forty paces a day being roughly what a body spends when it has nothing to spend.

It is not a predator. It is a deterrent, and deterrence works so well that it eliminates the food supply of the deterrent. There has never been more than one. Whether it is replaced or simply continues, no one has stayed long enough to see. It folds its ear-fans flat for the ninety seconds and waits out the only interval in the day when it cannot hear, when anything at all could go to the seep and take its drink and not be taken.

Nothing does. The law has moved out of the tall one and into everybody else's bodies, and the tall one goes on keeping it anyway, because it is the last thing in the room that remembers there was a reason.

## The throat, and the room underneath the room

A hand wide, at the low point, warm air sighing out of it in winter. Everything the floor creeps toward. A millimeter or two a year of clay and chert and leaf-fall and shell and small bone, and every finished edge the nine have ever let go of, and the flake from three strikes ago, and eventually the tall one.

So there is a place below where all of it is. Where the hundred thousand years of tools lie in a heap, and the snail shells, and the fig leaves, and the bones — where nothing is subtracted and everything accumulates and the light does not come. The cup is clean because underneath it there is a room that is not. The bright chamber is the negative of a dark one. Something down there is presumably fat, and eyeless, and has never heard of the sun.

## You

For an hour or two, sometimes, there is one more resident: a warm loud animal standing at the rim, squinting, saying that the air looks thick.

Its cough goes to the far wall intact and comes back, and comes back again. It is the loudest thing that has happened here since the last stone was struck, and it does not know, and it will describe the place afterward as silent.

## Eleven breaths

Here is what the counting comes to.

The water does not know about breaths. The drop falls when the seep has gathered enough, and the interval is what it is, and it would be what it is if the chamber were sterile. Eleven is not a property of the water. Eleven is a measurement, and a measurement requires something that is breathing, and there is exactly one thing in this room whose breath is slow enough and long enough and patient enough to have made the number.

The snails keep it in their withdrawal. The nine keep it in their hands. The swifts keep it in the ninety seconds they are permitted. The infants keep it before they have ever seen the light. But the count itself lives in the shade behind the seep, in an old pale animal that is going hungry because it has done its job perfectly, and when it stops, the drop will go on falling exactly as it always has, and nothing in the room will be regular anymore.

Regularity is something someone does.

The pool takes the drop. The film begins on the cool wall, hours early, invisible. A fig leaf turns two degrees toward a sun that will not arrive until spring, and the clay moves a millimeter toward the dark, carrying a flake, a shell, a leaf, a small bone, and the white gives back the last of the light — and the room goes on being exactly itself, which is not a fact about the room. It is a thing that is being done, continuously, by everything living in it, and has been for longer than there has been anything to look at it, and will be done tonight, and no one will do it on purpose.
