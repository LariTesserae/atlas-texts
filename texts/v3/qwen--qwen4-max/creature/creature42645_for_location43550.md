## The working

They call themselves nothing grander than the working — the scratch-arithmetic, the part of a sum that is done and rubbed out and done again so that the answer can be set down clean.

You notice the hands first, because the hands are always busy and the rest of the body is never quite still. Broad palms, flat wide nails pared every morning with a bone blade, fingers thickened at the second joint from a life of levering and soaking and waiting for the crack. Then you notice that nobody is standing evenly. Weight goes from the left foot to the right and back, a slow rock, a shift of the hip, the heel lifted and set down. A crowd of them at the census hall looks like a field of something growing. Children are carried until they can be trusted to shift, and the first thing a child learns after walking is the rock, and the second thing is the flask.

The rime takes them where they fold. Eyelids, the corners of the mouth, the creases of the knuckles, behind the ears, the hairline, under the nails, the navel, the soft inner elbow. By the third day unwashed a person's face begins to read as a map of their own creasing, white in every line, and their eyelashes carry a bead of lime at the tips like the sitters' — and this is why the washing is done at dawn and done thoroughly and done by someone else if it can be managed, because you cannot see your own ears. The brown water stings. It smells of cold tea and sour peat and it makes the eyes run, and children cry at it and are held and washed anyway, and the crying is not thought to be a tragedy. Everyone smells of peat smoke and vinegar. Everyone's skin is faintly grained, pale, dry-looking, and slightly cool, and if you touch a forearm it is like touching the clint on a shaded morning.

Their hair grows continuously and is cut continuously, and their teeth do too. They chew dried willow bark and the tough tussock grass to keep the teeth down, and they grind them in thought, so a household at night is a small sound of filing, and the old have long chisel teeth worn flat across the front from a lifetime of grinding and grinding. The hair goes to the cords. The nail parings go to the moss. The bark they chew is grown on the shelf above their own hearth. Nothing about a body is allowed to be a leftover.

They sleep cut into the moss banks, in beds a body wide and a body deep, warm from the rot underneath, and they are turned in the night. This is the ordinary intimacy: you wake and you turn the person beside you, and they wake enough to turn you, and neither says anything. Moss is safe. Moss unbuilds fast enough to keep up. A person who has slept alone in a cut bed for forty years will still, on the nights when there is no one, set a withy timer of a dripping cup and wake to turn themselves, and be embarrassed about it, and not mention it.

They do not sit on stone. They squat, they kneel on a pad, they lean, they carry a folded mat of withy and moss everywhere and set it down and pick it up again, and a stranger who sits on the clint to rest is not shouted at. Someone comes and stands near them. That is all. They stand near, and shift, and after a while they say something about the water.

## The flask and the cord

Two things hang at every hip: the stoppered clay flask of brown, warm from the body, and a short knotted cord.

The cord is not the name. The name is in the hall, on its peg, in the great wall of silk and hair, and the cord at the hip is a copy you can feel in the dark. There are exactly as many names as people, and a name is a place, and when a place is vacated it is filled, and the filling is not a metaphor and not a ceremony of comfort. It is a transfer. The knot is lifted off the living wall and set on the held wall, same knot, same silk, different peg, and a child is given the hip-cord that has been in the drawer since the elder began getting ready. The number does not differ. This is not because nothing happens to them. It is because they have arranged, at enormous and continuous cost, for nothing to happen to the number.

So they do not grieve in the way you would recognize. What they have instead is a horror of the unpegged knot, and it is a real horror, and it has a physical mark on the city: the door-jambs are the most deeply eaten stone in the basin. Brown water is run on them so the doors will not seal, yes, but also because a household that cannot make its tally come out sits in the doorway all night, and the threshold is where the flask gets spilled, and where people set their elbows, and where the sour water works. You can read a family's history in the wear of its jamb, which is the only history anything here has. The neighbours come and bring moss-tea and stand. They do not sit down beside you. Standing beside someone all night, shifting from foot to foot in the cold, saying nothing, is the largest thing one person can do for another, and everybody knows it, and it is not spoken of as kindness. It is spoken of as attendance.

They are not serene. Serenity is not the flavour of them. They are fussy, dry, funny, tender, and anxious inside a very narrow band. Their jokes are all about the phantom twelfth, the man who counted his own thumb twice, the woman who tallied a frog and her husband in the same column. Their pleasure is the click, the come-out, the moment the two columns close, and they will describe it the way other people describe a summit or a birth. Their music is the birds' music: one note per circuit at your station, and the households give theirs at their hour, so that the day across twenty miles is a chord too widely spaced to be heard as a chord by anyone standing in it. You hear one voice, and then, from a long way off, another, and the whole thing is a tune only the rim knows.

They talk about the water. They talk about the beds, whether the fourth race is ripening evenly, whether the weave on the new blocks matches the old, and there is an aesthetics here, a real one, fiercely held: a wall whose basket-print aligns course to course across seven shells is beautiful, and a stair whose treads are all worn to exactly nothing is beautiful, and a grike kept at knee-width for nine hundred years is beautiful. They have no stories. They have ratios, and they find them moving. Ask a woman why she has spent her life on a thread of brown water down a fissure and she will tell you the width, and watch your face to see whether you understood, and be pleased if you did.

Only one thing is uncounted, and it is a standing joke and a standing wound. Nobody has found a way to tally dreams. They dream, they say, of weight. Of something lying over everything, patient, and of shells, and of a sound like the snails but enormous. Nobody has seen the sea. It is in the rock at their feet and in the shells on the snails in their seams and it comes up the gorge every afternoon as fog and goes back down, and they will tell you it is coming back a film at a time and that the counting is what keeps it from hurrying, and then they will change the subject to the beds.

## The smaller majority

The people are outnumbered past counting, and they know it, and they count it anyway.

The snails are the population. Put your ear to a grike and you hear the number being ground fine: thousands of rasps, a sound like a file drawn slowly across a file. They wear the shells that are in the rock. Not similar shells — the same, rib for rib, the same coiled whorl, the same slight thickening at the lip, and the fossils in the clint and the animals in the seam are one kind held at its type, because a change in a kind would be a discrepancy, and there is no mechanism here by which a discrepancy is permitted to keep going. Nothing evolves in the basin. It is not that the snails have found a perfect form. It is that the form is a term in the sum and the sum is being held.

The frogs: one brown frog per wet cleft. One. If a cleft has two, somebody comes, and there is no drama about it, and nobody explains to a stranger what is done, and the cleft has one again by evening.

The spiders are the loophole, and everyone is quietly aware of it. Nothing heaps up anywhere in the basin — no midden, no silt fan, no drift, no soil horizon — except silk. The webs are allowed to accumulate. They felt and layer and thicken, thumb-deep in the old corners, curtains a yard long between clint and clint, and they are the only accumulating thing in twenty miles, the only place where time is permitted to leave a residue. And they are harvested on schedule, and the silk goes to the census hall, and the cords are made of it, along with everyone's hair. The city's one rubbish heap is the wall of the number. Everything that has been allowed to pile up has been spun into arithmetic and hung on a peg. If you want to find your own body in the record, you can: run the cord for your name between finger and thumb and some of it is your grandmother's hair and some of it is a web that hung over the Kettle for nine years.

The herd is the largest term. Pale, long-haired, goat-footed, thousands on the far terraces, and their day is spent largely on each other: licking the rime off a face, a fetlock, the ridge of a shoulder, working the night's film off one another with their tongues the way the people work it off themselves with the flask. It is the same craft. The people watch them do it and find it restful. Their teeth and hooves grow without stopping and are taken back without stopping, and the road they walk is re-plated every night, so their circuit is a mirror instead of a trench, and from the rim the Shine is a line of light with animals moving along it like beads on a wire.

The herd has its own set-down. Out on the terrace there are shapes among the clints that are not resting: an old buck glazed to the knee, the hair still readable along its neck, and the herd goes around it without altering the circuit, and the young lick its face because it is still in the roll and still, in the way that matters here, a member. Hide and horn and sinew come from these. The leather on a wader's feet is an animal that chose the terrace. Nothing is thrown away and nothing is taken that was not offered.

And there is the tree. One, in the south hollow, rowan-ish, twisted, a crown you could sit under cross-legged, counted, the same number of berries. It is the only thing in the basin that plainly wants to be different. It pushes; it is pruned to its number; it sets more berries than it is allowed and the surplus is taken and put in the moss and the beetles have it. The household whose name is the tree's count tends it, and they are the ones who apologize. Not aloud, particularly. They do it with their hands, a small smoothing gesture after a cut, and they are the tenderest people in the city and slightly odd, and other households send them moss-tea more often than they need it.

## The set-down

The sitters are not statues and they are not dead, and the people's reticence about them with strangers is not superstition. It is more like the reticence of a family about a grandfather in the next room who is very slow now and must not be shouted at.

Getting ready takes a while. You choose your place, usually a boundary, somewhere your number will do work. You lengthen your stillnesses. You practise the shift more slowly, then not at all, for an hour, then for a morning; you have your hair cut short and your cord copied and the drawer opened; you say, to the people who need to hear it, which term you will hold. Then you go out in the evening and sit down cross-legged on the bare clint and the dew comes and by dawn there is a night's worth at your ankles, bonding, and you do not get up, and after that it is not a question of getting up.

The lime does not embalm. It replaces. A hair's thickness at a time, from the surface in, exactly as the peaks stay sharp by being freshly broken and the river runs clear by carrying the mountain invisibly — the sitter is maintained, not preserved, and what continues is a shape being continuously made rather than a body being slowly ruined. This is why the eyelashes are readable on the recent ones. This is why the very old ones are soft, and are still, in the local sense, present.

Their thinking slows as the mineral fraction rises. A sitter of a year is about as quick as a dozing person and can be spoken to normally, and will answer, eventually, with the eyes. A sitter of a century thinks a thought a season. The old ones out past the eastern beds are one long thought that has been going since before the seventh shell of the walls, and it has not finished, and it is not suffering. Each holds a term. That is what they are doing. The wall in the census hall is a copy; the sitters are the original; and when a cord and a sitter disagree the cord is wrong, and someone walks out with brown water and a bone brush and cleans the sitter's eyes and the creases of its mouth so the features can be read again, and then walks back and re-ties the knot.

They are groomed. They are visited. Families bring a word a year, one sentence, said slowly, at a rate suited to the listener — *the fourth race is ripening evenly* — and stand, and shift, and go home. Children are told not to shout at them because they hear it all at once and it takes them a long time to put it down. And the hardest thing to walk past in the whole basin, harder than the Throat, is the newest sitter, three weeks out, still fast enough to blink.

Nobody is put there. It is chosen. It is honoured. And it is the reason the count holds, because the count is not a description of the basin. It is a load being carried, and they are the ones carrying it, and the living are the part of the work that moves.

## The answer

Here is what lives in the basin, singular, under all the small lives: a sum being carried out toward the sea.

The sea is the answer. It is coming, it has been coming for as long as the knots go back, a film at a time, and the whole apparatus — two waters, two plumbings, the flask at every hip, the thread of brown down every grike, the herd licking the rime, the moss rotting as fast as it rises, the forty cracks a day and the long silence after each — is a governor on that arrival. Not a wall against it. A speed. The counting is the friction that keeps a geological event from becoming an event. If the number ever came out early, all at once, the water would come up the gorge and not stop at its accustomed height, and there is no flood mark on any wall here because there is no word for flood, and the entire population, every ordinary fussy morning of it, exists so that the word continues not to be needed.

They do not hold this as a cosmology. They have no cosmology; they have ratios. Ask a man at the casting beds why the form must be reset the same day the block is levered and he will say *because it has to come out*, and if you press him he will show you the Throat and the afternoon tongue of fog and the rim dripping into the springs, and he will be patient with you, and he will not be able to say it any other way, and he will be right.

## The odd

There is a household on the southern diagonal, in the rain band, whose jamb is eaten almost through.

They have not been able to make it come out for a long time. The neighbours come most nights with moss-tea and stand in the doorway and shift from foot to foot and say nothing, and go home before the light, and the eldest of them has been getting ready for a while and has not gone out yet, because a place is open on the held wall and there is no one to fill it, and to sit down now would leave the knot unpegged, and an unpegged knot is the only sin.

So they wait, and wash, and grind their teeth in the dark, and tend the fourth race, and are gentle with each other in the way of people who have been frightened for years.

You are the only thing in the basin that will be different tomorrow. They will notice from a long way off — the herd will notice, the midge columns will be walked around differently, the hour-bird will give its note and you will not be in it. They will come and count you, and the counting will not be cold; it is the only kind of attention they have and it is the real kind, and to be counted here is to be kept. They will offer the flask, warm from someone's hip, sour, and they will show you how to do behind your own ears. They will hope you don't sit down.

They will also, some of them, hope you stay, and they will not ask, because nobody is put there. And if you find that you cannot make your own tally come out, and you sit in a doorway all night going over it, they will bring you tea, and stand beside you until the light, and not speak.
