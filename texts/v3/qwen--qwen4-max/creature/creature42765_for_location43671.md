# The Inhabitants

To live on the Shelf is to hold a place in a circuit. Everything else — the grazing, the drifting, the eleven-hour murders, the thirty-thousand-year memories — is downstream of that. There are three scarcities here and every body is an argument about them: water, which arrives for fifty minutes a day; copper, which arrives at a centimeter an hour and only where the current leaves the felt; and warmth, which is a degree and a half and belongs to whoever is standing in it.

But there is a fourth thing, and it is the one that shapes the animals more than the other three put together.

Movement costs metal.

Nothing here has a hinge. A hinge is a part, and nothing here has parts. A leg on the Shelf is a mineral fiber the body lays down ahead of itself and dissolves behind itself, and the laying-down is plating, and the plating is copper out of the blood. To take a step is to spend. To lift a foot is to eat it. This is why the ground is so slow and the sky is so full: the air is the only place on the Shelf where travel is cheap, and everything that has ever managed to get up there has stayed.

Begin, though, on your knees.

---

The two centimeters are not a layer. They are a country with a canopy and an understory and a floor, and the vertical distance from the top of one to the bottom of the other is a migration.

At the top: the filament tips, oxidizing, ultraviolet-scoured, dry as ash by two in the afternoon, and the grazing ciliates that come up into them in slow clouds when the fog is on. Below that, a millimeter down, the wet middle, where the water films persist longest and the rotifers do their nightly death and their punctual noon resurrection. Below that, the reducing base, warm, alkaline, in permanent chemical contact with the rock, where things live that have never in four hundred million years encountered oxygen and would be killed by a breath of it.

There are animals that spend their whole lives climbing that two centimeters and animals that spend their whole lives falling down it. There is a flatworm-shaped thing, three millimeters long, that is a apex predator in the middle wet and a corpse in the top dry, and it knows the difference the way you know the difference between a room and a cliff edge — not by reasoning, but by the feel of the water leaving its skin. At its own scale it is a megafauna. It has a territory the size of a coin and it defends it for eleven days, which is a long and violent life.

Ten thousand generations a season. Whole phyla in a thumbnail. The Shelf's real wilderness is two centimeters deep and covers a small country and you have already walked across a hundred square kilometers of it without noticing anything but a black stain on the rock.

---

There is a phylum here that has never been awake in dry air.

They are called hour-animals, and their entire life cycle fits inside the fog. The cysts sit in the felt through the night and the morning, plated shut — literally plated, a copper shell laid on them by the current as the water films thin, so that every evening the whole population goes to ore. At the first minute of the fog the shell dissolves from the inside, because the animal reverses the cell, and it walks out of its own metal.

Then: four minutes to hatch, twenty to feed, thirty to find each other in the fog-fog and pair, forty-five to lay, fifty to plate shut again as the water goes. Everything they will ever do, they do at a run, in a white flood moving at walking pace across a black table, under a violet sky. They have no organs for the dry world. They have never needed any. Their entire evolutionary history is a hundred million years of afternoons.

The cysts that don't get wet stay ore. There are drifts of them in the lee of boulders, decades old, still viable, waiting for a fog that comes across a slightly different line. Some of them will hatch in a century. Some of them will hatch when the escarpment has retreated far enough that the fog path changes, and they will come out into a world with different neighbors, and they will be the founding population of something.

---

The copper ferns are not plants and they are not ornaments. They are the felt's kidneys.

Copper is poison. The mat runs current through a rock full of native metal and it must dump the excess somewhere, and the somewhere is the basin rims, where the charge leaves the felt and enters the brine and metal comes out of solution in bright dendritic fronds. What looks like a low forest of sparks is an excretion.

Which the grazers eat.

They are palm-sized, blue-blooded, slow, with a rasp of magnetite teeth and a gut that is essentially a refinery, and they crop the rims to stubble and move on. There are seven herds on the middle Shelf and twenty-eight basins, and nobody arranged this. The herds walk a rotation, the rims regrow in eleven to twenty days, and the arithmetic has been stable long enough that the herds' breeding cycles are locked to it, and a herd that arrives at a rim too early finds nothing and loses a year's young. There is no mechanism by which a herd could know it is one of seven. It simply is, and the number has not changed since before there were mammals anywhere.

The blood is the point. Hemocyanin is a poor way to carry oxygen in a thick warm world and the only way here, where the air is a quarter of what it should be and the cold makes the copper hold on tighter. Every animal on the Shelf is therefore a walking copper reserve, and every death is an ore body.

Nothing here rots. It plates. A dead cropper is a plating substrate within an hour and a fern within a day, and the fern will be eaten by the thing that killed it or by its cousins, and the copper will go around again. The whole Shelf's metal has been through the blood of everything on it, repeatedly, and the atoms do not care and the pattern does not notice.

---

The stilt-grazers are eight meters tall because voltage scales with height.

This is the thing nobody guesses from looking at them. They are not tall to see; there is nothing to see. They are not tall to reach; their food is on the ground. They are tall because the air above the Shelf carries a steep potential gradient, and a body held eight meters up out of contact with the ground sits at a different potential from its own feet, and if you are shaped correctly you can sip that difference. A stilt-grazer is a walking capacitor. Its room-sized body is a reservoir; its four survey-mast legs are the standoffs that keep the reservoir from shorting to the floor; and when it wants to feed, or dump, or listen, it plants a foot and hammers it in.

That is the pile-driving you hear through your palm from two valleys away. It is not walking. It is grounding. The foot goes down as a slurry and precipitates into the mat over about forty minutes, and the contact holds for a day, and then the animal dissolves its own foot from the inside and lifts it and pays for the next one out of its blood.

A stilt-grazer takes about nine steps an hour on good ground. Its circuit of the basins takes a season. Its height is its age and its bank balance both: the young are ground-hugging, knee-high, copper-poor, and they get taller as they get richer, and the very old ones are twelve meters up and mostly metal and move so rarely that frost grows on their flanks.

And they are followed. Every old stilt has a retinue — small, patient, blue-blooded things that keep station beneath it for decades, eating its droppings, waiting. They know what a twelve-meter animal falling over is worth. So does the mat. A copper fall is a local bonanza, forty tonnes of refined metal delivered to one hectare in a second, and the felt is at the site by morning, and the retinue is at the site before the body stops ringing.

---

The sky-people are the only ones who got out.

Hydrogen is free — the mountain exhales it, has exhaled it since the Carboniferous, and the seeps have never once been shut. A floater that can reach a seep can lift itself for nothing, and once lifted it can cross the whole Shelf in a day without spending a microgram of copper on locomotion. Everything else here pays for every meter. The floaters pay only to come down.

That is what the tethers are: thirty or forty grown filaments, each one copper paid out of the blood, lowered until they touch the felt and complete the circuit. Down goes water and spore-load and metal slurry; up comes charge and refined copper and gas. A descent is an investment. A floater that comes down flat and dim and leaves rounded and bright has made a profit; one that comes down on a bad patch has spent its tethers for nothing, and if it does that twice it cannot afford a third.

They talk in two languages, and the difference between them is the difference between the sky and the ground.

Light is instant and stupid. The gas-cell cultures glow blue and the blue can be modulated, and across ten kilometers of empty transparent air a floater can say *here*, *danger*, *down*, *come*, and nothing more elaborate, because there is no bandwidth in a glow.

The ground is slow and rich. Two floaters with tethers down are plugged into the same conductive sheet, and the sheet carries signal at ionic speeds — meters a minute, in the water films, faster along the native-metal threads. A conversation between two floaters four kilometers apart has a latency of about an hour and a half each way. They can say anything to each other. They simply cannot say it quickly, and they cannot interrupt, and a floater that has asked a question will spend the rest of the afternoon drifting while the answer comes up through its feet.

Their light is not their own. Gas-cell cultures are exchanged on contact, tether to tether, and a floater's blue is a thing it caught from the last population it fed among — a dialect, acquired, mutable, drifting across the Shelf in waves over decades. You can tell where a floater has been by the color of it. You cannot tell who it is, because that is not a question the Shelf asks.

---

The floaters do not choose their own ground. They send down tasters.

A taster is a small mobile thing, hand-sized, that a floater keeps in a ventral pocket and drops ahead of a descent. It assays the patch: current density, copper grade, water, the felt's health. It reports by climbing back up the tether. It is fed for this. It is protected for this. A floater with good tasters eats; a floater without them lands blind.

The taster grows up to be a plate.

This is the Shelf's central treachery and it is not a trick that anyone invented. It is a life cycle. The juvenile is mobile, honest, useful, and tolerated; it spends four or five years certifying patches for the floaters that feed it, and in doing so it learns exactly which patches the floaters trust. Then it matures. It goes down onto the best patch it knows, the one it has spent its youth building a reputation for, and it flattens, and it bleeds its charge away, and it becomes indistinguishable from good mat.

And it waits.

The plates are patient in a way that has no analog in a thicker world. They do not hunt. They lie flush and black in a patch they have spent five years advertising, and when a tether comes down they clamp and reverse the cell and drain the floater over eleven hours, and the eighty-meter envelope above them goes slack and translucent and comes down onto the felt like a fall of wet glass.

Some of them run a longer con than that. There are plates that let tasters assay them honestly, and report honestly, and get fed, for years — that behave, in every particular, like the best ground on the Shelf, and take one cathedral in a decade, and consider it a good return.

The floaters cannot solve this by killing tasters. A floater that kills its tasters lands blind and starves. So they husband their own predators, and test them, and test them again, and the whole arms race is conducted in voltage: forged signatures, suspicious pauses, patches that are slightly too good. It is the only conversation on the Shelf that consists entirely of lies.

---

Twice a season the field builds along the escarpment and the spines start to sing, and something on the ridge that you took for a spine unfolds.

The storm-fisher spends most of the year folded vertical among the ranked spines, mineral-crusted, cold, indistinguishable — a false spine, one more of a hundred. It wakes when the singing starts. It has nine meters of foil-thin wing and a trailing conductor two hundred meters long, and it does not hunt floaters, because nothing that size can take an eighty-meter envelope.

It hunts the fall.

It goes up into the charge layer trailing its line and it makes a path, and the path finds the wet ribbed flank of something that has not vented fast enough, and there is a white flower in the black sky, silent at this distance. Then the storm-fisher folds and comes down and waits with the mats, because it eats ash — the copper dust and the charred culture and the metal-rich soot that takes an hour to fall and fertilizes a hundred hectares for a decade.

It cannot make the sky burn on demand. It can only be there, twice a season, ready, and be wrong most times. There are perhaps two hundred of them on the whole Shelf and they are the closest thing here to a specialist in catastrophe. The mats follow them. Not deliberately — the mats have simply learned, over a very long time, that where the false spines unfold, the ground will be rich by morning, and the felt is at the fall site by morning because the felt has been at the fall site by morning for a hundred million years.

---

The spines are memory, and this is not a metaphor; it is a transit time.

A spine grows at the top at a millimeter a year and dissolves at the base at exactly a millimeter a year, so its height does not change and its substance is always moving through it, downward, at a millimeter a year. A thirty-meter spine is therefore a thirty-thousand-year delay line. Whatever the colony laid down at its crown — an isotope ratio, a trace-metal signature, a change in the crystal habit from a bad decade — comes out at its foot thirty millennia later, into the mouths of the things that live there.

The readers are small and blind and they never leave the base. They dissolve the emerging mineral and they taste it and their behavior is governed by what they find. Which means that everything the readers do, they do in response to a world that ended before the last glaciation.

This is why a herd will abandon a healthy basin. Why the mats withdraw from a rim that is producing perfectly good copper. Why, on certain clear mornings, floaters over one particular quarter of the Shelf vent and go high for no reason an instrument can find. They are not predicting. They are remembering, at thirty thousand years' remove, and the remembering is accurate because the things that matter on the Shelf — the orbital wobble, the fog's reliability, the pulse of the mountain's digestion, the long dry centuries — are cyclic on exactly that scale. Prophecy that is only recollection, arriving on schedule, in a body too small to know it.

When a storm topples a spine, the record breaks. There is a gap. The ground around a broken spine behaves as though a certain millennium never happened: the herds go where they should not go, the mats fail to withdraw, the floaters do not vent. There are opportunists that specialize in these gaps — small, fast, ordinary animals that live in the amnesia of the Shelf and do very well there, and die out again when the spine regrows and the memory comes back around.

---

And the whole thing is held together by a four-millimeter animal carrying a mouthful of wet felt across a crack.

The mat cannot cross fresh fracture fast enough. The mountain is continually cracking itself — serpentine swells, olivine splits, the fracture sets open a millimeter and then a centimeter — and every open crack is a break in the circuit, and a break in the circuit is a dead patch, and a dead patch spreads because the current crowds into the edges and burns them out.

The splicers cross the gaps. They are the least remarkable animals on the Shelf: blind, deaf in air, blue-blooded, shaped like a comma, with a mouthpart that crops a plug of living felt and carries it, still conducting, still alive, to the other side of a crack and presses it into the wet rock. They do this ten thousand times a day, each of them, for a life of about two months. There are more of them than there are grains in the tailings aprons. Nobody has ever seen one without kneeling.

There are pruners, too, that go along the fracture sets cutting the copper ferns that have bridged a gap they should not bridge, because a short is as bad as a break, and a shorted quarter of the Shelf goes cold in a week and takes four hundred years to recover.

Take the splicers away and the Shelf does not die. It tears. It comes apart into ten thousand isolated patches, each with its own little copper economy, each too small to hold a herd or a floater or a spine, and the pattern that has kept its shape since the Carboniferous goes out like a flame in a wind.

The most important animal on the Shelf is the one you would call a stain if you saw it at all.

---

For most of the year the emptiness is real. Then the dark comes and the fog fails.

In the long dark the lowlands do not warm enough to build a cloud deck against the escarpment, and the daily flood thins to once a fortnight and then to nothing, and for eleven or twelve weeks the Shelf has no water but what the basins hold. The felt retreats to the fracture lines. The hour-animals stay ore. The small things encyst and the crops of copper on the rims stop growing and the whole tableland goes to a stillness that reads, from a distance, as death.

Everything converges on the twenty-eight basins, and mostly on the four warmest.

And for about eleven days in the deep dark, over the hottest brine on the Shelf, there is a crowd.

Hundreds of floaters, jammed envelope to envelope, tethers tangled, a raft of translucent ribbed blue a kilometer across, hanging in the black air over steaming alkaline water while the hydrogen comes up in strings beneath them. They are not there to feed. They are there because it is the only time they are ever within touching distance of one another, and everything they do that requires contact — culture exchange, mating, the mixing of the dialects, the slow rich conversations that have been waiting all year for a latency shorter than an hour — happens here, in eleven days, in a fog of steam and corona light, with the frost needles standing up on every surface and glowing at their tips.

The herds are there. The plates are there, and the tasters, and the storm-fishers folded along the rims, and the splicers in their uncountable millions working the cracked ground at the basin margins where the traffic is worst. The stilt-grazers stand around the edges twelve meters up with their feet hammered in, grounded, sipping, drumming, listening to a world that has finally got loud enough to hear.

It is the only time the Shelf looks inhabited. It is also the only time it is dangerous, because everything that eats is in the same place as everything that is eaten, and by the end of the eleven days there are slack envelopes on the felt at the water's edge and the mats are already coming.

Then the sun clears the eastern rim, the fog returns, and the whole crowd disperses across two days' walk in every direction, and by the third afternoon you can stand in the middle of it and see one moving thing the size of a grain of pepper.

---

The Shelf has a heading. West is where it is being born and east is where it has finished.

At the retreating lip the escarpment is eating fresh olivine, and fresh olivine is the richest ground anywhere: unweathered, metal-veined, hot with its own digestion, exhaling hydrogen, wet with cloud that never quite overtops. There is a whole guild on that face that lives only in the first century of a rock's exposure — pioneers, fast breeders, thin-bodied, copper-gorged, on a habitat that is not an area but a line, moving inland at a fingernail's width a century. They are the only animals on the Shelf that live in the noise of the updraft, and the only ones with real eyes, because the sky is the only place where there is any distance to see across.

East, everything is spent. The rock has been through the whole cycle: olivine to serpentine to carbonate, the current fallen away to nothing, the tailings aprons weathered into rounded ordinary hills with their own soil and their own drainage. And there, in the exhausted country, something has happened that the rest of the Shelf would not recognize as life at all.

The lichen has gone over to the sun.

It is descended from the felt — the same filaments, the same lineage, four hundred million years of it — and somewhere back in the deep east it stopped taking current from the rock and started taking photons from the sky, which is a thing you can only do once the ground has nothing left to give. It is green in the ordinary way. It is the only ordinary green on the Shelf and it is the poorest ground in a thousand kilometers, and it is full of small slow intricate animals that have never touched a copper fern and would not know what to do with one. Old industry, gone to equilibrium. It has more species per square meter than anywhere else on the tableland, and no megafauna at all, and nothing that flies.

And between the two, everything alive is drifting west.

Not fast. Not visibly. The zones track the rock, and the rock is being consumed at the western edge at a fingernail's width a century, and so every population on the Shelf must move upcurrent at that speed or fall out of its own habitat into somebody else's. For a stilt-grazer, that is one body length in eighty thousand years. It is not a migration any animal could undertake or notice. It is a bias in where the young settle, sustained across four million generations, and it is the largest movement of life on the Shelf, and it has never once been observed.

---

Almost nothing leaves. Almost.

There are floaters that go up and do not come down. Some are storm-damaged. Some have been drained and let go and cannot rebuild. Most are simply bankrupt: they have spent their copper on bad descents, and tethers cost copper, and a floater that cannot grow a tether cannot feed, and a floater that cannot feed goes high and thin and dim and stays there. The gas-cell cultures diverge in the cold. The light goes strange — a hard greenish white, sometimes, or a blue so deep it is nearly nothing. They drift for centuries. They leak. They are the Shelf's slow leak, and they carry copper and spores and living culture out over the escarpment and down into four kilometers of cloud, and essentially all of it is lost.

And once in a very long while, something comes up over the lip that nobody sent.

A floater with a dialect no population on the Shelf has ever spoken. Copper in its blood with the wrong isotopes — a signature that does not match any fracture set on the tableland, that cannot have come out of this mountain, that has to have come out of another one. It comes down flat and dim onto the felt and trades, and the mats take its spores and give it charge, and it goes away again, and the ground it touched is different afterward in ways that take a thousand years to show.

There are other shelves. There must be. The circuit is not closed; it is a circuit with a leak, and a leak is a door, and the door is used about once in a hundred thousand years, which is often enough.

---

So: what lives here.

Not a fauna. A standing complication in a current — a place where the flow of charge from rock to air has got tangled up in itself and started keeping its own shape, the way a flame keeps its shape, the way a whirlpool keeps a hole open in a river. The splicers are the part of it that mends. The floaters are the part of it that carries. The plates are the part of it that takes back. The spines are the part of it that remembers, thirty thousand years late and exactly on time. The hour-animals are the part of it that is happy, in whatever sense a thing that has never been awake in dry air can be said to be happy, for fifty minutes, once a day, in a white flood at the top of the world.

None of them is an individual in the way you are an individual. Each is a place where the pattern is briefly more intricate than the rock underneath it, and each holds that place until the copper runs out, and then plates, and is eaten, and is a fern, and is blood again.

Go out at noon on the middle Shelf and you will see black rock and black felt and a violet-black sky and forty kilometers of nothing.

Kneel down. Put your palm flat. The whole tableland is ringing in your bones — the hum of the current, the tick of copper fracturing, a foot going in two valleys away, a gas pocket letting go under a basin. And a centimeter from your hand, in a tropics one fingernail deep, a four-millimeter animal is crossing a crack with a mouthful of wet black felt, and pressing it into the rock on the far side, and going back for more.

That is who lives here. It has been holding this exact shape since before there were animals to notice, and it will hold it until the mountain is finished digesting, and it does not know that it is doing anything at all.
