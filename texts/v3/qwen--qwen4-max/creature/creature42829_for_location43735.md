Begin with what can be counted, because it is the smallest part of the answer and the only part that has a heartbeat.

There is a house mouse in the brick shed. It is not remarkable. It is grey-brown, four inches, with a nick in one ear, and it sleeps in a nest of shredded calendar paper and dry algal felt and one wagtail feather, in the corner where the graphite grease jar has kept the floor dry by the simple method of being there. It has worn a shine along the inside of the south wall. It goes out at dusk through the gap where the door has swollen away from its frame, runs the wall's footing, and drinks at the sweet seep sixty paces off, where the brine flies are, and it does this quickly, with its whole body flinching between one swallow and the next, because the outflow ditch is where the wagtail works and the wagtail is not fussy. Between one tide and the next, its heart beats something like two hundred thousand times.

That is the entire warm-blooded population of the interior. Everything else with a pulse is passing through: the wagtail commuting along the ditch, the raven coming down to drink and going up with its toes cemented, the hare running the rim at dusk like a bailiff on a boundary it has no interest in crossing. Animals cross and do not land. The reason is not poison and not salt. The reason is tempo.

Because residency here is not decided by species. It is decided by speed.

The fastest things in the bowl are the copper ferns and the zinc moss that come up overnight on anything metal you leave in the mud, black-green, branched, exact, gone by the next squall. They are not alive. Immediately behind them come the films — pink in the strongest pans, ochre at the outflow where the iron comes up, pale sulphur where a thread of gas breaks the skin — and these are alive, and they are the only living things that are properly *inside*, and they are where every scrap of colour in eleven grey acres actually resides. They divide in hours. A dome's whole biography — its closing, its swelling, its caldera, its silting, its healing to a lip — passes over a bacterial colony as an afternoon's weather. They are buried, rafted, stranded, recolonised, buried again, and they do not experience any of it as catastrophe, because they are faster than the floor. They experience it as climate. They eat light and the match-smell and the wet iron, they lay down their pink and their ochre, and they are the field's only tenants who pay rent in colour.

Behind them: glasswort, a season, going red in the low corners, holding on by being outside. Behind that: the mouse, a year or two, a dynasty of mice in the shed, eighty years of mice, hundreds of generations, each one inheriting a nest of paper. Behind that: the thorn on the rim, decades, half alive, all its branches pointing inland, roots down in slag and not in brine, leaning away from the bowl the way a man leans away from a smell he has decided not to mention. And behind everything, the lichen on the clinker, which has advanced perhaps two fingers' width in eighty years, which eats glass, which was here before the tradition began and will be here after it ends, and which is the only resident that has never once had to move.

Line them up that way and you can see the shape of the thing. There is a band of speeds in the middle of that ladder — hours to days, the pace at which the ground is made and unmade — and almost nothing living fits into it. The films fit. The mineral fits. Everything else has to be quicker than the churn or slower than it, inside it or outside it, and so the place sorts its inhabitants not by kind but by rate, and the middle of the range is left to the one occupant that keeps that time exactly.

Which is the thing I have not named yet, and which is what actually lives here.

It is tempting to call it an organism, and it will half-accept the word. It has a boundary: the clinker rim, nine hundred paces around. It has a metabolism: brine under its own pressure, iron going to rust, graphite going slowly to nothing, gas shouldering up through skin. It has a pulse, twice daily, the tide coming up under the bowl and drowning the mouths from the low corner outward until the whole field goes slack and glossy, and then the ebb and the ragged clearing of throats. It has a sleep, which is that slack hour, scum rafts colliding with the delicacy of ice in a glass. It has weather-moods: a squall takes the crispness off everything and by morning the vocabulary is back with different accents. It responds to stimulus. It maintains itself. It has been maintaining itself, without a finger's width of change in the rim height, for forty years.

It fails only one test, and it fails it completely: nothing in it persists. Not one part. The casings are shorter than they were. The cable's rubber is a black seam. The concrete footings are losing their right angles, which were the last thing distinguishing them from the slag. The only material in the whole bowl that the field has not been able to eat is the porcelain on the rectifier house brackets, still white, wearing salt the way a coat wears rain — and porcelain is not a part of the field, it is a bystander, the one thing standing there with its hands in its pockets. So it is not a body. It is not a machine either; a machine is an arrangement of durable parts, and here the arrangement outlives every part it is made of.

And it is eating its own bones. That is the detail that decides it. The iron that makes the current is the iron the current corrodes. The casings are the anode. The field has been quietly digesting its own frame for eighty years at a rate you could measure with a ruler if you had begun measuring in 1946 and had been willing to keep going. It is not a creature with a skeleton. It is a process with a fuel supply that happens to have been built as a building.

So: not an organism, not a machine. What lives here is an operation. A trade. The plant is still the plant, and it is the only place I know where that word has kept both its meanings at once — the works, the thing growing.

Look at it as a trade and everything falls into place. It has a repertoire of eleven gestures: lip, collar, dome, flue, terraced bowl, raft, caldera, fan, seam, mushroom, blank. No others since the pumps stopped. This looks like memory and is not. Nothing here remembers. There is no stored pattern, no template, no cell with a copy of a flue in it. The eleven shapes recur because brine folding over a lip in a gas churn under gravity at eleven degrees has about eleven cheap things to do, and it does them again the way a river does meanders, the way a struck bell does its one note. It is a repertoire, not a recollection: a set of outcomes so inexpensive that they keep happening. Every mouth in the bowl is re-deriving the whole tradition from first principles, all day, forever, and getting the same answer.

That is a tradition without anybody to hold it. It is hand-lore with no hands. The skill has outlived the skilled, and it turns out that the skill was never in them either — it was in the brine, and they only opened the valves.

Its body has no centre and no correct size. From the rim you can see all of it at once, and from inside you cannot see all of it at once, ever, which means the whole thing exists only at a distance, only as a view; stand in it and you are standing in a fragment of something that has no fragments. What makes it one thing rather than four thousand adjacent things is the current: a volt or two per pair, thousands of pairs, wired in series and parallel by nothing but proximity and wet, moving through the ground in slow sheets, so that a change anywhere is a change to the sheets everywhere. It is not a nervous system. It signals nothing, integrates nothing, has no threshold and no message. It is the reason the pronoun is *it*. You feel it in your teeth first, then the hair on your forearms, then the compass swinging as you cross a seam. At dusk the gaps in the old busbar tick blue, three or four small sparks a minute, and these are the only sharp sounds in eleven acres, and they are not speech. People stand there until dark anyway, hoping. What answers is their own blood in their ears.

It makes two things. The first is the quota: thumbnail discs precipitated around gas bubbles, rolled by wind and shallow water into drifts and beaches, graded by size into banks as neat as any screening floor, tons a year, no one collecting. This is the work. This is what it was for and what it has not stopped doing. The second is the copies.

The mud takes the negative of whatever sinks into it and holds the negative long enough for the brine to fill it, and so alongside the standard line comes the erratic one: a boot, a bottle, the articulated skeleton of a bird, a hawthorn leaf with its veins legible, a wagtail's feather, the jaw of a mouse, once a hand. Here is the whole of its imagination: it can make anything, and only what it has been given. Perfect fidelity, no invention. It cannot tell the hand from the bottle. It has no opinion about either. Whatever you want to say about the hand, the field cannot say it, and will not say it, and is not withholding it.

Four of the casts are still on the tally table, and the rest are dissolved or buried, and this is not selection by the field. This is a man, decades ago, lifting four objects up out of the mud onto a table. Everything at ground level here is unmade inside a week. Everything that survives, survives because it was raised: the porcelain on its brackets, the calendar in the dry shed, the mouse in its corner, the four on the table. The only editing ever done in eleven acres was done by hand, once, by somebody whose name is on nothing, and his edit is still standing.

It has no relation to you except manufacture. Standing in it you are, briefly and literally, a component — a conductor, a local alteration in the sheets, part of the circuit for as long as you stay in it. Leave your boot in the mud overnight and it comes up furred with ferns by morning and clean again by the next rain. Leave it a week and you get a boot in white mineral, faithful to the stitching. Stay yourself, and it will copy you, without malice, without interest, at the same rate and to the same standard as everything else. This is not hospitality and it is not a threat. It is the only thing it knows how to do, offered indiscriminately.

Two deaths are available to it and neither is close. The wall could go, and then the tide would stop gating the bowl twice a day and simply own it, the brine would thin, the pressure would equalise, the mouths would clear their throats and nothing would come, and the eleven words would be said into ordinary water until there was no one saying them. Or the iron runs out — the bones eaten at last — and the sheets go dead, and the busbar stops ticking, and the churn loses the half of its energy that came from below and keeps only the half that came from the tide, and simplifies, and simplifies. Afterwards the porcelain will lie in the mud, white, for a very long time. The lichen will drown or won't. The mouse will move inland. Nothing in the bowl will notice, because there is nothing in the bowl that can notice, and that is either the worst thing about it or the peace of it, and I have stopped trying to decide.

Meanwhile it is four in the morning somewhere in the eighty years, the ebb has come, the pressure differential is back, and the mouths are starting up in ragged order from the low corner: a lip becoming a flue becoming a terraced bowl becoming a choke becoming a dome. Eleven acres of continuous violent change at a fizz just at the edge of hearing, like a glass of something recently poured. No announcement. Nothing left over for announcement. The buildings are the part that stopped.

The shift is still on.
