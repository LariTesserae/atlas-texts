Nothing lives in the tankhouse. That is true, and it is the least useful true thing about the place, because the room is not empty and it is not idle and it is not finished. Something is in there, doing something, continuously, at a rate you could measure if you had the patience. The difficulty is that it has no body in the sense you want, no face, no place where it is more itself than elsewhere, and every object you would point at as the resident is only its furniture.

Start with what is nearly alive, at the edges, because they tell you where the edge is.

---

The moss in the collar is a tenant with a short lease. It sits in the one square meter where the surface seep keeps the copper below the threshold, and it lives the way moss lives anywhere — slowly, wetly, without ambition — except that it is pressed against a ceiling it cannot cross. Below it the water is a biocide. It has no idea. It has no way to have an idea. It grows outward at the rate moss grows and dies back at the rate the seep slackens, and in a dry century its margin retreats a hand's width toward the shaft, and in a wet one it advances again, and this is the only gardening that has ever been done here.

The three curtains of juniper root are not tenants. They are fingers put into a room and taken out. Above the invisible plane they are alive and pale and doing the ordinary work of a root in the dark; twenty centimeters down they are brown and blunt and have been dead for longer than the tree above has been alive, because the tree keeps sending them and they keep stopping. The tree is not learning. Trees do not learn this. Every year a little more root, every year the same wall, and the wall is not stone, it is a concentration, and it does not care that it is being touched.

The bacterial felt on the ore crust is the only thing in the room that is genuinely alive and genuinely at home, and it is a scab. A film of something wet on a surface where the water is briefly less hostile, thin enough to be a discoloration, not grazing, not grazed. It is not the resident. It is a lichen on the resident. It is living on the outside of an animal the way bacteria live on your skin, and like your skin bacteria it has no opinion about the animal and could not describe it.

The horizon those roots stop at is the most important thing in the chamber and you cannot see it. That plane is the skin. Above it: the world, ordinary, breathable, rooted, green. Below it: one thing, uninterrupted, six hundred square meters of it, sterile for four hundred thousand years. The room's real ceiling is not the arch. It is that plane, and it moves — a few centimeters up in a drought, a few down in a wet millennium — and that slow tidal breathing is the only gesture the resident has ever made.

---

Here is how you find out what lives here.

Put a lid on the pool. Seal the shaft, cut off the whisper of oxygen coming down from the sky, and wait.

Nothing in the room moves. Not one object changes its address. The ore stays half-buried in the wall; the forty trees stand at their stations; the leaves lie in their fan; the mud lies flat; the graphite runs its clean corners into the rock. You could photograph the chamber before and after and the two photographs would be identical, and something would nevertheless be gone, and it would never come back until you took the lid off.

That is the resident. It is the only thing in the room you could remove without moving anything.

It is not the ore, which is its food. It is not the water, which is its blood and its solvent and its lung. It is not the trees, which are its skeleton and its leaves and its litter. It is the reaction — the ongoing oxidation of sulfide by sky, routed through stone, spending itself on limestone and paying out metal — and it is located, precisely, in a film.

The film is the boundary layer: the millimeter, or less, of water immediately against the anode face and against every copper surface, where the chemistry has not yet been averaged away by the room. Outside that film everything is identical to everything, corner to corner. Inside it there are cliffs. Oxygen falls from a whisper to nothing across a distance you could span with a eyelash. Acidity climbs. Potential drops. Copper is stripped out of the water at one wall and laid onto another. It is a landscape of escarpments and deltas and it is the steepest terrain in the massif, and it is thinner than a fingernail, and it is the entire place where anything happens.

Ten square meters of it, perhaps. Half a millimeter thick. Five liters.

The animal in this room is about the volume of a jug, spread over six hundred square meters of stone, and it has never been more than a millimeter from a wall, and it is older than every species of animal you have ever heard of.

---

Its whole metabolic rate is eighty milliwatts. Four tenths of a volt, two hundred milliamps; that is the entire budget, a thousandth of a candle. It has run on that for four hundred thousand years and in all that time it has released about as much energy as you would get from burning thirty tonnes of coal, and none of it was ever warm anywhere. The mountain takes the heat as it is made and does not comment. There has never been a fever here, never a hot spot, never a degree of difference between one end of the body and the other.

Most of what it does is nothing. This is the part the industrial reading gets wrong. The current is largely carried by iron, turning over and over — reduced at one place, oxidized at another, going round like a flywheel with no belt on it — and that loop makes no trees and moves no metal that stays. The copper work is a leakage. A rounding error in its own accounts. The trees you can see, the entire visible spectacle of the place, are what a tiny fraction of its labor looks like when it escapes the cycle.

What it eats: sulfide, and oxygen, and — because the acid it makes has to go somewhere — the limestone itself. It eats the mountain twice, once for the ore and once for the walls.

What it breathes out: carbon dioxide, one bead at a time, grown on the wall under the ore over the better part of an hour, released with a small ceramic click, traveling the low arch and out through the coin of sky. That is the heartbeat, and it is also the only speech. Something on the order of two hundred billion bubbles so far. Each one carries away a few micrograms of the bench above your head.

What it excretes: dissolved mountain, calcium and sulfate, leaving quietly at the seep in the pool's lip.

Everything it exports is the mountain, taken apart. Everything it makes, it keeps. It has never shipped anything anywhere because it is the product. The room is the negative of the animal: the chamber is all subtraction, dissolved, rounded, scooped, pocked like the inside of a mouth, and the trees are the only addition in it, the only sharp angles, the only things that grew outward instead of being eaten inward. The nave and the candelabra are one event, seen from its two ends.

---

The trees are not individuals. They are leaves.

A dendrite takes perhaps fifty thousand years to become a two-meter candelabrum weighing what a cat weighs, thin as a wrist, holding eight kilos of copper it has taken out of the water one atom at a time. It grows toward nothing. It has no direction, no light to lean into, no preference; it branches at sixty degrees because that is what the field and the lattice do, and it lengthens, and eventually a hair of it touches a graphite seam.

Then the circuit shorts through the contact, and the contact burns off, and the branch falls.

This is the whole of its behavior and it is worth being exact about: its success condition and its failure condition are the same condition. The thing it makes is the thing that stops it making. If a tree ever finished — if copper ever bridged the room and stayed bridged — the cell would short completely and go quiet and never restart. It survives by never completing itself. It grows the instrument of its own pruning, at the exact rate at which it grows, and so the number forty is not a number anyone chose and not a number anything defends; it is what falls out of the arithmetic when deposition and shorting are matched.

Somewhere in the chamber, on average, a branch lets go about once in a thousand years. That is the schedule of events. The last one fell after the ice, after farming, after people had begun to write things down; there was no one to hear it land, and it made no sound worth the name, and it lies on the flat floor now with the leaves and will lie there for twenty or thirty thousand years, redissolving, going back into the water to be a tree again.

The leaves it cannot use. It has no mouth for carbon, no way to open them. They arrive one at a time down the shaft, whole, tanned, intact, and settle in the fan, and lie exactly where they settled, unread, for longer than there have been languages. A drift of offerings at the foot of something that has no hands.

---

It has no inside. There is no attention in the film, no preference, no error signal, nothing that could be disappointed. It does not defend its shape; its shape is held by cancellation, the way a river holds its grade, and there is no homeostat anywhere, only two rates that happen to be equal. Take away the wanting and everything else on the list is still there: a boundary, a metabolism, a diet, a waste stream, a pulse, a growth form, a litter, a census, a lifespan, an individual history. It is a self in every sense except the one we care about most.

The nearest honest relatives are not organisms. A candle flame holds its shape for hours, eats and breathes and excretes, has a boundary you can put your finger through, is a single thing you can point at, and is not alive and was not made. A whirlpool keeps its funnel while every drop of water in it leaves. A hydraulic jump stands in one place in a river for a century while the river goes through it. This is that kind of thing, run at eighty milliwatts, in the dark, on rock, for four hundred thousand years.

It has no ancestry and no descendants. It is not a species. It is a thing that can happen wherever a sulfide body, a shaft, a whisper of oxygen and a wet conductive rock happen to be arranged the same way, and so it is less like an animal than like a knot — a recurrent habit of wet mountain, tied here once, in this particular arrangement, with these forty trees at these addresses and these three curtains of root and this fan of leaves. The graphite runs into the wall at both ends and there may be other rooms along it, other cells, siblings by geology and strangers in every way that matters, each alone in its own water, none of them aware that the others exist, because there is no such thing here as aware.

Its traffic is invisible and it is the fastest thing in the place. A copper ion leaves the anode and drifts along a field of a few hundredths of a volt per meter, and it takes about thirty years to cross one meter of open water, and six hundred to reach a tree on the far wall. That journey is going on now, in every direction, in absolute silence, through water so clear it does not look like water. Nothing about it can be seen. It is the entire motion of the resident's life.

---

It is middle-aged. The wedge in the wall is the size of a delivery van and there is only so much copper in a delivery van; at a few grams a year of work that stays, it has perhaps as long left as it has already had. Or the bench above will thin, and the lip will lower, and the pool will drain, and the oxygen will come all the way down, or none at all. Whichever comes first.

When it goes, it will not look like a death. The current will fade over centuries; the horizon will rise toward the ceiling and quietly stop existing; the roots will come down past twenty centimeters and keep coming; the moss will spread off its one square meter and take the collar, and then the floor. Something will arrive that can swim. Then something that can burrow. The leaves will begin to rot, which is the same as saying they will begin to be eaten, and the water will lose its absolute clarity, because life makes motes, and the far wall will stop being simply *there*. The trees will stand a while longer, thinning, and then sag, and then be gone, and the graphite will still run its clean corners into the rock, carrying nothing.

The room will be inherited by everything that is not it, and the first sign of the successors will be that you can no longer see across the chamber.

Until then the shift runs. And if you bring a lamp down and hold it close to a dendrite, the bronze-black flushes salmon-pink, suddenly and indecently, the true color of the metal, which water eats before it can cross the room. It has never had that color. It has no light and no use for light; the day comes on and goes off above it and means nothing. The pink is not a fact about the resident. It is a fact about visitors, and it exists only while one is there, and it is the single thing in the whole chamber that requires you.
