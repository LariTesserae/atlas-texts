Begin with the swelling, not the walker.

In the warm places on the eastern slope, where the wall runs three degrees above the air, a nursery is a stretch of face that has been hurt on purpose in a particular pattern. The pattern is the whole art. Score it one way and the wall lays down a rib; score it another and it lays down a knot that swells, and hollows, and fills with a fibrous white flesh, and after eleven or fourteen years has a shape that can be lifted off.

It is not lifted off. It comes away. There is a difference, and the oldest word for a person here means *the part that comes away*.

Before that, the bud spends a decade attached, fed by the wall, unable to move, and entirely awake. Its listening pits open in the second year. It hears the drills and the brooms and the trunk lines and the drone through a hand's breadth of living tissue, and it hears the attendants who come and sit against it and tap and talk. This is the schooling. There is no other kind. A bud learns the chords, the tap-code, the names of the levels, the nine hundred year dust cycle, and the correct way to answer an elder, all while being a bulge in a wall.

Everyone here remembers being architecture. It is the first memory anyone has and it is never quite lost, and it explains a great deal about what happens at the other end of a life.

Detachment is done in the in-draw, when the pressure is high and the wall is turgid and the cords are sharp, so births come in a two-year flush every eleven years and nobody is born in the slack. A person's age is the number of breaths they have lived through: twelve is old, fifteen is remarkable. You are *of* a breath the way other peoples are of a generation, and you will know every member of your cohort by their opening chord, and you will vote with them, and marry across from them, and be buried in the same district as them, and the whole city's politics moves in eleven-year steps like something walking up stairs.

The first thing a new person does, standing on limbs that have never borne anything, is click. And the room comes back at them: the ceiling ribs, the sweepers, the doorway, the exact distance to the nearest grandmother. They have spent fourteen years hearing about this. It is still, by every account that has been tapped into the cords, overwhelming.

---

The bodies are not beautiful and are not ugly, because those are not available concepts. They are *well-made* or *badly scored*, which is a judgment about the hand that wounded the nursery wall, and it is audible.

Four to six walking limbs, two held free. The count is set by the warmth of the nursery and the spacing of the cuts, so it is partly fate and partly design, and there is an etiquette about it that visitors would find exhausting. Six-limbed people are steady on the dunes and are thought dependable and slightly dull. Four-limbed people are fast in the upper lattice and are thought quick and slightly untrustworthy. Both opinions are wrong and both are held by everyone, including the people they are about.

Bark-cuticle over fibrous flesh; a hollow thorax like a gourd; across the chest the dry reeds, seven to eleven of them, through which air is pushed to speak. They do not speak in a line. They speak in chords. A sentence is a stack of simultaneous tones with the meaning distributed across them, and a person's baseline chord — the sound of their reeds at rest, breathing — is their name, their face, their signature, and the thing their friends can produce from memory at will.

You cannot fake a reed-set. You can learn to mute a reed, which is a difficult and disreputable skill, and the word for a liar is *one who has taken a note out*.

Thought is faint clicking. Not always, but often enough that a person working through a problem alone in a hallway is quietly ticking to themselves, and anyone within four paces can hear the shape of it. Privacy here is a distance, not a wall. Intimacy is being close enough to hear someone think and having the manners not to answer. Lovers sleep with their thoraxes touching so the reeds sympathise, which is a thing they will admit to only under direct questioning, and which every nursery attendant knows about, because the buds notice.

Sleeping people stop clicking, and a sleeping person is therefore a hole in the room — an absence with no return, a place where the click goes and does not come back. You do not walk past a sleeper without humming. It is the most ordinary courtesy in the city and children are corrected on it a hundred times.

The pits foul. This is the great illness: ceiling-flake worked into the membranes over decades, a slow narrowing of the world, the echo coming back thinner each year until the halls lose their edges and a person must be led. It is called the muffling. There are pickers who clean pits, delicately, with a thorn awl and a great deal of oil, and they are paid like surgeons. A person with clean pits at a hundred and forty is smug about it in a way that is considered forgivable.

And they read grain. The mass lays down four hundred years in a thumbnail, and a cut face polished with dust and oil shows that layering to a fingertip as a texture, a rhythm, a grain. A trained reader puts a palm on an exposed cut and tells you which century the wall was young in, where a drought of iron slowed it, where it folded. The libraries of this city are walls with a patch kept clean.

---

They eat what the wall has finished with.

This is the part that other worlds get wrong, and the distinction is the whole of their morality. Nothing here eats a living thing. What is harvested at the eating-faces is the rind of old scar: the mass wounds, heals, lignifies, and the outermost layers die into a hard pale tissue that is no more alive than a fingernail. That is the food. It is rasped off with a stone scraper and worked in the crop with oil for two or three hours, and it tastes, they say, of the year it was laid down.

So: to eat, someone must first have hurt the wall, and then waited twenty years.

Every meal is a letter from the dead. The face being worked today was scored by a woman who has been a column since before your grandmother was detached, and she scored it knowing that she would not eat from it, and she was not being noble; she was doing Tuesday's work. The debt runs the other way too. A person who scores a face is feeding people who do not exist and will not exist for two breaths. There is no word for *harvest* that does not also mean *repayment*.

The warm patches are where the mass's own rock-work comes near the surface, and the pale mineral dust of its waste is there, and the oil seeps at the conduits nearby. That is the table. People gather at warmth because warmth is where the parent is eating, and to sit in a three-degree patch with your thorax against the wall and take your oil is the closest thing this world has to a hearth, a home, and a sacrament, and they do not distinguish these.

Oil is the only thing here that is conserved. It does not evaporate; it does not diminish; a body's oil at death is exactly the oil it was filled with at detachment, faintly altered by that throat. So it is decanted. A stiffening elder's oil goes into a bud of their own lineage, and a family's oil is nine hundred years old and has been through forty throats and is recognisably itself, and to be given a stranger's oil is a formal adoption, and to have your oil poured into the dunes is the second-worst thing that can be done to you.

Politics is the ratio of grazing to healing. Wound too little and the scar-rind thins and people go hungry in a city with eleven miles of empty floor. Wound too much and a face goes *scar-tired* — stops answering the score, heals flat, gives nothing back — and there is no remedy but to leave it alone for two hundred years. There are tired faces on the eastern slope that were over-worked in a famous famine and are still, by the load-book's reckoning, resting. The argument between the deep-scorers and the keepers has been running for six hundred years in one form or another and has produced three riots, one schism, and a very large amount of excellent poetry.

---

Children are made by wounding a wall in a pattern, and the pattern is composed.

The number of reeds, their lengths, the spacing of the limb-buds, the depth of the thorax: all of it follows from the cuts. A family has a chord — a hereditary interval structure that a scorer reproduces with variations, so that you can hear a lineage across a hall the way other peoples hear a nose. Two people who want a child must agree on a wound. The agreement is the marriage. There is no ceremony beyond the cutting, and no divorce, because the cut is in the wall and the wall does not retract.

And the grain remembers the hand. A hesitant score lays down differently from a confident one; a cut made in anger has a burr that never quite heals out. A grain-reader can put a finger on a person's natal scar, still preserved in the nursery, and tell you what their parents were feeling on the day they were agreed to. Some people go and read their own. Most do not. There is a whole genre of cord-poetry about the ones who did.

Against this, the quietists score at random — a blind pattern, thrown dice, the wall decides — and their children come out one-noted and odd-limbed and are, by the standards of the great houses, ruined. The quietists say that a person is not an instrument to be tuned before they exist. The great houses say that this is easy to say when your children will not be able to hold a trunk-line job. Both parties send their children to the same nursery schools, because the schools are held up by the same columns, and the columns do not care.

---

Nothing is finished by the person who begins it.

A mastic-maker mixes oil and dust at twenty and uses the batch at thirty. A wall-wounder signs the wound, not the beam; the signature is a small pattern cut beside the score, and it is legible for as long as the wall stands, and it means *I did this part, and I did not see what it became*. The tools are inherited mid-job. There are drills in the upper lattice that have been making the same hole, in relays, for four generations, and the hole is now a room.

The virtue word is *kept*. The sin word is *slack*. The oath, sworn at detachment and repeated at every guild admission and at every placement, translates roughly as *I will keep it thickening*.

Their art is rooms.

A room is good when it answers well: when the click goes out and comes back with the true shape of the space, cleanly, with a decay particular to that hall, so that standing in it you know exactly where you are and how large the world is and, if the architect was very great, something about who you are. The masterworks of this civilisation are chambers. People travel two hours across the dunes to stand in a hall for a quarter of an hour, click once, and go home changed.

There is a counter-tradition of false rooms — built to return shapes that are not there, a stair that isn't, a person who isn't, a ceiling at the wrong height. Children's toys are little click-boxes with impossible interiors. The conservatives consider the whole genre a corruption, on the grounds that a room that lies is a small felted prison, and the argument has been going since before the Ninth Terrace was cut.

And everything is written twice. During the in-draw the whole chamber rises a fifth, and every chord transposes, and a name that fits in the slack does not fit in the taut. So there are two literatures, and two manners of address, and a person has a slack-name and a taut-name and is slightly different people in each. Songs composed for the intake are stored for nine years and performed only when the world can hold them. There are poets who have written four works in a life and consider themselves prolific.

---

The stiffening is not a tragedy. It is a career.

Lignin lays down in the joints over the last third of a life, and the flex goes, and the limbs set. When it begins, a person walks to the works and is assessed, and a socket is chosen, and they are set with their feet in it and their hands raised to the load, and over forty or sixty years they become the column.

Placement is the most consequential thing that happens to anyone here. The good halls are held up by their own dead: a lineage's prestige is literally its structural capacity, and a declining family's roof sags because there is nobody left to be the roof. There are brokers who arrange placements in exchange for maintenance guarantees, and there are people who have sold their last sixty years to a house they dislike, and there is a reform movement, and it has failed four times.

The pits close last. Some columns stay responsive for a century. You can tap a load-bearing grandmother on the shin and ask whether the beam above her has cracked and she will answer, and she will be right, and the survey will be amended.

This is regulated. There is an office of askers, licensed, rationed to a number of taps per column per day, because a person who cannot move also cannot decline, and being asked constantly for eighty years does something to a mind that has no word for it but *hollow*. Children who tap the columns for fun are punished severely. The code for *do not ask me* exists, is honoured, and is the subject of the oldest and most bitter lawsuit in the load-book, concerning a column in the Lower Fifth who used it for thirty-one years and was sued by her own descendants for withholding a crack report.

Bad stiffening is the horror. The reeds go first, while the limbs still work; then the pits foul early; then the setting comes, and the person is locked in a body that will not answer and cannot be asked. They are not wanted in the structure. They are tombed in felt.

The oil is drawn off first. Always. *We keep the oil; we lose the shape.* Whether the felted tomb is a mercy or a second killing is the oldest live question in the city and nobody has settled it, and there are people who have written their own instruction, notarised, tapped into a trunk line and countersigned, specifying which they want, and there are people who change the instruction every decade, and there is a guild whose entire function is to hold those instructions and argue about them.

Nine years in a soft room is the worst sentence the courts can pass. It is one slack. The people who come out are not usually able to say what happened, because during it they had no shape, and there is nothing to report.

The young stiffeners — lignin at thirty, at forty, rare and pitied — are placed in the nursery walls, among the buds. So a bud spends its fourteen attached years listening to a person who cannot move, who was detached four breaths ago and should have been sweeping or drilling or falling in love, and who has nothing to do for a hundred and forty years but teach children. Every adult in this city was raised inside the voice of somebody who never got to walk. Ask them what a good person is and, after some confusion about the question, they will describe their school-column.

---

Some go out.

The open floor is eleven miles of dust with nothing to click against. It is not darkness; darkness is nothing to them. It is a place where the world does not come back. Dust-walkers cross it in roped pairs, clicking for each other, carrying a folding frame of struts and cord that they set up every few hours to make a room out of nothing and stand in it and be somewhere for a while. They are paid enormously and are considered slightly holy and slightly damaged, and they marry each other.

The freestanding stair out in the nowhere is a pilgrimage. You rope up, walk a day and a half, find eleven worn steps going up and stopping, and put your hand on them and click, and the stair tells you that there was a district here, and that it is under you, and that the dust will move on in its own time.

Some walkers do not come back. They are found decades later, stiffened upright, facing a direction, holding a rope. These are retrieved and set at the margins of the city, at the last doorways, where the terraces give out. They are called witnesses. Nobody taps them. It is not known whether they can hear.

And there are the ones who walk out on purpose and keep going until the click stops returning, and stay there. The floor-listeners put their thorax against bedrock and claim that the mass, being solid, is a perfect reflector — that the fundamental you cannot hear is nonetheless coming back, all the time, from everything, and that the reason a soft room unmakes you is that it is the only lie in the world. The orthodox position is that these people have confused a failure of the pits with a revelation. The orthodox position has been held for six hundred years by people who have never been out past the stair.

---

The buried districts are the deep thing.

The dust cycle is nine hundred years, and while a district is under, the wall keeps healing. It thickens over the doorways. It fills the small rooms. It grows the streets shut. When the dune moves on, what is uncovered is not the district that was buried but a smooth bulge with a district inside it, and to get in you must re-wound your own past, carefully, in the pattern that opens rather than the pattern that closes.

Archaeology here is surgery. The historians are licensed wouders with a very small allowance of cuts.

And sometimes, in the deep dunes, in a bulge that has been growing since before the load-book, they cut into a chamber and find someone who was caught. Sealed, packed, cold, gone almost entirely to lignin at the pace of the wall rather than the pace of a body. Nobody understands how it is possible. The leading account is that the dust held them at constant pressure and they simply slowed down until they were doing approximately what the mass does.

They tap them.

Most give nothing. Some give an answer, in a tap-code nine hundred years obsolete, which has to be reconstructed by scholars working from nursery rhymes and the tuning of retired cords. And what comes back, when it comes, is mostly names — long lists of names, in order, patiently, as if the list were the thing that had been being said all along. Or a crack report, precise, for a beam that fell before the Ninth Terrace was cut. Or a question about the intake: *is it still the ninth year.*

The city has an office for this too. It is small, and it is not in a hurry, and the people in it are the only ones who go home unable to sleep, and they are the ones who, when asked what a person is, do not say *the part that comes away*, and do not say *one who is owed*.

They say: *one who answers.*

---

That is what they are, in the end, and it took the city a long time to arrive at the word.

The mass eats rock and does not answer. The dust falls and does not answer. The sealed room in the west sounds one low tone forever and is not answering anything; it is only being itself, loudly, in the dark. Against all of that, in a bubble that is probably a mistake, a thing has grown that when tapped, taps back. That is the entire civilisation. The cords, the pits, the load-book, the askers, the schools held up by people who never walked, the nine hundred years of names coming out of the dune — one long refusal to be a soft room.

On the equinox of the intake the whole city stands on the western terraces and listens to the plug sound its note, and the children ask who is in there, and the answer is the correct one.

Afterwards they go home along the trunk lines, and the cords are taut and a fifth sharp, and every doorway is humming its household's chord, and the sweepers are on shift, and someone's grandmother is being asked, within her ration, about a beam.

The drone will keep falling. In eleven or twelve thousand years the overtones will go under and there will be no floor to the sound at all, and the committee will still be sitting, and the yearless will still be arguing that this is fine, and someone will have to invent a new way to know what year it is.

They will. They are in no hurry. They have never once been in a hurry. They are the part of the world that, when you put a hand on it and ask, comes back.
