# What Lives in the Rind

Start with the ones you can count, because they are the only ones that come in ones.

In the water films — those few-molecule-thick skins that exist on the underside of the rind for about four hours a day, between the thaw front and the freeze front — there are mites. Six species, or six arguments about one species. They are pale, slow, and blind in the ordinary way, and they graze the felt for the ten-thousandth of a degree that the sound-eaters make. This is their entire estate. Every pressure wave the mat traps and turns to warmth is a calorie deposited in a bank the mites draw on. When you walk out onto the felt, your boots feed them. When you shout — people do shout, once, to hear the shout die — you are, briefly, the richest event in the district's history, and for a day and a half the mite population under your footprints blooms.

They have no idea. Nothing here has any idea. But it is worth knowing that the silence is not empty; the silence is farmed, and the crop is very small, and something lives on it.

In the frozen core, eleven metres down, in ice that has not been liquid since the Holocene was young, there are tuns: tardigrades, dried to barrels, metabolism stopped, waiting. They are the only individuals in the valley with anything resembling a biography. A given tun was last wet when the upslope forests were still alive and their pollen was still arriving fresh. It has a birthday. It has, in principle, a future. Everything else in the Rind is a process wearing a shape, and here is this one small stubborn thing that is simply *itself*, held, for eight thousand years, at the cost of doing nothing whatsoever.

---

Once a season the black bird comes up the gorge.

It is flightless and unbothered and it knows exactly where it is going: to a warm well in the second basin where a bus-line bleeds resistive heat into a depression and holds a pool open at four degrees while the whole world around it sits at minus twenty. Midges come off that pool in a column you can see from thirty metres, a slow grey rope standing in still air. The bird eats. The bird nests in the felt at the pool's edge, in the one place in thirty kilometres that is not, at that moment, trying to be somewhere else.

The bird is the only resident with an opinion about the valley. Its opinion is that the valley is warm. It is wrong at every scale except one, and it is right at that one absolutely, and it comes back every year, and when it goes the mat grows over the nest site and takes the metal back.

This is not cruelty and it is not hospitality. The pool is a coolant loop that happens to leak. But if you want to know what it is like to live in the Rind rather than to *be* it, the bird is your only witness, and what the bird would tell you, if it had the equipment, is that there is one good spot, and it is reliable, and the rest is a long cold walk.

---

Then there are the walkers.

The mat's meristems detach and go root-legged across the felt at a centimetre an hour, browsing, and it is easy to look at them and see the valley's animals — the grazers, the fauna proper, the things that can *go* rather than merely *grow*. That reading is wrong, or rather it is too small.

Watch one for a day. It is carrying something. Held in a cradle of root-hairs, kept from the sun, wrapped in a sleeve of the same hollow fibre the mat uses to eat sound: a face. A small grown face of laminated iron, lapped by a press to the truest flat the district can achieve, no larger than a thumbnail. It is not food. The walker does not eat it. It carries it four metres, or eleven, or in one recorded case a hundred and sixty over three weeks, to another district, and there it lays the face against that district's own reference and holds it, and the two are compared — optically, by interference, in a film of water, in the four-hour window when water is permitted to exist — and the difference is read as a fringe pattern and taken up into the receiving district's growth.

Then the courier's face is dissolved. Its iron goes into the local stock. The walker browses for a while and grows a new face out of the local reference and carries it somewhere else.

They are not animals. They are the postal service of a standard.

---

Now the mat, which is not an animal either, and not a plant in any sense that will help you, and is best understood as a shop.

It has no centre. There is no organ that decides. Arbitration happens the way arbitration happens in a market or a river: pressure against pressure, potential against potential, a district's demand for water meeting another district's demand for iron at a junction that resolves the conflict by growing, and the growth *is* the decision, and there is no record of the alternatives having existed. It thinks two thoughts a day. It has thought roughly ninety million of them since the last interglacial. Its shortest thought is a stroke; its longest is not finished.

And what it makes, stroke after stroke, in the dark, at a tolerance no human shop could hold, is not billets. The billets are by-products; they are dissolved by Tuesday. What it makes is *agreement*. Fit. The condition in which the die and the piston and the socket and the bus and the bladder all say the same number. That is the product. It is the only product. Everything the Rind manufactures is manufactured in order to be compared to something else and then eaten, and the comparing is the point, and the thing that survives the comparing is not an object but a relation, held, passed hand to hand across thirty kilometres by blind crawling couriers at a centimetre an hour.

A standards laboratory with no customer. A metrology with no metre.

---

Except that there is one.

Go down under the mat, into the scoured places, and put your hand in a socket. Round-bottomed granite, worn a hand's depth. The press-feet have sat in that socket, in that hexagon, since before the last interglacial, and every autumn the mat grows a new foot and feels for the old seat with its root-tips and settles into it, because the stone is truer than anything it can grow.

The stone is truer. The stone is not true.

A hand's depth in a hundred and twenty-five thousand years is about eight-tenths of a micron a year. Per stroke it is a nanometre — a few atoms' thickness of granite, gone, every time the valley breathes. No press in the Rind can feel that. It is four orders of magnitude below the finest thing the mat can measure in a day.

But it accumulates. And over sixty years it becomes eighty microns, which is enormous, which is a catastrophe, which is a district running out of true and not knowing why. And so the couriers walk. And the fringes shift, a little, across a decade, in a direction that does not reverse. And the mat, which cannot perceive the wear in a day or a year, integrates it across a human lifetime of crawling comparisons and grows its feet a fraction longer, and seats them, and the number holds.

That is what the industry is for. Not production. Not reproduction. The whole apparatus — the ice presses, the seasonal tooling, the self-dissolving plant, the silence, the forty thousand years without a scrap of litter — exists to hold one dimension steady against the slow forgetting of the only thing that remembers it. And the forgetting is caused by the holding. The feet wear the sockets. The mat is compensating, continuously and forever, for the fact that it exists.

*That* is the resident. Not the cushion-plant, which is clothing. Not the mites, which are weather. A closed loop of self-erosion and self-correction, running at two strokes a day, that has been quietly chasing its own wear since the ice last went away, and has never once caught up, and has never once fallen behind.

---

It has met other makers.

In 1971 a survey party left an aluminium tag wired to a cobble at the rim of the third basin: a number, an anodised disc, a made thing intended to persist. The mat found it within a season. It did not attack it. It grew around it and seated it and, over eleven weeks, compared it — against the couriers, against the fringes, against the sockets — and found that it moved. Aluminium creeps. Aluminium swells with heat at twenty-three parts per million, which in a place that crosses zero twice a day is not a tolerance but a haemorrhage. The tag was untrue and could not be made true and was not, therefore, a reference, and by the following autumn it was gone into the root-ore, and its number with it.

The mat has no word for us. If it had one it would be *drift*.

---

At dusk, when the freeze front goes east to west and the potentials find their paths and the blue crawl runs the ribs, every magnetite needle in every comb in all three basins turns to the same bearing.

It lasts about eleven seconds. It is the only moment in the day when the entire organism holds a single state — thirty kilometres of it, one direction, unanimous. It is as close as the Rind comes to having a face, or a mood, or a thought about itself. And the bearing it agrees on is magnetic north, which wanders, which is not a reference, which nothing in the valley uses for anything.

Then it lets go, and the light goes off the far rim, and the second stroke begins.

You can stand there in that silence with your fillings aching and tell yourself it is making nothing but itself. That is nearly right. What it is doing is keeping faith with a stone that is wearing away under it, at a rate it cannot feel, by means it cannot see, on a timescale it does not experience — and the keeping does not stop, and has not stopped, and the keeping is the animal.
