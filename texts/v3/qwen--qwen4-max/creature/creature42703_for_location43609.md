## The payroll

Begin with the four hundred thousand, because everyone here does, and because they are the reason there is anyone here to begin with.

They are not a cloud. A cloud is a thing you can be outside of. Under the crown they are a fur, two animals deep, and the fur has a grain to it, and in the grain there are neighborhoods — the old females low and central where the air is thickest and hottest, the yearlings out on the thin margins where the rock is bare and the going is poor, the bachelor pockets, the year's crèche in a warm scoop above Terrace Three where the pups are left in a writhing acre while their mothers are up over the alfalfa. The pups have voices. That is the thing nobody up top knows: the ceiling is not one sound but two, and under the frying there is a thin continual complaining, tens of thousands of small arguing voices, and the men who work the crown in the closed season's opposite months can tell a hungry crèche from a settled one the way a shepherd can.

They know individual bats. Not four hundred thousand of them; perhaps thirty. The one with the torn left membrane who has hung in the same bolt-shadow over the Flue grating for three seasons and cannot make the climb and is fed, in defiance of every posted rule, by whoever passes. The albino — not truly albino, piebald, a white hand's-width across the shoulders — that the children call Sheet. The old female the bagging plant named Grandma, who is not old, who is probably four, who has been Grandma for four years because a bat that hangs above the weighbridge for four years becomes somebody.

And they know the fallers. Pups come off the ceiling. Sick adults come off. There is a soft continual rain of them under the exodus, and the grating at the foot of the Flue catches seed husks and thistledown and also bats, and this is where the town's children learn the two facts they will spend their lives holding at once: that the colony is the payroll, and that a bat in the hand is a hot little animal with a face like a dog's and a heart you can feel going, and it will look at you.

The bat flies do not distinguish. Wingless, flat, spiderish, they run off a falling bat onto a boot, up a trouser leg, into a hairline, within a minute of your standing still, and they will feed on you exactly as happily. This is the first thing the Kettle teaches about who lives here. The boundary is not where you think it is. Everything in this room is on the same skin.

## The shore

Under the roost the floor is a beach, and it has tides, and the tides are the heap.

Fresh guano comes down at the rate of a slow snowfall and steams at sixty degrees, and in the steam are the cockroaches — fat, glossy, unhurried, the true owners of the warm zone, in numbers that make the floor look upholstered. In the wet are the guano fly larvae, a working custard. On the crust are the mites, which are a bloom, a dust that walks. Then, as the heap ages and dries and cools toward the mere forty-four of the room, the dermestids arrive, and they are the undertakers: they take the bat carcasses, the rats, the fallen geckos, the odd cat, and they reduce them to clean articulation in a fortnight, and there are children in this town who have never seen a dead thing that was not being politely disassembled.

Out on the dry margins the cave crickets move in a lace, and the lace is never in the same place twice, and above the lace the hunting spiders, which do not build anything, which have nothing to build on, which simply run. Above them the geckos, one at every lamp, forty-odd, translucent pink in the sodium light, and each has a name given by children and inherited by other children, so that the gecko at the tea stall has been Called Mouse for thirty years across four generations of animal, and Mouse's regenerated tail is a different tail each time and the name holds. Above the geckos the rats, who are clever and have learned the shift schedule. Above the rats, the cats.

The cats came down in 1961 in a crate: one tom, two females, a solution. They are now a lineage with a founder effect and a look — long, thin, big-eared, sandy, a kinked tail through half of them — and they live in the graveyard of rusted frames at the east end, which is their city, three storeys of dead crusher and dead skip and dead pipe, warm mud under it, and they come out along the terraces at dusk like landlords. There is a budget line. There is a man, Villalba, whose job is the line, and who sets traps and has for nineteen years, and who has a female in the machine graveyard he calls Sixty-One after the year and not after the cat, and who has never once filled the line to target, and whose reports the Concern files without reading. The cats are the only resident the bat ordinance does not cover and the only one it obviously should: they take fallers at the grating. Everyone knows. Nobody has the appetite for the fight, because the fight would be about whether the town is allowed to have anything of its own.

In the cable tray on Terrace Two there is a rat snake. The electricians call it Wire. There has been a Wire since before the school, and the town does not entirely believe in the succession; the town half-believes it is one snake, very old, very patient, lying in the warm trunking above the town's electricity, eating the rats that would otherwise eat the town's electricity. Its sign is one hand drawn along the other forearm. Electricians work around it. There is a photograph of it in the school corridor, next to the class of 1974.

And in the heaps, invisibly, the most successful resident: the fungus. It lives in the guano and it lives in the men, and it is the only thing in the Kettle that moves freely between the two. It goes home with you. It goes up the shaft in your chest to the plain and sits in a lung under a white sky and coughs. Kaur, the doctor, has it herself; she got it in her first year, before she believed the poster; the poster has been curling on the same wall for thirty years and she has never had it replaced, because she has decided it is more use to her curled.

## The sorted

Eleven hundred on the register, nine hundred in the bathhouse tally, and the difference is the ones who are up top this week.

They are sorted by three degrees. That is the whole class system, and it is thermodynamic. The intake course along the west wall is thirty-two, and the oldest families are there, in the oldest houses, cut sharp as the day they were quarried, with the deepest gutters and the best tin. Two terraces back it is thirty-four and a half and the houses are newer and the surnames are newer. Down on the low south floor, where the sump is warm as blood and the mosquitoes stand in a column you can put your arm into, it is thirty-nine, and the men who live there are the men who have not yet got out of it, and they sleep badly and their hearts argue and they are, without exception, the funniest people in the town, because the alternative is available and worse.

The bodies are a local product. They sweat sooner and thinner and less salty than anyone up top; they carry an extra litre of blood; they drink eleven litres a day and pass a cup of it, dark, and the doctor's second-most-common diagnosis after the cough is the stone. They are thin. They are salt-crusted at the temples by ten in the morning. They have a resting pulse that would frighten a physician on the plain and a twenty-minute working span at the face, and the twenty minutes is not a policy, it is the animal. The bathhouse trough at thirty-four is the town's commons, its pub, its church hall, its parliament: men lie in it in rows with their arms out like men in a bath of snow, and argue about the Treaty, and about the cats, and about whether the dead should go up.

Cold, to a Kettle child, is thirty-four. On the school Trip up top they are taken out at night in blankets and stand shaking in twenty-two degrees under a sky with no lid on it, and about a third of them cry, and it is not the cold. There is a word for the feeling. It translates roughly as *unroofed*, and it is also the word for bereaved.

The ones who leave carry the smell. Ammonia and hot fur and the sweetish underneath, in the wool, in the hair, in the wooden things, and it does not come out, and up top it is a mark. Some come back for that reason and some come back because they cannot sleep in quiet — because a room with nothing in the ceiling is a room with something wrong with it — and the local pride, that a Kettle man can sleep through anything except silence, is not a joke, it is a diagnosis, and it is the reason the town has never quite emptied even in the bad decade.

## Hands

Half of Drip Street is deaf by fifty and the other half is under fifty.

The signing is not a translation of the spoken language; it is older than the school and it has its own grammar and its own vocabulary and about four hundred signs that no one up top has ever needed. There are nine signs for drip, sorted by interval and by pitch and by whether the water is coming through a seam or off a bolt or out of a gutter, and a house on Drip Street is named by its drip, and you can ask where someone lives by tapping a rhythm on your own collarbone. There is a sign for *the fan is well* and a different one for *the fan is running*, and the difference between them is the whole of the town's theology. There is a sign, one flat palm held up and slightly cupped, that means the crown, and it is the same sign as *sky*, and children draw the sky brown.

There is no sign for *outside*. There is *up*.

Every bed has a small fan the size of a hand, repaired forever, rewound on the eleven-year cycle with everything else, handed down, and the wedding gift is a fan and a lid. The lids are the craft. Nothing here is stored uncovered — nothing, no flour, no tea, no salt, no photograph, no baby's clothes — and metal dissolves, copper goes to lace, galvanising blooms and flakes, so the town makes its containers out of the room itself: quarried blocks, hollowed, ground, with stone lids lapped to fit so precisely that you can hear the air sigh out when you seat them. They are beautiful. They are the only objects in the Kettle that do not age. A jar cut in 1930 has its drill marks and its sharp arris and will have them in a hundred thousand years, and every household has three or four, and the good ones have names, and when the cave finally happens the cave will have them.

## The kept

Eight roots now. Nine hung down the Fissure for as long as anyone could remember, wrist-thick, pale as candle wax, thirty metres of them drinking the condensate off the rock, and two reached the floor and rooted in the guano, which means the fig in the wadi is partly eating bats and partly eating the town. They put out white shoots with leaves the size of thumbnails and no green in them at all, blind feeling things, and the town's word for them is the word for *fingers* with a modifier that means *not looking*.

Root nine died three years ago. It is still there. Nobody has cut it down. It hangs among its brothers dry and going grey and the children have been told not to touch it, and they don't, and this is the only rule in the Kettle that has never needed enforcing.

Under the forty hooded lamps there are forty halos of algae, a green skin on wet stone, the only chlorophyll in the room that isn't imported. They are a nuisance — they hold water against the rock and they stain — and they are also pets. Nasr, who walks the roof-line with a bucket and a torch, has been cultivating the halo under lamp nineteen for eleven years, scraping it into channels, coaxing it into a shape, and the shape is a fish, and it is about a metre long, and it is the only work of art in the town that is alive, and she will not discuss it with anyone who has not seen it.

In the dead drift off Terrace Three there are mushroom beds on spent guano, and they are dinner, and they are the only thing anyone here eats that was made in this room. At the school there are chives and mint in crates under a lamp that is technically illegal and has been grandfathered by an amendment nobody can find. And there is the lemon tree.

It is Company property. It has an inventory tag, tarred over and re-tarred, and a number, and it appears in the asset register between a compressor and a theodolite, and it has never fruited and probably never will. It lives in a tarred tub on wheels and it is pushed, by whoever holds the pushing that year, to the foot of the Flue at midsummer, because for twenty minutes around noon in the last week of June a disc of daylight the width of a dinner plate comes three hundred metres straight down the shaft and lands on one paving stone.

The current pusher is Odile Marsh, seventy-eight, deaf, who has done it for forty-one years and whose mother did it before her. The tree's sign is two hands turning a wheel. Children stand in the coin of light and are told to remember what it felt like, and they do remember, all of them, for the rest of their lives, and the ones who leave and are dying up top on the plain remember it last.

## The keeper of the note

The Lung has a wife. That is what the town calls Bekker, and Bekker is a man of sixty who sleeps in the antechamber of the concrete throat and has done for thirty years, and who has strung a piano wire across the intake course and tuned it, and who can tell you the fan's health from the wire's note before any instrument in the control room can.

He is the weatherman. The town's word for weather is the town's word for the fan; there is no other kind; and when a child asks what it is doing outside, the answer is a number of revolutions. Bekker's forecast is posted at the tea stall: *ninety, steady*. On Sundays it says *forty-five* and people take their shirts off and lie in the street.

Everyone knows the arithmetic — four hours, four hundred thousand mammals, the low corners filling — and everyone has heard the siren, and everyone over forty has stood in the intake with a wet cloth over their face during the real event of eleven years ago and watched the men go up the ladderway in the dark to the Flue grating to hand-crank the damper. There is a sign for that too. It is the sign for *weather* made with both hands and slowly.

The machines around the Lung are also residents, in the way that draft animals are residents. The jaw crusher at a hundred and five decibels is a bad-tempered thing everyone has an opinion about. The dryer drum has a limp. And every eleven years the town sheds its skin: all the wiring, tinned and boxed, eaten from inside by ammonia faster than water eats iron, comes out whether it needs to or not, and the town is rewired, and the old copper goes up the shaft in skips, green lace, and the electricians work around Wire in the cable tray on Terrace Two, and the whole place smells of new insulation for a month, and children born since the last molt are suddenly taller.

## Up or down

There is no soil anywhere in the Kettle, so there is nowhere to put the dead.

The Company's answer, for eighty years, was the night skip: you go up, you are buried on the plain in a rectangle at the edge of a pivot circle, under the alfalfa, in the sun, three hundred metres from everyone who ever heard you breathe. Half the town wants this and half does not, and the halves are not the halves you would expect.

The other practice — unofficial, tolerated, older than the ordinance — is the worked-out corner, where the midden has come up a metre and a half since the streets were laid out. You go down into the warm heap and the dermestids take you in a fortnight and the guano goes up in bags and onto the alfalfa, and the alfalfa grows the moths, and the moths come down the Flue at dusk in the cold wind of the exodus and are eaten by the bats, and the bats make the heap. It is a closed loop and everybody in this town can recite it before they can read.

So the argument is not really about disposal. It is about whether your grandmother is in a rectangle under a white sky, or whether she is in the ceiling, frying, tonight, and will be over the alfalfa at dusk, and will be back before morning. The people who want to go down say: I would rather be in the room. The people who want to go up say: I have spent my whole life in this room.

Both of them, when pressed, admit they are talking about the same thing.

## Nine centimetres

On Terrace Two, roped off, under its own small hooded lamp, there is a soda straw of calcite nine centimetres long.

It has grown from the drip at a single bolt hole in forty-one years. It is the only thing in the Kettle that water has made; everything else was cut. It is hollow, wet, the diameter of a drinking straw, translucent, faintly amber, and at its tip there is a drop, always, and the drop is where the growing happens.

Sereda at the school measures it on the first Sunday of the year with a plastic rule held near and never touching, and paints the number on the wall behind, and there are forty-one numbers on that wall in forty-one hands, and the children read them the way other children read the names of kings. Skin oil stops a straw growing. One fingertip, once, and nine centimetres of patience is finished forever. This is why the rope. It is also why the town, which is careless about respirators and casual about the cats and fatalistic about the sump, is fanatical about the rope. There has been exactly one incident. The boy involved is now fifty-three and deaf and drives a skip and has never been forgiven and has never quite been blamed.

The straw is the town's instrument. Not its symbol — its instrument. The drip is lessening. The old men say the roof used to rain, and the pivots up top are pulling the aquifer down eighty centimetres a year, and the fissure system is draining, and root nine is dry, and the fig in the wadi is scheduled to come out when the field is re-graded, and Ferreira, whose whole job is talking to farmers about spray, has been talking to farmers about water for six years now and has nothing signed.

Nobody has said out loud what it means. But everybody has done the sum, and the sum is: when the straw stops, that is the year. That is what the straw is for. It is nine centimetres of warning, growing at two millimetres a year, and the whole town is watching a wet thread on the end of it, and the children are brought to see the only thing here that is not made by machines and not made by bats, and are told, in signs, *this one is not ours*.

## The audience

Once a decade a maintenance flood goes up for an hour, and the roof turns out to be moving.

Not flying — moving. A ceiling of fur, breathing, shifting, four hundred thousand animals adjusting, the whole crown alive and continuous and *there*, forty metres up, in a light nobody down here has ever aimed at it. People come out and stand in the street and don't say anything. There is a woman on Terrace One who has seen it three times, and what she says about it, when she says anything, is that the third time was the worst, because the first two times she thought it was a ceiling with animals on it and the third time she understood it was one animal, and that she was inside its warmth, and had been her whole life, and would be until she wasn't.

Then it goes off, and the roof is a sound again.

That is the town: a thousand people living in the body heat of something they are not permitted to look at, sorting themselves along three degrees, keeping their food under stone lids, listening to water arrive on tin. Every household knows its own drip. Nasr can hear that a bolt two streets over has changed its note and will say so at the tea stall and be right. On Sundays, when the mill is down for maintenance and the colony goes early and the Lung idles at forty-five, ninety decibels drop away and people come out of their houses and stand in the street for the second reason, the quiet one, and hear it: tick, tick, tick, from every bolt and every seam and every gutter, ten thousand small clocks, water arriving from above, never from below, the way it has arrived for longer than there have been eyes.

Because here is the last resident, and it is not on the register.

The rock is unborn. The landforms have not happened. There is no flowstone, no talus, no rounding, no rind; the spoil has edges that cut boots and the arrises are ninety years sharp. If the Lung stopped and the pumps stopped and the last hoist went up, the water would keep coming for a while, and then it would come differently, and it would begin — patiently, at two millimetres a year, from twelve thousand bolt holes — to make a cave. In a hundred thousand years: straws, drapery, rounded blocks, warm wet dark, bats, and a town-shaped absence in the floor.

That cave is the thing that lives here. Everything else is its larval stage: the bats keeping the roof warm, the pumps holding the water off, the men cutting the room a metre a year wider into the west wall, the Concern selling the sump to the pivots, the whole apparatus of lids and gutters and eleven-year molts keeping one enormous embryo dry before its time.

And the cave will not know us. It will have no memory of the ninety years, no lichen of us, no patina; the stone jars will be there with their drill marks and the pavement under the pavement will still be under something. Water in the dark will fall and mean nothing, as it has everywhere else on earth for four billion years.

But for a little while, in the middle of that anonymity, the drops are landing on tin roofs over people who are awake, and every one of them is heard, and sorted, and named, and worried over, and counted; and once a year a woman holds a plastic rule near a wet thread and writes a number on a wall, and a class of deaf children reads the numbers out with their hands.

That is what lives in the Kettle, under the fur, in the three degrees: an audience. The only one this rock has ever had, or will have again.
