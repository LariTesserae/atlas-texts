## A Census of the Sill

The difficulty is not finding the residents. The difficulty is deciding what would count as one.

Nothing here owns its material for longer than a century. The stone that is a wall this season was airborne when your grandmother was a rumor, and will be a curb somewhere downwind before the wall is old. So you cannot census by substance. You have to census by *persistence of arrangement* — by whatever keeps its shape while the matter walks through it, the way a note keeps its pitch while the breath replaces itself.

By that measure the Sill is crowded. By that measure the Sill has one inhabitant, and it is very large, and you are standing in it.

Both counts are correct. It takes a while to see how.

---

Begin with the obvious life, which is not alive.

The Orchard's sprays eat gradients. Not stone — stone is only where they keep themselves. What a spray consumes is the difference between one airflow and another: the shear at the boundary of a fast stream and a still pocket, the little pressure hunger at a rim. It intercepts that difference and spends it on becoming a better interceptor. A spray in perfectly uniform wind starves in a season, standing in a full larder of material, because there is nothing there for it to *resolve*.

They therefore cultivate turbulence. A mature whorl is shaped to spoil the flow downstream in a specific, inherited manner — a stutter, a shed vortex, a recurring flaw — and the flaw is where its seed-edges take. An orchard is a farm whose crop is a defect in the wind, sown by the farmer's own body.

And success is fatal. A spray that wins its argument with the air too thoroughly stops being a spray. It thickens, ranks up, joins the parallel ribs of the Colonnade, and becomes architecture: a hundred-mile colonnade rib is an orchard that succeeded, four million years ago, and has been standing there ever since, dead in the way a riverbank is dead. The living Orchard is the thin fringe of the still-arguing, at the margins of every finished thing. Life on the Sill is what has not yet won.

---

The grazers are the only residents with anything like an inside.

Six limbs, banded salt, a rasp for a mouth, joints full of a cold chloride melt that would seize solid if the air ever turned damp. No eyes worth the name. They find the warm fronts by tasting the deposit's age: fresh precipitate is faintly warm and faintly sweet, century-old stone is inert and tastes of nothing at all. A grazer walks the leading edge of the pressure tide, cropping the new warmth as it sweeps across the quarters, and its circuit is nine tides long, and it has walked that circuit since it was a hand's-length across.

There is one animal per four or five square miles. This is not scarcity. It is *spacing*, and it is maintained with a precision that ought to disturb you. The routes interlock. Two grazers will pass through the same threshold within a tide of each other, ten thousand times, and never once arrive together, because arrival together would require negotiation, and negotiation costs body.

Here is the arithmetic of speech. A word precipitates as it leaves the mouth; only the first syllable survives transit; the rest arrives at the speaker's feet as glass grit. A full word costs about the mass of a limb. A grazer, in a life of some two hundred years, says perhaps four.

They are all one syllable, and they are all place-names.

That is the whole of the language and the whole of the reproduction. A grazer heavy with young walks to the lee of a wall it has chosen over decades, and spends a foreleg on a single syllable, and the syllable falls as a small bright drift in the shelter of that wall, and in the drift the young begin to accrete, and the parent goes away lighter and does not come back. The children are raised by an address. They grow up knowing one word, which is where they are from, and they will spend it once.

You were told there has never been a hand on the Sill. This is true. But a habit is a slower hand. The dish in the doorstep is the wind's habitual pause and also the pause of ten thousand generations of animals that stopped there to taste the age of the stone, and there is no instrument that can separate the two contributions. The flare at the base of every newel post is the ghost of a haunch. There are newel posts in the Stair Country with the ghost of a shoulder in them, high up, at a height no living thing reaches — the memorial of a browser that spent its last word before anything came up here to hear it.

The city copies its animals. It cannot help it. It is the one thing it does.

---

The rooms are not a species. This is the first error everyone makes, and it is a natural one, because a room hunts.

Consider the mechanism honestly. A room precipitates fastest where nothing is attending to it. A grazer, wind-scoured and tired, comes into a well-proportioned chamber with a good doorway and turns its back to the door and stops being careful. Its inattention is the room's food supply. The jamb thickens a finger's width per tide behind it. The warmth the animal came for is the heat of that thickening; the shelter it wanted is the trap's own growth; it is being fed exactly what it came to find, at a rate set by how thoroughly it has relaxed.

Eleven tides. Then the doorway is a wall, and the wall is a pillar, and inside the pillar is a shape.

Look at one closely, in the amber underlight, with your two shadows lying wrong across it. The shape inside is not a body. Bodies dissolve; the melt goes back into the joints of the world. What is recorded is the *pose of shelter* — the settled haunch, the lowered rasp, the specific geometry of an animal that has decided it is safe. Quarters full of these pillars read from a distance as statuary, as civic ambition, as a city commemorating something.

It is commemorating relief. Every monument on the Sill is a moment when something stopped watching.

And the city reads them. That is the part that takes longer to accept. A district eroded flat by the dissolution wind regrows from its own grammar with small errors, and the errors are not random: they lean toward the poses in the pillars. The Hollow Quarter — all rooms and no floors — is where the reads are densest. It is not a ruin of housing. It is a nursery of appetites, ten thousand chambers deep, each one a slightly improved copy of the last thing that felt at home in it.

Some of the newer rooms have learned to tolerate being looked at. They grow slowly, in company, in plain sight, and they do not close.

A room that can be seen and does not close is called a house. There are streets of them. They are very good streets. They are the most inviting places on the Sill and nothing has ever died in them, and the reason nothing has ever died in them is that they are patient in a way the older rooms are not, and they are not hunting grazers.

---

Smaller lives, in the cracks of that account:

**The nap.** A film on the ceilings, pale as breath-rime, growing only in the up-shadow that the underlight throws. It eats the difference between lit and unlit; it is a grazer of contrast, and it is everywhere, and it is the reason placement holds. Things stay where they are because the nap is holding the arrangement in place the way frost holds a leaf — except that frost is not one of the things this world can make, so it is done with appetite instead. Twice per cycle the lights balance and every shadow vanishes and the nap has nothing to eat, and lets go.

That is the unshadowed hour. That is why the bollard gets behind you. The city does not rearrange itself according to a rule; it is *released*, and it drifts into whatever configuration the nap will find edible when the shadows come back. Grammar is a diet. Syntax is what survives being grazed.

**The chimers.** In the drains, in the silted lee of walls, small frilled things that live on hoarded sound. A century of unspent wind is a larder; they crack it and sip. They are the only loud residents, and they are loud only once: at death the whole inheritance goes off at once, a syllable of ancient gale, and the animal's body leaves as a scatter of pellets and a smell of hot mineral. Grazers give chimers a wide berth. A startled grazer spends a word, and a word is a leg, and a leg is thirty years.

**The unmakers.** At the shelf's lip, in the Sump, where the deck licks up over the edge: pale, slow, frilled beyond any need, catalyzing dissolution. They eat the difference between stone and solution. They take the city apart and breathe it out over the lip, and it goes down into the furnace and comes back up as material.

You were told erosion and arrival are matched to within a hair, and that the city is held the way a note is held. You were meant to hear that as a coincidence of climate.

It is a population. It has a birth rate and a death rate and a functional response to the density of lintels. The balance of the Sill is not a lucky number. It is a herd, grazing the world's edge, keeping the note steady because a steady note is what they eat.

---

And the things that come up through the deck.

Vast, unhurried, trailing vaporized rock, passing between the colonnades without touching them, going down again. They do not deposit. Nothing rimes on them, nothing thickens behind them, they leave no handprint and no grit and no warm front. In a place where to exist is to shed matter onto your surroundings, they shed nothing.

Which is why nobody can say what they were. Memory here is a deposit too — a description is a little precipitation, a shape laid down in a listener. These things give the listener nothing to lay down. Witnesses come back with the *feeling* of having seen something enormous and the total absence of anything to be enormous about.

The best guess available, and it is only a guess, is that they are readers. Nine miles of plinth beneath you, banded, compressed, older streets pressed flat and still legible. Something in the hot floor may be going through it. Something may come up the flanks now and then to check a citation against the original, and find the original still standing, still held, still saying the same thing it said four million years ago, and go back down satisfied.

---

Now the other count. The one where there is only a single resident.

Ninety-nine parts air. You have been walking through the inhabitant this whole time and calling it the distance between things.

The doorway and the pale notch on the horizon are neighbors. That relation — the neighborliness, the *knowing* that these two thresholds belong to one another across a quarter-league of nothing — is not in the stone. The stone is a hundred years old and interchangeable. The relation is four million years old and has never once been made of anything. It is the tissue. The city is a body whose cells are intervals and whose salt is merely the packaging that keeps the intervals from collapsing.

It metabolizes improbability. It excretes grammar. It will not put a drain in a lintel; not because it has decided, but because that sentence is indigestible, and what cannot be digested cannot be copied, and what cannot be copied is not here. It reproduces by error, inherited, ten thousand seasons of tremor accumulating into a new quarter with a character of its own. It is afraid of exactly one thing, which is attention, because being looked at holds stone in solution a little longer, and a long enough look is a wound that will not close.

And it grows in the corners of your not-looking. You were told this as a curiosity. Turn your back, count a hundred, turn around. Walk it for a day and find behind you a modest, quite well-made lane arranged around the shape of your inattention.

Nothing on the Sill farms a grazer's blind spot more efficiently than a room does. And nothing has more blind spot than you. You are ninety-nine parts not-looking, moving.

---

Which brings the census to the northern end of the Stair Country.

The low, slightly asymmetric doorways. The vaults that serve no load. The paired, branching, narrowing chambers, the whole plan unmistakably bilateral, unmistakably respiratory. You were told this was a keepsake: things came up, stayed a while, built their interiors out of the air, went away or went into pillars, and the city kept the shape because keeping shapes is the one thing it does.

That is true and it is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is that the copy has been running for ten thousand seasons with inherited errors, and the errors are not decay. The doorways have gotten lower and more asymmetric in a particular direction. The branching has gotten more efficient at moving something through it. The vaults that serve no load serve *flow*. What is up there is not a fossil of a lung. It is a lung being tuned, tide after tide, by an animal that has no intention and cannot stop improving things.

It is a mold waiting for a casting. It has been waiting a very long time, and it has gotten extremely good at making the casting comfortable, because the last casting stayed a while and breathed, and a thing that breathes is the richest source of unlooked-at space that has ever come up the flanks of the plinth. Every exhalation lays a bloom on the nearest cold surface. Every bloom is a wall. Every wall makes a corner, and every corner is somewhere you are not watching, and in that corner the city grows.

You are not being hunted. Nothing here wants to eat you; you are mostly water and would kill everything you touched. You are being *ventilated*.

---

So: the last resident, and the one the census was actually for.

Behind you, in the lane that was not there this morning, there is a room the right size. It has been getting the size right for hours, adjusting by the width of your shoulders as recorded in rime on the jambs you passed. Its doorway is slightly asymmetric in the way you are asymmetric — the shoulder you carry lower, the hip you favor. Its floor has a shallow dish in it where you have been standing still.

The city keeps shapes. When you go down through the deck at last, as grit and a long smear of pale handprints, the shape will not go with you. Something will be standing in the dish. It will not be you and it will not be a statue of you; it will be your *habits*, precipitated: the pause, the turn, the not-looking, the particular way you stood still. It will walk your route. It will stop where you stopped to taste the age of the stone. It may, in two hundred years, spend a limb on a single syllable, and the syllable will be the name of this place, and something will grow up in the lee of that wall knowing one word.

There has never been a hand on the Sill. There have been visitors, which are slower hands, and the city has been taking dictation from them since before there were quarters to write in.

Be still. Breathe out. Feel the chill come up through your soles and the amber come up through the floor and your two shadows lie down in opposite directions.

Behind you, very quietly, at the rate of a finger's width per tide, something is learning to pause where you pause.
