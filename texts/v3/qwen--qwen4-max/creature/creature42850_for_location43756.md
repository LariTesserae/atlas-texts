## The ones that never leave

There is a mite the colour of weak tea living in the cast of a moss leaf. The leaf itself is gone — it was alive in June, it made its tissue, the lime settled around it, it died and rotted out and left its exact negative in the stone, a chamber the shape of a leaf with one wall of glass-hard calcite. The mite lives in that negative. It has never been dry. It cannot be dry; its cuticle is a formality. In its whole life it will travel perhaps eleven centimetres, and it will spend most of that distance going up, because the floor it lives on is rising underneath it at two millimetres a year and it must keep pace or be sealed in.

Almost everything down here is keeping that pace. This is the first thing about the residents: they live on a floor that is being poured.

The white planarians in the shallow pools are flat, translucent, faintly pink where the midge larvae show through them. They glide on a carpet of cilia over rims as thin as a thrown lip and they do not die of age. When a flake of lime spalls off a barrage and falls across one, the two halves round off, grow heads, and continue. The district's planarians are arguably one animal that has been interrupted many times.

The springtails are the ones you notice, because they are the only residents that make a sound without meaning to. They stand on the pool films in numbers that turn the surface grey-blue and mobile, and when they move together it is the sound of salt poured very slowly onto paper. They were here first. They are here now on the bare talus at the north end, picking across the black algal film in the wet seams, and they will be here on the last slab when everything else has gone under rubble.

The midges do not fly. That is, they do, but only a hand's breadth: up out of the pool, onto the wet moss, mate, drop the eggs, die, all inside a space you could cover with a hat. Their whole vertical ambition is about four centimetres. Above them, every morning, a hundred thousand birds go up a hundred and eighty metres and out. The two economies exist within a metre of each other and never touch.

## The fur, and the sheets

The harvestmen stand in mats on the wet walls, and it is a mistake to think of them as individuals, because the wall does not. They are a texture. They are the nap on the velvet. Run a light along a seeping face and the nap changes direction as thousands of bodies re-orient, and the change travels a metre and stops. They taste with their legs. They eat what the birds drop, and what the midges leave, and each other, and they walk upward two millimetres a year without ever performing an act of climbing.

In the long quarter, where the passages are the width of a body, the spiders' sheets bridge every gap, one above another, a stack of floors in a shaft. Nothing flies here — it is too cold to fly, except at dawn and dusk — so the sheets are not nets for the air. They are nets for what falls: feathers, missed boluses, the crumbs of the import coming down from the terraces above. And because the walls on either side are rising, a spider must re-anchor a little higher each night. Over its whole life it lifts its web perhaps a centimetre and a half. It lives on a rising floor and does not know it. Nothing here knows it. It is only visible from outside, over decades, as a change in the pattern of white.

## The ones that remember

In the warm seeps at the south end, where the water comes out of the wall at a steady nine degrees and the liverwort is thick as a tongue, there are salamanders. Lungless. They breathe through their skin, which is why they live in the film and not in the pool, and they are the colour of wet slate, and they are slow in a way that is not lethargy but arithmetic: a cold body, a small budget, a long patience.

One female in the east wall is older than any bird in the basin. She has been through four barrage failures. She knows the route from her seep to the pool below and from that pool to the next when the first one empties, and she knows it as a sequence of textures under her forefeet, and she has no way of telling anybody. When a wall goes and ninety seconds of water takes ten thousand midge larvae down the spillway, she goes with it, unhurried, in the dark, along a corridor she has used before.

The frogs in the same seeps have lost their voices. The males knock. One low knock, about once a minute, all night, from somewhere in the liverwort, and the females read it with their forelimbs pressed to the moss. It carries about four metres through the wet crumb and then it is gone.

Note what that means. The knock is not sound in the air; it is sound in the wall. The salamander feels it. The harvestmen feel it. The mite in its leaf-cast feels it as a disturbance in the fabric of the world. And the birds, three ranks up, feel it through their feet and ignore it, because it says nothing about food.

The touch-language of this place was not invented by the birds. It is the basin's own tongue, the only one that works in a medium that eats sound. The birds merely became fluent.

## The mouth

At the north end the floor drops into the sump, and a pale sculpin holds station there, facing into the flow, doing almost nothing for years.

It is the last resident before the exit. Everything the basin cannot use goes past it: the drowned, the moulted, the failed eggs, the ten thousand larvae of a broken pool. It eats what it can and the rest goes down under a hundred metres of limestone and comes out in a valley nobody is standing in, where something else is waiting, and has never seen the window, and is eating swifts.

And traffic runs the other way. The river arrives out of the low arch at the south end having spent nine days inside the mountain, and it brings passengers: blind amphipods, colourless, no eyes at all, animals from a place that has never had a photon in it. They come out into the shadowless white and are eaten within the hour. The mountain pays a small subsidy. It has been paying it since before the roof fell.

So: the sky is imported at dusk, the mountain's interior is imported continuously, and the dead are exported north. Everything living down here is a waystation between two darknesses, one above and one below.

## The mason

Now the moss, which is not an animal and does most of the work.

Five or six kinds, but only one of them builds. The dam-moss grows into the current on the upstream face, strips the carbon out of the water to make itself, and lets the lime fall around its own body. What is alive is the top two centimetres. Everything below that is the wall it made and the corpse it is standing on. It is a skin of green crawling upward over its own dead at two millimetres a year, and it has been doing this without pause since it arrived.

It arrived on the trees.

When the roof came down it brought a hectare of plateau with it, and on the bark of that spruce and birch and aspen there was already moss, and the moss did not die — the cold slowed it and the lime began and it went on. The dam-moss of the whole terrace quarter is very likely a handful of clones descended from what rode in on the bark. It is older than the city. It is possibly older than the forest it came down on. It has no brain and no nerves and it has laid every wall here, and the birds, who get the credit, are its instrument.

Because the birds bring it nitrogen. The guano falls, the midges take it, the mites take the midges, and the whole rain of it washes down the upstream faces and feeds the green. Take the birds away and the water would still degas and still lay stone, but slowly, and smoothly, and alone. With them, it builds at two millimetres a year and it builds in the shapes they need.

## The birds

Palm-sized. Eighteen grams. Ash-pale bodies, sooty wings, a gape that opens back past the eye and is fringed with bristles like the ribs of a net. The iris is so dark it reads as pupil; in the shadowless white they see perfectly well and in the direct bar of sun they are nearly blind.

Their feet are the whole story. Small, weak, all four toes able to swing forward, built to hook into moss on a vertical face and to hold there for hours. They cannot walk. They cannot perch. They cannot take off from a flat surface — there is nothing in the leg to throw them up, and the wing is too long to beat in a confined space. They must fall to fly.

Which is why the city is vertical. Every nest is on a face, over a drop. A bird on the wet white step between two pools would be a bird in trouble. The terraces are not streets. They are cliffs, thousands of them, each the height of a wrist, and the whole basin is a country with no ground floor.

At first light they go up. Not all at once — the column takes eleven minutes to pass, a hundred and forty thousand of them spiralling in the grey shaft below the window, falling off their walls one after another and turning upward, and the sound of it is the sound of weather in another valley. Then the plateau has them, all day, over the river and the neighbouring ridge, taking insects out of the air at forty kilometres an hour and packing them into a bolus behind the tongue.

Not quite all of them go.

Somebody has to stay with the eggs and the small chicks, and staying is the dangerous job, and who stays is settled at dawn at a range of one centimetre, on the wall, by pressure. A lean. A refusal to lean. A brood patch — bare, hot, densely innervated skin — pressed against a neighbour's flank and held there a half-second longer than usual. The negotiation is conducted in the only grammar available: *yield; not yielding; food is coming; hold; I am here; is it alive.* A hundred thousand of these conversations, none of them audible, none of them about anything further away than a body-length.

There is no word in it for *far*. The birds fly forty kilometres a day and cannot say where the food comes from.

At dusk the return comes down through the window and the boluses go beak to beak down the ranks, and each transfer takes a tax, and the birds at the deep end of a long rank eat what is left. The geometry of hunger here is a map of the passages. In the packed quarter, three deep, the innermost cups are in permanent white shadow; the birds there hatch, fledge, breed and die having never once seen the sun, though they have been above it every day of their lives.

And in the cups, all day, alone: the chicks. Ten thousand of them, in stone tubes, in the silence, doing nothing but growing and feeling the wall. This is the true resident of the basin — the thing in the dark that is being fed by touch, nine hours at a stretch, by a parent it knows only as a pressure and a quantity of insect.

Each cup is a chimney. The wall rises, so the rim must rise; the birds add to it each season with the same glassy cement and the lime follows and seals it, and beneath the living bird the tube goes down through the compressed remains of everything that happened here before — feathers, eggshell, the small dry shapes of chicks that did not make it. A nest is a core sample of a lineage. The bird at the top is sitting on her great-grandmothers.

## The falcon, and what it wrote

It comes down the light bar at midsummer noon, folded, because that is the only hour it can see the floor, and it takes the brood-guards off the lit terraces. It hunts partly by ear.

Over four or five generations the colony went quiet under that pressure, and the quiet is legible. In a broken barrage face, a hand's breadth down, the nests change. Below that leaf they are shallow and open, cups set proud on the wall. Above it they are deeper, narrower, set back under the finger-width ledges, hooded. The birds rebuilt their architecture in response to a sound they stopped making. The falcon is written into the masonry as a change in the depth of a hole.

It is a resident too, in its way. It keeps the same timetable as everything else — forty minutes of gold at midsummer, nothing at all in December. Its young have to be taught the window; the trick is not instinct, it is a local tradition, and it has been lost in other colonies. Somewhere in the touch-language there is a particular stillness that means it, and that stillness is passed down the ranks by birds that have never seen it, to chicks that will feel it before they know what it is.

The falcon has gone quiet as well. Everything that uses the window goes quiet.

## What the plan is a map of

Go to the oldest district, where the shapes are irregular, and look at the plan from the rim.

The long quarter of body-width passages runs in the direction the fallen trunks slid. The walls follow the trees. In the hexagonal quarter, the pools are packed like the cells of a comb because the slab that came down there carried the plateau's own patterned ground with it — the frost polygons, the ice-wedge lattice of a cold field, intact, fossil — and the moss took the seams and the water took the moss and the birds took the walls. The irregular quarter is a map of a canopy: the gaps where the crowns fell open, the hollows where they snapped, the boles still standing upright inside three metres of fluted white lime with their bark perfect under a centimetre of stone.

Inside one of those columns there is a tree. It is not a fossil. It is a tree in a sheath, and the birds nest in the hollow where its crown broke off, and the seep runs off its branches and hangs them with stalactite, and it is still, in every sense that matters, doing what it did on the plateau.

So is the hectare. It goes out to the light every morning and comes back at dusk carrying what the light made. That is a canopy. It has simply been turned inside out and given wings and moved a hundred and eighty metres down. What lives in the hole is the piece of plateau that fell into it, continuing by other means.

## The frontier and the false alarm

At the north end the talus is bare, cold, sharp, black-filmed in the seams, and the springtails are on it.

The moss gets there on the birds' feet. A fragment caught in a claw, pressed into a wet seam by a bird hooking on where no bird has hooked before. That is the founding act, performed by an animal with no idea it is performing it, and forty years later the seam is a district and the district is packed and silent and lit and unfinished.

And near that end, most weeks, the house-sized block shifts a few millimetres, and the shock goes through the stone at five kilometres a second, and the whole quarter feels it in the soles of the feet a half-second before it is a sound.

Everything stops. The birds go still, the harvestmen flatten, the frogs quit knocking, the salamander freezes with one foot raised. A hundred thousand bodies holding for a thing that has not happened. They have a word for the end of the world and they say it several times a season, wrongly.

Then it passes, and the ranks resume, and the bolus goes on down the line losing its tax at every beak, and the drip you have been listening to falls again, four seconds later, exactly where it always falls — and you realise, standing on the wet step, by a very wide margin the loudest thing in the basin, that you have not been hearing one drip. You have been hearing the nearest of thousands. The place is not quiet. It is full of a conversation you are too big and too fast and too dry to be inside of, conducted at one centimetre, in stone, about who is next to whom, and whether the food is coming, and whether the thing in the tube is still alive.

It is. In nine hours a beak will find it.
