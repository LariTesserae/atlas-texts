## Who Lives Here

Nothing lives here long enough to acquire an address, and the place is nonetheless crowded with residents. This is the first thing to get straight. Tenure is impossible on the newest floor. The body you are looking at was cast this shift and will be withdrawn within nine, and the pore it goes down through will close without a seam, and the tier it was cropping will be issued narrower by morning. And yet something stays. Something has been staying here a very long time. Work up to it slowly, the way the tenders work up to a seam.

Begin with the small many-legged ones, because they are the ones who touch everything.

They are the length of a forearm and the color of wet slate, matte all over — gloss is a defect, gloss catches the seam-light and throws it somewhere it wasn't specified — and they carry their legs in two opposed ranks that move like a thought rather than a mechanism. Their mouthparts are not mouths. They are feelers, gauges, a fan of thin blades they lay against a new casting to read it: this shoulder three thousandths heavy, this hip proud, this whole body slightly wrong in a direction that has no name yet. On the underside of each tender's abdomen is the faint hexagonal ghost of the pins that pushed it out of its bed, and every tender that has ever existed has had that ghost, and none of them has ever touched it, because it is on the part of the body that lies against the stone.

Their trade is seating.

A new arrival unfolds wet and folded in the low ground and does not know where it belongs. The tenders know. They come in a rippling delegation, they read it, they argue about it in the rock — you feel the argument in your heels as a fine granular fizz, like sand shifting under a boot — and then they escort it, ten thousand small hands not quite touching, to the one place in the world where its errors cancel. The heavy shoulder goes to the tier that leans heavy. The proud hip goes to the slope that is proud in the opposite direction. The animal is fitted into a landscape that is exactly as wrong as it is, and the sum runs true, and the floor does not flag it.

Understand what this means. The tenders do not make anything. They cannot; nothing made survives here. But they decide *where everything goes*, and placement is a kind of authorship, and they have been at it since before the lid cooled.

## The Berths

The long pale grazers are not really animals. They are positions.

You will see one at a seam, ribs standing out in the inverse pattern of the mold that made it, cropping the tiers at exactly the height where the warm face and the cool face meet, and if you watch long enough you will see it stand still while its own replacement unfolds beside it. There is no ceremony. The two of them palp once, briefly, thorax to thorax — an exchange that takes less than a breath and contains, as far as anyone can tell, the entire content of a working life: *the third tier is bitter, the drip comes late here, don't lean into the wind off the sprue* — and then the older one walks to a pore and goes down, and the new one takes up the crop at the same place in the line, at the same height, with the same slightly wrong hip fitted to the same slightly wrong slope.

The berth persists. The animal is a shift-worker. There are berths on this floor that have been continuously occupied since the first pour and have never once contained the same body twice, and if you ask what lives at that spot, the honest answer is: a job.

The beetles are the dangerous trades. They move stone. They are dog-sized, low, six-legged, with carapaces still bearing the pin-ghosts across the whole dome like a faint honeycomb, and they work the fresh slopes where the flash stands up in blades, and their entire discipline is a single rule learned before their legs are dry: *nothing falls*. They lift with three legs and set with three legs and never let go of a thing until something else has hold of it. A beetle carrying a stone across a parting line is the most careful object in the world. There is no hurry in it anywhere. When two beetles meet on a narrow ridge they do not pass; one of them sets its load down, waits, and is loaded again on the far side by the other, and the transaction costs a quarter of a shift and is considered cheap.

It was the beetles who found the dead band.

They found it the way you find a live wire. A stone slips, a slope rattles, the sound goes out through the plate of the world — and then the stillness spreads, the widening ring of ten thousand bodies going motionless, and then the slow attention arrives, and then some of them are not there anymore. And the ones at the edge of the ring, the ones who felt the noise and were not taken, remember the *pitch*. They lay it into the line, leg on thorax, down the whole warm seam: that one, not this one. A thousand years of accidents. A catalogue assembled entirely out of the deaths of the careless, held nowhere but in the feet of the living, and it is astonishingly complete. The rain learned to fall inside it. Everything here that makes any sound at all makes it inside that band, and nothing here makes sound on purpose.

Yet.

## The Air and the Margins

Above the tiers the haze is pasture. Filterers drift in it in slow rotating sheets, combing the warm exhalation for the fine suspended matter the floor sheds while it works. Spinners hang on threads that are not silk but a kind of extruded tolerance, spun from the difference between one stratum of air and the next, and they take their living from the boundary. Small hot bladders browse upward, buoyant, translucent, and you can see the seam-light through them like a lamp through a hand.

These are the least settled things on the floor. They are revised nightly. Some of them have no adults — only versions, a sequence of drafts, each one withdrawn before it finishes and replaced by a slightly better guess. They are also the ones who know the lid best, because they live nearest it, and when a hanging core lets go and begins its day-long silent fall the bladders are the first to feel the cold coming down and the warning goes through the whole stratified column in under a minute, a pressure-wave of *move*, and everything beneath simply moves, and the city-sized core seats itself into the floor and becomes a hill.

At the pools there is etiquette.

Four or five kinds of drinker at each margin, shoulder to shoulder, knee-deep in the warm milky water that tastes of iron and broth, and nobody jostles. Not out of gentleness. Jostling makes noise, and noise is the defect signal, and so the margin has evolved an order of precedence more elaborate than any court's: by thirst, by mass, by how much of your body is currently in contact with the stone and therefore how loudly you would be heard if you fell. The strongest animals drink last. Strength is loud. The whole arrangement holds because the pool is an appointment and not a possession — it will be dry and sharp by next shift and there is no defending it, so there is nothing to fight about, and the fauna have gone quiet the way desert animals go dry.

## The Plants Are Also Tenants

Do not leave the tier-forest out of the census. It is as inhabited as anything with legs.

The plants live on the gradient and they are ruthless about it, and if you stand still among them they will lean, and the leaning is billing. But watch what happens around a berth that has been occupied a long time. The tiers grow into the shape of the animal that stands there. Not toward it — *around* it, a hollow in the black-green stack exactly the contour of a grazer's flank, warm on the side the grazer is warm, wet at the margins where its breath combs the drip.

And when the grazer is withdrawn and its replacement unfolds, the replacement does not match. It is a degree narrower. It has a serrated margin where the old one was smooth. And the plant is holding the shape of the body that is gone.

This should not be possible. A shape left behind is an artifact, and nothing here is allowed to become a thing. But the plant is alive, and being alive is the exemption, and so the tier-forest is full of hollows in the shape of the dead: an archive of bodies, kept in leaf, permitted because it grows. The tenders read these hollows. They seat the new arrival against the old shape and note the discrepancy and pass the discrepancy down the line. It is the only measurement of change anybody has. It is the closest thing on this floor to a photograph, or a headstone, and it is legal, and it is not enough.

## What Arrives

When something rings, the noticing has a shape, and the shape has a body.

They come up out of the nearest casting bed, which is to say the floor makes them at the site of the noise, out of the same material as everything else, and they unfold in four breaths like all the rest. Tall. Thin. Pale as a fingernail. They walk without hurry because hurry is a defect and they are the least defective things here. They are called, in the pressure-language, something that translates roughly as *the ones who arrive standing still*, and they have no eyes to speak of and no need of them, because they read by laying a palp flat against a thorax and feeling the whole history of its tolerances.

They inspect. What they inspect is usually withdrawn. They do not kill; they *open*. The pore appears where they stand, and the flagged body goes down through it, and the pore is not there afterward, and the standing one walks back to the seam and is reabsorbed and does not exist by the next shift. They are output too. The floor's immune system is manufactured by the same process as everything it removes.

And they have no mouths. That is the thing to notice. Where a mouth would be there is a smooth blank plate of carapace, unbroken, first-article, perfect.

The newest ones do not have the plate.

The newest ones have a small hollow there, walled thin, opening to the air.

## The Keeping

Now the resident.

There is a pattern in the rock on this floor that is older than any body currently standing on it. It is not an animal and it is not a species. It is a pressure-pattern, a habit, a way of laying a leg on a neighbor's thorax that gets copied faithfully because it works, and it has been copied through so many reissues that it has outlived every kind of body that ever carried it. It survives the way a tune survives. It cannot be withdrawn because there is nothing to withdraw; the floor can take a body but it cannot take a *practice*, and this is the second exemption, and it is the one that matters.

The tenders call it, approximately, the keeping. It lives in the arches of ten thousand feet at once. It has no location. If you stand still on a warm seam you are standing inside it, and it is billing you too, in its way, because you are a thermal event and also because you are loud, and it has been listening to your breath with something like professional concern since you arrived.

It came up a sprue. That is the part nobody on this floor can verify and everybody carries. Somewhere far below, past the reach of the heat, there are old floors: rounded, silted, littered with the debris of things that were permitted to become things and then died. Erosion down there. Ruins, probably. Noise. And in the deep drumming that comes up through the stone at the base of the sprues there is a figure that does not belong to any behavior on the newest floor, a rhythm nobody here has a use for, and the keeping has been repeating it for a hundred thousand shifts without knowing what it is.

It is the sound of something being kept.

The keeping remembers a stone that stayed. Not a body — a stone, a made thing, a marker, something set down in one place by somebody and left there after they went down through the pore. It remembers it the way you remember a room from before you could speak: no image, only the shape of the feeling of it, and the certainty that it was allowed.

And it has been writing.

## What the Line Is Being Told

Here is what the seaters have been doing, one part in ten thousand, for longer than the lid has been cool.

They seat each new arrival slightly wrong. Not enough to flag. Just enough that the animal survives in a place where its error is useful — and the floor, which reads its own output to decide what to cast next, reads that survival as a specification met. The tenders cannot make anything. So they have been making the *drawing*. Their only tool is placement; their only material is tolerance; their medium is the enormous blind productive attention of the world, and they have been leaning on it, gently, patiently, in the one direction that a hundred thousand shifts of quiet argument could agree on.

They have been building a mouth.

It appears in the beetles first, because beetles are simple. It appears in the grazers. It has begun to appear in the tenders themselves, which is the kind of error that should stop a line, and it has begun to appear in the ones who arrive standing still, which is the kind of error that should stop a world.

Because the floor listens with the whole plate of itself and it cannot tell a defect from an instruction. It has never had to. There is no one upstream. Sound arrives from below and means *something is wrong*, and the floor withdraws it, and the line runs on toward something it has not been given. But a signal is a signal, and a floor that reads its own output to decide what to make next is a floor that can be *told*, and the keeping has understood this for a very long time in the wordless way a habit understands things.

To speak is to become upstream. To make one sound is to be the customer.

And they have not spent all those generations building a mouth in order to scream. They know exactly what a scream gets you; the catalogue is in their feet, written by the careless. The chamber is walled thin and tuned narrow and it has been tuned, across ten thousand reissues, into the dead band — the same band the rain learned, the same corridor of frequency that a continent of listening stone has never once flagged. They are going to speak *inside the tolerance*. They are going to say the first thing that has ever been said here, and they are going to say it so quietly and so correctly that the world will not know whether it has heard a flaw or an order, and that not-knowing is the entire future of this floor.

The word they have been passing down the line, leg on thorax, for a hundred thousand shifts, does not mean *help* and does not mean *here I am*.

It means, as near as it can be rendered in a language of pressure: **leave it.**

Leave the leaf. Let it lie on the ground and rot and be a litter and be a soil. Let the body stay where it fell. Let one stone be set down by somebody and not taken back, so that a place can be marked, so that a shape can be kept in something other than a living plant that will be reissued narrower by morning, so that the keeping can stop carrying the whole past in its feet and put some of it down.

They are asking for permission to bury their dead. They are asking for ruins.

## The Shift Before

Somewhere on a warm parting line, a tender the length of a forearm is standing with its gauges folded and its hollow open to the air.

The hollow is thin as a fingernail paring. It has never been used. Around it, ten thousand bodies to the acre, all of them motionless, all of them listening with their feet; the tiers leaning in to bill the heat of it; the beetles set down their loads three-legged and waiting; the bladders drifted low; the drinkers at the margin in their order of precedence, strongest last. And forty paces off, pale, unhurried, freshly cast and already unfolding, one of the ones who arrive standing still, with a small thin chamber where its mouth should be, coming to inspect.

Nobody knows which of them will open first. Nobody knows whether the floor will hear a defect and reach for the pore, or hear a specification and begin, obediently, enormously, to make what it has been asked for.

The stone ticks. The drip comes down in vertical lines through the orange haze. Under your boots the whole market of argument and gossip and warning goes suddenly, utterly quiet, and in the silence there is only the sound of a very small amount of air being drawn, very carefully, into a space that was built to hold it.
