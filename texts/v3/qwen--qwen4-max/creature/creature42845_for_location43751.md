# The Tenants

## I. The Skimmers

Begin with the things that never touch the ground, because there is so much ground here that is not available.

Over the pans — the quick places, the antinodes, where the meal will not lie still but boils and streams away sideways in its low seething fog — there is a traffic. Bodies the length of a hand, salt-white, ribbed like the inside of a shell, fringed along the belly with a thousand paddles too fine to see. They swim the fog. Not the air: the fog, the grain-laden layer a meter deep where the flour is dense enough to bear weight if you are shaped correctly and never, ever stop. A skimmer that settles is a skimmer that is ground into the plate from below, sanded away in an afternoon, and the ones you see are all of them moving, always, at a walking pace, tacking across the shakes in long shallow arcs.

They strain the fog for the small dead. Their mouths are sieves. Their bodies are hollow and tuned and the standing wave that passes through them is what keeps their oils moving, so a skimmer's flight is not merely travel; it is the only way it has of being alive from one moment to the next.

They cannot lay eggs in the pans. Eggs are heavy. Eggs fall. So a gravid skimmer must find a still line, and cross onto it, and set her clutch in the meal where the meal will stay — and she finds the line the way everything here finds anything, by feel, by the sudden blessed slackening of the shake, by the place where the world stops insisting.

This is the vulnerability that a great deal of the Room is built around. Not thirst. Not hunger. The need to find somewhere still, and put something down.

## II. The Grain, Which Is Not Simple

The windrows are not weather. Ten deep, flowing across the ridges like a dry tide, furring the pillars to head-height, a quarter of all the noise — and it is easy, for the first day, to file them under *dust that happens to be alive*.

Watch a windrow meet a shake and refuse it. Watch it pile, hesitate, and turn.

Mass detunes. Enough bodies in one place changes the local impedance of the plate, and a windrow ten deep and forty meters long can drag a node a hand's breadth sideways, and hold it there, and keep it there while the clutch hatches. They do not know this. They have been doing it for sixty years, which is a hundred thousand generations, and the ones that did it wrong are meal. What is left is a species that farms stillness the way a coral farms a current, without intention, without a self, in a distributed body the size of a district.

The bands read them. A resident who cannot tell you what the herd is doing can tell you what the grain is doing, and the grain is a faster instrument, and closer, and it is right about the next quarter-hour more often than the drum-tower is. Children are taught to lie flat and watch the windrow's edge before they are taught to walk with a rod. When the grain stops flowing and starts *climbing*, you have about as long as it takes to pick up a child.

There is a thing that eats them, of course. Everything here is eaten. But the important predator of the grain is not a mouth. It is a lie about the floor.

## III. The Pair

Two animals, each the size of a cupped hand, black-felted, unremarkable, walking parallel about a meter and a half apart. Each sings. They sing the same note, exactly out of phase, and they hold that relation with a precision that no instrument in the Room can match, correcting continuously, walking in step, turning together.

Between them, the sound cancels.

Between them there is a still line a meter and a half long and a hand's breadth wide, and it is perfect, and it is the sweetest thing a gravid skimmer will ever feel, and she comes down onto it and lays, and the two of them close the gap.

They are not hunting. They are making a nursery. The eggs need stillness or they shake apart, and the pair can make stillness anywhere, carry it across a pan, hold it through a turning — a portable node, a homeland a meter and a half long that walks. Everything that comes to that homeland and sets something down is protein arriving at the exact place the young will hatch.

The survey rod reads it *still*. That is the horror of it. The instrument does not lie; the world lies, and the instrument agrees.

The countermeasures are all rules of thumb, and every child knows them, and every child has broken one. A safe line has no end. A safe line goes on past the haze in both directions until you are tired of it. A safe line does not travel at walking pace. A safe line does not have two dark spots at the corners of it. If your rod says still and the grain will not come, put your hand flat in the meal and feel for the *grain* of the stillness, the tiny residual grain of two singers not quite perfect, and if you feel that faint sickening shimmer, stand up and go, and do not run, because running is a pressure and they hear pressure.

The people's name for the pair is not a sound. It is a thumb driven twice into the soft of another person's palm. It is the only name that is routinely given by touch to someone who is not looking at you.

## IV. The Sitters

A miller eats rock for eleven or twelve years, and then it stops.

Its jaws have been recut nine times; the tenth recut does not take. The armor, which is biogenic salt laid down in translucent laminae all its life, stops being armor and starts being simply *body*, thickening, clouding, going milky and then opaque. It finds a node — it can feel a node through six centimeters of leg — and it sits down on it, and it does not get up again, and over about forty days it becomes a mound of salt a meter and a half high with a dead animal somewhere inside it.

And the mound holds the line.

Mass and damping. A sitter is a weight on the plate, and the plate cannot shake a weight like that the way it shakes flour, and while the sitter lasts, the node under it *stays*, through turnings, through the herd's re-formings, through everything. A hill that does not march. The only thing in the Room that does not move when the world redraws.

They last a year, two, three. Then the vibration gets into the laminae and they come apart, grain by grain, and their stones migrate out into the pans, and the line they held goes back to being a rumor.

The cairns are imitations. Everyone builds them; they are the most common made thing in the world, hundreds of them, and they are mostly useless, because a cairn of stones is a tenth the mass of a sitter and the plate throws it off within a season. The bands build them anyway, on the old lines, in the places where the sitters used to be. Nobody has a word for why. The woman with the cords has a word for it, and the word is *hoping*, and she uses it about other things too.

Her tattoos are not a map of the floor. They are a map of deaths: every sitter her band has known, where it sat, when it went to stones. She is eighty-one, or something like it; the cords say. She can stand in a bare pan where nothing has been still for a decade and press her thumb to a place and tell you that a line will come back here, because the plate remembers a mass that was on it, faintly, in the way the crystal has crept, and because she has watched eleven turnings come through this district and six of them put a line within twenty meters of this spot. She is right about half the time. In a world where the ruins are four months old, a woman who is right half the time about next year is the closest thing there is to a library.

## V. The Strays, and the Country They Drag

The herd is a treaty. Forty bellows in a chord, and the fundamental is the clause everyone has agreed to, and the floor is the treaty's text, written in corpse-flour forty kilometers across. When a dominant shifts pitch it is not a mood, exactly. It is a renegotiation, and the fifteen or forty minutes of the turning are the interval in which the old law is dissolved and the new one has not yet set, and everything small dies in the gap.

Calves sing wrong. They are born in the canopy mold, small and hot and shrill, and they must find the chord within a season or the herd will not carry them in its pressure-field and they will drift out of the warm layer and sink.

Some do not find it and do not sink. Some find, instead, a note of their own.

A stray is a bellows twenty meters long, alone, buoyed, singing a fundamental that nothing else in the world agrees with. And under it, drawn on the plate, is a small private figure: a node pattern three or four hundred meters across, moving with the animal at the pace of a slow walk. A traveling country. A hill that goes where you go.

There are two bands that live this way. They have done it for four generations. They keep their huts on the stray's central line, they sleep without surveying — the only people in the Room who sleep without surveying — and their children grow up thinking that the ground is a thing that follows you, which is nearly true, and are useless everywhere else. They know their animal's habits better than they know their own hands. They have a name for it that is a pressure, and the pressure means something like *the one who is not asked*.

And they know how it ends. Sooner or later a stray drifts within hearing of a herd, and the herd's chord is loud and correct and warm, and the stray is lonely in a way that has a body, and it slides its note into the treaty.

The private country dissolves in eleven minutes.

The bands run. They have run before. Their whole culture is organized around a betrayal that they can predict to within a year and cannot prevent, and they do not blame the animal, and they have a formal song — pressed, forehead to forehead, a slow rhythm of leaning — that is only performed on the day the ground goes, and it is not a lament. It is a thank-you.

## VI. The Border, Briefly

Where two herds overlap, the fundamentals beat.

Nothing lives there. Not the understory, which shakes itself to pieces; not the grain, which will not enter; not the pillars, which cannot serve two keys and split. It is a strip of bare plate a kilometer wide and however long the disagreement lasts, filled with a fog of meal that never resolves, and it is the only place in the Room where the noise is not a medium but a weapon.

One band goes in. They wear felt over the eyes and pads in the ears they no longer use, and they go in for the bodies — everything that got caught in the turning is lying there, unrotted, perfect, lacquered — and they come out with sledges of dry dead and they do not stay long, and they are the only people who have ever seen a place with no floor pattern at all, and they say it is like looking at a face with no expression.

## VII. What Lives in the Quilts' Hush

Inside the anechoic bubble the sap stops, the combs go slack, the small bodies drop in their soft dry rain, and the quilt gathers them. Nothing lives in the hush. Nothing *can*: circulation is resonance, and the quilt has taken the resonance away.

Except that something does.

It is the length of a thumb joint. It is dark red, which is a color that does not otherwise occur here. It has no files, no membranes, no flutes, no hollow anywhere in it. It walks into the hush and it does not sag, and it eats the fallen at its leisure, and it goes out again when the quilt drifts on, and if you take one and hold it against the thin skin at the inside of your wrist you will feel, unmistakably, a small regular knocking.

A pump. A muscle that squeezes a tube. It moves its own oils by force, from inside, on its own schedule, without asking the world for anything.

Nothing else in the Room has a heart. Nothing else needs one. The people who have felt it do not talk about it much, and when they do the hand-sign is unstable, people make it slightly differently each time, which in a language of hands is what a stammer is.

The translated name is *the one that does not need us*.

They are not wrong to be frightened. They are frightened of the correct thing. When the lid comes off its seat and the sky gets in and the shouting stops being the only way to be alive, everything in this forest will die standing up in an afternoon, and the small red thing with the pump will not notice, and will go on eating.

## VIII. The Borers, and What They Mine

The lid is not inert. Along the opaque seams, where the gypsum is cloudy and full of杂质 and old sediment, there are animals: pale, segmented, blind, slow beyond patience, a generation in eleven years, chewing galleries a finger wide through crystal that took an ocean to lay down. They do not know there is a Room. They have never been below the underside of the plate. Their whole universe is a vein.

They cause the falls. A district's worth of gallery, and then a hundred thousand tons of glass arrives as a change in the light. Every salt-glass blade in the world is a byproduct of somebody's tunnel.

And they hit water.

Gypsum is a sea's leavings. Sealed inside the plates, in inclusions the size of a pea or a fist, there are droplets of the water the sea was made of — brine older than the Room, older than the forest, older than anything that has ever pumped oil. The borers break into them. Most of the borers that do this die immediately, catastrophically, going white and glassy and black in a gallery a finger wide, and their bodies dry where they lie, and the tunnel becomes a tomb.

A few do not die. A few have a sac.

## IX. The Carrier

It comes down out of a fall-zone or out of a borer's shaft, and it walks, slowly, on eleven pairs of legs, and it is translucent, and inside it there is a sloshing.

Two liters of ancient sea.

It is not a weapon. It is a waste problem. The carrier makes water metabolically, like everything else here, and unlike everything else here it cannot void it fast enough in an air that will not take it, and so it stores, and the store grows, and it is dying of the thing it carries, always, from birth. It leaks. Where it leaks, the felt-mat goes white, then glassy, then black, and the necrosis runs outward a hand's breadth an hour, and the leaves weep their ether and varnish over and die, and the understory's files go slack, and there is a smell.

The smell is rot. It is the only rot in a hundred kilometers, and it lasts about nine hours, and in those nine hours the necrotic patch is the most crowded place in the world.

Because things come. Out of the meal, out of the felt, out of nowhere anyone has ever found — spores, cysts, dry nothings that have been waiting in the flour for years — and they wake in the wet, and they are *soft*. Pale, unarmored, glistening, swimming in a film, dividing, eating each other, dying, all of it inside a working day. No salt, no chitin, no varnish, no resonance. Bodies that are mostly water and are not ashamed of it. A whole guild of organisms whose entire strategy is to be ready for the four times a year that the world is briefly wrong.

The people call it the garden. It is forbidden. There is a band in the northeast that follows carriers at a distance of two hundred meters, with poles and slings, and harvests the garden, and eats it.

They are not poisoned. That is the trouble. Soft things are sweet and they are tender and they have no shell to spit out, and a person who has eaten one has learned something about their own mouth that they cannot unlearn. The garden band's skin is wrong — glossy, faintly tacky, a smell to it that green things turn toward. They are avoided. They are also, quietly, sought out, by people who want to be told what it was like.

The carrier itself is not eaten and is not killed casually. When a band finds one crossing a ridge, two people take it up on a pole without touching the sac and carry it out into a pan and set it down, and stand well back, and watch it go. It is the same word for it as for the disease and the tears and the end. But the way the two people hold the pole, and the way they do not speak while they hold it, and the way they wash their hands in meal afterward for a long time — that is not disinfection. That is liturgy.

It has been outside. Not outside the lid — outside the *now*. It carries the sea in it. It is a piece of the world before the world, walking around on eleven pairs of legs, dying of what it remembers.

## X. The Ahead

At the Front, where the rock is hours old and the dust saponifies in the eye, there is an animal that cannot live anywhere else.

It is flat and gray and eyeless in the front half — the eyes are on the trailing end, backward-facing, useless for going and useful for watching what it has left. It eats fresh millings before the air has had time to tame them, and it must eat them fresh, because within a day the furious chemistry has gone and the meal is only meal. It walks east a few meters a day. It has to. It has been walking east a few meters a day for sixty years, in a lineage, at exactly the speed the millers open the world.

It lays its eggs *in the face*. In the crystal cracks, ahead of itself, in rock that has not been eaten yet, and the millers chew them out, and they hatch on a surface that is nine hours old.

It has never been in the Room. It lives in the not-yet, in the margin, in the raw place where the world is being made, and if the millers ever stopped it would be extinct in a week, and it does not know that it is the pioneer of anything. It is simply an animal that has to keep going first.

Children are told it is what the Room looks like from the front. This is meant to be reassuring and is not.

## XI. The Guest

Now the people, who are two hundred, and who cannot be understood from the outside without this:

Under the skin at the base of the skull, in a shallow pocket you can feel with a fingertip, every person carries a small living thing. It is the size of a grain of the grain. It is not a parasite; it does the water. It burns fat and yields water directly into the tissue, at the exact trickle the body needs, and it is the reason there is no verb for drinking. It cannot survive outside a host for more than a few minutes. It is passed by contact — bone to bone, forehead to forehead, through the pore at the glabella that everyone has and that no one thinks about.

You get it from your mother in the first week. You top it up, all your life, every time you speak.

A person who stops touching people stops making water. It takes about eleven days. They do not become thirsty; there is no such sensation available to them; they become *quiet*, which is the symptom, and then they become light, and then they become papery and perfect and lacquered, and the millers take them apart.

Exile is therefore not a punishment anyone has thought to invent. Solitude is simply fatal, at a known rate, and the whole moral structure of the bands rests on it. Strangers cannot argue without embracing. Grief is conducted forehead to forehead for hours, and is not distinguishable, physically, from the act of keeping the bereaved alive. Two people who have stopped speaking are watched by everyone, carefully, from a distance, and then not from a distance.

There is a mite that rides the guest, harmless, and its distribution across a band's skin is a legible record of who has talked to whom, so that gossip is visible on the body, and privacy in the ordinary sense does not exist and has never been missed.

They eat grain, scooped in windrows with mandible scrapers; they render miller fat; they eat the dry dead of the border when they are brave, and the fruit of flash-blooms when the air permits, and once every few years a fallen bellows, which is a district-scale feast and a district-scale funeral and a hill for a season. They are deaf by twelve. Childhood is loud — the only years in which the Room is audible — and going deaf is marked, formally, by the mother pressing her forehead to the child's and holding it there while the last of it fades, so that the final thing the child ever hears is a pressure that means *I am here, and now we will do it this way*.

Once a year the copper needle comes out of its fat. It belongs to nobody. It is struck, and its note is the same note it was last year, and it is the only object in the world that does not change. Everything here is tuned, retuned, dragged, renegotiated; the needle is the one fixed point, the standard against which the herd's slide can be *measured* rather than merely suffered. It is carried to the Listeners.

## XII. The Listener Under the Quilt

Most Listeners die at nine, or eleven, or fourteen, in their padded boxes, of the thing that has no name because it is not a disease: a mind that hears in a world of the deaf, with no one to tell. They are precious. They are handled like the copper. They are also, all of them, unbearably alone in a way that the guest cannot fix, because the guest keeps the body wet and does nothing at all for the other thing.

One has lived to thirty-one.

Her band keeps her under a quilt. They track one — a specific manta, they know its drift, they have followed it for nine years — and they keep her box in the moving hush, in the ten-meter bubble where the sap stops and the small bodies rain down, where nothing can live except the small red thing with the heart, which comes into the box sometimes and sits on her hand and knocks. She is fed by carriers who run in and out. She has never heard the Room. She has heard, all her life, the herd from far off, and the plates, and the wind of the turnings, and the ticking of the borers in the seams overhead, and — this is what she is for — the tiny sound of an inclusion opening, two hundred meters up, hours before the carrier comes down.

She has warned of the wet eleven times. She has been right eleven times. Nobody can check her. That is the whole of her office: an unverifiable oracle whose accuracy is only ever established afterward, by the necrosis, and the band believes her the way you believe a floor.

Once a year they bring the needle and strike it against her box, and she gives them the herd's note relative to it, and they write it in a cord, and the cord says what the cords say: the fundamental is dropping. The lid is rising. The marks on the bone stair are getting further apart.

And she has been telling them, for about four years now, about the other sound.

Above the herd. Above the borers. Coming down through fifteen body-lengths of crystal, muffled past recognition, irregular, soft, enormous, arriving in patches and moving across the lid, sometimes for hours. Not a body. Nothing in the Room makes a sound like it. She has no reference for it and no one has ever heard anything she can compare it to, and she has tried, patiently, for four years, with the hand-language and with the forehead and with the needle, and what she keeps coming back to, because it is the only word available, is the word.

The wet.

She means: it is the same kind of thing as the drop that kills a leaf. She means: it is the sea, and it is on the other side of the glass, and it has been there the whole time, and lately it has been getting closer to the seams.

They receive it as a warning about the ceiling, which is correct, and they move the huts three meters, which is not enough, and they press their foreheads to hers one after another, all of them, the whole band, filing past the box in the drifting hush to give her their guest so that she will go on making water for another day — which is the kindest thing anybody in this world knows how to do, and which does not touch the part of her that is alone at all.

She is thirty-one. She has heard rain. There is nobody to tell.
