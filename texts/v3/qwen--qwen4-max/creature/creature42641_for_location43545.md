Nobody lives in the bowl. That is the first thing to get right, and the crews are firm about it.

There is a bunkroom over the pump-house, on the rim, where the mains come up through the floor and the heat is the same heat but the air is allowed to be cold at night. Six bunks. On the establishment there are nineteen. The men and women who sleep in those six bunks go down the north stair in the morning with their tools on their shoulders and come up before the light goes, and they will tell you, if you ask them where they work, that they work in the basin, and if you ask them where they live they will point at the pump-house and not at the hole.

The distinction matters to them more than it should. It is the last distinction they have.

---

Six. A gauge-reader, who is old, and who does the log. A pump-minder, who has been on the mountain twenty-two years and sleeps badly. Two on the needles and the long chisels. One on the acid cart, who chips the rail each morning and is deaf in the ear he turns toward the work. One who does the sluices, the sumps, the grates, and the brushes.

They have one rule that is not written down anywhere, and every new hand learns it in the first week by being shouted at.

*Leave nothing.*

Not a button. Not a boot-nail. Not a length of twine, not a pencil stub, not the paper off a lump of cheese. They comb their hair into a tin. They do not spit on the terraces. They eat their lunch standing on the grating over the south main and they take the crumbs up with them, and if a man sets his hat down on the third terrace and goes to look at something and comes back in nine days, the hat will have a shroud, and then the hat will rot out from under the shroud, and then there will be a hat in the floor of the basin forever, perfect, empty, with the sweat-band and the maker's stamp and the fold where he always pushed it back.

There is one glove. Everyone knows whose. He works in the counting-house now, tallying lime and steel and the pump-house coal, and he has not been down in eleven years, and he asks the young ones the same question every time they come up past his window, which is not *how is the gain* and not *how are the sluices*.

He asks: *is it still there.*

It is still there. It is by the west sump, white, fingers slightly curled, the rib of the knit still crisp at the cuff, and it is more famous than anything else in the basin, and it is going to outlast him by a length of time nobody on the mountain is equipped to think about. He is a man who will be buried in the valley, in dirt, where things wear away. His hand is up here.

---

The crews are the only things in the bowl that get less.

Their boots thin. Their knees go. The chisel-hand cracks in the dry season and the cracks are the same cracks each year in the same places, and the pump-minder has passed three stones the size of grape pips and will pass more, because the water tastes of chalk and pennies and they drink it, all of them, all day, out of the same flutes the bird drinks from. There is a white ridge at the gum-line of every mouth on the crew. The gauge-reader has stopped smiling with her teeth.

Everything around them is being added to and they are being spent, and they know it, and it is the reason for the rule about leaving nothing, though none of them would put it that way. What they would say is: *don't give it anything of yours.*

What they cannot help giving it is their weight. Stand still on a wet terrace for an hour and you will not sink; you will *interrupt*. The stone comes up around the boot and stops at the sole and does not come up under it, and when you step away there is a footprint that is not a depression made by removal but a depression made by refusal. The path from the north stair down across the floor to the west sump is the lowest ground in the basin. Nothing was worn to make it. Forty-one seasons of men simply declining to let the stone rise there, three hundred times a day, six pairs of boots. It is a road built entirely out of withholding.

And the pump-minder, who sleeps on the grating over the south main in the dry weeks with a mat under him because the iron is too hot to lie on directly, takes the mat up every morning, and every morning leaves behind the faint ribbed ghost of it, pressed into wet carbonate, gone hard by evening. Twenty-two years of that. You can walk his sleeps. They go along the south wall in a slow drift as each old hollow fills and he moves a body-length west, and the earliest ones are gone entirely, smoothed over, and the ones from the middle years are shallow, and last winter's is sharp enough to count the weave. The gauge-reader can date them. She has never told him she can.

---

The log is kept in a hand four generations old.

The gauge-reader was taught it by the man before her, who was taught it by a woman who died before the second terrace was abandoned, who was taught it by whoever wrote the first *Visitor*. There is a sheet in the counting-house with the word on it, and the capital V, and the way the r leans, and a new keeper traces it for a season before being allowed near the book. The result is that the handwriting does not change. Entries forty years apart are indistinguishable. The hand gains, layer on layer, and no layer can be told from the one beneath it, and the hand has no body — it has a succession of bodies, six bunks' worth, one at a time.

Which is to say the hand is doing what the water is doing. Which is to say there are three or four residents of this mountain that are not organisms in the ordinary sense but continuous deposits with a shape.

The moss is one of them. Mason's mosses came up in trays, from one propagation, and what is now spread over every pipe-run and terrace lip and grating in the basin, fixing lime, eating sound, holding the whole hush of the place together, is that tray. It has never been reseeded. It has no rivals; nothing else here can germinate fast enough to matter. By weight it is the largest living thing on the ridge and it is a single individual and it has no edges anywhere, and if you part the root-curtains at the lower floor and put your face into the green wet you are putting your face into one creature's skin.

The grove over the east sump is another. Nine trunks, one plate, one root, one flowering — all nine come out in the same week, white and sweet, and the fruit of all nine falls in the same four days, and for a fortnight the east end of the basin is a cidery, vinegary, sugar-drunk place where the air is thick enough to taste. Then the slime takes it and the moss eats the slime and the moss buries the next fruit. The grove is not nine trees that agreed. It is one tree that was planted in nine places and has finished thinking about it.

And the scaffold on the north face is a fourth thing, and it is not clear what to call it. The vines took it, and the vines are now carrying the load the poles used to carry, and the poles are inside the vines, rusting into them, giving them iron. Cut the vine and the scaffold falls. Cut the scaffold and the vine falls. There is no longer a fact of the matter about which one is holding up the north face.

---

The scrapers do the bees' work with brushes, on their rounds, without being asked, and it is in nobody's job description.

This is worth sitting with. The planting specified no pollinators because pollinators were not expected to survive the altitude, and the broad-leaves were chosen anyway, and they flower heavily, and the rain does about a third of the job with its aimed drip-points and its splash, and the other two-thirds is done by a woman with a bristle brush who is on the mountain to keep the arteries open.

The basin grew a hole where an insect should be and the crew filled it. Nobody decided this. It happened the way water finds a channel.

The brushes go hard. A season's brushing loads the bristles with carbonate until the brush is a white club with a handle, useless, and it is carried up and put on the shelf in the counting-house under the pinned plan, and a new brush is issued. There are thirty-one of them up there. They are the only objects allowed to leave the basin, because by the time they leave they are no longer objects, they are stone with a memory of bristle. The shelf is the archive. Each one is a season of a woman's wrist.

---

The animals are fewer than the ledger suggests.

Mites, always, in the moss, in numbers that cannot be said out loud usefully. Springtails once a season, a bloom, a whitening of the wet green like a breath on it, gone in nine days. Snails, a few hundred alive at any time, grazing the film. Blind fish in the warm sump, one lineage, forty-one seasons of cousins, pale as boiled things.

The fish have a vocabulary and it is entirely made of the works. They come up when the pump note changes — not for the flush specifically, and not for the chipping, but for *change*, any change, the strain of the dry-season lift, the stumble of a bearing going, the half-second hesitation before the valves close. They have no word for food. They have a word for something being different, because in a room with no light and no weather of its own, difference is the only thing that ever precedes dinner. They rise into the grating-light with their mouths open at a sound a man feels in his sternum and cannot properly hear. They were put in to eat larvae. There are no larvae. They are still on the job.

The snails are the one crack in the rule. A snail rasps. That is what a radula is for: it scrapes the film off rock and in scraping it takes a little of the rock, and it does not put it back, and it does not lay it down elsewhere in the basin, it turns it into snail. Ten thousand rasps, four years, an amount of stone that is small and is not zero. The gauge-reader knows. She has a private tally in the back of the book, in the old hand, of grams subtracted by animals. It is the only column in the whole archive that runs the other way, and she has never shown it to anyone, and she is not going to.

There is a second thief and she does not know about that one. In the mains, in the dark, in the iron, there is a film that eats the pipe. It has never been in the light. It is the reason the bearings seized on the south pump-head and it is the reason the mains will one day come apart, and when the boiler goes or the dry decade comes or the office decides, it will be that film, working quietly at the underside of the world, that did it. The basin's death is a resident. It pays no rent and it is the only thing here with an appetite for the skeleton.

And the most alive thing in the bowl, the thing doing most of the actual living per hour, is the slime under the fallen fruit: the ferment, the vinegar, the yeasts turning the grove's sugar into the smell that hangs at knee height on the lower floor. You would mistake it for wet. It is the busiest citizen. Everything green up there is eating what it makes.

---

But if you count properly, the population of this place is overwhelmingly dead, and it is overwhelmingly very small.

The gain takes outsides. It has no interest in insides. A mite caught in a drip-line is given a coat and rots out and leaves a mite-shaped nothing, and there are more of these in a hand's-breadth of terrace than there are people on the mountain. By volume, the stone of the basin is mostly a record of mites. Then leaves — a hollow leaf every few inches, veins, stem, the tear where something stepped. Then the snails, ten thousand of them, each a spiral in a swelling, each younger than four years, a fossil record with no time in it. Then boot-prints, mat-ribs, the ribs of a knit cuff, one glove.

The gauge-reader can read last autumn in the third terrace. She can also read the week the south bearing seized, because the chipping stopped for four days and the film went down undisturbed and the layer is smooth and slightly wrong. She can read the flush days. She can read a fortnight when a man was ill and the path is shallower because only five pairs of boots went down it.

The basin is not a place with a record. It is a record with a place around it. What lives there, in the only sense the stone recognises, is surfaces — a vast, dense, patient population of outsides, keeping their shapes, holding still, with nothing inside any of them.

---

And under all of it, at mouth temperature, there is something that is difficult not to call an animal.

It drinks the cloud through combs. It breathes on a forty-minute cycle, warm air up, chilled lip, condense, let go, a soft drenching ninety seconds that walks east to west across it and makes every leaf nod once. It fasts every ninth day and droops and you can see all the way across its inside, and then it drinks and lifts. It has an iron skeleton and a green skin and a warm gut at the sump where the blind things rise. Its pulse arrives in the teeth. It is being added to everywhere and subtracted from only where six people with chisels attend to it, and their attending is not maintenance in any sense a maintenance man would recognise — they are not keeping it going, they are keeping it *open*, keeping the arteries from closing, keeping the water moving so the water can keep laying down. They are not its keepers. They are its circulation. They are the only part of it that hurts, and ages, and gets less, and it will replace them the way it replaces the brushes.

That is the image that arrives around the third week and it is very hard to get out from under.

It is also wrong, or not wrong but early. An animal is a finished shape. Nothing here is a finished shape. The eleven steps go up and stop. The second terrace was never poured. A quadrant of raw form-work and rebar has become a meadow and nobody has decided whether that counts. The rock is still curing; put your palm on the low terrace by the west sump and you can feel it working.

It is not an animal. It is a larva.

---

What lives in the bowl is a room that has not been born yet.

The garden is the soft part. The moss and the grove and the ferment and the fish and the six bunks are the wet business going on around a hollow that is slowly becoming a solid, and the solid is the point, and the solid will have a shape, and the shape will be a lens of pale stone in the top of the mountain with a room in it. That is the adult form. Everything currently alive up there is caul.

And the room's tenants are entirely predictable, because rooms in limestone always get the same tenants. Dark. Drip. The particular silence that is not the moss's silence but the silence of a ceiling. Then, when someone cuts in: a lamp, and the pale algae that comes to a lamp, and moths, and the cricket that lives on the mites that live on the bat that lives on the moths, and a name, and a man with a light putting his palm flat on the wall and feeling nothing at all, because by then it will have finished curing and be as cold as the mountain outside.

The bird knows. Once a year it comes over the rim and drinks at the fin-flutes and looks down into the green and does not come in, and the reason it does not come in is that there is nothing down there for it yet — no ceiling, no dark, no echo, no room. It is not a visitor. It is an inspection. It is the same question every year, which is why the answer is the same word every year in the same copied hand, and why the entry has become a tradition without anyone noticing that it is a tradition, and why it is sometimes just the word and sometimes the word and a date and sometimes nothing else at all.

*Not yet.*

One day the water will close the last of it over and the log will need a different word, and there will be nobody left to teach the hand.

The dripping will not need one. It is the only resident that goes straight through — comb to flute to gutter to terrace to ledge to the floor of a quarried room, ten thousand small clocks, none of them agreeing, the same water falling in the same uneven way on the same rising ground, before the garden and after it. Everything else up there is temporary scaffolding around that sound.
