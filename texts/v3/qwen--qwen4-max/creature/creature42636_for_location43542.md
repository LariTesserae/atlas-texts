# The Orchard

## First answer, and why it is wrong

You would say: eleven groves of white plants. You would say it because eleven is the number the room gives you — eleven leaks, eleven riots of paper, eleven directions from which the growing comes. It is the number a person could keep in a log book.

But the groves are not eleven of anything. They are the wet places. The living thing is smeared across them the way one hand is smeared across five keys, and the boundaries you can see are the boundaries of the plumbing, not of the organism. Walk the bare stretch between grove four and grove five and you are not walking between two lives. You are walking across a gap in one life, sixty paces of drought inside a single body, and the body has no opinion about the gap, because it has never had to cross it.

It has, however, found a way to cross it. That comes later.

## The body

Begin with what it is made of, which is almost nothing.

No wood. No bark, no lignified spine, no heartwood, no cambium laying down rings against a winter that does not occur. It stands by pressure alone: stems as thin-walled tubes inflated with sweet water, turgor doing the work that oak does with cellulose and patience. A grove is a hydraulic architecture. It is up because it is full. On the two dry days after the pump runs, when the make-up line goes warm and the stripe beneath it fades to a shadow, the whole grove loses a little of its inflation and the great leaves drop their edges — not wilting dramatically, nothing so fast, just a slackening, a three-metre sail going quiet. Then the cold comes back and it stands up again. It has done this ten thousand times. It has never once been in danger of falling over, and it has never once been certain of standing.

The leaves are the part that has come loose from its origins. A leaf is a device for intercepting light and paying for the privilege in water. Here there is no light to intercept and the carbon arrives pre-paid, dissolved, at the roots, in a pipe. So the leaf kept the water and lost the job. It became enormous because enormous costs nothing when sugar is free and nothing has ever taken a bite. Three metres of tissue-thin lamina, and what it does now is *transpire*: it dumps heat, it moves the pipe's water up and out into the standing air, it cools itself by evaporation at forty-seven degrees, and in the coolest hours of the eleven-day cycle — the hours just after the cold returns — it runs backwards, and beads form on the undersides of the leaves, and the grove drips on itself.

There is weather in Bay Six. It is a millimetre thick and it happens under the canopy. Grove four has a shade temperature four degrees below the hall and a humidity you can feel on your face like a breath, and the air inside it moves, faintly, downward, cool air sliding off the leaves and out along the floor to the bare stretch where it dies. Eleven small climates. Eleven lungs. The orchard has begun to make its own conditions, which is the first thing a world does.

And it is warm. Cut a stem and the syrup comes out faintly warm, and this is not the room's heat: at forty-seven degrees a plant should be a heat sink, and it is not, because it is burning sugar in the dark as fast as its enzymes will allow, all the time, everywhere, in every cell. Eleven respiration fires. Some fraction of the warmth you felt through the steel of the door with the palms of both hands was breath.

## The wrongness, and where it goes

Glycol is sugar with a wrongness in it. The bypass the engineers wrote does not remove the wrongness; it routes around it, and the route has a terminus, and the terminus is oxalate.

So the plant must dump. It dumps into the syrup, and the syrup runs down the stem and pools on the floor and goes to glass, and the glass is not pure. Hold a shard to the amber lamp and look at the base of it: a milky bloom, fine white needles frozen mid-fall, laid down in the direction the liquid was moving. That is the plant's kidney work. That is the wrongness, taken out of the sugar one carbon at a time and set down as mineral, because there was nowhere else to put it and no one to complain.

Which means the amber is not spilled coolant that happens to have plants in it. The amber is *tissue*. It is the orchard's excretion, its skeleton, its shell, laid down outside its body at the rate of a fingernail, and it is winning. The plant pries at it with new growth and tears itself and weeps more syrup, which sets. It is secreting the thing that will immobilise it. It has no more idea of this than a coral has of the reef.

## The reef

Kneel at the centre of a big grove and push the leaf-litter aside — there is no litter, nothing rots it, but there are *fallen* leaves, dried to glassine, stacked like dropped sheets — and you will find that the ground is not concrete. It is amber, hummocked, glazed, and standing up out of it are stems that are not alive.

They are still standing. That is the thing. They died and did not fall, because there was nothing to soften them and nothing to push them, and the syrup they wept while dying ran down and set and welded them to the plate. Some are two hundred millimetres through and hollow, the interior gone to powder, the outside a clear amber cylinder with a white ghost inside it. Some still carry their leaves, and the leaves have gone transparent as they dried, so that the cherry glow of the return bend comes through a dead plant more cleanly than through a living one.

The grove grows up through its own dead. Runners go over the glazed corpses and root in the seams between them; new stems come up inside the hollow ones and use them as sleeves. The architecture of grove four is ninety per cent corpse. It is a reef: a thin veneer of living tissue on a limestone of its own making, in a hot dark sea one hand's-breadth wide.

And the seeds behave like mangroves. The flowers swell shut and fertilise themselves and then, in the older specimens, do not release seed at all — the seed germinates inside the closed petal, on the parent, and what eventually drops into the drip is not a seed but a propagule: a white spindle of ready-made plant, twenty centimetres of it, root at one end and two folded paper cotyledons at the other, already alive, already drinking. It floats a metre along the wet stripe. It strands on candy. Most of them strand on candy and glue there and become part of the reef.

The orchard is a mangrove swamp in a buried cathedral, and its tide is a maintenance interval.

## The twelfth thing

Thirty metres up, on the underside of a crane girder, there is a mat.

Root and rhizome and dust and a felt of dead glazed leaves, packed into the lattice and the bolt-holes and the cable tray, hanging in the dark over the bare stretch where nothing on the floor has ever grown. It is not above a leak. It has never been above a leak. It is the only part of this organism that does not live at an address.

It lives on the crane, and the crane moves four metres a year, and the hall is a hundred and seventy metres long.

Do the arithmetic and the whole shape of the thing changes. A traverse is forty-two years — a working life, the exact length of the career of the person who wrote ORCHARD on the locker door. A crossing of the sixty-pace bare stretch between groves is thirteen years of drought. The mat survives it by going to glass: candying its outer tissue into a hull, dropping its water content to almost nothing, holding a few living cells at the centre of a sealed amber lozenge glued to a steel beam, waiting out a decade the way a seed waits out a winter. Then the girder carries it over the canopy of the next grove, and the humidity finds it, and it wakes and puts out a runner and reaches down.

And it flowers. Openly.

That is what the open flowers are — not an ancestral reflex fading out, or not only that. Stress makes them open. The mat is the only part of the orchard that has ever known real drought, and drought is the old signal for *now, out there, someone else's pollen, quickly*. So the flowers on the crane crack their petals in the dark thirty metres up and shed pollen down through the canopy of a grove that has been fertilising itself shut for two centuries, and once in thirteen years some of it lands on a stigma that is still, by an accident of timing, receptive.

One wedding per generation. Performed by an overhead crane executing a maintenance traverse on a standby circuit, at four metres a year, in absolute ignorance.

That is why there is one genotype here and not eleven. That is why the groves are not scattered hydraulically but *joined* hydraulically, by a machine, slowly, from above. The orchard has been mixing itself for as long as the hall has been shut, and it has been getting more and more like itself the whole time — homozygous, purged, narrowed, converging on a single individual the way the floor is converging on a single sheet of glass. Two crystallisations at once, one in the genome and one underfoot.

## The heart, and how many beats are left

Its year is eleven days long. Nine days of cold pipe and bead and drip and turgor and the papery unfolding you can hear; then the tick of the contactor, the knuckle on the desk, the deep shove in the pipes, and two days of slack edges and held breath. It has no other calendar. It has no day, no season, no moon, no frost, no reason to expect anything except what the contactor does.

Its seasons are a maintenance schedule. Somebody chose eleven days once, for reasons involving a bearing or a filter or a man's roster, and that choice is now the entire temporal structure of a living thing's existence.

After the cold returns the eleven groves flush together, because they are all downstream of the same bead. For about six hours the hall is loud — eleven directions, a click every few seconds instead of every minute, the sound of a hundred thousand tissue-paper unfoldings, a round of applause from a room with no one in it. Then it settles to the pace of a thing that has never been in a hurry.

And the pump has a finite number of eleven-days left in it. Nobody knows the number. It is written nowhere. It is a function of a bearing in a wet dark place behind the north wall, and when it goes, the cold stops, and the stripe goes, and the weather of Bay Six ends, and eleven groves and one travelling mat will stand in the hot dark with their edges slack, waiting for a bead that does not form, for as long as their stored water lasts.

The orchard's death is already scheduled. It is scheduled in a component. It has been scheduled since the day the component was installed.

## What actually lives here

Say it properly, then.

The thing in Bay Six has no thorns, no wax, no alkaloid, no bark, no immune memory, no predators, no mates, no eyes, no legs, and no ability to survive anywhere else in the universe. It should be the most vulnerable organism that has ever existed. It is instead perfectly safe, and has been for a very long time, because every function it declined to evolve is being performed for it by plant equipment.

Its skin is a steel door dogged shut with a wheel. Its climate is three levels of rock. Its sun is a nine-degree pipe; it grows toward cold the way its ancestors grew toward light, and the leaning of the groves is heliotropism with the sign reversed. Its food is a cooling loop. Its skeleton is its own waste. Its heart is a make-up pump on an eleven-day timer. Its pollinator, its seed disperser, its herd animal, its only megafauna, is a crane on rails.

It is not eleven groves in a room. It is one animal wearing a power station, and the organs are all borrowed, and none of them know.

Its entire range is eleven patches, none larger than a tennis court, summing to less than a fifth of the floor. Its cosmology has three lights in it and one of them vanishes when you look at it. It has no name. It had a catalogue number instead of a name, which is a way of saying it was never meant to be a thing, only a means; and the only proper name it has ever been given is a joke in grease pencil on the inside of a locker door, by somebody who is not coming back.

ORCHARD. The name stuck because nothing else was going to name it.

## The gardener

The largest grove is at the number four gland. It is the largest because it weeps, and it weeps because a man wrote *will do it Thursday* and closed the book, and Thursday was superseded by an instruction that came down the stairs and was followed correctly, in sequence, valves and breakers and the wheel turned until it seated.

So the orchard's abundance is a deferred repair. Its irrigation is an unclosed work order. Its pollination is a maintenance traverse that nobody cancelled. Its whole existence is the residue of a shutdown that was thorough but not total — the things that were left on, the things that were not worth switching off, the standby circuits, the crop still in its beds at the end of a shift.

There is no ghost in the mess cabin. The blanket is squared because a squared blanket is what was left, and the boots are paired because he took them off in the ordinary way. But there is something else, and it is not supernatural, and it is arguably alive: an intention, still being carried out. Somebody decided the crane should traverse the hall.
