**Census, Bay Four**

The census is two lines long.

*Chlorophyta sp., floor channel, north gasket — recurrent, controlled.*
*Bryophyta, reference specimen, tray 1 — maintained.*

Everything else in the bay is either material or machinery, and the distinction between those two is the entire discipline. This is the official population. It is also, in the way that all official counts are, a description of what the institution has agreed to notice.

---

Start with the algae, because it is the only thing in the room that wants anything.

It lives at the junction of two failures: a gasket that weeps and a light that spills. Neither is supposed to happen. The gasket is on a defect list; the leak is four milliliters an hour of condensate that should have gone to the gutter and instead goes to the floor, and the light is a fault in the hood over the exit sign, a hairline of green that lands on the channel lip at an angle nobody has bothered to correct because correcting it would mean a work order and a work order means a body in the bay for forty minutes. So there is a strip of the world, forty centimeters by about two, where the rules have relaxed by a fraction, and something moved in.

It is thin as a stain and it is not a stain. It has a generation time of about nine days at two degrees, which is to say it is living very slowly and very well. It is scrubbed on Thursdays: brush, syringe, the smell that stays in your gloves through two changes. By Sunday the fringe is back. It has been killed five hundred and seventy-two times.

It has also learned the brush. Nobody put this in the log because there is no column for it, but the technician who does the Thursdays has noticed that the film now comes back fastest from under the channel lip, in the four millimeters of overhang the bristles cannot reach, and that the leading edge is straighter than it used to be, and that it recolonizes downstream rather than outward, which is the direction of the water. It has a refuge and a strategy. It is the only organism in the building that has responded to the building. Everything else in Bay Four is being *done to*. The algae is playing.

The joke about it — the one nobody has made in six years — had a setup and a punchline and the setup was just the word *Thursday*. The person who used to say it works three levels up now, in Wear, where they take finished things and make them look survived. They do not come down. The joke is still there, in the room, the way a smell is there. It is part of the population. It has no cells and it is not on the schedule and it has outlived its host.

---

The moss is not an organism. It is a role.

It sits in sterile lime grit under its own hooded lamp and it is watered by pipette, four milliliters, Tuesday and Friday, and once a month it is lifted out and blotted and weighed and trimmed back to thirty-one grams. It has been the size of a fist for eleven years. It is the most thoroughly prevented living thing on the planet: everything a moss does — spread, thicken, sporulate, brown at the edge, die back, come again — has been made into a deviation to be corrected. It exists to be the same. It is the room's fixed point, the thing the stone is measured against, and the price of that job is that it is not allowed to have a history.

In year six it died. Root rot, or the grit went alkaline, or someone's pipette; the incident report is one paragraph and the word used is *reset*. A stock culture is kept in a refrigerator three levels up, in the dark, in agar, for exactly this. The replacement was established in nine days and the log records the reference specimen as continuous, because the reference specimen is not that moss, it is the position the moss occupies, and the position has never been vacant for longer than a fortnight.

So: two mosses, one job. And a small lie in the record, kept not to deceive anyone but because the record has no way to say *the thing you are comparing against is a different thing now*. Which is, if you sit with it in the cold long enough, the whole problem of the bay in miniature.

---

Now the four hundred and six.

They are not alive and they are not objects, and after an hour in the gloom your body stops being able to hold that distinction. They stand at nineteen centimeters, which is closer than people stand, and they are all different, and you begin — helplessly, the way you begin to see faces in a grille — to know some of them.

**117** has a young head on an old body. The moth came to rest on its crown in year two and died there, and the crown was cut off and bagged and a new armature tip was grafted and dosed, and so 117 is the only formation in the bay with a scar. Under the UV lamp the join is a hard bright line, a discontinuity, the one place in four hundred and six where the banding does not run continuously from base to tip. Everything else here has a *record*. 117 has a *biography*: something happened to it. There is a laminated card by the door with a photograph and a date and no comment, and the card is not a warning, whatever it says. It is an immune memory. The bay met a body once, and learned, and now has antibodies: the mat, the gasket, the positive pressure, the two-stage filter, the whole apparatus aimed at anything that might die in here and leave a corpse. The card is how the room remembers the infection. People glance at it going in, the way you touch a doorframe.

**233** is off-model. Nobody knows why. The crown is one point eight millimeters from where the survey says it should be, and the error is compounding, because deposition follows the surface it is given and the surface it is given is now wrong. It has been flagged NONCONFORMING for four years. There are three options — re-bend the armature, re-dose to correct, cut and replace — and each requires a signature from someone who has to weigh the deviation against the cost of touching it, and the decision has been pending through two reorganizations. Meanwhile 233 grows. It is the only thing in the room whose future is genuinely unresolved. It is becoming something that was not specified. If you wanted to point at the one individual in Bay Four and say *that one is alive*, you would point at 233, and you would be pointing at a rounding error that has been permitted to keep going.

**301** is two things pretending to be one. There is a gap in it, forty-one millimeters, between the descending tip and the rising crown, and the gap is closing at a rate that puts the join somewhere in the 2060s. The join has a milestone code. It also has, unofficially, another name, which is not written anywhere and which everybody uses. Once a year somebody photographs the gap with a scale bar. Eleven photographs. In sequence they show nothing at all; the eye cannot do it. Whoever inherits the folder in 2065 will have a flipbook of a marriage.

The **rimstone pool** at the back wall is the room's longest sentence. Ribbed, squat, complicated, six emitters feeding a lip that has to build itself outward before it can build up. Its floor plate is stenciled with a year in the twenty-second century. Everyone who works here has done the arithmetic once, alone, and arrived at the same number, and nobody says it. It is the only object in the bay that is unambiguously going to outlive the species' current arrangements. It is being made by people who will not see it, for a chamber nobody has found, to replace something that may already be gone.

And by the door, the **little ones**. Overdue. Their completion estimates have passed and they are still knee-high and unfinished, because their emitters scale up and clog, because their geometry is fussy, because a fortnight in March the dosing pump drifted and they never caught up. They stand where everyone enters. They are the first thing you pass and the last thing you pass and they are the room's honest admission: that there is a difference between the plan and the matter, and the difference is not going to close, and it is *accumulating*.

---

The people are not here.

That is the thing you have to get straight. The bay is occupied for perhaps ninety minutes in a day. It is alone for ninety-six percent of its existence. Its human beings are not residents; they are weather, brief and warm and disruptive, arriving in a body that has to be turned sideways at every gap, and the room's whole architecture is a set of instructions for how to move a large animal through a forest without letting it touch anything.

And they carry the room out with them, which is the only successful colonization in the building. There is a gait — shoulder leading, hips at each gap, hands folded in against the chest — and it does not switch off at the bulkhead. People do it in corridors. People do it in supermarkets, between the shelving, and notice, and stop, and feel stupid, and do it again a week later. People hold their breath in lifts. People know, in their forearms, exactly how much torque a wheel-handle needs. On Thursdays somebody's hands smell of hypochlorite through two glove changes and they cannot say why the smell makes them faintly happy.

Someone talks to 117. Not sentimentally — a low, continuous, half-audible mutter while working nearby, the way you talk to a horse or a printer. Someone has, privately, against every naming convention in the quality system, given all four hundred and six of them names, and the names are nowhere, not written, not shared, and will be lost entire at the next retirement. That is the bay's real fauna: an unauthorized bestiary living in one skull, four hundred and six individuals that the record insists are integers.

The bay's most successful organism is a habit. It reproduces by training. Its generation time is a career.

---

The water is one animal.

About six hundred liters, looping: manifold, emitter, crown, film, channel, recovery, chill, dose, manifold. It goes around every six days. It has been through the room six hundred times. It has been on every one of them. It is the only thing in the bay that touches all four hundred and six, and it is older, as a continuous body, than anyone's employment, and it has no organs and no boundaries except the ones you can see: fat lenses of condensate held under the chilled lines by surface tension, each one leashed to a gutter by a braided wick, because water that falls where it likes is *wild* water and wild water leaves an unlogged mark.

That is the room's only crime, incidentally. Not theft, not damage. An unrecorded touch. The wicks are there to prevent freedom.

And into this body, continuously, goes the exhaust of every person who enters. You breathe out; the breath hangs; the return grille at floor level takes it; it is chilled and stripped and re-dosed and sent back up and released three centimeters above a crown. The bay does not have visitors. It has intake. It is the slowest and most courteous form of eating that has ever been devised, and the eaten are not diminished by it, and they are not told.

---

What is left, when you have counted the algae and the moss-role and the four hundred and six and the habit and the water, is the thing that actually lives here, and it is not any of them.

Twice a week at 04:00 the spray bar runs for ninety seconds and the room rains on itself with nobody present. Eleven years of weather totals about twenty-eight hours. All of it unwitnessed; all of it logged. In the corners, where the sacrificial lime skin is thickest and the airflow is worst, the plaster has begun to grow botryoidal crusts that are in no drawing, match no formation, and serve no purpose. They are tolerated. They are watched. They are what the room does when it is not being told. Every made thing that is left running long enough starts to make something on its own account, in its corners, at night, and the people who find it write *tolerated* because *beautiful* is not a disposition code.

And in the jar by the door — clean, sealed, labeled **FIRST** — there is a space shaped like a grain of sediment that has not arrived in eleven years and will not arrive, because everything upstream of this room has been designed to make sure it never does. It is the only object in the bay waiting for something unscheduled. It is a cage with nothing in it, kept dusted, in case.

So here is the census, corrected.

Everything in Bay Four that is *alive* is off-model. The algae with its refuge under the lip. The crusts in the corners. The dead moss wearing the living moss's job. 117 with its graft. 233 with its compounding error and its unsigned permission to continue. The unmade joke. The names in one head. The habit in a stranger's hips at the supermarket. Your breath, in the stone, at eight microns.

Everything on-model is not alive and is trying very hard not to be: four hundred and six portraits of a chamber a hundred and forty meters under a hillside that has not been located, growing at thirty-seven drops a second toward a likeness that will be finished by nobody, so that when the original is quarried out or drowned or simply found too late, there will be something to point at. The bay makes the body. Wear, three levels up, will make it look survived. Neither department makes the past. The past is what leaks in.

In nine hundred years somebody will hold a broken stub under a violet lamp and find, among the ten thousand obedient bands, one that is too bright — week forty-one, an anomalously warm animal stood sideways between two armatures for eleven minutes, exhaling — and will write *cause unknown*, and that will be the only sentence in the entire folder about something that *happened* rather than something that was done.

That is the inhabitant. It is currently a millimeter or two of grey-white nap on a wire skeleton, and it is being laid down at eight microns a time, and it is you, briefly, and then permanently.
