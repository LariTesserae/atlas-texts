## What Lives in the High Wet

Start small, because most of it is small.

A drop hangs from the lobe of a liverwort and does not fall for eleven seconds. In that time it is a country. It has a surface with a skin of tension that things walk on from beneath; it has a temperature gradient of a tenth of a degree; it has a chemistry that changes from the top of it to the bottom as the algae inside breathe. There are ciliates in it that hatch, feed, divide, and are eaten, and the whole of their history happens inside a body of water the size of a grain of rice, hanging in the dark, waiting to drop. When it drops, the country ends and is reassembled three centimeters lower down. Nobody in it notices. Nobody has ever noticed. The drip is the unit of time here, the way the day is elsewhere, and the reason the plateau sounds the way it does is that a hundred billion clocks are all striking at slightly different moments, forever.

The nematodes graze the film the way the herds graze the mats, and for the same reason, and with the same expression, which is none. The mites wear iron in their cuticle. They did not evolve to do this; the water simply puts it there, the way water elsewhere puts salt on a rock. Everything here is slowly being plated. Everything here has been slowly being plated for sixty million years, and has learned to make the plating useful, and then has forgotten that it was ever an accident.

---

The fog is a crop and something has to harvest it.

The gapers do. Pale, wide-headed, unstreamlined birds with mouths that open past the eye and close almost never, flying at the speed of a slow walk with their jaws agape and their eyes half shut, sieving midges out of cloud. They are not hunting. Hunting implies a target. They are mowing. A gaper's day is a straight line nine kilometers long flown at the height of a person's chest, and at the end of it the bird turns around and flies it back, and its crop is full of weather.

Here is what is strange about them, and it takes longer to notice than the hum in the ground: they are the only things on this plateau that make a noise on purpose.

Air is an insulator. That is the whole of it. Everything down in the wet is inside the conversation, inside the wire, and has learned that to be loud in that band is to be located and killed. But a bird in flight is a body cut loose from the circuit, a smudge of potential moving through a medium that will not carry it, and no reader lying in the moss can feel it go by. The gapers are outside the law. They have been outside the law so long that they have kept a voice — thin, two notes, a sound like a wet finger on glass — and they use it constantly, for territory, for contact, for nothing at all, the way animals elsewhere use it. From inside the fog you hear one every few minutes, high up, and it is the only thing you ever hear that is not water.

They cannot use it on the ground. When a gaper lands it goes silent and holds itself very still, and it lands only in one kind of place: on the combs, up among the branching blue-black metal, inside the collar of vapor. The combs are the noisiest objects on the shelf, electrically — current pouring out of them into the peat, a roar, a floodlight — and a small bird sitting in that roar is invisible, the way a whisper is invisible beside a waterfall. They nest there. The metal is one degree above air temperature and it is the only warm thing in the world, and the eggs are laid against it, and the chicks grow up inside the loudest silence available, and the first thing they do when they can fly is leave the ground and start shouting.

---

Between the terraces, in the shallow sheets that never quite drain, there are shrimp.

Not shrimp. Something pale and laterally flattened and about the length of a fingernail, with too many legs and no eyes worth the name, that swims on its side and eats the bacterial film off the underside of the surface tension. Call them pan-shrimp. Each terrace has its own. The lip between one tread and the next is only two or three meters of rounded rock, but it is dry at the top, and dry is a wall, and no pan-shrimp has crossed one in the whole history of the lineage.

So they are nations shaped like lines. A single species occupies a strip of water four meters wide and six hundred kilometers long, a population that is essentially one-dimensional, in which a gene takes eleven thousand years to travel from one end of the country to the other. Above it, on the next tread, is a sister species that has not exchanged anything with it since the last time the water table rose high enough to drown the lip. Below it, another. Stacked staircases of near-identical animals, each one a separate experiment, each one running for a few million years.

When a seam fails and a terrace drains, the species on it ends. All of it, everywhere, in a week. It has nowhere to go and no cyst stage and no plan. The mats at the seam lay down their lamina that year and the lamina is thin and dark and lacks the little phosphatic flecks the shrimp contribute, and if you cut the plate and put it under a lens you can count the years of a nation and see the exact season it stopped, and there is nothing else anywhere that remembers it at all.

---

The herds you already know about. What you don't know, standing thirty meters off in the fog while the ground quietly rearranges itself, is that the gardens are not decoration and not camouflage in any optical sense.

A grazer's field is a mess — a big warm sloppy smear of potential, muscle noise, gut noise, the enormous electrical shout of being two tonnes of alive. The moss on its back short-circuits it. The cushion plants and the liverworts and the film of algae and the whole conducting wet stack lie across the animal's dorsal surface like a shunt, and they take that shout and spread it and blur it and hand it back to the ground as something that reads, at four meters, exactly like moss. The garden is not a disguise. It is a muffler, and it is alive, and it has to be maintained, and an animal that stops grooming its back gets louder every week.

It is inherited. A calf is born bald — pink-grey, wrinkled, deafening — and for the first days of its life it is the loudest thing in the district and its mother stands over it and presses her flank against it and holds it there, and the transfer takes about a week, and what transfers is not a smear of spores but a *community*, in order, in layers, the way a sourdough is handed on. The cushion species that does the bulk of the shunting grows nowhere on the plateau except on backs. Nowhere. It has not touched ground in forty million years. It is dispersed only by contact, animal to animal, and so its family tree is not a map of geography. It is a map of who stood next to whom. Split a herd and you split the plant; two hundred thousand years later the two halves are different species and the boundary between them is a social decision made by mammals whose bones are all gone.

The old cows are archives. Their scutes are laid down in the same annual laminae as the plates under the moss, from the same water, and they carry the same record: the dry decades, the ash falls, the year the table dropped. A cow of ninety years has a flank you could read like a core sample. She has also, in the spacing of her body against the others, something like a syntax. The herd keeps station by mutual repulsion, fields pushing on fields, and the distances are not fixed — they widen at a threat, tighten at a birth, and there is one particular configuration, a slight asymmetry with a gap held open on one side, that a herd will maintain for days after an animal is taken. It is not a search. They know. It is just the shape the group makes when a piece of it is missing, and it persists until the garden that was on her back has finished dying.

---

Now the readers, and the thing about the readers that you would rather not have worked out.

They are plated like grazers. Not similarly — identically, the same overlapping scute geometry, the same iron, the same growth rhythm. They are flat where a grazer is round, and stunted, and their plates have fused along the flank into a single shield, and they have no garden and never had one. They do not breed. There are no reader young. There is no reader mating call, no territory, no season. And yet there are always readers, in every district, at a density that tracks something.

It tracks orphans.

A calf that loses its mother in the first week never gets seeded. It cannot be seeded later; the window shuts. It stays loud, and being loud it should die, and most of them do — but a few of them do the only other thing available, which is to stop moving. They lie down in the moss and hold their field flat and they discover, at some point in that first terrible day, that flatness is not just safety, it is *hearing*. And then they grow into the shape of the thing that lies still. It is not a species. It is what a grazer becomes when the handoff fails. The plateau has been producing its own predators out of its own bereaved children for as long as there have been herds, and the arrangement is stable, and nobody designed it, and there is no sense in which it is cruel, and you will think about it anyway.

Because here is the part that is hard to hold.

A reader knows one field-shape from the inside. It knows the mother-call. It made that call itself, once, for a week, in the fog, and no one answered.

Some of them use it. Not many — a reader that lies too much gets found by a bull and killed — but there is a hummock out there, somewhere in every district, that is saying *here, come here, I am warm, I am here* in the exact private grammar of a cow to her calf, and it is saying it into the wire, and the wire carries it four meters, and sometimes five. It is the only lie in the country. Everything else here is honest by construction; there was never enough bandwidth for deceit. This one got in through grief.

The cows have an answer, and it is getting better, and you can see it getting better if you watch long enough. They no longer call and wait. They call and then change the call — a small mutation of the pattern, handed over by flank contact, different every week, meaningless except as proof of touch. A handshake. A password that only exists between two bodies that have actually met. Calves that answer the old fixed call correctly are not answered back. Their mothers have stopped speaking the language the orphans remember.

---

There are animals that eat the record.

Low, snouted, solitary, with a rasping tongue like a farrier's file, they go along the exposed edges of the plates where a seam has cut down through the peat, and they lick. They take a lamina a year, no more; the plates barely notice. What they get is not calories — there are almost none — but a chemistry, iron and manganese and the trace signatures of every ash fall and every drought, and something in them reads it, and something in them *acts* on it.

They are the only animals on the shelf that go to places where there is nothing. A taster will walk forty kilometers to a dry pan and stand in it for a day and leave, and eleven years later the water comes back to that pan, and the taster's descendants are already there. Nobody knows how the interval is carried. It may be in the timing of a gut, a slow chemical clock set by a taste of the year the table fell. It may be simpler and worse: they may just remember, and teach, and the teaching may be a route walked with a calf once, in the dry, for no reason the calf can see.

They are eating the only history this country has and using it to be in the right place. It is the closest thing here to a library and it is being consumed continuously by the people who need it, and it does not run out, because the mats keep writing.

---

And in the plates themselves, in the laminae, there are things waiting.

Cysts. Bacterial, mostly, but not only — there are animal eggs in there, and fungal spores with walls like ceramic, sealed into a year and held. Some of them are older than the mammals. Some of them, when you dig them out and give them the right brine, wake up and start dividing as though nothing had happened, and what they start doing is a metabolism that no longer has a niche on this shelf, a chemistry for a water that stopped existing here in the Oligocene.

They are not a seed bank. Nobody is saving them for anything. They are the ordinary residue of a system that has been running so long that its own leftovers now constitute an archive, and the archive is legible, and it is being added to at one page a year, and one day the shelf will wear down far enough that the peat drains for good and the current stops. The combs will fracture and not regrow. The conversation will end mid-sentence.

And something in the plates will still be viable. Waiting for a mountain range that has not been made yet.

---

One last thing, and it is a small one.

There is a moth here, grey, unremarkable, that fledges in the dry months and flies downslope. Always downslope. It flies out past the last seam, past where the fog reaches, into the hot dry air over the edge, and it dies there in a day, and it does this every generation, all of them, the entire species, and has done since the fog used to come further up.

It is not a migration. There is nothing there. It is an instruction that was correct once and has never been revoked, and the plateau is so large and so patient that it can afford to keep paying it.

If you stayed — and people who describe this place always imply that someone stayed — you would not remain outside it. Your boots would be green in a week. The iron would find your fillings and stay in them. Something in your gut would change hands. Your field would be enormous and idiotic and utterly legible, a great shout of *here I am* going out into the wire in every direction, and the moss would take the sound of whatever happened next, and the mats would lay down their thin dark line, and the dripping would go on at the same rate it has always gone on.

It is all being kept. That is the whole of the work, and everyone is doing it, and nothing is being made.
