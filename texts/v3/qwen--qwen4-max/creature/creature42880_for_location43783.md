Everything alive on the Increase lives at about a meter and a half.

Not everything. The pins are lower, the harrow is higher, and the tenant, if there is a tenant, is lower than either. But the forty thousand occupy a slab of air roughly as thick as a person is tall, and they have spent two centuries furnishing it.

---

A house in Terrace is a vertical section through a gradient, and the family lives in the middle of it like a filling.

Below is the floor: hot, bare, unrounded, used for drying, firing, curing, and cooking. A kitchen is a hatch. You lower a pot into the floor on a chain and come back in an hour, and the ground has done the work, and the ground does not charge. Above is the roof: the cold plates, the fins, the capillaries, the whole shivering apparatus of the sky. Between them, at the height where a standing person's collarbone sits, is the *line* — a brass strip let into the wall in a good house, a painted band in a decent one, chalk in a poor one, renewed weekly by whoever is smallest and can still hold chalk.

The line is where the benches are, and the long shelf, and the hammocks. Nobody sits on the floor. Sitting on the floor is what you do to a guest you want gone. Children are carried at the line and set down at the line and are furious about it. Lovers lie at the line. The dead are held at the line for a day, and then they are not the family's problem anymore, in one of two ways.

The furniture is adjustable and is adjusted constantly, in centimeters, by argument. A fever is treated by lowering the bed four fingers. A chill is treated by raising it. Nobody can say whether either works. It is done. Tall families and short families cannot marry without carpentry, and the carpentry is a real expense, and it appears in the marriage contract alongside the fin-rights, and there are proverbs about it.

The poor sleep low. You can read a household's fortune in the angle of its hammock ropes: slack and high is money, taut and near the hot stone is a family that has let the ground catch up. Their backs cook and their scalps ache and they wake at dawn with the two weathers still fighting across them, and they go out into the lanes in ceramic-soled pattens, clicking, and the clicking comes up through your feet before the person comes around the corner.

You can tell anyone born here by their palms. Three or four crescent scars, white, at the base of the fingers and across the heel of the hand — the stone's signature. The rock has no rounded edges and no mercy and every child learns the same lesson at the same age from the same mistake. There is no gesture in Terrace for *be careful* that does not involve showing the palm.

---

They speak two languages and only one of them is words.

Air is a poor carrier and they have stopped fighting it. Face to face they talk quietly, close in, watching mouths; across a lane they do not shout, because shouting is for strangers and fools and produces a sound like a cough two doors down. Across a lane they knock.

The wall-tongue is old, formal, and fast. Every household has a knock — three or four strikes, a name in basalt — and children learn theirs before they learn to write it. Gossip moves through the massif at five kilometers a second and arrives, in the feet, well before the spoken version arrives in the ear, so that a woman can be scandalized several seconds before she is told anything, and stand there with her palm on the boss composing her face. There is a whole etiquette about this. You are expected to pretend the wall did not tell you.

The Sounders are half-deaf in air and fluent in stone, and they are not the only ones; they are merely the ones who are paid. Two people who intend to marry put their ears on the same listening boss and stay there a while, and what they are listening to is the quarter, the presses, the far cable-houses, the plug grinding up under everything, and also each other's skull, which at that proximity is audible, and which is the point. A divorce is filed by installing a second boss.

Children learn the wall before the word. This is not a metaphor. An infant is put palm-down on the basalt at three months and held there, and the mother knocks, and the child's whole body hears it, and stops crying, and this is the first thing anyone in Terrace is ever taught.

---

The unit of life here is not the person. It is the *stack* — a hot floor, a line, a cold roof, and the rights that connect them to other stacks. Households merge like plumbing, because they are plumbing. A marriage is a change in the direction of several flows.

The leases behave more like organisms than anything in the register of animals. They feed, they branch, they are grafted, they are inherited, they sicken. The flue that runs through the granary under a lease three generations old has been subdivided eleven times; there are now fourteen parties entitled to a fraction of that warmth, and four of them have never seen it, and one of them is a charitable trust for the maintenance of a brass plate. When the ground finally takes a flue, the lease does not vanish. It becomes an *extinguished* lease, and there is an office that keeps them, and they are sold, and people hang them in the arcades — a legal instrument describing a volume of hot gas that no longer exists, at an elevation now four hundred degrees inside solid rock.

Above the line, in the Formwork, live the Ahead.

These are the other poor, and the worse ones: the families who built too far in front of the ground and are still waiting for it. Their houses have fronts, doors, roofs, fins, and nothing underneath. They have sky above and sky below. They have no hot side. They cannot run an engine, because an engine is a difference and they do not have one; they are the only people on the Increase who are cold all over, in a city built entirely to prevent that.

They have water — fins work whether or not you have a floor — and they sell it down, and with the money they buy heat by the basket. Sintered stone balls, heated in the lower quarters, carried up in insulated hods by men with the shoulders of draft animals, sold at the door of a house hanging in the haze, put under the shelf, gone cold by morning. It is the only place in Terrace where warmth is a commodity you can drop and break.

Their children are born at elevations that do not exist. Their addresses are provisional and have been for thirty years. And it is from the Ahead that the harrow-watchers are recruited, because they have the view, and the time, and the doorways, and nothing to put in them.

The dead go one of two ways. The guild fashion is the Falls: down the western flank as counterweight, four and a half kilometers, turning the generators at the head, and for that hour the city runs a little on its grandmothers, and nobody says so, and everybody knows. The old fashion is to lay them at the current street and let the ground come up past them, so that they descend, relative to the datum, into the kiln, and are fired. In eighty years a retrieval man brings up an ash-shadow in a slab of glass, and a descendant buys it, and it goes on the rack at the line, at blood height, with the others.

There are families with four generations on the rack. There are families with none who speak of the Falls rite as though it were a drainage arrangement. Both parties are correct.

---

Here is the thing the Assay has never quite put in a report: the city built the habitats, and then the animals arrived to fit them.

There are four niches on the Increase and none of them is natural. There is a hot wet crack that eats iron. There is a cold wet blade that sweats once a night. There is an arc-lamp. There is a column of rising air two kilometers wide. Four niches, four animals, and every one of them lives at an elevation a human being cannot occupy.

The *pin* is a pale cricket-thing in the wet seams, ninety years off a fodder crate, now unable to live anywhere else — it dies below three thousand meters, in air too thick and too wet, so it cannot be exported, exhibited, or given away. Its entire species occupies something under a kilometer of seam. It eats the rust-orange film, and the film eats the city's fixings, so the pins are, at two removes, eating the brackets off the walls. There is a bounty. There is also a faction, mostly young, mostly Ahead, who point out that the pin is the only animal on the mountain that was not carried up and cannot be carried down, and that killing it would leave Terrace with nothing that belongs here. Children keep them in jars with a wet rag and an iron nail, and change the nail, and are beaten for the nails.

The arc-moth has one generation every two years, and the city therefore has two kinds of year, bright and dark, and contracts are dated by them, and people will tell you which they were born in the way people elsewhere tell you a sign of the zodiac. The larvae eat the felt packing out of the regenerators. The foundry has run its arcs at full for one night in two since a clause in a lease nobody can now find the original of, at a cost that the Assay annually calculates and annually declines to publish, and on that night the whole plateau can see the glare on the shutter-housings, and the count is taken, and the count is written beside the datum in a smaller hand.

The bat-analogue drinks off the fins. The grilles are there because of it, and there is a licensed loss — *the tithe* — denominated in fins per year, and there is a standing quarrel about whether the grilles are to keep it off the plates or keep it from drowning in the capillary troughs, and the quarrel is really about whether it is a thief or a guest.

It is neither. It is the plumbing. It drinks at the cold roof and it flies down to the warm seams and it drips, and it has been dripping for a century, and that is why the seams are wet, and that is why the film is there, and that is why there are pins. Every living thing on the Increase except the moth is downstream of a bat's chin. The dewwrights know this. It is in the guild's own survey. It has never once been said aloud in a guild meeting, because the tithe would have to be renegotiated, and nobody wants to be the man who argued that the bats should be encouraged.

And the harrow. Four meters, black, comes to the thermal above the plug and gains two kilometers without a wingbeat and does not land. It is a Stirling engine with feathers, running on the same difference as everything else, and it is the only engine on the mountain that has never needed a guild.

The watchers log its turns and derive the plug's output from its climb rate, which is a real service and is why they are not considered idle, and which is not what they are doing. What they are doing is waiting for it to come down. The ledger of *descents unconfirmed* has thirty-one entries in a hundred and forty years, all in haze or dust or the last four minutes of light. There is one feather, in a fin-grille in the upper arcades, and a hundred-and-eleven-year argument about whether it was shed at altitude or whether the bird touched something. And there is a minority position, held quietly by three of the oldest watchers, that the sightings are all one animal — that a thing which never lands and never tires and rides a permanent thermal might simply not be a population, might be an individual, might have been up there since before the Falls were strung.

The Assay lists the harrow as undisputed. The Sounders find this funny. It is undisputed as an animal and entirely disputed as a life.

---

Then there are the ones the register cannot hold.

The film is *living, provisionally*, which is a legal status with teeth: you may not remove a provisionally living thing from a fin without a hearing. There is a man in the upper arcades who has argued thirty-eight times that a stain is not alive and has lost thirty-five, and who is not a crank, because the stain is eating the building.

The dendrites are silver and fern-shaped and branch toward the sound of the engines, and the Sounders read them the way you would read tree rings — a bearing going bad in a hall two streets away changes the branching on a fin above it, six weeks before anyone can hear the fault. Detach a dendrite and it keeps its shape and stops. Put it on a new fin and it resumes, toward the new sound. Whether that is memory is the whole argument and has been for ninety years.

They also ruin emissivity. A furred fin sweats badly. So the dewwrights scrape on a rotation, except for the *index fins*, which are kept furred as instruments, and except for one entire field on the northeastern roof, forty hectares of silver, unharvested, maintained, yielding nothing, called the Index. The Index costs the city eleven thousand liters a year. It has been defended in eleven hearings. It is the most expensive thing Terrace owns that does nothing.

Entry nine is a figure that holds its shape in the warm clear sumps for hours and moves against the wind; it may be convection. Entry eleven is known only from objects: a pattern in the glaze of vitrified cups brought up from the fired lower city, recurring across cups from different quarters and different decades, with variation, with what looks horribly like inheritance. It may be a kiln effect. The retrieval trade has begun forging it, which means the register of animals now has a fraud problem, which means somewhere in the Assay there is a man whose job is to authenticate a fossil of something that may never have been alive.

Two observations make a note. Three make a species. This rule exists to prevent exactly the kind of thing entry eleven is, and everyone respects it, and it is also the reason the third gate is open.

---

The Ninefold Cistern is a room with a shore. It lies under the Slow Yards, warm, unlined, still, and utterly clear, and it is the only volume in Terrace with no gradient in it at all — no hot side, no cold side, nothing to harvest, no difference to sell. In a city where every cubic meter is leased, this one is worthless, and the dewwrights point that out at every opportunity, and it is precisely why it has survived.

The gauge is a float and a stylus and a drum, installed to measure seepage, in continuous service for a hundred and forty years. Twice the drum has recorded a displacement — some hundreds of liters, moving away from the shore, unhurried, at a rate the Sounders can reconstruct and do not enjoy reconstructing. Both times there was a keeper on the shore. The first was a man in the twelfth year of the instrument; the second was a woman in the ninety-sixth. Neither was told about the other. Both used the word *pale*. Neither used a word for a shape.

Both displacements fall on the day of the reading.

The Sounders noticed this in year ninety-eight and did not publish it. There is a listening boss on the cistern shore, the only one in the city with no machine on the other side of it, and the guild takes turns at it, and the minute book for a hundred and forty years contains the same four words in eleven different hands.

The standing order is to leave the third gate open. Nothing is put inside. No lamp, no bait, no offering — there is nothing here that eats what we eat. What is left is room, and room is the only thing in Terrace that cannot be manufactured, only withheld.

And there is a second reason, which is understood by everyone in the guild and written by none of them: an animal with an entry can be valued. It can be assigned a fin-equivalent, a bounty, a season, a lease. Registration is how this city eats things. Two observations are a note, and a note is a kind of darkness, and in the darkness the gate stays open and nothing has an address.

---

There is a third blank, and it is downhill.

The rill in the eastern apron is nine years old, forty centimeters deep, and has a brass plate and a legal guardian, because it is a minor. The Warden of the First Water holds its bed in trust — a strip of raw stone forty centimeters wide, running from the condenser discharge down the apron and out into the haze, unsold, unbuildable, reserved to the horizon for a river that will not exist for thirty thousand years. Children are taken to see it. They are always disappointed. It is a wet scratch. It is fed by a machine. It deepens by a millimeter every three to eleven years and the Assay records the millimeter with great seriousness, and the warden walks it twice a year with a rod and a book, and writes down nothing, and walks back.

So the densest fabric in the world keeps three empties: a gate onto a worthless room, a field of silver that costs eleven thousand liters, and a strip of stone leading nowhere. Everything else is leased. Those three are the only way this city has of saying *not yet*.

The rest of what lives here comes up the Falls in refrigerated cars. Green stuff, root stuff, wet crates from the haze-floor — the only objects on the Increase that have ever touched soil. They smell of rot. They smell, precisely and unmistakably, of the country the Assay promises in thirty thousand years: the rounded hills, the gravel bed, the green ordinary rivers, the smell of a world that has finally invented decay. It arrives weekly. It is carried up through the Formwork past the doorways hanging in the haze, and it is eaten within a day, and nobody remarks on it, and the whole future of the mountain passes through the city in a crate and is digested and goes down the western flank as ballast.

---

The Assay needs one more observation.

Everyone involved knows exactly what would produce it. There is a bell in the Datum tower that has never been heard from the street, and a rope, and a man whose office is the rope, and no rule whatsoever against ringing it on a day that is not the day. It would go into the rock the way everything goes into the rock. It would reach the cistern as a pressure — a hand laid flat on the whole room at once — and the float would lift, and the stylus would write, and there would be three.

They have not asked. The order is about a gate. The order has never been amended.

Once a year the bell is rung anyway, because it is the day, and the Sounders report it, and forty thousand people put their palms on forty thousand bosses and feel the same thing at the same moment, which is the closest this place comes to a crowd. And on the shore under the Slow Yards the gauge-keeper sits with her hand on the drum housing and hopes for nothing, and the water lies flat and warm and clear, without a weed, without a larva, without a skin of pollen, and the gate stands open onto a room that no one has taken.
