## The seam

Start at the working face, because that is where the bodies are.

The snow there is not white. It is grained, like film pushed past its speed, and the grain is animals: springtails, two millimetres of blue-black, packed at densities that make the surface look wet when it is only crowded. Each one carries a spring folded under its belly, held by a catch, released by a muscle — a catapult it built out of its own abdomen. It fires itself ten centimetres, which is a person throwing themselves over a house, lands on its back more often than not, rights itself, and goes on eating. What it eats is pollen. Beech pollen, pine pollen, alder, from forests thirty kilometres away and two thousand metres down, lifted on the afternoon upslope and dropped here on a cold surface that acts as a trap for anything the air is carrying. The springtail has emptied its gut. It has to: a full gut is a place where ice can start. It runs its body dry of nucleators and supercools and sits at minus five in a liquid state, waiting, and when the sun comes over the eastern ridge at nine the film of water reaches it and it resumes a meal it stopped in the middle of.

Twice a day it is switched off and on. It does not experience this as an interruption. It experiences it as Tuesday.

Working the same seam, one animal per two square metres instead of a million, is a wolf spider. Dark as wet slate, which is not an accident — she basks flat on the dark stones with her legs spread and lifts her body eight degrees above the air, and hunts at a temperature that would put a lowland spider into torpor. Two of her eight eyes are large and forward-facing and catch the light coming up off the snow, so that in this underlit world she looks lit from inside. She builds nothing. The only silk she makes here is a dragline and a nursery: an egg sac slung from her spinnerets, dragged over gravel, dipped in melt, hauled out. When they hatch they climb onto her back and ride there, twenty of them, a small moving crowd on top of the crowd, and she carries them until they are ready to be a crowd of their own. She is the only thing on this plain that carries its children.

Fifty metres back, in the older rubble, an ice crawler goes through the interstices at the pace of a minute hand. Pale, wingless, longer than anything else here has any right to be, antennae sweeping the gaps ahead of it, cerci reading the air behind. It hunts frozen midges and eats them thawed. It takes three years to become an adult and may live five more. Its metabolism is set to a temperature most animals would call a bad night; the warmth of a closed human hand can kill it. There are no hands here. This is the largest single fact of its life.

## Addresses

Almost none of them live here. This is the thing the census hides.

The finches roost down-valley in crevices. The accentors nest in the older ground where there is something to nest under. The choughs are not foraging, they are commuting — six birds dropping through the col on the katabatic because it is free lift, going over the ice to somewhere with turf in it. The kestrel works a beat that begins eight hundred metres lower. The fox is not a resident, it is a road: four kilometres of trot with a body attached, and by morning the road has been drifted over.

The only animals with an address are the ones inside the stone.

The old moraine looks like a heap of spoil and is, in fact, a building. Between the boulders there is a void network, room after room, connected by passages a hand's width across, roofed in slabs, floored in gravel, and it goes down four metres. In February, when the air above it is minus twenty-five and the wind is stripping the ridge, the inside of that heap is at plus one, held there by a metre of snow and the slow leak of the ground's summer. A snow vole lives in it. Grey, blunt, long-whiskered, tail longer than a mouse's, it does not hibernate. It works the night shift all winter in a warren it did not dig, eating cached seed and moss and the bark of the willow that is barely a willow, and its predators cannot follow it because its predators are too big for the doors. The whole architecture is a waste product. The machine dumped its swarf in a pile and the pile turned out to have rooms in it, and something moved in.

## The dead

Now count the citizens properly.

Take a cupful of last week's snow from the melt line and let it stand. What settles out is a grey sediment with a shape to it: midges by the hundred, aphids, a moth's wing with the scales still on it, spiderlings the size of full stops, a lacewing's eye, beetle elytra, and pollen past counting. Almost none of it is alive. The springtails are alive, the spider is alive, the crawler is alive, and they are a rounding error in the population. The overwhelming majority of the organisms on this plain arrived dead, or arrived alive and were killed on landing by a surface at minus four.

There is no kitchen in this canteen. Nothing at the working face is fixing carbon at any rate worth measuring; there is no leaf, no blade, no green. The entire economy runs on imported corpses. Somewhere below, a valley is making a summer — a real one, with nettles and aphid colonies and midge swarms over a river — and the afternoon wind is lifting a fraction of it and carrying it up and dropping it on the only cold thing in the sky's path. The snowfield is a plate. The cold is a refrigerator. The melt line is where the food comes out of storage, and everything with legs is standing there waiting.

An ecosystem with predators at the bottom and no plants underneath them. A food web that begins at the top and is held up by a delivery.

## The ones who eat the machine

The smallest residents are not in the crowd. They are in the ice, and they are doing something the crowd cannot do: they are damaging the landlord.

On the bare ice below the icefall there is grit, and the grit is not lying flat. It has been gathered. Filamentous cyanobacteria have wound themselves through the grains and bound them into granules the size of peppercorns, dark, coherent, heavier than the dust they came from. This is manufacture — the first thing on this ground that makes an object rather than a film. And the object is dark, so it warms, so it sinks, so it melts itself a hole. The hole is a house: a cylinder of water twenty centimetres deep with a floor of the granule that dug it, shaded from the ultraviolet by its own walls, and it finds an equilibrium depth where the melting and the light balance and it stops. Inside: rotifers, tardigrades, nematodes, ciliates, a whole grazing chain in a vessel the width of a pencil, at a density that would be a slum anywhere else. At dusk the lid freezes and everyone is put away until morning.

On the ice between the holes, a purple alga. Almost black in mass, a pigment it makes to survive the light, and the pigment absorbs, and the absorption melts the crystal it is sitting in, and it sinks a millimetre into its own house.

Every organism here that makes a living from the ice makes the ice go faster. This is not a metaphor and it is not a small effect; the darkening is measurable, and the melting it causes is measurable, and the tenants are dismantling the building at a rate the building notices.

And there is one more tenant, and it has no body.

The source of the fallout is not only beech forests. In the same cupful, in the same grey sediment, there is soot from a diesel engine that has never been up this valley, and nitrogen that came off a fertilised field two hundred kilometres away and is now feeding the cyanobacterial film, and a blue fibre eight microns long. In the outfall river there is lead that was smelted when the ice was three hundred metres thick over this spot, and caesium from a year everyone can name, released now because the archive that held them has started to melt. Nothing here was made by hands, and everything here contains hands. The one human resident is a chemistry, distributed through every body on the plain, and it is fertilising the film and darkening the ice and it has never once been visible.

## The nation in the outfall

The loud thing has inhabitants too, and they are the strictest citizens in the valley.

Where the river comes out from under the arch it is at nought point two degrees and grey with flour and running hard. On the undersides of the stones, in the millimetre where the flow slackens, black midge larvae cling with hooked prolegs and graze the bacterial film that grows on the rock flour. They are the only animal that can do this. Below four degrees nothing else competes; below two, almost nothing else can breathe fast enough. The first two hundred metres of that river is a country with one species in it and a population in the millions, and its border is a temperature.

The adults come out onto the dirty ice at the snout, walk about with their wings held flat, swarm in the sun in the one hour when the air over the ice is still, mate, lay, and are finished in four days. They do not feed. They have no working mouthparts to speak of. They are a delivery of protein with a mating behaviour attached, and half of them end up back in the water and half on the snow, where the finches take them.

When the ice withdraws far enough, the river will run clear, and warm, and everything will move in — the mayflies, the caddis, the stoneflies, the fish eventually, the whole ordinary crowd — and the midge will be eaten and shaded and outcompeted out of its own country. The river with a name will not have it in it. It is a species whose entire nation is the first two hundred metres of something that is being deleted.

## Clocks

In one square metre of the oldest ground, at the far end of the plain, the residents are holding wildly different amounts of time.

A map lichen the width of a shirt button, growing at a fifth of a millimetre a year, ninety years old, has not moved since it settled and is the reason anyone can say how old the street is: the resident is the instrument. A least willow with two leaves and a stem like a thread, putting on a millimetre of wood a summer, twenty years old, older than most of the ground's insects and younger than the lichen it is leaning against. An ice crawler in its fourth year, still not adult. Springtails that will not see the next winter. A vole with one winter in it, maybe two. Midge adults with four days. A fox passing through with eight years and a territory the size of the whole valley.

The ground is ten years old. It is hosting a ninety-year-old. Everyone arrived from somewhere else, carrying their own clock, and the clocks do not agree about what this place is. To the lichen it is a lifetime's work. To the midge it is a birthplace. To the fox it is nothing at all, a corridor between two things that matter.

## The ones coming back, and the ones not here yet

Some of the residents are returning from underneath.

Where the snout has pulled back off a patch of old moss, the moss is black and brittle and looks like something scraped off a boot. Give it water and light and a fortnight, and some of it greens. Not all — most of it is properly dead — but shoots come up from stems that were alive when the ice took them, that were buried and frozen in the dark for four hundred years and have simply been holding it. The ice is not a tomb. It is a waiting room with a terrible success rate, and every year it hands a few of the previous tenants back.

And the swarf itself is the previous world. The flour the machine is making is not all fresh rock; the ice has been overriding old soils and old peat and grinding them in, and the nitrogen the cyanobacteria are living on is the bodies of a forest that stood here in a warm interval before this one. The new residents are being fed the old ones, pulverised.

Then there is the majority. The mycorrhizal fungus that cannot arrive until there is a root to arrive at. The earthworm that needs a soil that needs the moss that needs the film. The birch, whose seed is a kilometre away and downhill and will be here in eighty years, and after it the shade, and after the shade everything that only lives in shade. The crow. The beetle that eats the fungus that eats the birch. None of them are here. All of them are, in the only sense this ground understands, on their way.

Ask who lives in the foreland and the honest answer is a long list of arrivals and a long list of departures with almost nothing in the middle.

## What actually lives here

There is one resident with a stable body temperature, and it is not an animal.

The melt line is at nought point nought degrees and stays there, because that is what melting is: a phase change holding a temperature against everything the sky can do to it. It is a kilometre long and a few centimetres wide. It has an edge and a direction and a metabolism, in the loose sense that it consumes ice and produces water and heat and a crowd. In the morning it climbs the valley at about the pace of a walking person, and everything with legs walks with it, and in the evening it comes back down and everything comes back with it. Twice a day, a migration. Every animal on this plain lives inside its body or within a stone's throw of it, and away from it there is a kilometre of nothing.

It is the only warm-blooded thing in the valley and it is made of ice.

And it is leaving. The daily commute is nested inside a one-way walk: each summer the seam quarters a little higher up the valley than the last, and the crowd follows, and the ground behind it is handed over to the slow shift — the film, the moss, the lichen, the willow, the ninety-year clock. The springtails can follow. The finches can follow. The midge can follow, for a while, until there is no arch to come out of. The ice crawler can follow until the ridge stops. Above the col there is no more mountain, and the animal whose whole body is set to a temperature the world is quietly abolishing will arrive at the top of its country and find that the country has ended, and there will be nowhere left that is cold enough to be home.

That is the true population of the foreland: a crowd at a moving door, most of them following it up, some of them about to run out of wall.

---

At three in the morning the plain is locked and hollow-sounding, the needle ice has lifted the whole skin of the silt an inch off its bed, and the residents are distributed like this. The springtails are in the ice, mid-gesture, alive. The spider is under a quartz slab with her sac against the stone, and the stone is giving back yesterday's sun one degree at a time. The crawler is hunting, because the inside of the rubble is always its own hour. The vole is awake four metres down in the moraine's warm dark, eating something it put away in August. The finches are asleep in a crevice a kilometre below. And at the snout, where the portal breathes out water that has never seen the sky, the midge larvae are grazing in the film on the underside of the stones, at nought point two, in the dark, in the roar — the one address in the valley where the shift never ends, and the last lights on.
