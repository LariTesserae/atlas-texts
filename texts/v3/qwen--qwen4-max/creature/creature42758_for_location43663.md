The bowl will hand you a noun in the first minute and the noun will be wrong.

*Tree.* It is the obvious one. Nine crowns, ten thousand leaves apiece, held out flat and stiff in the warm standing air, and every leaf tipped with a point aimed back at its own roots, so that each crown spends its life drinking itself. Nothing here reaches for anything. The leaves do not follow the light; they were built once, in the dark, aimed at the strip of sky that comes over the rim at midmorning and leaves at three, and they have been aimed there ever since. The green part of this creature is a ceiling. It is not where the living happens. It is only how the living is paid for.

Go down instead. Under the pan, under the crust of algae, the trunk resolves into a bundle of roots the color of candle-wax, and the bundle is not a support. It is machinery. It is full of plumbing under pressure, of collars and valves and bladders, of glands that make acid a molecule at a time, of hollows grown to exact tolerances around stones. Some of those hollows hold cobbles. Some hold workpieces. One of them, in a spoonful of gel behind a plug of cortex, holds a child.

The first thing to understand about the hand at the bottom of the bowl is that it cannot move quickly, and does not need to, and has arranged never to have to.

It lifts by water. Five months to raise a melon-sized fist a foot and a half. It builds the fist around the cobble rather than picking the cobble up: a socket of its own tissue, a lattice of finer roots laid over it like a wrapping of string, and the stone is inside the hand the way a tooth is inside a jaw. To aim, it does not aim. It grows a cradle. It hollows a guide, walls a channel, quarries the pan beneath the workpiece to a shape that permits exactly one path of fall, and it spends years on that carpentry, and then it lifts the hammer for a year into the mouth of the guide, and at the top of the stroke a collar of cells empties itself in the time it takes to say a word, and the arm goes over.

A year of lifting. A tenth of a second of falling. That is the only fast thing that has ever happened in this place.

And the whole art of it is not force and not skill. It is *constraint*. The crown cannot swing well; it can only build a small world in which the wrong fall is impossible. It does not throw. It arranges for a fall. Everything you would call intelligence in this bowl is in the arranging, and the arranging is done by taking away — acid off the platform, stone off the core, floor out of the pan, hollow out of its own flesh. It has one verb. It has been conjugating it since before the ice.

---

Consider what it is holding when it drops the hammer.

The workpiece has been in preparation for three seasons: a nodule washed out of the wall, its platform etched and roughened by secreted acid until the stone will break along the plane the parent has decided on. Inside the butt, sealed, is the embryo. It has been in there the whole time. It was in there while the platform was dressed. It is in there now, under the raised fist, in the dark, in its spoonful of gel, and the parent has spent a year lifting a rock over it.

Most of the time the fracture goes wrong and the child is in the pieces.

The pavement is the pieces. Fans of them, drifts of them, sorted by the dawn-drip, sharp enough to open a hide, and under them more, and under those more, a week of digging deep. And the shattered ones wash down to the swallow-hole and are rounded there in the gravel, and the crown reaches into that gravel for its next hammerstone, so that the stone it strikes the next child with is the ruins of an earlier one. Nobody here would call that anything. It is simply where the cobbles come from.

Once in a long while the fracture runs the plane, and the case opens along the same edge that would open a hide, and the child is lying alive in its fracture-scar with a working edge above it, and the bowl takes the knock off its four wet walls and hands it around and holds the ringing a while.

That sound is the only news that has ever arrived here.

---

Nine crowns, and each of them hears perhaps forty of those in a life, and a life is six or eight hundred years.

They cannot tell whose it was. Or — this is the part the bowl will not settle for you — each pan has its own note, the water ticks a different pitch in every quarry, and the ringing comes back around off the walls in a fixed order, and a root system that has been reading vibration through wet limestone for eight centuries may well know which pan spoke. It is possible that the nine are not nine strangers knocking in the dark but something more like a room of people who can say one sentence to each other, and say it once a decade, and the sentence is *someone has succeeded, over there.* It is possible the decade itself is negotiated: that a clean knock runs through the pavement and into every root-fist in the bowl and something in them lets go of its tension and begins the slow lift again from the bottom. One thought, shared, ten years wide.

The bowl does not say. Write it down as a maybe and move on.

What is certain is that everything else the crown knows, it knows as chemistry and pressure. It knows the saturation of the water, which is to say it knows how fast the stone is coming onto its skin. It knows the sourness of its own pan, because it makes the sourness. It knows the weight of the cobble in its socket to a precision no eye could match, because it grew the socket to that weight. It knows nothing whatever about the sky. There is a strip of ordinary sunlight that crosses this floor every day and ten thousand edges light up under it and nothing in the bowl turns, or leans, or notices, or has ever noticed, in two million years.

The clever part is blind and underground. The green part is a wage.

---

A crown's whole life is a refusal to be still.

The water wants to give it stone. It will fur a fallen leaf white in a week and sheathe a dead root in a month, and a crown that stopped shedding, stopped extending, stopped quarrying its own pan down against the upward creep of the lip, would be plastered into the floor inside a season. So it does not stop. It drops leaves and makes leaves; it sloughs bark; it grows away from its own encrustation at exactly the rate the encrustation forms; it deepens its quarry a hair a year, forever, to stay at the same height in a place that is trying to fill in around it. Eight hundred years of treading water. That is the biography. There is no other event in it.

And at the end it sits down.

That is all death is here. The pace slips, the water catches up, the wax-colored bundle goes still and the travertine takes it, and under the moss the acid seams open and take it back, and what is left is a stump going soft, and then not even that, and then a pan: a quarried vacancy in the floor, the exact shape of a place where something stood.

There is a tenth one at the eastern end. It has been empty four hundred years.

---

Around the rim of it lie eleven seeds, at various distances, like people outside a door.

They are not striving. This is the thing to get right about the drama here, because you will want to make it a drama and it is not one. A freed seed is moved a few feet by the sheet-water and stops where it stops, and where it stops is the whole of its fate, decided in an instant by a fracture, centuries before anything matters. It cannot creep. It cannot reach. It has no reserves for trying. It lies in its scar with the gel gone to varnish and it waits, and it can wait four hundred years, and what it is waiting for is a change in the water.

A living crown sours its pan. When the crown sits down, the sourness stops, and the pan goes sweet, and the nearest varnish swells.

That is germination. Not spring, not light, not warmth — the taste of an absence. The seed does not know anything has died. It knows the water changed. And if it is not the nearest, it feels the pan go sour again as the winner takes hold and begins its own acid, and it stops, and varnishes over, and goes back to the wait, and it can do this more than once.

So each of the nine stands in a thin halo of its own children, arranged by nothing, waiting for it to finish. It makes them anyway. One tool at a time, a decade a tool, most of them shattered, a few freed, all of them laid down at random within a few feet of a vacancy that will not exist until it stops. There is no word in the bowl for what that is. It is not grief and it is not waste and it is not hope. It is the rate at which the place makes more of itself.

The youngest of the nine got its pan four hundred years ago, the same century the eastern one emptied. It got it by landing eleven inches closer than something else. That is the entire competitive history of this lineage. Everything that looks like fitness here was decided by where a piece of stone happened to fall.

---

The others who live here are the ones small enough to afford it.

Moss in the wet seams, deep enough to lose a forearm, growing at the tip and dying at the base and letting the dead part go to peat and acid — which is where the sourness under the floor comes from, which is what takes bone back, and what keeps the pans from sealing, and possibly what dresses a platform when the crown's own glands are slow. Liverworts like wet green leather. Horsetails in the south shallows, an old family, older than the crowns by three hundred million years, here for the same reason they were once everywhere: they can outrun the stone by growing. Ferns in every grike. One colony of ribbed cartwheel leaves that catches a pint of dawn-water and tips it, slowly, at noon — the only clock in the bowl that isn't the sun.

And under every turned stone, the white thread. The fungus is the only eater in the place. It has nothing to eat but shed bark and furred leaves and the slow surrender of crowns, and it eats them without hurry, and it is the reason anything here can be taken back at all.

None of them are colleagues. None of them are prey, or pollinators, or threats. They are the conditions. The crowns live among them the way a hand lives among the air.

---

Now the two things at the bottom of the bowl, which turn out to be one thing.

The first is the old hand-axe in the swallow-hole gravel, worked on both faces, straighter than any root has ever managed, the exemplar the whole tradition has been copying and drifting from and drifting back toward for a hundred thousand generations.

The second is the bulb of unworked cortex at the butt of every finished tool, palm-sized, palm-shaped, kept true through all that copying, which fits your hand.

Here is what I think lives in this bowl, and it is not nine trees.

The trees barely change. They cannot: no flower, no pollen, no mixing, nothing but a bud in a spoonful of gel, copied and copied, as near identical as anything can be that has been copying itself since before the ice. The wood is not where the history is. The wood is a stage.

The stone is where the history is. The stone is what varies, generation to generation — a platform dressed slightly differently, an angle drifted a degree, a bulb kept or lost — and the stone is what selects, because the fracture that frees the embryo is the fracture that makes the edge, and a shape that breaks well leaves descendants and a shape that shatters does not. The selector here is not weather or predation or appetite. It is fracture mechanics. Stone likes certain shapes. Two million years of copying has been a slow walk toward the shapes stone likes, and the old axe in the gravel is not a teacher and not a god. It is a fixed point. It is where the walking was always going.

So: a stone phase, lying in a scar, sometimes for four centuries. A wood phase, standing in a quarry, eight centuries, blind, patient, quarrying its own grave. And between them a single blow, which is birth in one direction and death in the other, and which is the only loud thing, and the only fast thing, and the only thing anyone in the bowl could call an event.

That is the organism. It has two bodies and it wears them in turn. The crowns are not making tools. The tools are making crowns, the way a fern makes a frond, and the hand-axe is not an artifact of this species, it is the other half of it, and the debitage on the floor is not a workshop litter. It is a nursery with the dead left in.

And the bulb fits your hand because a grip is not a human thing. It is what happens where a force meets a stone it must not let go of. A root-fist solving that problem and a hand solving that problem arrive at the same hollow, and the hollow has been passed down the copying for two million years, refined, kept, held true, and when you pick the tool up you are not touching evidence of yourself. You are touching a relative. Another solution to holding on. It fits because you are also a thing that holds stones, and there was only ever going to be one shape.

Nobody made it for you. Nobody made it. It was arrived at, by removal, in the warm wet dark, by something that has never had a hand and has never needed one.

---

There is a hammer in the air right now.

Somewhere at the base of one of the nine, in the dark under ten thousand stiff leaves, a fist the size of a melon is at the top of a guide it spent three years hollowing, over a workpiece it spent three seasons dressing, with a child sealed in the butt of it, and the collar of cells is full and tight and will hold for another few months yet.

It has been falling since before you were born. It will land, and the bowl will either take the knock around its four walls and hold the ringing a while, or it will not, and either way the water will go on ticking from leaf to pan in notes that do not form anything.

It is not waiting for you. It is not waiting.

It is falling.
