## Small

Begin where the numbers stop being numbers.

The moss is not boiling. The moss is *occupied*. In a good square metre there are four hundred thousand midge larvae, and they are wingless, and they have been wingless long enough that the wing is now only a rumour in the thorax — a folded plan, a set of muscles with nothing to drive, kept the way a house keeps a staircase to a floor it no longer has. Anything that flew here was removed from the population by the wind in about four seconds. What is left crawls, and breeds in the top three millimetres of wet green, and is the entire economy.

They run on a tide. Every forty minutes the seiche crosses eighty kilometres of pan and the water goes somewhere else, and for ninety seconds a given hand's-breadth of mat is aired instead of drowned, and the larvae come up. So there is a forty-minute pulse in the animals, and the frogs time their breeding to it, and the sundews open on it, and if you sit still long enough you can feel the whole skin of the plateau inhale and let go, inhale and let go, like something asleep and enormous underneath you.

There is a beetle here that eats the paper snails. Because the snails are almost nowhere, the beetle is almost nowhere: perhaps four thousand animals in a single seep on the north-north-east side of a doline the crews call the Kettle, and not one anywhere else on the pan, and not one anywhere else at all. Nobody has counted them. Somebody has counted the swifts.

## The landlords

Nothing on this floor begins on bare ground. Nothing has for a very long time. Every seed that germinates here germinates inside somebody else's eight hundred years.

The cushions are the only architecture. Inside a dome, on a bright afternoon when the air is four degrees and moving at ninety, it is nineteen degrees and dead still, and there is a smell of warm rot, and there are midges in numbers that make the surface twitch, and there is a frog overwintering with its eyes open, and there are eleven other plant species that have never in their lineage managed to establish anywhere else. The cushion builds a millimetre of this a season. It has been building since before the language you are thinking in existed. It is not a plant so much as a slow animal that has decided to be a place.

The crews name them. There is one near the hut called the Bishop, and one out past Intake 14 called the Sofa, and the Bishop has a brown seam across its crown where a man stood on it in 1961 to get a photograph, and the seam has grown four centimetres since, and will be there when the man's grandchildren are old.

And in a big south-westerly the mat calves, and an acre of somebody's four centuries comes off the stone it has spent that long gripping, and sails across the shallows with the sedges still upright and the frogs still on it, and grounds on a trash rack, and is cut into strips by a man with a hay knife who is swearing in three languages and does not know, and could not be expected to know, that he is butchering the oldest living thing he will ever touch.

## Under

The holes are the other country.

Sixty metres down, out of the wind, in wet warmth, the tree ferns put out fronds two metres long that have never been torn by anything, and the myrtle lays down rings that record not years but squalls, so that a trunk cut through is a history of weather rather than of time. It rains inside the dolines about four minutes after it rains on the pan. The moss on the branches is not hanging; it is *upholstery*, thick as a wrist, and if you put your arm into it you will find it is inhabited to the depth of your elbow.

Each hole has its own fish. Ninety millimetres, cold-blooded to the point of being nearly optional, thirty years old, eating drowned midges and, once a decade, a frog that misjudged. The Kettle fish has a hump behind the head. The Cathedral fish has an orange throat and eyes that are more suggestion than organ. They have not met since the water was young. They exchange perhaps once in a thousand years, when a storm drives enough depth across the fluted stone to make a road, and otherwise each population lives its whole existence under a lid of white light with weather on it, and knows the sky only as a sound.

The crews say the holes feel different in the chest. You go down the ladder out of the gale and the noise drops away and something else arrives, low, slow, right behind the sternum, and they blame the hum. But the hum is a hundred hertz and comes up through the rock everywhere equally, and it does not vary from hole to hole, and it does not stop.

## Imported

The swifts cannot eat here. The midges do not fly; there is nothing in the air of this plateau to catch. What the swifts eat is *delivered*: aphids, flies, ballooning spiders, the whole light rubbish of a lowland summer, lifted two thousand metres up the face of the escarpment by a wind that has not changed quarter since anyone was counting, and sifted out of the air by ten thousand birds going in and out of the falling water like needles into a pincushion.

The nest is glued to wet rock behind a cataract with spit. The chick hears the cataract from inside the egg. It will fly through that cataract four hundred times a day and it will never once touch the pan, because there is nothing on the pan to hold.

In a calm, the conveyor stops. The air quits delivering. And the column goes over the rim to feed, all of it, in about eleven seconds, and the sky empties.

Which means: the only time you will ever see this place without birds in it is the only time you could hear anything.

## The loop

The eels come up the wet rock at night in their thousands, thin as bootlaces, working twelve hundred metres of cliff in the spray, and arrive on the pan and go into the holes and the channels and grow there for forty years, and then want to go down. They find the bell-mouths because the bell-mouths are where the water goes. This is not a mistake they can be taught out of. It is the whole of their instruction.

So the crews rake them off the screens into buckets, and the buckets go over the rim on a wire, and this has been happening for sixty years, which is nothing, which is one and a half eels.

The eel's life now contains a stage that is a bucket, and a stage that is a man in rubber over wool. Not metaphorically. The species' route to the sea runs through a hand. If the crews stopped for two seasons the run would fail, and if the scheme were decommissioned tomorrow the animal would go with it, and no one would decide this, and no one would notice for eighty years.

There is one that has not gone. A metre and a half of her, grey about the head, living in the rack bay at 14, taking elvers, taking anything, known to every crew since the first one. When Intake 6 was abandoned she was netted and flown in a tank and put into 14, and the entry is in a logbook in a drawer. She is the only animal on the pan with a service history.

The ones that go down the penstock do not arrive. Nothing arrives. At the bottom the water hits the buckets at a hundred and forty metres a second and after that there is only water, and it goes out through the tailrace carrying the pan in suspension, and two days after a big storm the taps in a city two thousand metres below taste faintly of wet air and nothing, and nobody there has ever heard of the Bishop.

## Tenants

The otters cannot see the tramway rails under ten centimetres of tea-brown water. They feel them: two parallel lines of steel, running from a hole full of fish to a concrete apron where you can haul out and be out of the wind. The line was laid in 1938 to service intakes that have been re-sited twice. It goes nowhere. It has one customer. The cubs learn it by whisker, following the mother, and the mother learned it from hers, and there is a road on this plateau that exists only as a behaviour, and it is older than the scheme and younger than the stone, and it will end when one bad winter takes the old female and nobody left knows the way.

They raid the eel buckets. Hence some of the swearing.

And there is a harrier the crews call the Auditor, because she follows the gauge round.

She does not listen for frogs. A hundred thousand frogs calling at once is a texture, not a signal, and no ear can pull an individual out of it. What she listens for is the *hole* — the small moving silence where a frog has seen a shadow and shut up. She reads the gaps. She quarters at head height, tilting, and the shape of the quiet tells her where the sedge is worth dropping into.

Which is why she works the gauge-reader. A man walking the mat silences about two square metres continuously, and the frogs go back on behind him, and the Auditor has learned to fly twenty metres back along his line and take the ones that flush late. She sits on the bronze staff gauge post while he reads the staff. She has been doing this, or her mother was, for longer than anyone has kept records. She is probably eleven. She has raised nine chicks and the pan has taken seven.

## Nine

They wear rubber over wool. They read lips and use their hands, and the hands have become a language with about forty signs in it, half of which are for things that do not exist below two thousand metres: a sign for *the water is going the wrong way again*, a sign for a raft grounded on a rack, a sign for the Landlady, and a sign for the calm, which is a flat palm rising and then stopping, and which is used in conversation more often than you would expect.

Within a year everyone has a notch in their hearing between four and eight kilohertz. This is the frogs' country, that band; the frogs moved up into it over a very long time to get above the water-noise, and the crews move out of it in twelve months. New arrivals lie awake at night listening to a hundred thousand small brown bodies making a sound like a workshop. Old hands lie awake listening to the wind, and the cables, and the hum, and their own hands, which talk in their sleep.

There is a room in the hut with double doors and a nail with ear-defenders on it. Men sit in there sometimes without doing anything at all. It is the only room on the plateau where you can hear yourself think, and most of them discover they would rather not.

They rotate. Some come back. One has done eleven winters and cannot sleep below two thousand metres and keeps a fan running in a bedroom in a city, and his wife has stopped asking.

And once or twice a season the wind drops, and the rain goes vertical, and the cables stop singing, and the swifts go over the rim, and eighty kilometres of cataract arrives at once as a bass floor with no direction to it — and every one of them, every time, including the eleventh winter, stops walking and stands still in the water and looks up.

They are not all listening to the same thing.

## The pulse

In the floor there is a beat.

It is slow. It is somewhere under forty hertz, which is to say it is mostly not a sound, and it repeats about every eleven seconds, and it is coming up out of the holes, and it is very close to the rhythm of your own heart, which is the entire difficulty with it. The first time you notice it you assume it is you. The second time you assume it is a pump. The third time you go and stand at the lip of the Cathedral in a calm and look down sixty metres of blue-green tea at a canopy level with your shins, and you understand that the quiet down there is full of something, and has been the whole time.

It is a frog. Brown, moss-backed, squat as a dropped stone, the size of a hand, forty years old, lying in the leaf litter under the tree ferns where it is nineteen degrees and dead still. It calls low and long and slow, all year, into the acoustic shadow of the hole, where the pan is only a lid of light with weather on it and the noise floor is forty decibels lower than the one you are standing in. It is not rare. It is almost certainly the commonest vertebrate on the plateau. It lays ten thousand eggs and the bladderworts take most of the tadpoles and two survive, and it does this perhaps five times in a life, and the rest of the time it lies under the upholstery and keeps time.

Two have ever been collected, both by accident, both out of an eel bucket. One is in a jar in a museum in a city that drinks this water, and the label has a question mark on it.

A single call runs about ninety seconds. The longest calm on record is seventy-one. Which means that no human being has ever heard one of these animals finish a sentence. Everyone who has heard it has heard the middle of it, and everyone has heard a different middle, and they compare them in the hut with their hands, and the arguments are long and entirely good-natured and nobody has ever won one.

The young ones cannot hear it at all; they have the top of their hearing still, and the workshop is deafening, and they think the old men are talking about tinnitus. The old men have the notch. The old men have spent a year going deaf in exactly the right place.

You do not lose your hearing here. You get retuned. It takes about twelve months, it is not reversible, and at the end of it you can hear the thing that lives in the holes.

## Early

Eventually a hole takes the pan. One doline deepens past its neighbours and captures the drainage, and the sheet thins, and the stone comes up blinking, and the water-noise — the thing every loud animal here has spent two hundred million years shouting over — falls away.

Then the high, sharp, metallic frogs are calling into a silence that carries a kilometre, and every harrier on the plateau can hear each one individually, and the workshop closes. And out of the holes, at the speed of tree fern, comes the quiet nation that has been sitting under the myrtle in the warm wet dark, keeping time in a register nobody could hear, waiting for the room to go quiet.

So: a hundred thousand loud things that are loud because the water is loud. A larger number of slow things that are quiet because the holes are quiet. A beetle in one seep. A road that exists only in the whiskers of four otters. An eel with a service history. A bird that hunts the shape of a silence. Nine people being moved, one year at a time, out of the first country and into the second.

And the forest at your shins.

They are not short. They are early.
