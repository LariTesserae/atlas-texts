## The Majority

Begin with the largest citizen, which is not an animal, and is not one.

The black crust on the quiet faces is alive on its outer skin only. Cut a patch and you get a black line a fraction of a millimeter thick, then the pale carbonate beneath it, then bedrock. Everything below the line is spent — deposited, finished, architecture. The living part is a front, an advancing margin, and behind it lies the accumulated corpse of its own making. A patch the size of your hand is nine hundred years old at the center and one year old at the edge, and the center is not dead so much as *converted*. It has become the wall.

The black is not decoration. It is a sun-trap. On a morning when the pavement is at minus eight, a black patch reaches melting an hour before the grey stone beside it, and so gets an hour more life per day than its neighbor, and so grows, and so makes more black. This is the whole of its strategy and it has been sufficient for a length of time that does not bear saying.

Under it, in the pores of the rind, the consortium: the alga that fixes carbon, the fungus that holds the water, three or four bacteria that do the arithmetic of turning dust into shell. None of them can be counted as individuals in any way that survives inspection. They are a metabolism distributed through a mineral, and the mineral is their skeleton and their leavings and their proof.

And they are not slow. That is the mistake the plain invites. They are *rare*. The rind is metabolically active during the dawn drip, and during rain, and at no other time — perhaps five hundred hours in a year. A nine-hundred-year-old patch has been alive for about twenty years, in slices, spread thin across the Holocene. It does not experience the millennium as a long afternoon. It experiences it as twenty years of ordinary mornings, with the gaps not experienced at all.

Everyone here lives that way. The pan does not stretch life out. It samples it.

## The Grazers

The thumb-sized thing is not white. It is colorless, which is different: a waxy, faintly blue-clear cuticle through which you can watch its lunch. There is a green line down the middle of it, the gut, and the green moves as it feeds, and when it has been eating moss the line is emerald and when it has been scraping crust the line is grey-gold. This is the only color any animal in the pan possesses, and it is borrowed.

No eyes. Not reduced eyes, not eyes under a hood of shell — the lineage never spent anything on them, and there is nothing to find in the sockets because there are no sockets. Light reaches it anyway, as heat, through a shell thin enough to be warm on one side. It knows morning by which flank is being touched.

In the abdomen there is a sac: thin-walled, air-filled, connected to the tracheae, its tension adjustable by a small muscle that never entirely rests. It is a resonator. It is not an ear. The distinction matters and it is the whole of the animal's life.

The hall plays the sac. The grazer does not listen to the room; the room *rings the grazer*, the way a struck tuning fork rings its twin across a table, and the animal reads the amplitude of its own body. Where the note is strong it buzzes. Where the note is weak it hangs slack. It moves up its own loudness the way a moth moves up a smell, and it has no more idea what the hall sounds like than a compass needle has an idea of north.

It is not deaf to everything but the building. It is a *part* of the building, temporarily detachable, and it navigates by feeling how well it is currently attached.

A grazer lives sixty years. It is awake for perhaps two hours a day — the drip and the calm evening — and frozen solid the rest of the time, glycerol in the hemolymph, ice in the body cavity and none in the cells, thawing at dawn to resume a bite begun the previous afternoon. Sixty years old, and it has been conscious for about three of them. It has never once experienced a night.

It lays one egg at a time, in the wet seam, and does not guard it. The hatchling is tuned to whatever chamber it wakes in. There are no families here and no inherited addresses. A hall's population is not a lineage; it is a congregation, assembled out of strangers by a frequency.

## What the Loud Places Are

Here is the thing that makes the pan a single organism rather than a place with organisms in it.

In a standing wave, the pressure antinode — the loudest point in the room — is the place where the air moves least. Loudness is stillness. The grazer, climbing the gradient of its own buzzing, arrives at the stillest pocket of air in the chamber.

Which is exactly where the rind can grow.

So the animal is led by loudness to quiet, and by quiet to food, and it eats the food. It crops the crust back to a finger's thickness and no further, because below that the crust is too thin to be worth the scraping and because there are never enough grazers to do more. Cropped, the wall stays porous; porous, it keeps taking fog; taking fog, it keeps weeping; weeping, it keeps the seam green. Uncropped, it would seal, and harden, and thicken, and change the throat, and the note would climb until the room said something no sac in it could answer.

The grazers are not living in the standing wave. They are one of the terms that holds it standing. They are the reason the rind's rate and the wind's rate have a crossing point at all. Remove them and in four hundred years the halls close like fists.

Their cuticle is mineralized with carbonate they took from the wall. When they die the mites take the soft parts in a week and the rest goes back into the seams as dust, and the consortium lays it down again somewhere quiet. There is no bone in the pan and no midden because there is no waste stream. The citizens eat the city and are afterward used for repairs.

## The Hour of Millions

The count of forty is a count of what a hand could pick up.

There is a second census, and it occupies the same coordinates in a different substance. In the water films of the moss, in the drip-lines, in the meltwater under the ice lids of the pits: nematodes, rotifers, tardigrades, ciliates, in numbers that make the word *population* useless. A thimble of the wet seam holds more animals than the plain holds stones.

They are not counted because they are not, most of the time, here. Nine-tenths of the day they are tuns — dried, contracted, metabolic zero, indistinguishable from the grit they lie in, and they will stay that way for a decade if the seam runs dry and come back in eleven minutes when it doesn't.

So the pan's population is forty at noon and something on the order of ten million at half past six in the morning, and it makes that transition every day, and nothing about the grey swept streets records it. The city is enormously inhabited for one hour and then is a cathedral with a moth in it, and both descriptions are exact.

## The Long Thing

Once in some years, from the direction of the cold.

Longer than your forearm and low enough to fit a hand under. Six legs, the same six, the same waxy colorlessness, the same absence of anything like a face. Along the length of the body the sac has become a tube, and the tube is not tuned to a note. It is tuned to a *relation* between notes. It can hold two halls at once and feel the interval.

You hear it coming as a hole in the chord. It is big enough that walking a hexagon corridor spoils the corridor's flute-note, and the spoilage travels ahead of it through the net, a notch moving in the spectrum, and every grazer in the pan feels its own body falter a half-second before the thing arrives at their doorway. They do not flee. There is nowhere to flee to and no organ for recognizing a predator. They simply stop buzzing, which is the only alarm they have, and it is an alarm they broadcast to themselves.

It takes one from every third hall. Always the one at the antinode — the loudest, the best-settled, the oldest, the one that has most completely become a piece of its room. Then it goes, out over the rim, onto the plain where there is nothing.

There is no way to know what it does out there. The reasonable guess is that it lies in a frost crack and stops, for years, the way everything here stops, and that what wakes it is a change in the pan's chord — a hall breaking its note, a winter opening a window, the whole instrument going briefly out of tune. If that is right, it is not a predator that visits. It is a response the pan makes to instability, and it arrives on the schedule of instability, and its cropping is the reason a broken note resettles in a decade instead of a century.

The other guess, which cannot be tested and which the grazers' behavior on the open floor makes hard to dismiss, is that it is one of them. A grazer whose hall broke, that crossed the pavement and did not find the new note, that kept walking with a slack sac through a night and another night — most of those die out there — and that a very few do not die, and molt, and molt again, with nothing to tune to, and get long.

## The Visitor

Twice a summer, a bird.

It comes over the rim, drops to a pit, drinks, and stands there stupid for a minute with the chord going through its skull at a volume that makes its own thoughts unavailable to it. Then it leaves. It has no reason to stay; there is nothing here it can eat that is worth the noise.

It is the only import route. Everything in the pan that did not come out of the rock arrived on a foot or in a feather — the mites, the moss spores, whatever bacterial passenger founded the consortium a hundred thousand years ago and is now the walls. The bird does not know it is the postal service. It knows the pan as a place where water is, briefly, on the way to somewhere with more in it.

## The Room With Nothing In It

The silent hall near the eastern rim has no citizen, and this is not an absence. It is a function.

Walk into it and the sac goes slack. Not quiet — *slack*, unplayed, the muscle straining against a body that has stopped being an instrument. For the first time in its life the animal is not a part of anything. It is a thumb-sized object standing on fluted grey stone in ordinary daylight, containing a green line, and there is nothing in the world telling it where it is.

Most of them back out. That is the whole of the superstition: a doorway where you stop existing in the only sense available to you.

Some go on through, and up, and over the rim.

And the hall itself is the one place in the pan that is not in balance. The rind will not take there — no still air, no antinode, no purchase — so the bedrock is being eaten without armor, and the room is very slowly getting larger, and its openings are very slowly changing shape. In some thousands of years the cancellation will fail. The wind will go in and come out having said something. The rind will find a quiet pocket and start, and the moss will follow the weeping, and a hatchling will wake tuned to a note that has never existed before.

It is the only thing in the pan that is beginning.

## The Citizen

Stand at the drip hour with the light coming in bars and every vault weeping and count what is actually alive: a black front advancing a hair, a consortium turning dust into shell, ten million tuns opening like small fists, thirty colorless animals climbing their own loudness toward the still air where the food is, and one long thing asleep in a crack on the plain, waiting for the music to change.

None of them is the resident. The resident is the loop they close — stone eaten, stone laid, stone cropped, stone eaten — which has no body and no boundary and no place where it can be pointed at, and which has been running without interruption since before there were mammals, and which uses forty animals at a time the way a river uses a particular hundred gallons of water.

At the end of the drip a grazer stops mid-bite as the cold comes back into its joints, and holds, and freezes with its mouthparts against the crust, and the green line stands still in it. In the morning it will thaw and finish the gesture. It will do this for sixty years. It will never notice a gap.
