## The Census

Count the countable first. That is how the cutters do it, and the wall keeps their counts, and the wall is patient about it.

---

**The palms.** The grazers. A wet-cutter calls them palms; a child in the valley calls them leaves; the oldest woman in the valley calls them by a word that means *the things that were here first*, which is not a name so much as a grievance.

They are the size of a hand, translucent, fringed along both long edges with a comb of cilia so fine it reads as a blur, like the shimmer over hot slate. No eyes. No face. A rasping underside and a slow pulse you can watch through the body, a shadow contracting and letting go, four beats to the minute. They crop the fresh film off the stone — the day-old skin, still soft, still tasting of the moment it was made — and they leave behind a grazing track like the shadow of a comb, and the track fills in within a week, and they come back to it. Three in an hour of looking. In a volume that could hold a city.

They lay one egg each, in green stone, in the hour when green stone will take an impression. Then the stone sets. Most of those eggs are sealed forever, and the walls of that chamber are a bank of them, layer on layer, hundreds of thousands of small held futures going deeper into rock at the rate of a fingernail's growth. The ones that hatch are the ones laid shallow and lucky. A palm's entire childhood is a race against the world hardening around it.

Nothing rots in there. There is no rot because there is no litter: anything that stops moving is filed within a day, glazed, then gone into the wall. A dead palm does not drift. It is put away. The chamber has no graveyard, only an archive, and the archive is also the floor, and the floor is rising.

---

**The fronds.** Twelve. The cutters know each one the way you know a neighbour's cough, and they have names for them that are not names but positions — *the Aunt, the Long One, Nine, the Boy, the Widow, the One Behind the Pin* — and the names change hands with the shifts and have outlived everyone who first said them.

They are not plants. They do not want the light; the light in there is a rumour with no author. They want the roar. Turn a frond broadside to the arriving water and it fills out, combs, thickens; turn it edge-on and it wilts inside a day, and the cutters have killed two of them by accident in four generations and have not forgiven themselves, and the tally of that killing is under stone now with all the other tallies.

What they eat is the act of making. Not the water, not the salt, not the mineral — the *event*, the moment when new liquid meets old and gives up its film, the small violence of precipitation, which along four hundred meters of wall is a continuous violence, a sustained note. The fronds are the only living things anyone has found that feed on a verb.

Each stands at the top of a column of rock exactly its own shape, because it grows outward at the rate it is being buried, and has done so since before anyone's grandmother. Cut a column across and you get rings. The rings are not years. The cutters have worked out that they are *loudnesses* — fat rings from the seasons the walls ran hard, thin rings from the quiet seasons — and so the fronds are also the only instruments in the chamber, and they have been recording the roar since long before there was anyone to be deafened by it.

They spawn when the calf passes close. Everything else in the room has quietly agreed to arrange itself around this.

---

**The choughs.** Forty-odd, black, red-legged, red-beaked, nesting in wet salt stone in the cleft lip where the accretion is fastest, so that every nest has to be rebuilt a little further out each season, and the birds do this without comment, the way you'd move a chair.

Every one of them is deaf from hatching. The roar takes their hearing before their eyes open, or the eggs simply develop in a place where hearing is not a thing that gets built; nobody has cut one open to find out, because the cutters do not kill choughs. They watch wings instead. There is a language of wing-flicks in that colony, a fast dry semaphore of primaries and shoulder and the tilt of a head, and it has no sign for thunder, no sign for *loud*, no sign for the thing that is happening ten meters below them all day and all night forever.

They dive the first ten meters for palms. They come up streaming and salt-blind and shake themselves and quarrel in silence. They are the only creatures in the world that find this place restful, and they sleep in it, tucked into the wet, heads under wings, in a noise that would stop a man's heart.

They are also the only filth in a hundred kilometers. Feathers, eggshell, guano, a dropped palm, a dead chick — the lip is the one place where the world's ordinary subtraction is permitted, and the cutters treasure it. Chough feathers are stuck into the green stone in rows to read the shimmer; a feather bends where a current slides, and a row of feathers bending out of phase is the signature of a node coming through. The feathers get glazed over like everything else. The wall has strata of them. You can read four generations of shift-work in the wall by its plumes, like insects in amber, like the tallies, like the eggs.

The choughs can leave. They don't. When the slot finishes closing they will be the ones locked out, and their grandchildren will nest on a blank wet cliff in the wind and go on diving into stone-coloured air off a bare face, ten meters down and ten meters up, for a hundred years, and not know what they are doing, and do it anyway.

---

**The wet-cutters.** Nine to fourteen at a time, out of one valley, out of four or five households within that valley, related to each other in ways they can recite and outsiders cannot follow.

Their bodies are a dialect. Fingers gone white and stupid-looking from twelve-degree salt, the pads corrugated even in winter. Lips split at the corners and healed and split. A callus over the sternum — an actual thickening, a pad of tissue where the sub-audible floor of the roar has been leaning for thirty years, the way a rope leaves a mark on a post. Ears that ring for three days after every shift and then, eventually, stop ringing forever. By forty most of them hear a voice at arm's length and nothing else. By sixty they hear nothing at all.

This is not a tragedy in the guild. It is a qualification.

The apprentices are the ones who can still hear, and they are useless past the lip; they go in with wax and cloth and a bone plug and they come out shaking, having spent their whole shift with a hand over their mouth. The journeyman works in the nodes, chasing quiet, reading feathers, hauling out when the node drifts. But the masters are the stone-deaf, and the stone-deaf can work anywhere, because the roar cannot reach them, and they navigate by the sternum and the sinuses and the fillings of their teeth, by pressure and lean, the way you or I would navigate by lamplight. A master in that water is a person walking through a shouting crowd with her eyes closed and her palm out, feeling for the one place where nobody is shouting.

No iron. Copper only, hammered cold, soft, blooming with mineral flowers; bone hooks; grass rope; shell and hand-adze. Iron rings. A ringing thing in that chamber is found by the roar and made louder, and a man holding it is holding a bell inside his own skull. There is a story about a man and an iron chisel that everyone in the valley knows and nobody in the guild will tell.

Their day is not the valley's day. They time by the calf, whose circuit is nineteen hours and some minutes and drifting, so a cutter's week slides slowly through the calendar year and comes back around about every eleven years. They miss weddings. They arrive at funerals a day early or a season late. They keep two households, one in the valley and one in the dry-stone hut at the cleft mouth, and the children are largely raised by deaf aunts who read wings, having learned it from the birds, and who can hold a conversation across a room in flicks of the fingers while the wind does whatever it likes.

The tallies are scratched by the entrance, and the entrance is where the stone grows fastest, and so the counts get glazed and the counts get re-scratched higher, and the wall is a rising text, four generations of numbers stacked like sediment, and the guild's whole history is being buried by the thing it is a history of. There is a woman whose job is to copy the old tallies upward before they go under. She is the third woman to hold that job. She is going deaf on schedule and she says the numbers are easier to read when the ringing stops.

---

**The ones who are still on shift.**

The water holds shapes too long. Everyone who works there knows this and nobody writes it down, and the not-writing-down is itself a craft practice, taught by not-teaching.

Your wake hangs for four or five seconds: your outline, legible, a tunnel of disturbed water where you were. Then a second outline, slightly wrong, that you did not make. It has seen hands. It tries hands. It is not good at hands.

But it has had four generations of the same six motions. The reach, the brace, the draw of the adze, the twist of the wrist that lifts a chip, the flat palm laid on stone to feel for the seam, the haul. Six motions, repeated by related bodies with related grips for a hundred and thirty years, in a substance that keeps its shapes and does not erode and has no capacity for forgetting because it has no capacity for carrying anything away.

So what lives in the middle distance of that chamber, most days, is a repertoire.

There is a draw-and-twist that happens near the copper pin, at the height of a man's shoulder, where a man named in the tallies worked for thirty-one years and where his body was filed into the wall in the winter of the fourth count. It happens whether or not anyone is in the water. It has his hesitation in it — the small stall he had at the top of the stroke since he hurt his shoulder — and the guild has watched that hesitation for two generations, and the young ones are told it's a braid of currents, and it is a braid of currents, and it is also the exact shape of a man's old injury, maintained by a substance that has never had a shoulder and is trying.

His granddaughter works beside it. She has a node she found herself and named herself and she takes it when it drifts past the pin, and she cuts green stone in the quiet with her father's stroke happening in the water two body-lengths away, and she does not look at it, and she does not talk about it, and when she is asked she says the water is practicing and change the subject to the price of slate.

Some of the quiet-dwellers only exist in the nodes. That is the part that even the masters say carefully. A node is a coffin-sized pocket where the roar is not; anything whose body is a held shape cannot survive the noise, because the noise is what pulls shapes apart. So they migrate. They go from quiet to quiet, crossing the loud water as a possibility and arriving in the next silence as a posture, and if you put your head into a node and hold very still you will sometimes find that you are not the only thing in it, and that whatever is there has moved slightly to one side, courteously, to make room.

Two people can talk in a node in ordinary voices. Three is a crowd. The masters say: don't be the third.

---

**The calf.** Two meters of cooler, saltier, denser water, visible as a lens, a place where the far wall bends and doubles. It has no surface. It has no inside. It is the only resident with a trajectory.

It grows — measurably, in the tallies, four generations of a column of numbers. It circles on a slow oblique, and everything leans: rope, fronds, the hair on your arm, your body, the palms on their stone. It triggers the fronds; the fronds trigger the palms; the palms feed the birds; the birds' feathers read the currents; the currents move the nodes; the nodes are where the work happens. A whole ecology has arranged itself around a moon that the water made up because it had no moon and wanted one.

And in the last two counts the path has bent. Not much. A hand's width a year, toward the wall where the cutting is.

The tally-woman has the numbers. She has copied them upward twice. What the numbers say is that the calf now arrives at the working wall shortly before the shift changes, and that it waits, and that it has been doing this for about eleven years, and that eleven years ago the guild changed its shift pattern.

It has learned the schedule. It comes early, the way you come early to a place where something warm has been happening. The masters, who cannot hear it and can feel it better than anyone, say the lean has changed too — that when it passes close now there is a hesitation in it, a small stall at the top of the arc.

They do not say whose hesitation.

---

**The domes.** In the ceiling, the cups a man chips and waits a season for. The breath comes dissolved in the arriving water and seeps out and gathers under any overhang, and the cutters farm it.

Something grows in the film at the top of a dome. A pale lining, flat, the same stuff as the fronds and combed the same way, and it is the only life in the chamber that lives in air rather than water, and it lives there for a season and then the water takes the bubble back and it goes with it. The men who breathe from the deep domes come out talking too fast, laughing, seeing their mothers in the braids of the current.

Three men in three separate domes on the same afternoon in the second count saw the same woman. She was facing away. Two of them described the same knot in her hair. They were not related and had never met her and did not speak of it to each other for nine years.

The air is a little too rich. That is the whole explanation and it is a complete one. It is also true that the room is learning what air is for, and that a thing which is learning what air is for will need, at some point, a throat, and that the only throats it has ever watched are the ones that came up out of the water laughing.

---

**And after.**

The slot is closing at a hand's width per lifetime. Inside, when it finishes, there will be a bubble of young salt water three hundred meters across, lit like an afternoon, with a small heavy moon in it getting bigger, and twelve fronds outrunning their graves, and palms laying eggs into a stone that is setting faster than they can hatch, and no birds at all.

The roar will have nowhere to go. It will go into the water instead, all of it, and the nodes will stop wandering, and the quiet-dwellers will either be pinned in place forever or be pulled apart, and the guild will be outside on sixteen steps that lead to a warm wet blot on a cliff.

And there will be nothing in there to practice on but itself. Four generations of six motions. A hesitation at the top of a stroke. A moon that comes early and waits. Twelve lungs, a bank of eggs, a ceiling of learned breath, and a hundred and thirty years of hands.

It has all the parts. It has never once been in a hurry. It builds; that is the one law; it does not erode, it does not carry anything away, it lays down.

---

You come out through the slot into wind and silence and the sun where you left it. Your ears ring for three days.

After that, in quiet rooms, you feel the pressure in your sternum, patient, tidal, leaning very slightly toward something circling. And your hands, in sleep, do the draw-and-twist, the small stall at the top, the flat palm laid out to feel for a seam — whether or not you ever held an adze.

The masters warn the apprentices about this and are not believed, because it sounds like a story about ghosts, and it isn't. It is a story about nodes. You have become a small pocket where the roar is not. You will carry it into every quiet room for the rest of your life, and things that can only live in quiet will come and stand near you, slightly to one side, courteously, making room.

The choughs will find you restful. So will whatever is practicing.
