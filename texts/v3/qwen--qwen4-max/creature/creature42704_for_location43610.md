# WHO LIVES ON THE SILL

## i. Four hundred, and the same again in liverwort

Ask a count-house keeper how many people live in the basin and she will say four hundred and some, and then, after a pause whose length is a matter of manners, she will add the mantles.

The people first. They are long and narrow and they cannot float. This is the fact that organizes everything else about them: the bones are dense, denser than any lowlander's, dense as the pale stone they live among, and a folk dropped into three meters of warm water goes down like a dropped tool and stays down. There is no word in the language for swimming. There is a word for *punting*, and a word for *the mistake*, and children learn both in the same week. The observatory forty meters out on the largest sump is not a mystery. Everyone knows what happened there. They know the names. The names are still in use, because names on the Sill are not retired by death, only by the letting-go of a tower.

Their skin is ridged fine as the inside of a shell, and they do not sweat in beads. They sweat in films. The water comes up and lies along the ridges and runs in ordered lines down the flank and up over the shoulder-blade, where the mantle takes it, and the pattern of those lines changes with what a person is feeling. Fear combs them straight. Grief breaks them into cross-hatching. This is why the mantle covers the shoulders and nothing else, and why bare shoulders in company are an obscenity, and why the folk find lowland clothing — which hides the genitals and leaves the back of the neck free — not immodest but *unreadable*, the dress of people with something to hide.

And they are held, not standing. Put an instrument on a woman of eighty and you will find that her skeleton is nine years old. The calcium goes through her the way it goes through a tower: in at the sole, out at the crown, the shape kept, the substance passing. She has the same collarbone she broke when she was seven. She has none of it. The break is still there, a thickening in the pattern, faithfully rebuilt out of new material every decade for seventy-three years, and she can tell you the weather on the day it happened, and she will tell you loudly, because everything here is said loudly.

They cannot wear their damage away. A folk body is a shape that remembers.

## ii. Names, and the etiquette of distance

A child is named at birth for the chord.

The fixed part is given by the tower whose voice was loudest at the hour of delivery — the midwife's whole art is listening past the fall and the boomers and the mother to establish this, and she is paid in ice. The moving part is the interval the child's cry made against the Note, and it is retuned every breeding season as the pipe-birds plug and unplug the flutes, so that a person's name shifts a little each year, within a range everyone recognizes, the way elsewhere you recognize a face that has aged.

You address a folk by whichever of their names is currently audible. To use last season's interval is either affection or insult, depending on volume.

When a tower lets go — sags over eleven days into warm rubble, and the mat walks over it — everyone born under it carries a rest in the middle of their name for a generation. You greet them by leaving the gap. Four hundred people can leave the same gap at once, in a scattered basin with no town, and the gap propagates outward at the speed of sound in air twice as thick as yours, and it takes about eleven minutes to reach the rim, and the far rim says it back muffled. That is a funeral. It is also a census. It is also how the folk know, to within a handful, how many of them there are.

Kinship is acoustic. A family is the set of houses that can hear one another shout. Marriage joins two audible ranges; the eleven words for *far* are the eleven degrees of relation, and to marry out to the eighth or ninth degree of muffled is considered romantic and impractical, and the children of such marriages are said to be *translated*. To separate, you move out of earshot. There is no other procedure. There is no authority to appeal to. There is only the fact that after a certain distance you can no longer be heard asking for anything.

And nothing tender can be whispered. The Note eats quiet speech, and things that hear quiet speech are not the things you want, so lullabies are bellowed, and comfort is delivered at a volume that visitors mistake for rage, and a proposal is shouted across a sump at dusk with the whole parish listening, and the answer must come back at the same volume, which means refusal is exactly as public as acceptance. Lovers on the Sill shout endearments at each other from four hundred meters apart, standing on stone, with the water ringing in cups behind them.

For the other kind of speech there is the ice-house.

## iii. The cold room

Officially the ice-house at the rim stair is where the folk go at midday to lie against the packed wall and let forty-four degrees of saturation come out of them. This is true. It is also the only place in the basin where the Note is thin — cold stone, the Hedge's breath, the boundary a hand's width away — and therefore the only place where a person can speak quietly and be sure that quiet speech goes no further than the wall.

So the ice-house is the confessional, the sickbed, the courtroom, the place where the truth is told. You pay for a whisper in shivering. An hour of honest conversation costs you an hour of shaking, and there are folk whose teeth are ruined by a long marriage. Children are brought up to be loud everywhere and are taken to the rim, once, at about nine years old, and shown how to be quiet, and most of them cry, and the crying is also quiet, and this is the first time in their lives they have ever heard themselves.

Secrets on the Sill are cold. This is not a metaphor the folk use. It is simply the arrangement.

## iv. The mantles

The liverwort across the shoulders is alive, and it is the second person.

It transpires for its wearer, which is the difference between living here and dying here, and it has a temperament. It must be fed — a slurry of crab-bank chalk and fish, worked in with the flat of the hand at the fourth hour — and trimmed, and spoken to loudly, and it sulks in the dry phase and grows thick and greedy in the wet, and a sulking mantle lets its wearer's heat pool at the nape until they see rings at the edge of their vision.

Mantles outlive people. A good one is inherited, and a mantle worn by six generations fits none of them exactly and all of them approximately, and it has learned the shoulder-shape of a family the way a tower keeps its waist: substance passing, pattern held. There are mantles older than the houses. There is one, on the eastern shore, that is said to have been on the back of everyone who ever kept the count-house, and it is thin and yellow and enormous and it does not like strangers, and the current keeper is nineteen and has stopped apologizing for it.

When a person dies, the mantle is lifted off before the body goes to the sorting stone. It is the only part of them that is not sorted. The folk are precise about this. They will tell you, loudly, that a person is a shape and a mantle, and that the shape goes back into the ledger and the mantle goes on, and that this is not a consolation but a description.

## v. The work

There is no town and there is no market and there are perhaps nine occupations.

The ice-cutters, who work the Hedge's inner face where fog becomes water and water becomes ice, and who are the only folk who regularly touch the cold, and who are deaf in one ear from the ringing of the sheets.

The roof-feeders, who go along the nacre shingles with a bucket and a brush, and whose houses are therefore animals of a slow kind, and who talk about their roofs the way lowlanders talk about dogs.

The count-house, three or four people at a time in a single room on piles, full of graduated rods — some cut, some grown, one of them older than the language's word for *around* — keeping the water. The balance closes to four decimal places. It has closed to four decimal places for as long as there have been rods. No one has ever explained how the fourth decimal is obtained. Every count-house keeper has the same look about the eyes and the same habit of counting under the breath in the tenth hour, and every one of them, as a child, was found somewhere they should not have been, and the folk do not drink from a return, and the folk do not ask.

The tide-watchers. The snail front is forty kilometers long and eleven years wide and it moves three meters a day, and there are people whose entire working life has been one hundred-meter section of it. What they watch is not snails. The snails are not individuals in any sense the folk find useful; the front is the animal, and the pale mat in the east and the dark mat in the west are its wake, and a hundred-meter section is about eleven days of it passing. A watcher sees one thing go by, very slowly, and writes down what it did, and hands the notebooks to a child who will see the next thing. Six generations of notebooks. The tide turns at the rim with the Turn, three hundred and forty years, so no watcher has ever seen it come back, and the notebooks are the argument about whether it is the same tide.

The voicer, who goes up the flutes with a hook and pulls the pipe-birds' felted nests out when the chord goes wrong. This is the only violence the folk permit themselves and it is not permitted freely: every nest removed is entered in the ledger, and something of equal weight is left in the flute, and it does not have to be the same kind of thing. The voicer is necessary and is not invited to things. There is a documented year when the Bell went silent under nests and three sumps drained, and the voicer of that year is remembered by a rest in the middle of a name that nobody has filled in for two hundred years.

The boundary-walker, who goes out along the markers with one hand extended and finds the edge, every year, to within a hand's width, and files the finding, and has never once found it moved.

And the ledger-keeper, who is not the count-house, and whose book is the oldest continuous document on the Sill: what was carried out, what was left, in weight and in kind. Ice out, hair in. A fish out, a tooth in. A slab of pale stone carried down nine hundred steps to the lowlands, and a song left on the sorting stone at the foot of Aunt, and the song weighed — the folk do not know how it was weighed, they only know the entry balanced, because nothing came of it, and the ledger is not enforced. It is settled. If you get it wrong you find out over the following year, gently, in your body, a gram at a time, and you will not be able to say what is missing, and neither will anyone else, and the shape will be kept.

Nothing works between the tenth and fourteenth hours. The world is loudest then and emptiest, and the boomers come down to drink, and four hundred people lie in the dark on the flexing floor with their mantles pulled up and listen to the fall begin on ten million leaf-tips, and sleep through the loudest thing that happens all day.

## vi. The neighbours

The boomers are not livestock. They are addressed. There is a nine-meter female over the western sumps with a healed tear in her gasbag that the folk have been watching for sixty years, and she has a name, and the name is thirty hertz and can be pulsed back at her, and she comes when it is pulsed, and the children do it constantly and the adults tell them not to and do it themselves. At dusk her underside lights faintly green from the scum in her skin, and it is the only light in the basin not owed to the sun, and folk navigate by her.

The sieve-crabs are the masons. Four thousand per square meter, working the mat for what the water brings, and their molt-shells drift into the tower waists and pile in white banks that crunch and shift and hiss in the fall, and that chalk is the stone, and the stone is the towers, and the towers are held. The folk know this. When they say a tower is *held* they are not being mystical; they mean something is holding it, and they can point at the banks.

The fish in the two-millimeter films are eaten from the weeps, constantly, incidentally, and taste of the tower they came off. A child can put a palm flat on a flute and take forty of them. They flicker up the vertical face in a way that from a distance looks exactly like reading, and the folk word for a tower's face is the word for a page, and this is one of those facts about the language that nobody bothers to remark on.

And the nulls.

You will not see one. You will notice that a small patch of the night has gone *cancelled* — an antinode of the hum folded back on itself — and you will step off the mat onto stone and wait, because whatever is standing there has been standing there for six days. The folk have a word for them that translates as *the careful*. Children are taught to sing across a null so it cannot close. Old folk walk out into them on purpose, when they are tired, and this is not spoken of loudly.

Here is what the count-house has noted, and filed, and not resolved: the careful stand still for six days. The thing in the sorting stone takes eleven hours to take a body apart into calcium and iron and folded carbon, without smell, below the scale of seeing. The thing that leaves a twin beside a knife on a sorting stone takes about a year, and the twin is very slightly better. The thing that grows the boundary markers to a hand's width takes a century. The thing that keeps nine low huts on the eastern shore clean and dry and never rotting has been doing it for longer than anyone has been counting.

Different speeds. The folk hold, politely, that these are the same resident.

## vii. The nine huts

Roofed. Swept. Dry. Occupied by no one within memory.

The folk do not maintain them. This is important, and they will say it twice, loudly, to be sure you have it: *nobody sweeps those*. And they are swept.

Inside each, on the sleeping platform, under a cloth, there is a shape about the size of a person.

The folk do not lift the cloths. This is not a rule anyone gave them. There is no rule. There are four hundred people who have simply never done it, and who take their children past the ring at a distance of about eleven meters, and who leave the huts out of the census, and who will tell you — in the ice-house, quietly, shaking — that the shapes are warmer than they were when their grandmothers were young.

A knife takes a year and comes out slightly better. Nobody knows how long a person takes.

There are three schools of thought about what is being held still beneath the lattice in the waist of Aunt, where the limestone stops being limestone and the structure goes down past the best lens, patient, unbothered, at nineteen hertz, doing something for which a hundred gigawatts of forest and steam and standing hot water is only the exhaust. The first school says the Sill is a machine that grew. The second says it is a machine that was grown. The third says the folk are the current draft, and the nine are the next, and the sealed upper floors with their own doors and their own hearths — provisioned, swept, waiting two hundred years for a great-great-grandchild to pull a boat up to the window — are not early.

They are on time.

All three schools are polite about the others. All three sweep the step.

## viii. What answers

When the Note goes to a fifth, you leave the basin and do not come back for a year. This is the fourth rule and the only one nobody has ever tested twice.

The folk who have come back from that year are fine. They are, by every account, slightly better: clearer, denser, calmer, longer-lived, and their mantles fit them as if grown for them. They do not remember the year. They remember going up the stair. They remember the cold and the small hard sun and the cloud floor, and then they remember coming down, and in between there is a rest in the middle of the name, and their families leave the gap, and nobody asks, loudly or otherwise.

The largest resident does not live in the Sill. The Sill is what it is warm by. It is not clear that it knows there are people here. It is clear that it knows there is something here that answers, and that answers in weight, and that keeps a book.

Four hundred people. Four hundred mantles. Nine hundred held shapes of pale stone with fish reading their faces. One tide, three meters a day, eleven years wide, thinking whatever it thinks. Some thousands of named bladders full of green light. Four thousand masons per square meter. One attention, working at every speed from six days to a million years, sorting the dead and improving the knives and sweeping nine floors. And underneath, the thing that all of this is the warmth left over from, breathing once every three hundred and forty years, holding something still.

The pillar has leaned very slightly east for sixty years. The count-house has noted it. The note is filed.

And in the last sixty years, without discussion, without decision, without anyone remarking on it, the new upper floors have all been built with their doors facing east.
