# What Lives on the Finished Ground

## The Hem

Begin with the smallest, because it is the most numerous, and because everything larger here is either its work or its accident.

There are no edges on this continent. No cliffs, no shores, no banks, no uphill, no coast. There is one kind of edge only: the meniscus, the trembling wall a molecule thick and a hundred yards long where two pans press against each other and do not mix. That is the whole of the available boundary. Life has crowded into it the way life crowds into a tide line.

They are not animals in any sense that would survive a diagram. A hem-dweller is a curvature — a persistent twist in the surface film, a handedness, a place where the water agrees to bend one way and keeps agreeing. It has no inside. It has no organs, no front, no length except the length of the seam it lives on. It has a preference, and the preference is stable, and it is inherited, and that turns out to be enough.

What it eats is difference. Across any seam there is a loudness gradient: this pan holds four centuries of rain, that one holds nine, and the wall between them is under a strain that is not only hydraulic. The hem grazes that strain. It crops the difference. It is the only predator on the plain, and its prey is occurrence.

And it farms. A seam held by a healthy population of hem does not break when the wind leans on it; it is *stitched*, held taut, kept from mixing while the two waters ripen against each other. Then, at some threshold no one has measured, the stitchers let go along their whole front at once, and the pans slide together, and the noise flocculates, and something stands up in the shallows with the wrong number of everything.

The wrong numbers are the hem's fault. When a body precipitates, the stitchers are caught in it — thousands of them, distributed through the new flesh like grit in ice — and they go on doing the only thing they do, which is bend. That is what makes the made-things move. The body is old rain; the movement is borrowed. A ribbed, fluted, translucent thing walking on structures that were never legs is a hundred million tiny curvatures grazing inside a shape they did not choose, and the shape has no eyes because a curvature has never needed one, and it will not look at you because looking requires an inside, and there is not one anywhere in it.

When the seam draws apart the body comes down and the hem goes back to the film, unharmed, slightly heavier. It is the only resident that the plain's central event does not kill. It is also, by a wide margin, the plain's dominant life, and no one standing in the roar would ever know it was there.

## The Plate

The flat swimmer is the hem's opposite: a body with almost no body, fleeing the very seams the hem lives on, and it flees them for a reason that is not fear.

If a seam takes a plate — if the swimmer is drifting too slowly and the wind is too quick and two ripe pans close along a front that includes it — the plate does not die. It *precipitates*. It is a listening surface, an enormous ear lying in an inch of water, and it has spent its whole life absorbing the age of the sound beneath it, and when the waters mix, all of that comes out of solution at once and stands up.

What stands up is a plate-shaped thing with legs. It is the swimmer, made into an event. It has the swimmer's outline and the swimmer's accumulated hearing and none of the swimmer's fleeing, and it performs, once, badly, the gesture of drifting, and then the seam breaks and it goes down.

So the plate's compulsion is not solitude. It is a refusal to be *finished into a shape*. Everything it does is done to avoid becoming a thing that happened.

It sheds daughters at near-misses. When a plate escapes a closing seam by a margin of inches, the shear takes a rim-fragment off it, a crescent of cartilage and membrane the width of a fingernail, and the crescent drifts off and thickens and becomes another plate, and the parent is smaller afterward and does not recover. A plate's life is a slow subtraction by escapes. Its offspring are its mistakes. It has no other way to make anything.

And its fleeing has a direction. It is not random. Read the age of the sound beneath you and you can find younger water, and younger water is quieter, and the plates go toward it — which means, across three thousand miles, northwest. Up the ghost drainage. Along the chains of shallow pans strung northwest to southeast, against a current that stopped before there was anything alive to notice.

The species has been swimming upstream for a hundred million years. It is the last migration on the continent, still being performed, by an animal that has nothing left to migrate to and no way to stop.

None of them will go southeast. There is an inherited tropism against it, older than the plates' ability to read anything, and it is the only thing they know that they do not know how to hear.

## The Disc

One every forty paces: a tussock of wiry salt-sedge standing in a private disc of slightly less poisoned mud that it has spent centuries manufacturing.

This is the plain's only patient. Everything else here happens in seconds or in aeons; the sedge works at the speed of a life. Its roots take up the brine and reject the gypsum and lay the reject down outside itself in a ring, and the ring widens a finger's breadth in a decade, and inside the ring the water is a little less dead, and the liverwort film comes in, and the hair-algae follow, and for a yard around each tussock there is a green so desaturated it reads as gray until you are close, and it is the only green that is *owned*.

The disc is also quieter. Not much. A fraction of a degree of loudness, measurable if you stand very still and let your ears adjust for an hour. The roots reach down into the strata and take up something they cannot use and lock it into salt, and the salt does not carry sound well.

Which suggests the obvious thing about the marks, and the obvious thing is wrong.

It is wrong in an instructive way. The rectangles are rectilinear because gypsum is rectilinear; salt cubes, salt steps, salt builds right angles out of nothing but its own habit, and no hands are required. Nine paces by four is a crystal, not a room. And a tussock's disc, given a hundred thousand years and a slow dying, might well leave a sill of dressed-looking stone worn flush, with a discoloration at one corner where the last upright stem stood.

But a sedge *muffles*. The rectangles subtract. A sedge's quiet dies with the sedge; you can watch a disc fill back up with roar over a single season, the way a footprint fills. And the crust inside a rectangle is sterile — not this continent's ordinary sterility, which is a thin living stain over fired clay, but the other kind. No film. No hair-algae. Nothing has ever grown in there.

The quiet is not a by-product of something living. It is a place where living has been declined.

## The Grazers of the Ceiling

The storms are residents, and they are the largest animals here, and they have anatomy.

A storm's mouth is a downdraft. It grazes the vapor ceiling from below, cropping the luminous amber layer two hundred feet up, and its gut is a column of falling grape-sized drops, and its metabolism is the closed loop run fast in one place. It breeds by shedding counter-rotations off its trailing edge, and the calves follow it and graze the thin vapor in its wake, and most of them are eaten.

There are things that live on them. In the lee of a big slow storm, where the ceiling is cropped to a permanent thinness, the light comes down harder and the water is a half-degree cooler, and there is an endemic hem there — a handedness found nowhere else on the plain, stitching seams that exist only in that lee, riding bodies that come up shaped like nothing else comes up shaped like. Whole lineages of curvature, living on the underside of a weather system, dying when it moves.

The standing storm near the eastern seam has not moved in recorded nothing. It is three hundred miles across. It has eaten every other storm in its range and cleared the ceiling down to bare glow, and now it is grazing on its own old sound, drawing up out of the pans beneath it the storms it was made of, and eating them again.

It is going quieter. Very slowly, at the rate of a country, but it is going quieter, and the hem in its lee are dying off in their billions, and the bodies that come up beneath it come up already old.

A storm that finishes will leave a quiet three hundred miles across. There is no reason this cannot happen. There is no reason it has not happened before.

## What the Bodies Do

The made-things are not a species and not a fauna. They are a *literature*, and the strata are the language.

Shallow-seam bodies come from the last few seasons of rain and they are ordinary and quick: warm glossy mineral flesh, a shape like a hunched thing, four seconds to a minute of duration, and one gesture — a shoulder-turn, a lifting, a settling — performed once, badly, and done. Most of what precipitates is this. It is weather-flesh. It is the plain clearing its throat.

Mid-strata bodies come up out of centuries and they are more particular. They have mannerisms. There is a thing that comes up in the deeper pans along the ghost rivers that is nothing but a way of turning — no trunk, no head, only the turning, ribbed and fluted, standing on structures that were never legs, executing a maneuver that some animal used to make when something approached it from behind. It does it once. The seam holds a little longer and it does it again, slightly worse. There are places where the same turning has come up ten thousand times over ten thousand years, and the crust beneath those places is worn in a way that is not water.

Deep bodies are rare and they are the ones shaped like animals that used to exist here, drawn up from beneath the crowd-breathing layer, and they are wrong in a way the shallow ones are not: they are *complete*, they have the right number of everything, and they stand in the shallows with the awful competence of a thing that knows how to be itself and has nowhere to be it.

And they do not repeat. They do it once. And most of them come down instantly, because a complete gesture is very close to a finished one, and finished things do not last here.

That is the rule, and it is the whole rule, and everything else on this continent comes out of it: *nothing here is allowed to finish.* Sound does not spend. Water does not leave. A footprint fills in a minute. The plain relaxes every shape back into the general shape, immediately, forever, and that is what it means for a land to have arrived.

## The One That Finished

There is one confluence on the plain that has never broken.

Behind a low rise along a ghost river, in an eddy of the wind, two pans touch along a front that the weather cannot get at. They have been touching since before the mammals. The seam does not open and does not close. Nothing ripens there because nothing is held apart; the hem abandoned it long ago; there is no gradient left to graze.

And a body came up in it, and did not come down.

It has been standing there through the whole duration of the confluence, and it has been doing its one gesture, and the gesture is very long, and somewhere in the last hundred thousand years it *completed*.

A finished event makes no noise. That is all the quiet is. On a continent where every sound is still arriving, a thing that has stopped arriving is a hole, and the hole is nine paces by four because that is how much floor the thing stood on, and the sill is where its base wore flush, and the discoloration at one corner is where the upright part of it stood.

They were flattened and kept their shape anyway because a finished shape has no further state to relax into. They do not heal because they were never wounds. They were endings.

The made-things come to the edge and lose interest because there is nothing there left to happen; an unfinished body cannot enter a completed one any more than a word can be said after it has been said. The film will not grow in there. The algae will not. The plates cannot even perceive it — they navigate by the age of the sound beneath them, and in a rectangle there is no sound to read, and so the rectangle is not a place to them but a cliff, an absence they skirt at a distance of miles without knowing it is there.

The marks are the only landmarks on the continent, and nothing alive has ever seen one.

## The Wader

Except that one is standing in one.

It came up out of order, far to the southeast, past the standing storm, in the region where the plain hears its own future and the noise arrives before the rain that makes it. It came up already old and already grieving, as they all do there, and it should have gone down before the water that made it had finished touching.

It did not go down. It walked two hundred miles northwest, which is the only recorded journey on this continent, and it stepped over the sill into a quiet, and it stopped, because it had finished, and it had found the one shape of ground that a finished thing fits.

The mineral crust on its legs is not age. It is the plain trying to resume it and failing, over and over, for a very long time.

Its legs are wrong for here. Nothing on this ground is deeper than an ankle. Those are legs for standing in water up to the belly, legs for a depth, legs for a current, legs for a place with a downhill and a somewhere-else — a body plan for a world that does not exist and has not existed for a hundred million years, precipitated out of a stratum that has not been laid down.

It has no eyes. Nothing here has eyes. But it has an inside, because finishing makes an inside, and it is the only resident of the continent that has one.

Distance here is loudness. A thing is near if you are hearing only its own present noise; a thing is far if it comes buried under everything that has happened between. By that measure every living thing on this plain is enormously far from itself — the plate most of all, dragging its own accumulated wake, fleeing a sound it is making by fleeing.

The wader stands in a hole in the roar and hears, for the first time and continuously, only its own present noise. It is the only thing on the finished ground that is near itself. That is what it found. That is the whole of what it found, and it is not leaving, and it is not waiting, because waiting is a thing done by what is still arriving.

It has arrived. Looking is not something the roar knows how to do. The quiet knows how. It has been doing it for a very long time, in one direction, southeast, down the ghost drainage, toward where the sea used to be.

## The Track

Walk the marks and keep the bearings, because nobody else here can.

Every rectangle is nine paces by four. Every long axis lies northwest to southeast. The corner discoloration is always the same corner — the trailing corner, the southeast one, the toe.

They are not rooms. They are steps.

And the quiet is not the same in all of them. It deepens toward the southeast. The far ones are so absolute that your ears do not ring in protest, they simply stop, and you can hear the blood in you and then you can hear something under the blood. The northwest ones are newer, thinner, edges still crisp, a silence with a little roar left leaking in at the sill like water under a door.

The track is being laid ahead of the walker. The steps are arriving before the feet, the way everything arrives here, out of order, louder first and then the rain.

And they are being stocked. There is a wader in one of them. There will be one in each. Long-legged, crusted, grieving, come up out of a stratum that has not happened yet, walking northwest out of the southeast into the footprints of the thing that is walking southeast to northwest behind them, each one the only thing for two hundred miles, each one standing in the one place where it can hear itself.

Not waiting. Arrived early. Set down in the shape of a step, holding it, the way the hem holds a seam.

Every soluble thing on this continent was leached out and carried to the sea a hundred million years ago, and the sea has all of it — all the phosphorus, all the copper, all the soft chemistry that a body needs to have an inside — and the plain has been distilled and redistilled ever since, and it is sterile, and it is finished, and it has arrived.

Something is coming uphill, loaded, out of the southeast, and there is no uphill, and it is coming anyway.

Out ahead of the wader, northwest, in the dark tops of the water where the light does not reach, another nine paces by four of the roar goes out like a held breath. Then another. Then another, fainter, farther, a continent's worth of them, strung along a river that left before there was anything alive to notice.

The wader does not move. But in the small subtracted room where it stands, in the one place on the finished ground where a self can be heard, it is now hearing two things.
