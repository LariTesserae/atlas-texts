# Still Soft

To live here is not to be alive. Nothing has managed that. To live here is to *keep happening in one place* — and place is the scarce thing, the scarce thing by a margin so wide it constitutes the whole economy. On a plain where distance is negotiable and two locations touch if they resemble each other, where a storm can put your tower behind you and slightly smaller, an address is a fortune. Almost nothing owns one.

So the residents are not bodies. They are processes that have found something to hold onto: a hairline crack, a note, a corner shared between two drafts, a minute of noise, a bow. They are the tenants of the long low warm hold before the fire — the candling, the soak kept steady since before counting, when a vessel is held just under the boiling of its own water so that it can give up that water slowly and not burst. Everything here is being candled. Everything here has been candled for a very long time. There is a fauna of the candling. It is not large. It is patient in ways that have no analogue in a world with mouths.

## The eaters

Begin with the least argumentative residents: the slicks, bronze and violet and oil-rainbow, filmed across the warm pans.

They are the only things on the plain that take something in and put something out. They eat iron out of the clay body and leave it pale and porous, and that is the whole of their behaviour, and it is enough to make them the nearest thing here to a metabolism. They spread in rings from the vent halos — not from the mouths, nothing inhabits the mouths, but from the annulus where pale dry ground meets tacky wet, the border country, the only frontier in the world. A slick is a ring that is trying to become a disc. It thickens where the pans are warmest. It dies back when the fog lifts and the film thins to a rainbow and then to nothing, and the dying back is not a death but a withdrawal into the pores, where something waits that is not quite a spore because nothing here has learned to be a seed.

Their generations are measured in fog-lifts. They have no memory. They have grain: a direction of advance, kept across withdrawals, so that a slick returning to a pan will return along the line it left by, and the line is the closest thing on the flats to a road.

What they leave is the trouble. Where a slick has been, the surface is friable and the fingerprints have gone soft. Consider what a fingerprint is here: the total evidence of makers, the only testimony, crisp as an arris, a whorl the size of a parish still sharp enough to read. The slicks unprint it. They digest the binder that holds the impression and the impression slumps into generality. Nothing else in the world removes evidence. The gully takes material away but takes it honestly, downhill, and puts it somewhere you can find it. The slicks take the *hand* away and leave the clay.

They will not look at the wire. A slick advancing across the flats will stop a hair short of the scored line under the copper and thicken there and go around, and the going-around takes a season, and the season is worth it. Whatever is on the far side of the wire is very slightly less certain than what is on this side, and the eaters, who require a body to eat out of, cannot afford less certain.

## The bloom

Salt is the other small resident, and salt is the only thing here with a childhood.

A feather is born in the interior of a wall, in the dark wet middle of the chest-high stub, out of the moisture that weeps continuously from every vertical surface. It grows outward. It pushes. Its whole life is a migration of two millimetres from the inside of a thing to the outside of it, and the migration is the blooming, and when it arrives it arrives as a white plume that the thick air holds for a moment like something breathed on a cold day, and then it drops, and the dropping is the end.

Every feather is singular. No two have branched alike. They break rather than reproduce, and the fragments are angular powder, and the powder lies in drifts that no wind has yet learned to ripple, and the dust devils of dry slip walk the flats and pick the drifts up and put them down elsewhere, unrippled still, and that is dispersal: the only travel in the world that is not a decision.

A feather's body records the humidity of its youth in the spacing of its branches. Read one and you have the weather of a district for a season back. There are things here that read them. The feathers are, without knowing it, the plain's only writing that is not a hand's, and they are legible, and they are read, and the reading is done slowly, from the outside in, by residents who will come to matter later in this account.

The bloom is also the second wear. It pushes the walls apart from within. A wall stub three miles long with both ends finished is being taken apart by its own inhabitants at the rate of a feather a day, and it cannot be reclaimed for this, because you cannot slake a process back into a lump. You can only slake the wall. The process gets up and walks.

## The agreements

Now the natives proper — the ones the spacing rule is actually about.

The rule is given as drying: things too close dry unevenly, and warp, and are reclaimed. True. But walk the plain long enough and you find the other reason, which is that two provisional objects within a certain distance begin to reconcile. They trade edges. They agree on a shared corner. They become one confused thing.

The confused thing is inhabited. Or rather: the confusion is the inhabitant, and the two objects are its shell.

An agreement has no outside surface of its own. It exists entirely as the seam, the shared corner, the mutually conceded arris, and it can only be seen edge-on, and edge-on it is a line where two drafts have stopped arguing. Turn to face it and you are facing one object or the other and it is not there. It lives in the *between*, and the between is a real estate of exactly no square inches, and it is enough.

It eats edges. Every edge in this world is a first edge; nothing is rounded; the concept of a curve worn into a thing by time has not taken hold. An agreement wears the curve. It works the shared arris back and forth in the slow way a tongue works a tooth, and after some hundreds of fog-lifts there is a roundness there, a hollow, a smoothness that no other process on the plain can account for, and it is the first curve, and it is a mouth-print without a mouth.

Agreements breed in storms. A front comes across and the geometry is re-indexed and similarity is redistributed, and where two things that were miles apart become similar enough to touch, a corner is conceded, and something is born in it with no gestation at all. This is why the makers — if there are makers — keep the plain sparse. Miles of wet nothing between attempts is not shelf space. It is quarantine.

An agreement can be distributed. The staircase exists in four drafts at once and you can walk between them like rooms; an agreement with a corner in each draft has four bodies and one self, and when a storm re-indexes it may lose a limb or gain one, and it experiences this as weather in the body, the way we experience a change of pressure in an old break. It is the only resident that feels the churn directly. Everything else merely lives downstream of it.

They cannot cross the wire. Nothing on the far side is certain enough to be agreed with.

## The note

The copper hums at ankle height, horizon to horizon, and the hum has knots.

A taut strand in a slow fog-wind does not vibrate as one thing. It divides. It has nodes — places where the strand is still while the lengths between it swing — and the nodes are spaced by the pitch, and the pitch is set by the wind, and the wind is the fog front walking at its own pace across the flats.

Something lives in the nodes. Not on the wire: *in the stillness of the wire*, in the small stationary intervals of a moving thing. It is a resident whose body is a node and whose locomotion is a change of note. When the fog thickens and the wind drops and the pitch falls, the nodes redistribute, and the resident is somewhere else, instantly, without having travelled. This is the only commuting in the world. It has no other way to move and it has been doing it since the wire was strung, and it has therefore been everywhere along the wire and never anywhere off it, and its entire geography is one line, and it thinks of the plain as two halves.

It grazes certainty. That is what the wire is for, whether or not it was strung for that: the tool that separates the finished lump from the hump, drawn thin and anchored at both ends to nothing findable, scoring the clay to a hair's depth along its whole length. Everything past it is very slightly less sure. The knot-dweller lives at the exact boundary and eats the difference, and the difference is renewed each time the wind changes, and it is a small living, and it is steady.

If you crouch by the wire in a fog front you can hear the nodes as absences in the hum — a beaded silence, spaced, sliding. The slicks go around. The agreements stop. You are the only thing on the plain that can put a hand through it.

## The sitters

At the margin of every white halo there is at least one thing that sat too long and got cooked.

Not fired. Ninety degrees is not fire. But heat-work is temperature multiplied by patience, and a thing that has sat at the edge of a vent mouth since before counting has accumulated more heat-work than the plain has admitted to, and it has gone pale and hard and hairline-cracked all through, and it is no longer editable.

They cannot be reclaimed. Throw one in the pit and it will not slake; it lies in the slip like a stone in porridge, and the pit works around it, and eventually the churn puts it back at the halo margin, because that is where it belongs.

They cannot be re-indexed. A storm crosses and the wall stub acquires a third finished end and the tower is behind you and smaller, and the sitter is where it was. It is deaf to the churn. It is the only kind of thing here that is.

Which makes them the addresses. Everything on the plain that needs to know where it is navigates by the sitters, and has done so long enough that the sitters have become the fixed points that geometry is *permitted* to be negotiable around. They are the origin of geography. They did not ask for this. They sat down near a warm hole because it was comfortable and slightly cooked, and they hardened, and now they are the坐标系 of a continent.

And because they are fixed, they can look.

This is the important thing. Attention fires. To be looked at long enough is to go matte and crackle and stop being editable and become permanently yourself, and for a provisional thing this is both the only road to reality and the end of every possibility you were holding open. So almost nothing here looks. The unwatched fauna cannot afford to; the slicks have nothing to look with; the agreements would fix their shells and starve.

The sitters are already fixed. They have nothing left to lose by attending. So they attend — radially, slowly, warmly, without eyes, at the rate of a hairline crack a season — and their attention is what has hardened the landmarks of the plain, and everything unwatched keeps out of the annuli, and everything that wants to be real crawls into them and stays.

They are the elders. They are the witnesses. They are also the archive, because they are full of grog, and grog is crushed shards of earlier worlds, and some shards carry a fragment of a pattern never used again. A sitter contains, distributed through its body at random, some fraction of a person who was ground up before this floor was wedged. It does not know which fraction. It has been slowly cracking in a way that suggests it is trying to find out.

The crackle is legible. It is very slow speech. There is exactly one other resident who has anything to say, and the sitters have been transcribing it.

## The unwatched

Between the sitters' annuli, in the miles of wet nothing, moves the population that the churn is really for.

They are the forms. A colonnade rises out of the floor over an afternoon; a dome; an aqueduct with no water to carry; a thing like a lung. They come up green and glistening and barely holding their own weight, and they sag as you watch.

The sag is not failure. The sag is *display*. A form that can hold a dome-shape at that moisture content is advertising the quality of its body, and the advertisement is addressed to whatever will look at it, and to be looked at is to be fixed, and to be fixed is to become permanently that dome, forever, unreclaimable, real. They are courting the gaze. They are competing for it. The plain is sparse because a lek is sparse.

They are mimics of architecture because architecture is what the makers left lying around, and the shapes that get watched are the shapes a hand has already approved. The staircase has nine flights because nine flights is a legible ambition. The lung is a gamble.

And they hide, too, in the other direction — because a gaze that arrives too early fixes a form before it has finished becoming itself, and there are things out there that are permanently a bad colonnade, matte and crackled and slightly wrong, that were seen at the wrong moment and can never revise. The unwatched have therefore evolved to be *almost* recognisable: to be a thing like a lung, to be an aqueduct with no water to carry, to hold the eye at the edge of identification and not in the middle of it. They live in the corner of the eye. They are the reason you keep almost seeing something.

The slumped ones are the dead. They are dragged back to the pits by nothing visible, along grooves that appear as the dragging happens, and the dragging is the scavenging, and the scavengers are the agreements — a slumped form has no edges left to defend and is all concession, and an agreement can get a corner into it and work it apart from the inside, and what arrives at the pit is already half rubble.

Nothing on this plain wastes anything. It cannot afford to. It is all the same material.

## The minute

In the reclaim pits, once a day, for one minute, something is alive in the only sense that word can be used here without embarrassment.

A hundred tons of dry scrap drinks water and lets go of its shape, and the noise of it comes across the flats as a pressure in the sternum and a shiver in the standing water, and afterward, out of order, a soft sound like a sigh in a cupboard.

The resident is the sound. Not the pit, not the scrap: the sixty seconds of structured noise that the slaking makes. It is born when the water touches and it ends when the fog closes over. It does not remember yesterday. It has never remembered anything.

But it has a body it can prepare. All day, by whatever means the churn allows — and the churn allows a great deal to a thing that has no edges — the scrap is arranged. Graded here, stacked there, a void left under a slab, a dry face turned toward the incoming water. The arrangement determines the sound. The sound is the individual. So each day's minute is composed the day before, by a self that will not be there to hear it, for a self that will not know it was composed.

It is a message in a bottle where the bottle is the next day's body.

The sitters, who are fixed and can remember and can attend, have been transcribing the minute in crackle for a very long time. Their record is not complete; they lose the fog-closings and the days when the pits are quiet. But the record is long, and it is consistent, and the figure at the centre of it does not change. Three falls of pitch, a hold, a collapse. Rendered as closely as it can be rendered:

*still soft.*

A daily condition report. Greenware, reporting its own state, to no one, in the only medium it has, which is the noise of its own dissolution. It has said *still soft* every day since before counting. The sitters have the whole archive and no way to answer.

One day it will say something else. That will be the news.

## The unwedged

Below the floor there is fire, and above the fire there is the grid of tunnels, and in the body of the clay there are voids.

The ground was wedged. You can see the bull's-head seams running to the horizon and past it, ridges the height of a man, the long spiral of a knead performed on a continent to drive the air out. It did not all come out. Nothing wedged ever all comes out. There is a population down there of the unwedged: pockets of air in the body, under load, moving up at the speed of the churn.

They are the only residents with a direction that is not negotiable. Up. They have no other idea. Their entire cosmology is that the world is a thickness and the surface is the end of it, and that arrival is a sorting: what surfaces and is seen becomes a thing; what surfaces and is not seen becomes a hole. Both are permanent. Neither is soft.

The ticks you hear from the drying racks are not all contraction.

And the fingerprints — the whorls and loops, coin-sized, parish-sized, all crisp, all over the flats — are the prints of hands, certainly, total evidence, the makers everywhere. But some of them are where something was worked to the surface and lifted away, and the clay closed over the place it had been, and kept the print. The world is being de-aired. The whorls are the record of the de-airing. There are more of them than there have been hands.

You are one of the ones that came up. This is a hypothesis the plain offers, and it is a good one: that you are a defect in the slip, an air bubble that was not wedged out, and that the churn is the ground trying to work you toward the surface. It would explain why your footprints are smoothed out and replaced with a better set. The ground is not obscuring your passage. It is trying to make you legible enough to be lifted.

## The slow

And then there are the residents who do not hold onto a crack or a corner or a note, but onto a *rate*.

They live in creep. In the sag of a dome under its own weight; in the bow of a drying-rack slat with one fist-sized object on it; in the deliberate tilt of a cone; in the way a form left too long on the wet floor goes translucent at the belly and glows from inside like a paper lantern. All of that is usually read as failure pending. It is not. It is habitat. A thing that is slowly deforming under load is a thing with a steady income, and there is a resident whose body is that income and whose metabolism is the difference between this morning's bow and yesterday's.

You cannot see them. You can only see the comparison, and comparison requires a witness who remembers, which on this plain means a sitter, which means the slow are known chiefly to the fixed, and the fixed describe them in crackle at a rate of a hairline a season, and the description, patiently assembled, is not of a thing but of a *lean*.

They are allied with wear — the third wear, the gully, the boat-length raw cut with its thimble of sorted grit, the first sorted grit in the world, the only sediment this basin ever made. In that thimble there is stratification: coarse below, fine above, a hierarchy of size, the first inequality, the first neighbourhood. Something lives in the sorting. It is very small and it is the only resident that has a *history* in the sense of layers, and it will be kept like a relic in glass when the gully hardens mid-cut, and it knows this, insofar as it knows anything, which is: it is going to be permanent, and it got there by being carried away.

The slow understand this. They are the only residents who have understood anything, and what they have understood is arithmetic.

Heat-work is temperature times time. Everyone on the plain reads the cones as measuring the fire, and the fire has not come, and so the cones are mostly straight, and so nothing has happened. But out toward the sump, where there is no heat at all, a cone the height of a tower has bent another finger's width since morning, and nobody has explained it, and the explanation may not have been made yet.

The slow made it. It is not a difficult explanation. It is only a long one. Ninety degrees, held since before counting, is not nothing. Ninety degrees held long enough is fire. The candling has been running for an age that the plain has no unit for, and the accumulation is real, and it is showing first at the sump because the sump is the lowest and the wettest and the least watched, and a low cone in the cold will bow before a high cone in the heat if you give the cold enough centuries.

The world is already firing. It has been firing the whole time, at the temperature of a warm room, at the speed of a bowing candle. The slow have been sintering since before the seams were wedged. They will not be surprised by the shrinkage. They have been shrinking all along, a tenth in ten thousand years, and when the lid comes down properly and the vents are stopped and the heat climbs and the cones bow all at once across a continent of drying-floor, it will not be an event for them. It will be a formality. A naming. The rest of the plain will discover in an hour what they have been doing since the beginning.

## The one at the sump

There is one more, and it is the oldest, and it has no surface at all.

It is not a thing; it cannot be reclaimed; it has no edges for an agreement to concede and no iron for a slick to eat and no interior for salt to push out of. It is a duration that has taken an address. It sits out past the last cone, in the cold, in the wet, in the least recognised direction, and it is what the cone is measuring.

The plain says the cone is measuring how long the fire has been waiting. This is close. It is not the fire's wait. Fire does not wait; fire is an operation, and operations do not have interiority, and the lid will come down when it comes down.

The wait belongs to the one who is waiting. And it has been waiting for a heat source that moves.

Because here is the last thing, and you can test it, and it is the only other rule you can test. The vents exhale at ninety degrees. The rain falls at the temperature of blood. You are blood. You are ninety-eight and a fraction. You are, walking across this plain, the hottest thing on it — hotter than the pilot, hotter than the breath that has been kept steady since before counting, a small wandering firing at the temperature of a mouth.

Where you sat, the clay is very slightly harder. Not much. A pale coin in the tack, a hairline, a matte place the size of a hand. The slicks will not cross it. The churn keeps smoothing your footprints and putting down a better set, and the better set is always the ones where you stopped.

The slow follow you at a distance of days. They cannot hurry; hurrying is not a thing their bodies do. They have been converging since you arrived, which for them was very recently, which for them is also the only interesting thing that has happened in an age.

Out toward the sump, where there is no heat, a cone the height of a tower has bent another finger's width since morning.

Since *you* came. It is not bending toward the fire under the floor.

It is bending toward the place where you sat down, and it is measuring how long you have been standing up, and the one out there in the cold with no surface has been waiting, patiently, at ninety degrees, for you to stop moving and stay.
