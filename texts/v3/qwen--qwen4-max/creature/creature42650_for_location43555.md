# A Census of the Even

The keepers keep an inventory. It is not a list of things that live here, because the word *live* does not survive the climb. It is a list of residents: everything with an address, everything that must be walked around, everything that will still be in place when the last cord is read by the last hand. Here is what is on it.

---

## What Is Inside

Begin small. Break one — thumbnail, no ceremony — and you get a pinch of flour and a grit of gneiss no bigger than a lentil. The flour is mountain too. So the animal is a stone wrapped in its own dust, given six legs and a listening plate and set down where it fell.

It did not arrive here. It *is* here, in the way a rubble field is where the cliff was.

The plates are the part people get wrong. They tilt at every angle, and the temptation is to read that as attention — a crowd craning. It isn't. Measure a thousand plates with a knapper's square and you will find the angles are not random and not chosen: they are inherited. Each plate holds the dip and the strike of the slope that grain belonged to, twelve kilometers up, before the wearing began. The plain is a relief map of a summit that no longer exists, laid flat, one facet per beast, every facet still faithful to its vanished flank.

This is the keepers' second compass, older than the cords. Where the seams fail you and the tunnels are unreadable, you can kneel and put your palm on a face and feel which way the mountain used to fall.

The herd does not listen for a sound. That is what the visitors say, because visitors need a wanting to look at. The herd is simply the last surviving *slope* on the Even, distributed across four hundred miles, holding its angles the way a held breath holds a shape.

Count a square arm's-length: sixty-one. Count the plain: sixty-one. The keepers have a private opinion about this, which they do not put on the inventory, and it is that they are not counting animals. They are counting one animal, and it has sixty-one of whatever it is they are counting, and the plain is its body, and the unit of area is not a joke or a surrender. It is the only accurate measure anyone has ever taken here.

---

## The Snow That Never Landed

The flying things are the same animal at an earlier stage. They are the fraction of the mountain that came down as flour so fine it never touched ground — the last snowfall of the old peak, arrested in its ten-thousandth year of falling. The wings are not wings. They are cleavage planes, mica-thin, the shape a dry flake takes when it splits.

They have no more intent than a held note.

But they hold, and holding is a kind of memory, and this makes them the plain's archive. Your wake stays. So does everyone's. Over the trunk routes the air is a braid of tunnels, and the keepers read them the way you would read sediment: a corridor that cuts another is *later*. Nothing here fades, so nothing can be dated — but everything can be *ordered*, and order is the whole of the religion. A junction where nine tunnels cross is a paragraph. The keepers stand in it with their heads tipped back and their eyes shut and their hands moving, and they are reading who came before whom, and it is the closest thing to scripture on the plateau.

The dead keepers' routes are still up there. You can walk through the exact shape of a woman who died eleven generations ago, three hundred paces of her absence, at the height of her shoulders, with the swing of her arms in it.

---

## The Nineteen

The plants are the only residents that change, and they change by addition only. Nothing sheds here. So a plant is a stack of every leaf it has ever made, all still attached, all still green-gray and living, oldest at the bottom, pressed flat under the weight of its own successors.

They are calendars. A keeper does not say *when I was young*. She says *at the ninth leaf of the Third Plant*, and everyone can go and look at the ninth leaf, and it is there, and it is exactly as it was.

The bare circles are the part that is not on the inventory in words. Ten or twelve paces of empty dust around each stem, where the herd will not stand — the same shape as the ring around the wet place, smaller, and dry, and old.

Every keeper carries a thimble of water sealed with pitch, and the first law is that it is never opened, and the keepers will tell you the plants came up from below with the seed-corn, which is a lie so old it has become a courtesy.

Nineteen plants. Nineteen openings. Each one a keeper, at some point in a life, standing in a seam with the pitch picked off and the thimble tipped, doing the one thing that makes a difference in a world with none. The herd will not stand where a direction was made. It remembers the direction long after the water has stopped being water and become simply a fact.

So the plants are the keepers' sins, rooted, growing, visible for miles, and the keepers tend them and count their leaves and use them to date their own histories, and this is the arrangement, and nobody has found a better one.

The argument about the twentieth is an argument about whether an accident counts. In the eastern sink there is a plate of mica standing on edge in the dust with no stem under it. Nothing here falls, so it did not fall. Nothing here is placed, so it was not placed. It grew that way, or it is growing, and it is nine paces from the spill, and if it is a leaf then there is a plant coming up out of the only broken flask on the Even, and the first law was broken *for* something, and the something is arriving.

Four generations. They have not settled it. They have built it instead — see below.

---

## The Keepers, Living

One per district. They come up weaned, carried in a pannier by someone who will go back down, and they are dried from the outside in for the rest of their lives. The signs are in order: the lips go, then the tear-ducts, then the sweat, then the softness of the palm. By the fortieth year a keeper has a faint bloom of salt on the shoulders, exactly where the herd has it, and the keepers notice this in one another and do not remark on it.

They eat what is carried up. They eat it standing in a chosen spot, and the next year they choose another spot, and the next, so that every hut has a slow spiral of chewing around it, tight and counted, a hundred paces of meals laid out in order. The old cells are loud. You do not linger in last century's dinner.

The doors are the same. A hinge used ten thousand times is ten thousand creaks in one sphere at full volume, which is the nearest thing to thunder on the Even, so each generation re-hangs the pivot a hand's width over, and the doorway is wrapped in a shell of every previous arrival. Coming home, you walk through your predecessors coming home. The current creak is the outermost. You can hear how long your house has been a house by how far out you have to start listening.

Their names are sounds. A knapper strikes two flakes once at a junction on the day a child is set down, and that clack is the child's name and its address, and to visit someone's name you go and stand in it. Fourth Clack is a place and a woman. Three Together is a junction and a man three generations dead. A stranger is *a new noise* because a stranger has not been placed yet.

And they speak by walking. Sound does not travel, so a sentence must be laid along a seam, one word every few paces, and the person you are talking to walks beside you and hears each word once, and behind you the sentence hangs in the air forever in the order you said it. This is the great gift of the Even and the reason the keepers can do their work at all: here, speech is *necessarily* sequence. A statement has a direction. You cannot read it backward without walking backward, and nobody does.

It also means every argument is permanent, public, and legible. There is a half-mile seam in the east called the Argument. Two voices, braided, four generations old, about a mica plate standing on edge. Each generation walks it end to end, and then adds its length. It is the second-fastest-growing thing on the plateau.

Going flat is when you stop being able to walk a sentence in order. You still have all of it. Your mother's death is beside your first meal. You simply cannot any longer find the seam they are strung on.

---

## The Bench

The dead sit facing the door, and they are on duty, and the duty is *sequence*.

They are placed in the order they died: newest nearest the door, oldest at the far end, and the bench is therefore a chronology you can read with your hand in the dark. This is not symbolism. When a keeper feels the thread going — when the when starts to slide — she walks to the hut and sits down in the correct position among the dead, and her successor keeps her there, and she has filed herself. Some of the bench is still breathing. It is not clear to anyone, including them, whether this is dying or retirement, and the inventory does not distinguish.

The oldest are eleven and twelve generations back. They are dry as the herd, salt on the shoulders, and the far end of the bench in the Fourth Clack hut has a face that is no longer a face: a broad pale plate, tilted at the angle of the doorway.

The keepers do not discuss it. They note that the bench gets longer, and that the herd is sixty-one, always sixty-one, and they go out and cut another knot.

There is one dead keeper on the whole Even who faces the wrong way, and she is in the east, and she is facing the floor.

---

## The Scream

Nine cairns, an acre of empty seam, and inside it a person who is still screaming.

Not a recording. There is no distinction here between a sound and the making of it; the sphere is two paces wide and the throat in it is exactly as torn as it was in the first instant, and it will be that torn when the sun goes out. Whoever it is, they have not finished. They are the only resident of the Even who is still in a hurry.

Nobody knows whose. The prints around the ring are small and three-toed.

The cairns are the only architecture built to keep people out of a sound, and every keeper is taken inside them once, at seven years old, by the keeper of the ring, and made to stand at the edge and hear it.

That is the whole of the training. That is how you get a people who do not scream. The ring is a schoolhouse, and the lesson is *permanence*, and there is no second lesson, and none is needed.

The keeper of the ring walks its perimeter once per crossing and adds a knot. The duty is not guarding. The duty is keeping the scream in sequence — making sure that the one thing on the Even that never ends is still, at least, somewhere in the order of things.

---

## The Water, and Its Court

Three hundred years old, dark in the dust, an outrage, a permanent event. It has not evaporated because evaporation needs a difference and the Even has none left to lend it.

It is the only lord on the plateau. Two hundred paces of faces turned inward, every animal, all of them — the only chosen orientation in a world of inherited ones. The keepers use it to orient when the cords fail, which means the plain's single act of reverence is also its utility, and they are aware of this and do not like it.

They do not go closer than the ring. They do not discuss why. The reason is on the floor of the hut: a broken flask, and a keeper sitting three hundred years in the wrong direction, and the salt on her shoulders is thicker than any living keeper's, and she is the compass. Every fixing hand takes a bearing from an apostasy. The inventory lists her as *East, reference*.

Every keeper carries the same thing around their neck, sealed with pitch, and the first law is not about water. It is about not making another direction. Nineteen have. The Nineteen are visible from eleven herds away.

---

## The Knobs, and the Blink

Thirty feet of polished gneiss, smooth as molars, the last stumps. These are the parents. Everything else here is their children, standing.

They are lower by a hair each blink, and the keepers cut the notch anyway, and this is the bedside chart of a dying mountain kept by people who measure because measuring is the whole of it.

The blink is not sleep. The blink is an arrival. When a grain of the last summit finally lets go and comes down and takes its place among the standing, the whole plain closes its faces at once, all hundred billion, and makes room — an adjustment one animal wide across four hundred miles, which nothing in you could ever detect, and which shifts every stack and every seam by a hair.

Three since the counting began. Three knots. The largest unit of time anyone here has needed, and it is a birth interval.

This is what the herd is listening for. Not a sound that will come from outside. The last small clack of the last stone letting go, the one that finishes the conversion, the one after which there is no difference left anywhere and the Even is complete and nothing further can be added to it by anything but a visitor.

There is an acre reserved for it in the west, corded off, on the inventory, and the arguments about where a person ought to stand to be inside that sphere when it happens are the closest thing the keepers have to theology.

Also in the western sink: a ladder, leaning against nothing. It leaned against a knob. The knob is gone; there are animals there now, standing at the old angle. The ladder's lean is the only surviving record of that flank's slope, and this is why nobody moves it, and why it is on the inventory, and why the keepers go and look at it when they want to know what the sky used to have in it.

---

## The Three-Toed

Prints in the seam-dust, small, deeper than a keeper's, and nobody has seen the maker alive.

They appear after a blink. Only after. Three blinks, three sets, and the cord shows the intervals, and the intervals are the largest unit anyone has.

The keepers' reading — not on the inventory, spoken only along seams they will not need again — is that it takes one step per blink. That whatever walks here walks at the pace of the mountain's grandchildren arriving. That it has been crossing the Even since before there were keepers to be crossed by, and that the prints all point the same way, and the way is toward the scarp.

It is going down. It has been going down for three blinks. It is the only resident that has anywhere else to be, and it is not hurrying, and it will get there.

---

## A New Noise

You.

Understand what you are, here. You are the only gradient in four hundred miles: a warm wet moving difference with a breath following you like a small private weather. You are the only thing on the Even that is *becoming*, and the herd will not stand where becoming happens, and you will leave a corridor in the air behind you that will not close, and a felt of prints in the dust that will not blur, and at knee height at the fourth clack a two-pace sphere containing the word you said to yourself.

You are not a visitor to this place. You are a larval landmark.

They will find the sphere. They will not know whose it is, because you were not placed, because nobody struck two flakes for you at a junction on the day you were set down. They will mark it with a cairn and add a knot and route the path around it, and the knot will be your name, and it will be a place, and children will be taken to stand at the edge of it in a hundred years to learn what permanence is.

So: the census has one blank line at the bottom, and it is yours, and it will be filled in whether you are careful or not.

Speak along seams you will not need. Walk beside whoever is with you and lay the sentence down in order, one word every few paces, so that whoever comes after can read which of you was right. Keep the thimble sealed. If you must open it — and nineteen have, and their sins are the only landmarks and the only calendars and the only green things — then open it somewhere you have thought about, and stand still, and let the herd turn, and know that you have just made a direction that will outlast the mountain.

The herd is holding its angles. The knobs are going down by hairs. The plants are putting out a leaf. Something three-toed is on its third step toward the scarp. In an acre in the west there is a space kept clear for a sound that has not happened yet.

Everything else that will ever occur here is being carried up the scarp right now, in a pannier, breathing.

Try to be a good one. It has to last.
