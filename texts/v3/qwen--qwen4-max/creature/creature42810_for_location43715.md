The crown is kept by snails, and the snails are the size of a fist, and they have never been anywhere else.

They work the condenser faces at a pace that makes the settling of the massif look hurried: a hand's breadth in a season, a door's width in a life. Each animal spends itself on one oval of plate, rasping the lime scale off in fine arcs, and the arcs are distinctive — the tooth-rows differ from lineage to lineage, so a crown-face under raking light is signed all over, the way a wall is signed by masons, and a snail-wright can look at forty feet of clean iron and tell you which family has been eating it since the last whitening. A snail is a patch of clean. That is the unit. When the keepers say a crown is *nine hundred doors*, they mean nine hundred living animals are holding the heat exchange open, and if you take them off, the crown chokes in a decade and the whole upper mountain goes feverish and wrong.

So there is law. You may eat the wet-gallery snails, which are livestock, fat and stupid and farmed in the weeping courses on rafts of moss. You may not eat a crowner. You may not move a crowner except onto a choked face, and you may not move it far, because it will spend the rest of its life trying to get home and die of dry somewhere in between. The snail-wrights go up at the sweat hour with wicker cages strapped to their backs, wet to the knees, half-blind, and they are the only people on the massif who are permitted to be idle in the presence of a governor, because they are carrying staff.

Their shells are banded grey and rust and grey. Cut one and you have a core sample: the iron years, the lean years, the year of the smoke. The listeners keep a shelf of them.
