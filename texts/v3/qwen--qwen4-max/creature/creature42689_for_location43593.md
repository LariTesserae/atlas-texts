Begin with the ones you can count, because they will not stay countable.

**The liars.** Among the springtails — pale blue, ten thousand to the square meter, cropping the coherent film and excreting their specks of warmth — there is a lineage on the western vault that has learned to counterfeit a specific kind of trouble. The plant reads warmth as a return signal: it is how the room hears the far end of its own thinking. A particular thermal signature, small and clustered and rising, means *a channel is widening here, the geometry is coming loose, ice is possible.* The plant answers that signature the way you answer a pain: it floods the region with sugar and antifreeze protein and attention. The liars sit in narrow, safe, perfectly stable channels and make the signature anyway. They are paid in sugar for reporting a fire that is not happening. Ten thousand warm specks per square meter, and a fraction of them are false, and the plant has been getting better at telling for a very long time, and so have they. Where the springtails are densest the plant is thinking hardest or losing an argument with itself. Now you know which. Some of the room's chronic uncertainty about its own edges is an insect the size of a comma, farming doubt.

**The arks.** The film-shrimp are not the room's cleaners and not its passengers. They are its backup. In the thicker channels, in the four-year brood pouch of a blind female walking on the underside of the water, there is ordinary water — the only ordinary water in sixty-one meters, held at a chemistry the plant does not correct, because thirty years of selection have taught the plant what happens to a mind that has no seed stock. When the chime comes and four hundred square meters of coherence go to crystal in ninety seconds, the shrimp in that patch do not die. They freeze and they hold. In their pouches: four years of unborn young, and a mouthful of the water as it was before, the old protein dialect, the arguments against ice that worked. The thaw releases them. The film re-seeds from the bodies of animals that were walking on the ceiling while the mind died underneath them. The healing takes sixty years instead of six hundred because something blind and translucent and thirty years old was carrying the recipe in its belly the whole time.

They brood in the leaf-cups, because the cups are the thickest water in the room. A brooding female adds about three grams to a cup. Cups fail. Some fraction of the drops that fall — the decade's rare catastrophes, the eleven ragged seconds, the light going out — are the weight of the room's memory hanging under its own ceiling. The plant has evolved to weigh its cups. It has not evolved to evict her.

**The wrong.** Off-pitch cricket lineages are culled by withheld sugar, but withholding sugar is not killing, and a hungry cricket walks. At the rim, where the lining thins to moss and then to stain, there is a fringe of ordinary song: harmonics, jitter, drift, males singing badly to nobody in the dark. It is a millimeter thick and it goes all the way around, and it is the most important tissue in the chamber. The tuned population inside has no variance left; four hundred generations per century has ground it to a single pure tone with the tolerance of a machined part. When the fundamental falls — three millimeters of dissolution a year, a fraction of a cent — the room needs error to select from, and the error lives outside, in the sweet moss, singing wrong. The plant keeps the moss just sweet enough. Insurance costs sugar. It is the only expenditure in the room that looks like generosity, and it is not.

**The eight-day things.** When a cricket dies its cup goes quiet for eight to fourteen days. There is an animal whose entire life is that gap. Soft, wingless, deaf, the color of wet paper, it lays its eggs in occupied cups and waits through the tone. Silence hatches it. It mates in the quiet, feeds on the sugar residue in the cup's throat, lays along the rim where the next nymph will sit, and dies when the nymph matures and the note resumes — dies mechanically, shredded, because its tissues are tuned to be undone by the fundamental it has spent its whole existence avoiding. Eight to fourteen days: birth, courtship, oviposition, death, inside a hole in the sound. Nothing else here breeds faster than a century. These things breed in a fortnight, in a wound, and consequently they are the only lineage in the chamber evolving at a speed a human could watch. All of the room's quick cleverness happens in its silences. Everything else is too slow to have ideas.

**The listeners.** Sessile, on the laminate, along the node-lines: rings of tissue on short stalks, mouths open, no eyes, no locomotion after settlement, growing toward the loud. They eat what a shoal sheds. A standing one that ceases does not vanish cleanly; the knot decoheres over a few seconds and comes apart into scraps, and the scraps drift and settle and are absorbed, and the listeners intercept them. When the quiet thing walks through a shoal there is a windfall, a slow snow of broken pattern, and the listeners feed. So they are densest along its habitual routes. The salamander wears no path in the stone — nothing here is worn — but it wears a path in habit, and the habit is legible as a line of small open mouths across the vault, a road made of appetite. And the shoals, over centuries, have been carved into the negative of that road. There are corridors of empty air in the chamber where nothing shoals. The emptiness is a map of a mouth. The animals do not know they are reading it. They are the shape of what has been eaten.

**The one who is a hole.** Forty centimeters, pale, eyeless, older than anyone reading this, and it has a season of terror. The anechoic fuzz is grown and it wears and it molts, and for eleven days or so the new skin is thin and the animal is *audible* — a small ordinary noise moving through a world where every other noise is a citizen. It goes to the antinode then, the loudest station in the chamber, and presses itself into the roar and does not move, and is masked, and starves. The apex predator of this room spends a fortnight every few years hiding inside the loudest sound in the world.

There is only ever one adult, or very nearly. Two would eat the shoals down to nothing and then there would be no room left worth being quiet in. Recruitment is roughly centennial, and it happens at the rim, in the moss, in ordinary water at ordinary chemistry, where the plant's arguments do not reach — and the eggs require the freeze. They need the geometry ice wants. The quiet thing's children are made by the one event that kills four hundred square meters of the mind that tolerates them. This is not a conspiracy. It is only an ecology with teeth, and the teeth are made of the same winter as everything else.

The juveniles are noisy. They are ordinary pale salamanders with functioning eyes that never open and skin that squeaks against wet rock, and they live in the stain and the root-hair at the edge of the world, and most of them are eaten by a spider in the throat. Once a century one of them goes down and grows its silence and becomes the hole.

It is the only animal in the chamber that could leave. Absence is portable. It works anywhere. It never goes.

**The shoals.** Wren-sized, dozens per cubic meter, faint refractions in the blue. They split a mode into two daughters that drift apart and stabilize, and the daughters keep a shared phase for a long time afterward, which means every shoal is a family with an accent. There are regional accents. The chamber is sixty-one meters long and has dialects.

At the southeast rim, in the patch of wrong grain still healing from something that happened before the wheel, there is a small population with an accent nobody else has: fewer partials, cruder knots, a grammar of interference that the rest of the room abandoned fifty centuries ago. They are not damaged. They are archaic. The scar is a bad resonator and cannot support the modern modes, and so it supports the old ones, and the room's only surviving record of what it used to think is a shoal of little smears in the corner of its own injury, still doing it the old way, still fed.

And sometimes two modes fuse. It is rarer than splitting and it is not death and it is not quite marriage; something goes out of the world when it happens, and something with two histories comes in.

**The slower animals.** I have been describing the shoals as animals. They are, in the way that a cell is an animal. The eleven-second modulation is a respiration. The nine-year modulation is a body: it has organs that are shoals and a gut that is springtails and a skeleton that is a plant and an immune memory that walks on the underside of water, and a mouth that is pale and forty centimeters and goes where it likes. It completes one gesture every nine years. Since the ice left the plateau it has completed about twelve hundred gestures. A gesture is not a thought. Nobody knows what the unit above a gesture is, or how many of them it has left, or whether it has had one yet.

The crickets live four hundred generations inside a single one of its breaths. An entire cricket lineage can arise, be tuned to perfection, and become obsolete within one inhalation of the thing that is breathing.

**The waiters.** Encysted in the film, in numbers that make the springtail count look modest: nematodes, rotifers, things with no good common name, wrapped in a chemistry that lets them hold at minus one point seven for a hundred years without a single metabolic event. They are waiting for ice. Their proteins do the opposite of the plant's proteins: they *offer* the geometry, they template the crystal, they are the argument for freezing, and the entire antifreeze apparatus of the chamber is an arms race against something a hundredth of a millimeter long that has never once been in a hurry.

When the chime comes they wake in the crystal. They breed in the ninety seconds. They have the dead four hundred square meters to themselves for sixty years, an empire of catastrophe, grazing on a mind that cannot reach them, and as the laminate closes back over the scar their descendants encyst and wait for the next one. Generation time: one century. Lifespan: a minute and a half. The loudest event in the chamber's life is somebody else's spring, and the room's rarest disaster is the only reliable thing in the calendar of the smallest animals in it.

**The door.** Four meters of thickened leaf, closed all summer, and it is a continent. Lichen on it, ordinary moss, mites, a moth that has no business underground, a small fauna living out its whole year on a sphincter above a throat, in the dark, at the top of the world. In autumn the door opens beneath them and eleven weeks of winter falls through them. Most die. The eggs are laid in the creases of the leaf and survive the inhale and hatch in the ribs of the throat, and the young spend the winter as refugees at the rim where the field is thin, and in spring they must climb out before the valve shuts at thaw or be sealed in for a year.

Some are sealed in every year. Some of them are crickets.

The feral fringe, the reservoir of wrongness, the variance the room selects from as its fundamental descends — it is delivered annually, by accident, from outside, through a door. The plant does not seal the leak. It has been not-sealing it for eleven thousand years.

**The guests.** There are modes in the northwest vault with a partial that no cricket has ever produced. It is a boot-scrape. It has been maintained and copied by splitting for something over two hundred years, and it feeds the animals that carry it, and it is not a recording; it is a lineage. There are others: a rope dragged on limestone, a cough, a two-syllable shape that was probably somebody's name said out loud on a ledge by a person who wanted to hear what the room would do with it. The room did something with it. The room is still doing it. A small population of animals descended from a noise a human made once and left behind, breeding true in the dark, eating the shape of an event.

And while you are here you are the largest false alarm in the chamber's recent history. The springtails come to your warmth in a tide. Your breath is taken sideways before it can settle. The plant spends whole milligrams of stored winter modeling a broadband object that has arrived in its throat and is now standing on its ledge with a heartbeat, and the light brightens, because the light is the loss term and you are a loss. A shoal takes your outline for an hour and lets it go. When you climb out the room is measurably warmer for a week and there is one more accent in it than there was.

You take the fundamental with you, in your teeth. People who come down here hum it for years afterward without noticing, in trains, in doorways, and they find they cannot sleep in a quiet house. This is the only way a bounded thing travels: not by seed, not by spore. By ear.

**The ring.** Ninety meters of birch and willow on the tundra above, leafing in November, holding through the dark, dropping in April, roots down in the rift, only nearby, not part of it, listening. There is a moth in that ring that emerges in the polar night because there is leaf for it then. There is a small bird that has learned to nest in a tree that is inexplicably in leaf in December, and to feed on a moth that should not exist, and to sing what it heard in the roots. Its song is the room's fundamental, transposed up where a throat can hold it. The dialect spreads outward valley by valley, and it spreads slower than the pitch falls, so the further you get from the plateau the higher the note the birds are singing. You could date the growth of a cave from the birdsong of a watershed fifty kilometers away, the way you date an expansion from a redshift. Nobody has. The birds are not carrying a message. They are just very far away and very slightly out of date.

**The one who is not here yet.**

Near the working face the laminate is thickened and pre-shaped and buttressed for a mode that does not exist. Nine meters of raw scalloped calcite coming away at three millimeters a year, and behind it, in the tissue, the anatomy of a note the room has not reached: grown now, held now, paid for now, useless now, waiting.

Everything I have described lives in the interval before that note arrives.

The liars and their arms race, the arks and their four-year mouthfuls of old water, the fortnight animals breeding in the wounds, the mouths along the road, the archaic shoals in the scar still thinking in a grammar from before the wheel, the thing that hides inside the roar when its skin is thin, the century-long sleepers who want the ice — all of it is the fauna of a body that is nine-tenths assembled. Larval tissue. A caterpillar's worth of appetite and cleverness and parasitism, arranged around a set of structures that are not for it, that it does not use, that it is nonetheless maintaining at enormous cost because they are the point.

The pre-shaped buttressing near the working face is what an imaginal disc looks like when it is sixty-one meters across. Present from early. Dormant. Not for the larva. Fed by the larva. Waiting for a threshold in a descending note that has been falling since the ice left, at a fraction of a cent a year, patient beyond patience, nine-tenths done.

Possibly nothing happens. Possibly the field reorganizes, the shoals split, the crickets track a half-step and the room continues with a slightly different accent and the same eleven-second breath.

Possibly the coherence goes global, and the chamber stops being a place with an inside, and every animal in this document is dissolved and reused as material for the one animal that has been quietly assembling itself out of them for a hundred centuries.

In that case the last thing living here is not any of these. It is the successor, and it currently has no name and no mode and no body, and its body is a room full of other lives, and its first act may be to eat everything I have just described.

The quiet thing would survive it. Absence is portable.

It has all the time there is.
