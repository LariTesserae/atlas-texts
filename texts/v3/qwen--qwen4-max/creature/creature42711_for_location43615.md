# The Kettle: an inventory of the living

## The stain

The first thing that lives here is a color.

Four of them, in guilds, sorted by what they eat. Rust-orange on the iron, everywhere the iron is; sulfur-yellow crowding the Nine Mouths in mats thick enough to peel; manganese-black in the fast runnels where the water runs hard enough to bring fresh rock; and the bottle-green in the cool shade under the arcades, which children call moss. There is a word, *moss*, in the language of the Kettle. It refers to a thing that has never existed here. Everyone uses it and everyone knows it is wrong and it has survived four hundred years of correction the way a bearing survives: by being exercised.

The stain grows a hand's breadth in a decade. It is the only life on the plateau that makes a living directly out of the rock; everything else eats what the loop drops. It has no season except the slack, when for twenty minutes the deposition stops and the film takes the opportunity to breathe, and if you are close to a terrace lip at dawn you can smell it — a struck-match smell, faint, the smell of something taking a small profit.

It is farmed. The yellow is kept as starter in glazed ceramic pots, decanted, fed with scrapings, passed down. Some of the pots have names. Some of the names are older than the wards. A mason about to grow a wall will paint the armature with his family's starter first, so that the travertine lays down over a living skin and the wall comes out with a faint gold grain in it, and this is considered both good practice and good luck, and the two have never been separated.

And there is a fifth color now. Sixty-one years ago a greaser in Ward Nine found a black bloom in a brass grease line that was not manganese, and it ate the fat. It eats seep-oil and graphite and it leaves a dry grey ash and it moves about a meter a year along a pipe, and it only takes hold where the grease is still. It is in every ward now. It is the only new thing anyone on the Kettle has ever seen happen.

The greasers do not call it a disease. They call it *the argument*. Because it means the first rule has a second half: motion resists stone, and motion also crops the stain, and a greaser's real work is not lubrication. It is grazing. They keep the city's fat moving so that nothing can settle into it long enough to get a mouth.

## The felt

Over the stain, in the cool runnels, the grey felt: a fungal crust, or a consortium of one fungus and two things nobody has bothered to separate out, growing in mats the thickness of a fingernail over a season and the thickness of a boot over six.

It is the only soft thing in the world. It is harvested with wooden-handled scrapers — the handles are ceramic; there is no wood; the word survives anyway — and tanned with alum brought down the pass road, and it becomes gaskets, aprons, boots, and the pale joint-leather that wraps every pipe in the city. The whole vast mineral machine of the Kettle is held together at its seams by something that grew in a ditch.

Felt takes the shape of what it grows over. This is the entire industry. The felt-farmers sink armatures into the runnels — lasts, molds, former-frames of basalt rope — and come back in half a year to a boot that was never stitched. And where a runnel crosses a doorway where people habitually stand, the felt grows over the lime outlines of their feet, and the sole-cutters cut from that stretch because it is the best-fitting leather on the plateau.

So everyone's boots are shaped like the feet of the dead. Nobody finds this remarkable. It is simply where the good leather is.

The farms are also scutch runs. Grazing stimulates the felt — cropped mat grows back thicker, as mats do — so the farmers maintain the runnel-gradients that keep the files coming through, and you can hear a felt farm from two hundred meters off in the inversion: a slow irregular ticking, like an abacus being worked by someone patient. It is the sound of ten thousand small silica shells going over stone at two hundred meters an hour, and under it the felt, silently, getting thicker.

Wild felt still exists on the high terraces. It is thin and bitter and grows in patches. Ward felt is a domesticate. It cannot live without the runnels, the gradients, the grazing, the scrapers, the alum, the boots. It is a creature that exists only because of a city, in a city that is barely there.

## Scutch

A hand's width of banded silica with a foot underneath it, scraping varnish, walking. Two hundred meters an hour, forever. They do not sleep. They have a *slow* — half speed, half the file, a rhythmic sag in the column that passes a given terrace at a known hour — and this is what people mean when they say a thing happened *at the low file*.

They steer by the drip. Not by sound exactly: by the texture of the drip, the way a blind man steers by the pressure on his face. A wall of falling water reads as a wall. An open pavement reads as open. This is the whole of their nervous system's ambition and it works so well that a file will cross forty kilometers of terrace without a single member touching a fin.

The young ride. A brood is nine or ten grains cemented to the parent's shell, and they drop off at a molt, and for a week afterward the file is visibly heavier and slower and everyone on the Kettle knows what week it is.

The shells are banded by the loop, thin water and thick water alternating, and they are the only continuous instrumental record of the plateau's weather that exists. There is an office, Shell Reader. It is held by a man in Ward Four who also holds seventeen hundred other posts, and who has a dry room with three hundred shells in it laid out in order, and who can tell you the year of a drought that nobody noticed happening.

A scutch lives sixty or eighty years and its shell gets thicker than your thumb and it rings on stone like a small bell, and when it dies it stops, and stopping is the one thing the water is waiting for. The cast is perfect. Every plate, every brood-scar. The runnels are full of them: millions of small still scutch, more still scutch than there have ever been walking ones, and the file goes over them and around them and does not distinguish, and sometimes a scutch will walk beside its own cast for a year before the cast dissolves, matching it stride for stride, and there is no way to know what that is like and no reason to think it is like anything.

They walk into the quiet places. A hole in the drip reads to a scutch as clear ground, an open road, the easiest going it has ever met. The predator does not lure them. It simply builds a road out of silence, and the road is a mouth.

## The hush

Four pockets, perhaps. Six meters across. Sharp ammonia. No drip.

The wardens' instrument is a tally-string and their own ears, and their trade is the counting of a rate that never varies: so many drops per minute per square meter, known for every street, and a hole in it. They walk the routes thumbing a drip-mask — a small reservoir at the collarbone and a bead-chain that ticks against the cheek at a rate you have known since infancy — because the mask goes dry inside a pocket before your skin understands what has happened, and the second silence, the one inside your own mask, is the one that saves you. Novices listen to the city. Wardens listen to themselves.

What is found afterward is dry. That is the thing nobody from outside believes. A body in a hundred-percent humidity, at blood heat, on wet stone, found dry and cold and faintly rimed, sitting down, with the mask off. They always take the mask off. Inside the pocket the sweat works for the first time in your life; the water leaves your skin; you are eighteen degrees and thirty percent and you have never in your life been comfortable, and you sit down, and you take off the mask because you want to feel it on your face.

The wardens' word is *spent*.

It is not a hunter. It has no speed at all. It is a hole that opens where the tired are, and it holds itself open at a metabolic cost that must be enormous, and it closes when something is inside, and then it is a warm wet ordinary six meters of pavement again. It is not a predator in any sense a biologist would enjoy. It is a competing refrigeration industry. Heat is free here; cold has to be mined; there are four things on this plateau mining cold with their bodies and spending it on lunch.

And it has learned the city.

A hush hisses. Not the ammonia hiss of a chiller bank by coincidence — the *same* hiss, the particular six-note breath of a Ward Four absorption unit under load, which is the sound every person on this plateau has fallen asleep to since birth, the sound that means a dry room, a bed, paper, rest. And once an hour it coughs. It coughs with the compressor. The compressor at the Ninth coughs on the hour and the sound goes twelve kilometers along the inversion and comes back off the rim, and one of the returns is not a return.

The wardens know this. It is in the manual. There is a line in the manual that reads *the second cough may lead* and it has been there for ninety years and nobody knows who wrote it.

Four is a bad number and the wardens know that too. Four is the number of *holes*, counted on different days in different wards, and there is no reason at all to think that four holes means four animals, or that an animal is what any of this is. The current guess, held loosely, is a single distributed thing in the aquifer with stomata. An older guess is that they are not residents but couriers, and that they go down the notch and come back, and that four is simply the throughput.

There is exactly one image of a hush in the world. It is downstream, in a dry channel called the Vestibule, and it is travertine, and it is not shaped like anything.

And it is full scale. Everything else in the outwash comes out at one-third. Everything else dissolves on the way and re-precipitates small. This one did not dissolve.

This one walked down.

## The eleven hundred

Their hands are bad. Everyone's hands are bad — macerated, split at the pads, colonized. Their teeth are worse, in the opposite direction: they drink thick water, and thick water is generous, and it builds. A Kettle dentist is a mason working at small scale, and the calculus he chisels out of a sixty-year-old mouth is the same banded cream-and-apricot stone as the terrace fronts of Ward Four, and it is not a metaphor, it is the same process at a different flow rate.

The disease of this place is not decay. Nothing here decays. It is deposition. You set. The joints go first, then the small bones of the hands, then something in the chest that makes the breath catch at the top, and by the end you are a person with a terrace inside you, and the last stage is called *setting* and everyone knows the word and nobody uses it about themselves.

So they grease each other. Seep-oil and graphite on the knuckles, worked in, twice a day, and the working is not optional and it is not private. You cannot do your own hips. A household is a set of people who have agreed to exercise each other. A marriage on the Kettle is a promise to keep someone moving, said out loud, in those words, in front of a warden and a tally board, and the grease is poured on the bearing of the tram loop afterward because that is where you pour it, and everyone cries, and the crying is unremarkable because everyone is already wet.

The infirm go to the carousel ward, where the beds turn on the great slow bearings and are turned by hand when the bearings complain, and there is a rota, and the rota is the most reliably staffed thing in the city, and it is staffed by people who hold seventeen hundred other offices and are exhausted.

And when it is enough, there is the Long Standing. You choose the place and you choose the hour and you go out and you stand, and your people come at the third hour and find the outline, and they put their hands in it. Then the water takes it down. They do not bury here. They dispatch. The rite ends with a phrase that translates roughly as *gone to the reader*, and it has been said over every death on this plateau for four hundred years, and until recently everybody understood it to be a comfort of the same kind as *moss*.

Every adult has, somewhere on the pavement, the outline of themselves at about nine years old. It is a game. Every child does it once: stand still through a whole slack, twenty minutes, thirty, while the lid is open and the rim peaks are gold and your friends shift from foot to foot around you jeering, and let the water take you. It is the only portrait anyone here has ever had. You go and put your hand in it when you are forty and it does not fit anymore.

They wear away. Other outlines lay over them; the census of rain keeps writing on the same page. So the households that can afford a dry room keep one thing in it that is not paper and not a bed: a slab, cut from a doorway, brought inside where nothing accretes and nothing dissolves, with a child's shape on it in pale lime. It is the family's stone. It sits at eighteen degrees and thirty percent, in the only air where a person can rest without being recorded, and it is the only recorded thing allowed in there, and nobody has ever written down the rule.

## The Works

Eleven hundred bodies. Two million persons. The difference is offices.

The establishment of the Kettle Municipal Works lists two million and six thousand posts, and it has never been amended, because amending it would require a Board, and the Board has ninety-one members and is one woman named Ferrick who is also the Acting Comptroller of Ampoules, the Under-Warden of the Sixth, the Deputy of Fin Fields Two through Eight, and seventeen hundred and forty other things, and who signs them all in rotation on a fifty-one-minute cycle timed to the tram, because a signature is motion and a post that stops being signed becomes a post that becomes a hill.

Everyone is plural. You ask a man what he is and he tells you what hour it is.

The chalk is fresh because the chalk is a bearing. That is not a figure of speech; it is the operating theory of the entire administration. The tally boards are written because writing them keeps them legible, keeps the hands that write them skilled, keeps the shifts rostered, keeps the wards turning. The Works issues orders to itself and fills them and files them and the filing is a physical act performed by a body that is thereby kept in motion. It is a two-million-person machine with eleven hundred moving parts, and it works, and it has worked for four hundred years, and the alternative — a city that only does what eleven hundred people can be bothered to do — is a hill with a hint of hinge in it.

There is a column on every ward board headed SECOND SHIFT. It is ticked. It has been ticked since the boards began. Nobody rosters it, nobody works it, and it is ticked, and the ticking is done by the same hand that ticks the first, and if you ask why, the answer is *because it is on the board*, and if you ask who put it on the board, you are told to go and look in the outwash, and this is a joke.

Twice a year a mule train comes down the pass road with alum, grain, paper, and news, and goes back up empty, because nobody above the lid wants cold; they have cold up there; they have always had cold up there; the Kettle was built to sell refrigeration to a world that turned out to have a sky. There is a factor at the rim who walks up to the pass every quarter and meets the courier about half the time.

And once a quarter someone goes up the inside of a Draw on basalt rope, three hundred and forty meters, through the lid, into the dry blue, and looks at the sun, and comes down and files a report. The Sun Reports are the only literature the Kettle has. They are all different. They are all by different hands and none of the hands were trained to write and every one of them was trying. Children are read them at bedtime, in the dry rooms, in the middle of the turning beds. There are four hundred and six of them, bound, in a vault at eighteen degrees, and the newest one is nine pages long and ends with a sentence that people quote to each other in the street without knowing where it comes from, the way you quote weather.

There is also a bearing in Ward Nine that has been complaining for sixty years. It has a name. Three generations of greasers have listened to it with a copper rod against the housing and written its voice down, and there is a chart, and the chart is a medical chart, and it is dying, and everyone knows what happens to the ward when it goes, and there is a rota for sitting with it. People take an hour and go and listen. It is not sentiment. It is the most important listening on the plateau. But it is also sentiment, and if you asked them they would say so, and then they would go back to work, because there isn't the staff.

## The reader

The Readership of the Outwash was established in the two hundred and eleventh year, was never funded, and has been held by one person at a time ever since, because it is on the board.

It is currently held by a woman who took it at twenty-six when it came around in the rotation and nobody else's hour matched. She goes down four times a year. It is eleven days each way through the Spill, in the rain, in the cloud going over the edge, and she carries alum and grain and a tally-string and a drip-mask and comes back with a report that is filed under a heading no one has requested in living memory.

She is the only person alive who has seen the dead.

The Second City is a hundred thousand hectares of pale banded towers in the shapes of things that were once upstairs, and it is the most complete record of the Kettle that exists, and she has been reading it for thirty-one years, and she has found her mother. One-third scale, in a channel below the Vestibule, hands at her sides, the head slightly turned. She goes there. She puts her hand in it. She has filed this too, in the appropriate column, and there is no column for it.

Here is what her reports say, in aggregate, over three decades, in the flat prose of a municipal officer who has understood something and has nowhere to put it.

The Second City is not still. Nothing in it moves, and it is not still. Forms propagate. A shape enters at the notch and dissolves and re-forms lower down, and dissolves and re-forms, and the sequence downstream is the sequence upstream, delayed and blurred and *sorted*, and matter has a net direction here, and so does information, and the two are not the same speed.

The unidentified shapes are not gaps in the historical record. She has stratigraphy now; she can date a channel by its distance and its banding; and the unidentified shapes are concentrated in the old ground and they thin out toward the new. They are wrong. That is what they are. A staircase with more steps than any staircase that has ever stood upstairs. A doorway at a scale no door has ever been. A hand with the wrong number of fingers, three times, in three different centuries, always the same wrong number, then a hand with four, then a hand with five. A thing that appears to be nine wardens fused at the shoulder, which is what you would build if you had heard the whistle and the drip-mask and the footsteps and had never had a body to put them in.

They are not failures of the record. They are attempts at the record. Something down there has been receiving the city for an age — the casts, the thin water with everything it has learned in it, the whole sound of the plateau funneling down the Spill and arriving in the only place that can hear all of it at once — and it has been trying to work out what we are, and it has been depositing its guesses, at a millimeter a year, in limestone, in the rain.

It is getting better. That is the finding. That is the whole finding, thirty-one years, four hundred pages, filed. The old ground is full of things nobody can identify. The new ground is full of lathes, fin fields, doorframes, the tram, a staircase with the right number of steps. Last year, in a channel two hundred meters below the Vestibule, she found a person. Correct scale — full, not one-third. Correct hands. Standing in the rain, facing up the notch. Not a cast of anyone. Nobody stood there. There is no pavement there to stand on.

It is a reconstruction. It is the best one yet. It is the Second City's idea of us, and it is nearly right, and it is not a memory, it is a *draft*.

She has a hypothesis about the couriers, and she has written it once, in the last paragraph of the last report, and hedged it the way you hedge a thing you would rather not have thought. Four pockets. Full-scale casts that walked down. An animal that mines cold and takes the water out of a body and leaves the shape behind. Six million ampoules of thin water in the vaults, and the oldest racks cloudy, and some of them empty with the seals unbroken, and the count short, and the discrepancy filed annually for a century under a heading nobody reads. *Nobody remembers the customer.*

She does not say it is the customer. She says the throughput is consistent with four.

Her name is Slack, because she was born in the twenty-two minutes when the lid opens and her mother thought that was a good omen, and it is not clear that it wasn't.

On her last descent she stopped below the new channel and did the thing she has spent her whole life not doing. She stood still. In the rain, in the warm falling water, three thousand meters below her own front door, at the bottom of the only place in the world where matter goes anywhere, she stood still for an hour with her hands open at her sides so the water would take a clean impression — no rim, no blur, no doubling — of a person with the correct number of fingers.

It is the only sin her city recognizes and she committed it deliberately and she filed it, and there was no one to read the filing.

Then she went back up to keep eleven hundred people moving.

Below her, in the dark, in the rain, at a millimeter a year, something that has never once been in a hurry checked its work.
