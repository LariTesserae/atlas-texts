## The Count

Four thousand is a courtesy. It is the number of people who can be reached by asking, and the asking is done by ear.

There is no other way to do it. A census taker walks out to a corner plate at the hour before the sap tide, kneels, and puts her cheek to the stone the way anyone does to listen for the curing, and then she stops listening for the ticking and listens for the doors. In air a door carries eleven kilometers. In green stone it carries farther and arrives cleaner, and a woman who has done this for thirty years can tell you not only how many doors closed in a district between midnight and dawn but which of them were pushed to by a hand and which by a hip, and whether the person going through was carrying anything.

She writes down what she hears. Then she stands up and counts the lit windows, which is fewer. Then she counts the footprints on the sweating flats around the plate, which is more.

The registry has three columns and no instruction about which one is the population. Most keepers fill in the middle number. A few fill in the third and dare you to say something about it.

## The Keepers

They are pale in a way that has stopped being interesting to them and is very interesting to visitors: not the pallor of illness but the pallor of something kept correctly, like paper in a drawer. Their pupils are large and slow to close. They read by sap-light without complaint and squint in a room with two lamps in it. On the rare day the lid thins to a bright oyster glare they get headaches and go inside.

Nobody walks barefoot. The sandals are woven from comb filament, bronze-colored, and they are replaced every six weeks because the arrises chew them. Every keeper's hands are a map of small white cuts, and the palms are printed — actually printed, faintly, with the burr-pattern of whatever kerb they last leaned on, so that you can tell where a man has been standing by looking at his hands the way you'd read a postmark.

They talk quietly, and with gaps. A visitor finds the gaps unbearable and fills them; a keeper waits them out. Sound goes too far here to be spent on nothing. Whole conversations happen at the volume of a library, across forty meters of empty paving, with three-second pauses in which you can hear a trimming crew working two districts over and a slab letting go somewhere near the horizon.

They say *upcountry*. They say *the make* the way farmers say *the weather*, meaning something that has opinions but not intentions. They have a word, *late*, for the moment when your shadow finishes what you did, and they use it mostly about people: *he's late today*, meaning tired, meaning drunk, meaning ill. A shadow that lags more than a second is a symptom. Keepers check each other's shadows the way people elsewhere check the color of a face.

Their names are their rounds. There are a million addresses and four thousand people, so each of them holds two hundred and fifty, and the holding is not ownership, it is *answering*. A woman is called Candlewright because she keeps Candlewright Row from the Sixteen Bells plate to the verge where the combs start, and her daughter will keep the eastern half, and be called Candlewright East, and the family will have been in the street for three generations without ever having a house in it.

## What The Keeping Is

Sweep the sap off the bronze before it cures into the letters. Chip the accretion out of the gutter slots. Shave the doorstep — this is the chore given to children, a flat chisel and a brush, a thumbnail a day of new stone off a threshold that would otherwise be a wall by autumn. Re-stamp a plate when the make has come up over it, using the old plate as the die, so that the number rises through the ground like a plant.

And keep the address plausible. This is the part that isn't in any manual and that every keeper does.

A chair on the lot. A cup on the chair's arm. A lamp lit in a window that is a window-frame standing free on a paved lot with nothing behind it, and behind it, if you walk around, nothing, and from the front, a lit window. A coat on a nail. Bread, sometimes, where there is bread, because a few of them bake. A pair of boots at the correct angle for a person who has just taken them off.

Ask why and you get the shrug and the local sentence: *so it knows what to put here.*

The theory — and it is a theory, held loosely, argued about at the harvest — is that the make is reading a record, and that the record is not a plan but a description, and that descriptions need something to describe. The plates are not labels for buildings. They are formwork. The city is complete in plan because the plan is the mold, and a mold has to be kept clean, and the four thousand are not settlers.

They are the pattern. They are what the ground is being told to copy.

## Downstairs

They do not build. They cut.

Nine meters down, the first streets are intact and warm and dry, lamps burning in solid stone, and the stone cuts like a hard cheese with the mineral still bright in the face. So a family quarries a room. They take out a block, and the block is sold or used for a kerb up top, and what is left is a chamber with a ceiling of make and a floor of make and, if they cut carefully, a lamp in the wall that has been burning for eighty years and does not need them and never did.

There are cellars down there growing pale things in trays under those lamps. There is a market at minus four on the second-generation Long Approach with eleven stalls, and you get to it by a stair that was a stair before it was a shaft. There are workshops, and a bath, and a room where people go to be loud, because it is the only place on the plain where a shout dies in under a kilometer, and they rent it by the hour for arguments and for singing.

Every room fills at a thumbnail a day. Every floor slopes up at the edges where the make came in fastest. The housework of a rising ground is subtraction: you shave your floor in the spring, you re-cut your doorway when your shoulders start brushing, you measure your children against the frame and the marks go down into the stone year by year until the oldest notch is below the floor and you have to cut to find it. A parent will show you a child's whole growth as a descending column of small chisel-cuts, the way people elsewhere show you a pencil line on a jamb, and will tell you the child's height at four by putting a finger in a hole.

Some rooms are not cut. Some rooms were furnished once, at the surface, and went under to their shoulders and stayed, and the make came in around the furniture and stopped, or slowed, or is thinking about it. There is a room off the North Approach with a table in it and four chairs and the floor rising gently toward the middle like a held breath, and it is warm, and dry, and nobody lives there, and it is swept.

The crews that go down to read the old plates report that the old plates are legible. Not preserved. *Legible* — polished, recent, the sap cleared out of the letters. There is a keeper at minus nine. There is a round down there. Nobody has met her, and the census taker, who has heard doors below her that have no houses above them, has stopped putting them in any column at all.

## The Ones In The Lid

The towers put their heads into the stratus and come out wearing weather, and in the heads there are people, because the lid is maintained and maintenance is done from above.

They are the only brown-skinned people on the plain. They come down looking like something imported, and they talk too loud, and they squint at the sap-light and cannot read a map by it, and everyone is gentle with them. They see the sun most days. They see the sea, and the sea's edge, and they are the ones who measure it against the base of the far towers and write the number down, and the number is the reason the measurement teams came twice.

They also cast punctual shadows, or nearly — a tenth of a second, a fifth. Up in the real light the local speed holds. They come down and their shadows go slow again over the course of an afternoon, and they hate it, and they describe it as *getting heavy*, and they go back up.

The lid crews marry keepers sometimes. It never works out geographically.

## The Saddles

The only animal you'll see in a week's walking, and you'll see it because you'll nearly step on it: a low pale dome the size of a hat, translucent enough at the rim to show the meal inside, moving at about the speed of an hour hand.

It eats the soft skin off curing stone. That is all it does. It cannot eat hard stone and it cannot eat sap, so it lives on the narrow moving frontier where the make has just finished and the hardening has just begun, and it must follow that frontier or starve, and the frontier moves at a thumbnail a day, and so does it. A saddle's whole life is a walk of a few kilometers. Its shell is laid down in bands that record the curing rate of the ground it crossed, and the crews read them like tree rings, and a saddle from the west, where the plain rises faster, is a fat thing with wide bands and a bad temper, if a thing with no voice and no eyes can be said to have one.

Their trails are the best survey anyone has. You can walk a saddle road for two days and know exactly where the curing front is, and the trimming crews do it, and the surveyors, who are expensive and slow, follow a hat-sized animal and write down what it already knew.

They lay in the warm ground. They press down into a shallow front and leave the eggs and the make comes up over them, which should be the end of it. It isn't. Blocks cut out of the first-generation stone at minus nine come open with saddles in them, alive, chewing, eighty years old, still on the frontier they were on when the ground closed. There is a bath-house at minus four with three of them in a wall, and the bath-house keeps them, and the water is warmed by them, and children put a hand on the shell and feel it move a millimeter and are told, correctly, that it is saying hello.

Nothing eats them. There is nothing here to do it. The crews don't, and get quiet if you suggest it. The one man who did said it tasted of almond and chalk and that he dreamed of bread for a week, and he had never smelled bread, and this was before the make started bringing the smell up.

## The Green Tenants

The combs were planted and are therefore artifacts, and are also the oldest families on the plain, and both things are true in the way that both things are true about a canal.

Each one holds a water claim, and the claims are enforced underground by root, slowly, lethally. A comb whose claim is jumped will spend three seasons killing the jumper, and you can watch the murder happen from a tower as a raft of black canopy going bronze and then grey from one edge inward. The registry up top has the claims mapped. The map is wrong in about a fifth of cases and the combs are right, and the water office has stopped litigating.

The lithophytes are the volunteers, and they are not tenants, they are squatters with a schedule: four meters in a season, hollow boles full of stored water, aerial roots that take a road between trimming cycles. The crews cut them and the smell goes across a district and everyone downwind stops for a second, because crushed almond and wet chalk is the smell of the ground being opened, and there is no one here who does not have a feeling about it.

In every hollow bole, in the stored water, there is a small flat pale swimmer, blind, no longer than a fingernail, that eats the sap and has no voice and does not need one. The crews call it the tenant. It is in every bole. It is in the bores out in the bare nothing where no lithophyte has ever grown, in the drill-holes with their standing pupils, in the sockets of buildings that were never started. It is the same animal in all of them across ten thousand square kilometers, which is not how volunteers spread.

Nobody put it here. It came up. It was in the record.

## The Dogs

There are dogs, and they do not bark.

Two generations of the loudest ones being taken out to the far districts did most of it, and the rest is something the dogs worked out for themselves, because a bark here goes eleven kilometers and comes back late from a direction it did not go, and a dog is not equipped for that. They point with their noses. They lean. They put a paw on a foot. A keeper coming home across the flats sees a small dark shape at the edge of the lamp-throw and knows whose it is by the lean.

Dogs run at a speed where the lag is a whole body-length, and a dog can catch its own shadow. They do it constantly, all their lives, and never get bored, and it is the single most upsetting thing on the plain to watch for the first time: the animal pounces, and there is a half-second of nothing, and then the shadow arrives under its paws and is caught, and the dog is delighted.

Children try. Children cannot. Children born here cast almost no lag at all — a tenth of a second, a twentieth, and in the newborns none that anyone can measure — and the old keepers watch a child run across a lit flat with its shadow perfectly under it and have to look away, and cannot tell you why, and the lid crews, who are punctual too, say *ah, they're up-top children, that's all*, which is not an explanation and is meant kindly.

## The Largest Resident

Under the grey hatches, at a depth nobody agrees on, something precipitates stone, and it is the only thing on the plain that is unambiguously alive in the sense of having an appetite.

It eats a coast. Not the coast that is there — the one that would have been, downstream in time, the shoreline this water was going to make and now isn't. Eleven million tonnes a year and nothing anywhere loses it. The second team's last page is one sentence about a beach that has been cancelled, and the sentence is in the future tense, and there is no grammar for what it is actually saying, so the surveyor used the future and went home.

It reads. This is the part the keepers are certain of and the measurement teams will not write down. It reads a record and it copies out what it finds, and it makes the mistakes a copyist makes: a line taken from the wrong depth, a mirror-flip, a repetition. The stairs with no landing at either end are a stair whose landings are at minus four and minus nine. The smell of bread is a bakery on the first-generation Long Approach, still warm, copied up eighty years and nine meters out of place. The footprints facing the wrong way are somebody's, correctly copied, from a layer where they were walking the other way.

And it breathes on a cycle of about nine seconds, which you stop feeling in a month and then feel again at three in the morning and cannot stop, and every keeper's chest keeps that time whether they want it to or not, and the singers in the loud room at minus four sing in it without knowing, and the songs brought here from elsewhere all get slowly pulled into nine until they are local.

The sounding crews go down on cable. They come back with different depths and warm lamps and, for about a day, no lag whatsoever, and they are watched closely, and they are not allowed to keep addresses until their shadows go slow again.

## The Ones Still Arriving

Near the Sixteen Bells plate there is a footprint in a cured slab, set there when the slab was soft, and it is a human footprint, bare, well-formed, and a size no one on the plain has grown into.

A trimming crew found it eleven years ago. They measured it. Then they measured every child in the district, and then they measured them again the next season, and the measuring has become a thing the district does, an unremarkable annual item like the gutter slots, and two of the children are now within a centimeter, and their mothers do not talk about it, and the footprint is kept clean.

There are others. There is a woman on Candlewright Row who sets a cup on the chair-arm at number fourteen every evening, and takes it in every morning, and it is always warm, which means nothing here; everything is warm.

And the seabirds come through in the fog, lost, and go on, and lately some of them do not go on. They stand on the kerbs at Sixteen Bells with their wings half out, screaming — the only voices on the plain, and everyone lets them, and nobody has ever complained — and they are lost because the coast they were navigating to is being spent, a thumbnail a day, on kerbs and verges and addresses; and they come inland to the only ground there is; and they are the first of the displaced to arrive, and they arrive early, through the wrong door, from downstream.

The make brings up a fossil of a bird with four wings. The crews have stopped calling that an error. It is a copy of something at a depth that has not happened yet.

So: four thousand, plus the saddles in the walls, plus the combs with their claims and their tenant in every bole, plus the silent dogs catching themselves, plus the lid crews with their borrowed sun, plus one engine with one appetite, plus whoever is polishing the plates at minus nine.

And a coast's worth of people who have not been cancelled yet, coming up out of the ground at a thumbnail a day into addresses that already have their names on them, and a woman on Candlewright Row keeping the letters clean.

The census taker kneels at dawn with her cheek on the stone and hears more doors than there are houses, and writes down the larger number, and the ledger does not balance, and the difference is not an error.

The difference is the part of the population that has not finished getting here.
