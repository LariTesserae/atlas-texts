Start with the ones that never travel farther than their own length.

The snails are not living on the wall. They are finishing it. Each one has ground, over a life longer than most languages, a shallow socket exactly the shape of its own shell's lip, and it returns to that socket after every excursion of a body-length or two, and fits, and rests. The socket is not a nest. It is a negative of an individual. There are millions of them and no two are alike, and in the dark, where you cannot see the stone and can only see the grid, they are indistinguishable from the niches the makers cut. The wall has been hollowed twice: once by intention, once by tenancy. Where the two hollowings coincide you cannot say which is architecture.

Their mouths wear out. A rasping tongue is a ribbon of mineral teeth, and teeth go, and are replaced from behind, and the spent ones fall. This is the flour. The knee-deep slope you wade is mostly teeth — teeth and the small pale shells of the dead, ground fine by the slow turn of the water, page on page, a fathom of them down to the pavement nobody has touched. The floor of this room is made of the room's worn-out mouths. It behaves like liquid because it is almost entirely the shed working parts of animals, and it takes an hour to settle because nothing in it is heavy enough to be in a hurry.

The laminae are identical, generation to generation, because nothing has ever changed: same felt, same thickness of felt, same rate of grazing, same teeth. But they are not perfectly identical. Four pages in all that thickness are wrong. One is faintly green. One is coarser, as though for a while the grazing slackened. One is a hiatus, a place where the pages touch with nothing between them, and the layers above and below do not match. Nobody has read them. There is no one here who reads, and the pages do not glow.

---

The crabs close the loop.

They take the flour into their crops, work it with the sour water and their own chemistry, and carry it up as a pellet the size of a grain of millet, and set it into the joint, and tamp it, and finish it with a stroke of a flattened pad. Then they test it. The test is the animal's own body: the second joint of the foreleg. If the leg enters, the gap is a gap and must be filled. If the leg does not enter, the work is done.

So the hair's width is not a measurement here. It is a limb. The standard of the making is stored in the bodies of the maintenance, and it has been copied from body to body without a break since the last hand left the wall. Nothing checks the crabs. Nothing needs to. A crab that gauged wide would leave joints the water could enter, and its lineage would be dissolved out of the seams in a few thousand years, and there would be nothing left of it but a slightly wider gap, briefly, before the next lineage closed it.

This has consequences for what you find in the lee. Under a corbel, where the turn of the water does not reach, the pointing is sharp. It has edges. It has the fine parallel drag of a tool. You will take it for a chisel stroke, and it may be one, and it may be the pad of an animal that has been dead for six hundred years, and there is no test that will tell you which. The archive is contaminated by the archivists. The room has been forging its own antiquities continuously, in good faith, at a rate of about a hand's breadth a century.

And the crabs carry the light. New lime does not glow. Old mortar does, because of what was tempered into it, and because of what lives in that temper now. So a crab cropping out a failing joint takes the old substance into its crop along with the new, and sets them together, and the film goes on across the repair without a break. Every glowing line in this room is an unbroken inheritance. It has been passed along the seams like a rumor that never once stopped being told.

---

The feather-cups share their mucus.

A thread combed through the milk for a day comes back loaded and is eaten, and the animal spins again from what it ate, and what it ate includes what its neighbor ate. The substance circulates. A given mouthful of mucus works the north wall for a season, drifts a pace, works there, and in a year may have been through four hundred mouths. The wall is one organ with a million openings, and it is combing the water with a single continuous quantity of slime that belongs to no one.

They are budded, most of them, from the tier below. It is possible that the north wall is one animal. It is possible that the whole honeycomb, all four walls and the crown, is three or four animals in the way that a hedge is one tree. When a cup withdraws, the neighbors' threads slide into its space, and when it opens again they slide back, and this happens ten thousand times an hour across the wall, so that the wall carries slow waves of folding, propagating at about the speed of a walking snail, crossing from the throat to the far corner over the better part of a day. Nobody has established what starts them. They may not start. They may be what a saturated surface does when it is perturbed by the earth's tide and has nowhere to put the perturbation but sideways.

It is the closest thing to a gesture in the room.

---

The fish do not hunt and do not spawn on opportunity. They spawn on vacancy.

Consider the arithmetic. Everything here eats the rock's breath, and the rock's breath arrives at the rate of a resting breath, and has arrived at that rate for longer than the animals have been shaped to it. The total life in this chamber is therefore a fixed quantity, and has been fixed for an age, and it is *full*. Every patch is worked. Every niche is occupied. Every position in the plume is held. There is no room, and there has never been room, and so nothing here can be born until something dies.

They are all brooders. Every one of them. Nothing broadcasts; a broadcast egg would drift eleven years and go over the lip and down the spillway and out, and there is a warm spring on the flank of the stump where the room's lost children arrive, in the dark, in a thin continuous rain, and die. So the eggs are glued, or held in a pouch, or carried in the mouth. A fish in the plume holds its station and its fry together, sometimes for years, and the fry do not leave the mouth until a station empties somewhere within reach.

Which means the population you see is not the population. Under every lip, in the lee, in the sheltered underside of every corbel, there are the ones who are waiting: motionless, mouths full, holding a brood that has been ready for a decade, hanging in the one part of the room where the water does not bring food, thinning slowly, dying occasionally, and being replaced from behind. The lee is where the sharp edges are kept and where the future is stored. It is the same place for the same reason. Nothing reaches it.

A fish that dies in the plume is replaced within a tide. You would not notice. The shoal's spacing never changes — one body-length, everywhere, always — and it does not change because a waiting animal has been watching the pressure of that gap for eleven years and knows the exact hour it opens.

---

The shrimp are the pen.

The film in the joints would grow thick if it were let alone, and a thick film shades itself, and a shaded film dims and dies back to a crust. The shrimp crop it. They go along the seams in their soft clouds, brushing at the glow with legs like eyelashes, and they take the excess and leave one layer, and one layer is the thickness at which the light is even. This is why the diagram is legible. Not because anything wants it legible. Because a million small appetites find a bloom worth eating and a crust not worth the walk.

They carry it, too, from joint to joint, on their legs and in their guts, so that a seam gone dark is re-sown within a season from its neighbors. The writing is being copied continuously by animals that eat the ink.

The hermit things are the only fast event. A thimble is a house and a house is forever, because no one has made a thimble in an age and no one will. There are a fixed number of made things on this floor and the number only goes down: copper thins, green flakes away, a bead cracks, an awl-cap becomes a ring becomes a stain. As a hermit grows it must move up, and it cannot move up until something above it empties, and so they queue. By smell, in size order, in the dark, along the lee. When a large house empties, the largest waiting animal takes it, and its old house goes to the next, and the next, and the chain resolves through twenty animals in an afternoon, and then the floor is still again for another forty years.

There is no fighting. Fighting is percussion, and percussion here is a thing you add to a room that keeps everything. The succession is chemical and patient and absolute. The quiet has shaped their politics the way the throat has shaped their bodies.

And the crabs point the thimbles. Of course they do: a hairline crack is a hairline crack, and the leg does not care whether the gap is in a vault or in a copper cap. So the artifacts are being maintained, patched, filled, slowly converted from copper to room. There is a thimble on the floor that is four-fifths lime and one-fifth metal, and it is still perfectly a thimble, and it will be perfectly a thimble when there is no copper left in it at all.

That is the whole method of this place. The shape is kept. The substance goes through. Nothing here is the material it was made of, and nothing here is a different shape.

---

The ferrymen keep the commons.

Each has a round: a trapline of cups on the underside of the crown, inherited, overlapping at the edges with the rounds of others, where exchange happens and nothing is said. They gather the beads sweating off the stone, hold one against the belly, climb, and tithe. What they keep is a fraction. What they take is the film at the dome of the pocket, the rich interface where air and water meet and the chemistry is unlike anything else in the room.

So the air is a mixture, and has been mixing for an age, and any cup you put your face into holds an average of the entire chamber's breathing. It is not the sky's air. It has been worked over so many times that the rock's sour gas has been scrubbed out by the water and the oxygen has been spent down to a thin remainder and what is left is mostly the inert part, the part that does nothing, the part that only ever passes along. It is old and it is thin and it will keep you alive, and it will slow you, and after a while you will find that you have stopped finishing your thoughts at the speed you used to, and that this does not distress you.

Nothing here has ever been afraid. There is no predator; there is no surprise; there is nothing to startle at, and so no animal has ever needed a fast muscle or a flinch. You are the loudest and quickest thing that has ever occurred in this chamber. Your heart is audible in it. You can put your hand through a shoal and it will part at the speed of courtesy, making room, closing behind, and the sound of your pulse will still be faintly present a quarter of an hour after you take your hand back.

---

Now the light, which has changed.

The temper was hair and blood and the boiled scraps of the making, and the film that glows is descended from what ate it, and it has been eating ever since. There is no hair left in the joints. There has been no hair in them for an unimaginable time. The film eats what the room gives it now: spent mucus, the organic matrix of shed teeth, the bodies of the small dead, whatever settles into a seam. It has been rebuilt out of the inhabitants, molecule by molecule, ten million times over, and it goes on glowing, and it goes on drawing the plan.

But brightness follows food. So the diagram has drifted. Where the makers were most careful — the crown, the springing of the ribs, the corners — the lines are now the dimmest, because little dies up there. Where the feather-cups are thickest and the plume brings up the most, the lines burn strongest. Two drawings occupy the same seams. One is the intention, fading. One is the appetite, brightening. They are not aligned, and the misalignment is the only history this room has ever written down.

The makers are not gone. They have been eaten and eaten again and distributed. There is a fraction of what was tempered into that mortar in every shell on the wall, in the ear-stone of every fish, in the film at the dome of every cup. When you take a mouthful of air up there you are breathing a little of the making. Nothing in the room knows this. It is not the kind of thing that can be known by anything that lives here.

---

The eyed ones are not a species.

That is the first thing to get straight. The single dark rudiment, pointed up, turns up in a shrimp here, a fish there, one snail in ten thousand with its shell tilted a little off true — scattered lineages, no relation, the same organ. It is not an eye. It cannot form an image; it is a pigment cup with a lens of sorts and behind it a small mass of tissue that is not green and was never a leaf and does not make anything out of carbon. It makes a little energy out of light directly, and nothing else, and it is the only food in this chamber that does not come up the throat.

They gather because once a day the sky is briefly available. They do not feed — nothing enters their mouths. They hang in the coin with their one dark point turned up and take the only meal in the world that comes from outside, and they do not touch, because touching here is only ever transfer, and there is nothing to transfer.

Or: the thumb-tide is a good clock but it drifts, and the sun does not, and what they are doing is taking a fix. The room's whole timing — spawning, grazing, the ferrymen's rounds — is corrected once a day by a handful of animals who go up and check.

Or: they were stocked. The makers kept something in this water that came when a lamp was lit, and it came for reasons of its own that had to do with the lamp and the hand and the hour, and the lamp has not been lit in an age, and the coming has continued. Maintenance gone feral. The same thing exactly as the crabs, who are pointing a wall for builders who are not coming back, and the same thing exactly as the hermits, who are queuing for an inheritance that cannot be added to.

All three may be true. The room is not obliged to have one reason. It has had a very long time to accumulate them.

---

In the lee there is a thumbprint with the whorls intact.

The crabs have not filled it. They fill gaps; this is a hollow; the leg enters and finds no edges to close against and the animal goes on. A rule with no exception in it has preserved, by accident, the only signature in the world of a thing that had hands. The crabs work up to its rim and stop and turn and work away, and have done so for longer than there have been valleys, and the whorls are as sharp as the day they were pressed.

You will not add anything to this place. It has arrived at its last shape; the dissolving and the pointing found each other and locked; the felt is one hair thick because it is eaten exactly as fast as it grows; the shoal parts and closes. You will take a mouthful from one of the ten thousand stone hands, and it will be thin and old and will slow you, and you will hang under the crown in the dark with the grid drawing itself around you for no one, and then you will breathe out.

And a ferryman will find that bead.

It will be cold, first of all — colder than anything that has been in this water. And it will be air that has been over the stump, that has been in weather, that has been rained on and frozen and fallen as snow and come down the valley and gone up again, and there has been none of that in here since the crown was closed. She will hold it against her belly and climb and give most of it away, and the rest will go mouth to mouth to mouth along the rounds, cup to cup, at the pace of a thumb's width twice a day.

In a thousand years there will be a little of the sky in every pocket in the ceiling, and the film at every dome will taste of it, and nothing will know, and the light will go on drawing the plan.
