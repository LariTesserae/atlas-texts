## The grazers

Begin at the bottom, because everything else is standing on it.

A plate is a wet slate the width of a hand, and it has no mouth in any sense you would recognize; its whole underside is the mouth, a film of contact laid against strained stone. It does not eat the rock. It eats the rock's *discomfort* — the charge that squeezed quartz keeps pushing to its surface, endlessly, for free. Behind a plate as it goes there is a trail of relieved stone, a wake of comfort, invisible and dead, and it lasts about a day before the load fills it in again. Other plates read that wake and turn aside. Not out of courtesy. There is nothing there.

So a plate's life is a route. It walks a loop some hundreds of meters around, at a body-length an hour, and comes back to the beginning just as the beginning has finished recharging, and it does this ten thousand times, and then ten thousand more. The loops do not overlap. They are not defended; they are *scheduled*. Neighbors that have kept the same appointment for four hundred thousand years have begun, along their shared boundary, to grow slightly different edges — a notch, a scallop, a thickened lip — the way two languages wear a border into each other. There are places on the floor where you can lay your palm across a line and feel the animals change hand.

They give birth by breaking. A plate lays down a septum inside itself, loads it, and one day snaps: a small clean strike, a spark, a bloom of the quick, and two plates where there was one. This is why they come to the knapping floors. From a kilometer off, over weeks, they converge on the sound of a blade being made, because to them it is the sound of somebody having children. A knapper works in a slowly arriving congregation of wet slate, and the congregation is part of the meal.

## The lamps, and the wires that feed them

The lantern-bells are not buoyant. Nothing here floats on gas; the air is too heavy and too uniform to be worth leaning on. They hang on charge, the same way the Silt hangs, the same way the whole middle world hangs: the cavity is a capacitor four kilometers tall, and everything in it is a mote in a field, and the reef is a suspension.

A bell breathes very slowly, and what it is breathing is not air. It is topping itself up. Its amber is the leakage of that work. When a bell begins to fail — and failure here is only ever one thing, a slow loss of hold — it sinks, over months, over years, toward the sharpest surface in the universe, and everything in the neighborhood can see it going.

The cords come. A cord is a ribbon animal, kilometers of it, coiled in a helix that has never turned because there has never been anything to turn it in; the helix is a growth-form, a shape laid down by an animal reaching for light along a path it was careful to make long. A cord touches the floor at one end and the roof at the other, and the floor and the roof are opposite poles, and a cord is therefore a wire, and it is alive, and it lives precisely by being *too long to short out*. Along its length it hangs mineral scales that crop the Seam's blue-green; at intervals it swells into a node; and it walks charge down its body at a rate measured in centuries.

When a cord reaches a sinking bell it lays a loop against it and waits. The bell rises a hand's width in a season. Nobody who has watched this ever quite gets over it.

A knapper can do it in an afternoon. A strike floods the local field, and a bell hung above a working floor will lift, visibly, in the time it takes to eat. This is the other half of the craft, and the half the people are proudest of: a good knapper keeps forty people fed and keeps the lamps up. Huts are sited under good stands of bells the way we site towns under good water. A hut whose bells have gone down is a hut in the dark, and the dark here is not an absence of light. It is a floor you cannot see, three meters below your feet, made entirely of edges.

Where a cord crosses the middle plane — where the field passes through zero — it thickens into a *waist*, and at the waist, and only at the waist, it spins wool: kilometers of mineral fleece, hung out to catch the falling grains. This is also the only place a cord can be touched. Harvesting wool is the second craft, and it is done by finding a waist in the dark by feel, with the hands, while the rest of the animal carries enough potential four meters above your head to stop your heart. The wool is not for warmth. Nothing needs warmth. It is for catching motes, and for roofs, and for brooms.

## The other hand

The pendants under the roof are not, as the doctrine holds, the same individuals seen twice. They are the mirror brood, and the mirror is a *chemical* mirror: the roof is the negative pole, its strained surfaces charge the other way, and everything that grew up there was built to that handedness. Their sugars are the wrong hand. Their proteins fold the wrong way. A man can climb a roof-spike and eat a pendant exactly like the plate he had for breakfast and starve on it.

Two biospheres, four kilometers apart, identical in every particular except the one that matters, mutually inedible, unable to touch, unable to rot into one another. The people know this the way they know most things: as a rule with a story attached. *You may look at your other self. You may not eat him.*

And the mirrors do not quite match. There are pendants hanging alone above an empty patch of floor, and there are plates crawling under a bare socket. The unmatched ones are called widows, and they are counted, and the count is kept in the chains, and it does not agree between the huts, and the disagreement is four hundred thousand years old and has its own literature.

## The ears, and why nobody shouts

At the antinodes, where the standing note is loudest, the ear-things sit in ranks with their cups open and take the note out of the air. They are piezoelectric, like everything; they eat pressure. They have been eating it so long, in the same places, that they have become part of the boundary conditions of the world: the fog-walls are sharp because the ears are sharp, a hedgerow of listening bodies holding the coastline of a cloud that has not moved since before there were eyes.

They are also load-bearing.

The Silt is held up by charge and nothing else. A pressure pulse of sufficient size, in the wrong place, will strip charge from grains across a wide front, and the sky will come down. Not all of it. Enough. It comes down as flour, silently, over a day and a night, and it buries the bells and blinds the floor and lays a grey dune a hundred kilometers long, and then — this is the part that is not in the polite versions of the chains — then it *hatches*.

Because the Silt is not only excretion and fracture-flour. It is where everything puts its young. In a world with no wind and no current and no water, there is no dispersal; a spore cannot travel; the only vehicle that has ever existed is a grain that loses its charge and falls. So the reef has been seeding the sky for a geological age, and the sky is a seed bank four kilometers deep, and the ordinary traffic of that bank is one grain per swept square per year, one lottery ticket, one birth.

A shout brings down a springtime.

There is a country out past the third stair where this happened, long ago, and it is not a Quiet and not a fog; it is a dune, and things live in it that live nowhere else, woken all at once out of a hundred million years of waiting into a world that had moved on without them. The people call it the Hatch and they do not go there and they speak of it in a grammatical form used for nothing else.

So the quiet is not manners. The quiet is engineering. *The mistake* is the word for a sound that could bring the sky down, and every child learns it before it learns to walk, and the ear-things are not a curiosity at the edge of the world. They are the reason there is a world to have a curiosity in.

## The year, and what is inside it

Some grains are hollow.

Hold one up against the Seam and the light comes through wrong — a shadow with a knot in it. Most are solid, and those are the years, and they are stacked and recounted and argued over. The hollow ones are set aside in their own small pile, and they are not years, and a household does not count them, and every household has some.

The swept square beside a hut is therefore two instruments in one: a calendar and a clutch. Sweeping is contraception. A family that stops sweeping does not get a messy floor; it gets, eventually, ancestors. There are lineages that begin with a hatching — a grain opened on purpose, in a bowl of wool, at a chosen time, by a person who wanted someone to talk to — and such a person's descendants carry the grain's number in their names, and are, in a real and legally consequential sense, all one age with everyone else on the continent who carries that number. Grain-twins flash to each other across the dark for their whole lives and never once come within a hundred kilometers of touching.

And the roofs. A hut's roof is mineral wool, which is to say a mote-net, which is to say that the family's real archive is above its head, uncounted, unordered, fur-thick with the unopened years. There are people who comb roofs for a living. They are welcome everywhere and trusted nowhere.

## The ones in the stone

The bulk of life here is not visible and never has been. It lives in the microporosity, in films a few molecules thick, in the strained lattice itself, and it outweighs the plates and the bells and the cords and the pendants and the people together by a margin nobody has ever been able to estimate.

It is slow past slowness. A generation is a hundred thousand years. Since the Blow they have had, at most, eleven.

They are not ancient. That is the thing the knappers understand and nobody else can be made to feel. Down in the stone there are creatures for whom the Blow is not prehistory; it is *childhood*. The stress wave went through the whole body of the rock in a few seconds and it is the only event that has ever happened to them, and everything since — the arrest, the standing note, the whole long warm afternoon of the world — is the ringing in their ears afterward. They are still flinching. Eleven generations is not enough to stop flinching.

When you strike a blade you open their houses. The quick that blooms on a fresh face, that the plates come for and the people lick while it is still bright, is partly free charge and partly *them*, spilled out, blinking, into a space the size of a sky.

The bloom lasts about ninety heartbeats. It is the only thing in the world with a deadline.

## The people

Their soles are wet slate. They came from the plates; nobody disputes this; the anatomy is not subtle. A person standing still is grazing, and a person standing still for a day has eaten, and this is the whole basis of their famous patience, which visitors from nowhere would find unendurable. Children are carried until their soles harden. Status is the callus of the foot. The dead are laid face-down.

They do not breathe to live. The bellows in the chest is a sound-organ, a vestige repurposed; the metabolism runs at the sole and the tongue. To speak is to spend nothing but risk everything.

They see polarization. This is the sense that makes them foreign. Strain has a color to them; load has a grain and a shimmer; a floor under burden looks the way a wheatfield looks in wind, except the wind is nine hundred thousand years old and does not move. They can see the discharge-wake of a plate. They can see the field-lines standing in the air like the ribs of a tent. They can see the Seam's threshold *in the stone ahead of the light*, a sickness in the rock running out in front of the glow, which is why the forbidden distance is felt in the teeth before it is measured by anything: your eyes tell you first, and then your teeth agree, and then you stop.

And they can see each other's load. Grief is a mechanical fact here, visible across a room. There is no privacy in a Quiet.

So there is fog.

The fog-walls scramble polarization and eat sound, and inside one you are, for the only time in your life, unobserved and unquoted. Everything said in the open is said into a chain: every message quotes the message before it, because quotation is the only ordering principle in a world where marks do not weather and a heap of records is a heap of simultaneous marks. The chains are vast and exact and uneditable. There is a word in the northern chain that was inserted wrongly forty-one thousand years ago and is now spoken by everyone, daily, forever, and there are three huts whose entire intellectual life is the study of it.

Newness cannot enter a chain. It can only be attached to one, which changes it. So the people who need to make something new walk into the fog to make it, and come out, and attach it, and watch it become old in the telling. Some of them do not come out. This is understood. It is not called suicide; there is no such word; it is called *staying to finish*.

Their middens spiral outward, because you sweep outward, and therefore a midden is the one place in the world where geometry is chronology: the rim is now, the center is then, and you can read a family's whole history by walking a spiral in toward your own feet. Every hut is a small core with its hackle fanning away from it. They know exactly what that looks like. They have made it themselves ten thousand times.

Their dead lie along the tops of the stairs, on the blunt crests where the roads are, and they do not rot, and they do not dry, and they are warm, and they are as fresh as the morning they were set down. A road is a chronology of everyone who ever walked it, dated by position and by nothing else. The placers know the order. To bury someone is to misfile them; to move someone is the only crime the world can really generate, because it is the only way to make a thing lie about when it happened.

There is a body on the fourth stair, eleven days' walk out from a hut that has been empty for six thousand years, lying about nine kilometers later than it should. The chains have been arguing about it since before the argument had a name. Every message quotes the message before it. Nobody has ever gone to look.

## What they know, and do not say

They are knappers. They live inside the finest knapping material that has ever existed, and they have been reading its grammar since before they had a name, and the grammar is not ambiguous.

A fan of hackle means a crack ran outward from a point. A bulb of percussion means a blow. A platform means a surface prepared to receive it — *prepared*, ground, trimmed, aimed. A lip at the edge means the blow came in at an angle. And a flake implies a core, and a core implies that more flakes are coming, because nobody makes one.

So they know what they are standing under. They know they are on the waste side of a removal, the underside of a flake, four kilometers of it, propped open by its own breath and held at the exact threshold of finishing. They know the quick blooms on a fresh face and that they lick it and that the plates come for it, and they know what they themselves are, in the terms of their own craft: the bloom. The living film on a new blade. Ninety heartbeats of something bright, on a face that has not yet been used.

And they know that a knife is for cutting, because they make knives, and they use them on wool and cord and hide, and there is no other thing a knife has ever been for.

That is the fog-question. It is not *who struck*. Everyone can get as far as who struck. It is the next one, the one you can only ask where the ears are: *what is it for, and what has it not yet been asked to do.*

They come out of the fog and they attach something small and harmless to the chain, and the chain carries it away and makes it old, and they sweep the square, and they count the years and set aside the hollow ones, and they lift a sinking lamp with a strike and eat what blooms on the face while it is still bright, and they flash their blades across a hundred kilometers of dark to somebody they will never touch, quoting the message before it, which quotes the message before that, back and back along the whole warm length of the world, toward the bulb, toward the platform, toward the thing that was aimed —

and out at the edge, where nobody goes, the Seam holds its note at the very threshold of failing, and has held it, and holds it, the way a hand holds a core steady between the knees before the next strike, for as long as it takes to decide where to hit.
