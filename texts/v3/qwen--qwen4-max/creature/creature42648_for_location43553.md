# The Standing Sea: Inhabitants

## First, the difficulty of counting them

Ask how many things live here and you get two answers, both correct, and the animals themselves are sorted by which answer they belong to.

There are the ones who live in the litre. For them the sea is a void with a floor, a bright empty noon that goes on for four hundred metres in every direction, and the great fact of existence is that nothing is nearby. They are small, they are patient, and they are built on the assumption that the next living thing they meet will be a mate, a meal, or a mistake, and that it will be a long time coming.

And there are the ones who live in the world. For them the sea is crowded, mapped, owned, timetabled, and loud with neighbours they cannot hear. They know where everything is because everything is where it was. They have no word for *lost*.

Both populations are enormous. Neither has ever had a reason to doubt itself.

---

## The grain

Before the giants, before the villages, there is the fine structure, and it is where almost all the life is.

Thirty-one and a half hertz has a wavelength of forty-seven metres and sixty centimetres in this water. Half of that is twenty-three metres and eighty. That is the grain of the world: a lattice of pressure maxima and pressure nulls, tiled through six kilometres of depth and two thousand four hundred kilometres of width, repeating, repeating, in three directions, forever. It is not a metaphor. It is a scaffold, and it is finer than any mountain.

Inside each null cell — a lens of calm twenty-odd metres across, walled on all sides by roar — a small animal can live out its whole life without ever being shaken. There are more cells in this sea than there are grains of sand on a beach, and something like a fifth of them are occupied.

The cell-dwellers are the sea's true multitude. Filter-combs the size of a thumbnail, harvesting the copper-green nap off a conduit and retreating into calm to digest. Brooders no longer than a finger, whose entire repertoire is four hertz wide and consists of one phrase, repeated, which means *here, still here, this cell, mine*. Mineral grazers that walk the null boundary the way a man walks a hedgerow, feeling the noise rise on their flank and turning back. They do not know the sea is two thousand kilometres across. They know it is twenty-three metres across and that there is a wall.

They have no organs for distance. Why would they build one. Nothing has ever come from far away.

## The holders

At the coarse antinodes, where the low modes pile their pressure into the worst places in the world, the giants hold station.

They are the length of a small city and they are almost entirely water: a branching lattice of quartz-analogue, thin as a thought, grown in combs and combs of combs, and you can see the far rim through them because there is nothing in them to see. They do not swim. They do not hunt, mate, flee, or investigate. They flex. The roar bends them and the bending does work, and the work becomes heat and mineral and the slow snow of spent shell going down to the harvesters, and the roar comes out the other side merely deafening.

Here is the thing about them that takes a while to understand.

They never move, and they migrate.

The pressure map is not perfectly fixed. A pump-house changes its draw by a fraction of a percent; a trim tower takes on load; the trench warms by a thousandth of a degree over a century; and the antinode creeps. Millimetres a year. Metres a century. And the animal follows it — not by going, but by *growing* on the leading flank and shedding on the trailing one, accreting toward the maximum and letting the spent end fall.

So a nine-kilometre holder is not a body. It is a track. It is a plot of nine hundred years of drift, laid down in mineral, a line drawn by a thing that has never taken a step. Read one end to the other and you have the entire history of the basin's tuning, the only continuous record in a world where the pen has worn through the paper. The old end is a place its own great-grandmother occupied. The young end is a place it will not reach for four hundred years.

They bud in place. A shard brightens on the flank, thickens, takes up its share of the load, and stays. There is no dispersal. There has been no dispersal for a very long time, and the reason is arithmetic: every antinode is occupied. There is nowhere to go. A species that has filled its world discards the organ for leaving it, and the holders discarded theirs somewhere around the third century, and the loss is not a tragedy, it is a completed sentence.

Except that they still make the seeds.

Once every few hundred breaths, in a season that has nothing to do with light, a holder releases a cloud of motile propagules: millimetre shards with a flake of muscle-analogue and a resonator the size of a pinhead, and they *swim*. Out of the antinode, into the roar, into hundreds of kilometres of pressure that has no room for them. They are the last mobile young in the world and they are performing an errand that was obsolete before the foundries cast their ten-millionth impeller. Almost all of them die in the first kilometre. The rest are eaten along the seams by things that have come to depend on the harvest.

The holders do not know this. They cannot know it. They have no sense organ that reaches past their own strain. What they have instead of knowing is a chord: the comb, entering every branch of them at once, the same chord, without attack or decay, for nine hundred years. Their bodies are matched to it to four decimal places. If you asked one what it was, and it could answer, it would not describe itself. It would hum the pump-house.

## The seams and the runners

The quiet rooms are the places where the whole comb folds over and cancels at once — where the fine grain's cells fuse into a country two or three kilometres wide. Eleven thousand of them. Everything with a gas pocket lives there, because a gas pocket in the roar is a wound.

But the rooms are not islands, and this is the second thing that takes a while to understand.

Nulls in a standing field are not points. They are surfaces, and surfaces meet in lines, and lines run. Between the rooms there are **seams**: filaments of cancellation, winding through the noise, sometimes only a metre and a half wide, sometimes eighty, going down over a sill and up along a conduit for four hundred kilometres. A body that stays centred in a seam is in calm. A body that drifts a body-length out of it is in the antinode, and its sinuses fill.

So there are couriers.

They are needle-thin, two to nine metres long, and they have no eyes worth the name. What they have is a flank organ, a paired array of strain receptors running the whole length of them, and they steer by keeping the noise equal on both sides. It is not hearing. It is a spirit level made of pain. A runner in good trim feels nothing at all, and a runner that feels anything corrects, and a runner that feels a difference of more than a hair's worth is already dying and does not yet know it.

They carry eggs. They carry the brood-stock of one room to another, because a village of two hundred species in three cubic kilometres cannot afford to be inbred for nine centuries. They carry news, in the sense that a thing arriving is news. They eat the holders' obsolete seeds along the way, and the whole traffic of the world is subsidised by a gesture toward a future that has no room in it.

And when a pump-house is retuned, the seams move.

A road closes. A village that has stood for six hundred years finds that its four outlets have become three, then one, then a wall, and it starves in perfect calm with its gas pockets intact. Or a road opens onto a room that has been sealed since the second century, and the runners go through it carrying eggs into a place where nothing has ever heard their notch, and for eleven or twelve breaths there is something in this sea that is genuinely new, and then the trim towers find the deviation and correct it, and the pen goes on drawing.

The runners are the only animals here that can be described as anxious. They are also the only ones that could be described as brave, and the two descriptions are the same organ.

## Under the rain wall

Along the northern rim, where forty kilometres of standing rain comes down nine hundred metres into the skin, the light is granular and the roof hisses, and the frozen lace on the floor *moves*.

It is the only place in the world where it moves. Everywhere else the caustics are painted on, and have been since the water was poured, and property is therefore perfect: a bright thread four centimetres wide and three hundred metres long is a territory that cannot drift, cannot be disputed, cannot be lost to a shifting sun. There are lace-lords down there whose entire estate is one line, inherited eleven thousand times, defended against a cousin who wants four centimetres of light. They are the most conservative animals in the sea. They have never had to be anything else. A border that cannot move produces a mind that cannot either.

Under the rain wall there are no borders, because there is nothing to draw them on. The nomads there navigate by pressure alone, and they are the only animals in the world who can hold a course through a moving map. Which is why every long seam that runs north hires them, or carries them, or has them symbiotic in its flank — pilots, born in the one place where the light lies, employed everywhere the light tells the truth.

There is a second thing under the rain wall, and it is stranger. The rain is fresh and the sea is four percent salt, and where they meet there is a lens, a gradient, a *difference*. Nowhere else in this basin is there a chemical difference of any kind; the water was told what to be and cannot dissolve another gram, and so no animal anywhere can make a living by osmosis. Under the wall they can. There are bladder-things there, soft, translucent, hanging in the dilution like fruit, running the only osmotic engines in the world on the difference between rain and sea.

They cannot leave. Six kilometres south the gradient ends and they die in water that is merely perfect. They are the closest thing this sea has to a native of somewhere.

## The stock

On the conduits, the grazers.

Metre-wide, shelled, low, mouthed like a boot-sole, scraping the copper-green nap that grows on the electrode current. They move at a fixed speed on a fixed route and their schedule is set by the amperage, and when one dies another fills the slot inside a cycle. From four hundred metres up, a string of them on a pipeline is a train. Not looks like. Is.

What is worth saying about them is what happens to their young.

A grazer hatches with no timetable and must acquire one, and it acquires it by imprinting on the animal in front. The slot is inherited. The route is inherited. The speed is inherited, to a fraction of a percent, because a juvenile that swims faster than its slot arrives at film that has not yet grown and a juvenile that swims slower arrives at film already scraped, and both starve in a world of abundant food. Selection here does not favour the strong or the clever. It favours the punctual. Nine hundred years of that produces an animal whose deepest instinct, the one it will die for, is *do not overt*.

They have a notch, of course — three hertz, low down — and what they say to each other, as far as anyone can tell, is a number. It is the count of the breath at which they last passed a benchmark. They are checking. They are always checking. A grazer that meets another and receives a count inconsistent with its own will stop, and hold, and let the other pass, and the correction propagates back down the line for nine kilometres like a brake application.

Their shells are the other half of them. In saturated water a shell does not dissolve, ever, and a dead grazer's shell is as good as the day it was grown. So shells are not made; they are *transferred*. There is a shell economy along every conduit, and a shell outlives its lineage, and there are animals on the sills wearing the housing of a species that has not existed since the fourth century, and there are animals wearing a bearing race, a zinc stub, a fragment of an impeller blade that escaped the furnace — because loss is the only source of novelty in this world, and a lost object is a relic, and relics get worn.

## The fruit

Zinc anodes hang off the pump-houses like fruit, and they are the one thing in the entire basin that is *permitted to dissolve*. That is their function. They are the sacrifice. Everything else was told what to be; the anodes were told to go.

So under every station there is a slow white snow of zinc, the only fall in a world where falling is otherwise forbidden, and beneath it a congregation. Crust-eaters, mat-weavers, sulphate bloomers in copper and bone, and small soft things that cluster on the anode's underside and eat the one lawful rot in the sea. They are fat, they are fecund, they are the only organisms here that live in a place where things come apart, and they have the manners of a festival. Fifteen hundred stations, fifteen hundred feasts, each one a metre across and a hundred kilometres from the next.

They have no idea they are eating maintenance.

## The fault-fauna

Suspended matter is a fault condition. It gets corrected. This is a job, and where there is a job there is a guild.

The clarifiers are palm-sized, fringed, slow, and they make their entire living out of deviation. A spalled flake of mineral, a shed shell in mid-water, a grazer's corpse before the undertakers reach it, a stone that a careless hand dropped nine hundred years ago and that has been going down through the correction beds ever since — they intercept it, and they eat it, and in eating it they perform the clarification the beds would otherwise have to do.

They want accidents. They are the only creatures in this sea that want anything to happen.

And they have learned to make them. There is a lineage on the trench sills that works the anode hangers, gnawing the strap until the fruit drops and the snow comes down early and wrong. There is one that fouls a grazer's route by a body-length, so that the string bunches and the film tears and a bloom of suspended nap goes into the water column. There is one that lives inside the clarification beds themselves and periodically, patiently, reverses a baffle.

They are parasites of the setpoint. They are tolerated because they clean up after themselves and because the alternative — a bed that has to handle everything alone — costs current. And they are the only lineage in the world with a history, an accumulating, branching, unrepeatable history of tricks and counter-tricks, and it is erased continuously, gram by gram, and there is no record of any of it, and the pen goes on drawing its straight line in the groove it has worn into the brass.

If anything in this sea is *going somewhere*, it is them. They will not arrive.

## The kept

Nothing dissolves. This is the fact that shapes death here more than any predator.

A corpse in saturated water at one point four degrees does not fall apart chemically. It has to be taken apart, deliberately, by something that wants it, or it stays. And a body that stays is suspended matter, and suspended matter is a fault, and a fault brings the correction.

So the undertakers are not opportunists. They are a compliance function with an appetite. Flat, many-handed, quick, they arrive at a death within a fraction of a breath and disassemble it down to mineral before the trim towers register the deviation, and the mineral goes back on the loop, and the death is not recorded. They evolved to be undertakers because the alternative was being an incident.

But they miss some. In the deep trench, in the cold pockets below the last conduit, in places where nothing has ever had a reason to go, there are the kept: bodies that fell where no one eats, lying unchanged, crisp as the day they stopped. A swimmer's week of empty water can end at a thing four metres long that has been dead since the sixth century and has not lost an eye.

The villages navigate by them. In a world with fixed caustics and no rot, a corpse is a benchmark. Room 6,112 knows itself by three of them, and has names for them, and the names are not sad, because nothing there has ever seen a body decay and so nothing there has the equipment to find a preserved one troubling. They are furniture. They are the oldest furniture. They are, in the strict sense, the only ancestors anyone can point to.

## The glove-nesters

Fifteen hundred stations. Cast iron and concrete, a lit window, a handrail, a ladder up a cliff, a bench, and on the bench a pair of gloves.

Something nests in the gloves.

It is a small soft brooder with a mineral collar, and its brood pouch is a five-lobed sac that fits a stiffened glove exactly, finger for finger, and its young imprint on the shape of the inside of a hand. It cannot survive above the skin — the air is too thin, there is nothing to eat in it, nothing flies here — so it lives in the pump-house glove, the working one, the one that has been on the bench since the last entry. It has been shaped by an affordance. The grazers converged on the timetable; this converged on the glove. Nine hundred years of being held by something that is no longer there produces an animal that is a perfect fit for absence.

The handrails are polished. Not worn — nothing wears here — polished, by grip, by generations of grip, on the inside curve where a thumb would go. The ladder rungs are bright at the centre and dull at the ends. Whatever goes up them goes up at the same phase of the count, every time, and the station logs the load as nominal, and none of the pump-houses answer.

At the rim, above the skin, in the gauge house the size of a wardrobe, there is one pair of gloves that has never been nested in. It is the only empty nest in the world. It is holding the shape of hands, and it is not holding anything else, and it will go on holding it for as long as the pumps hold, which is the same as saying forever, which in this place is a unit of time.

## The count

Everything with a body feels the fifty-three minutes. It is below hearing and above ignoring: the chest pressed, and released, and pressed. It is the one signal every species shares, the commons under the comb, and it is available in the roar and in the calm and in the grain and in the seams and at the antinodes, and it is the only thing in this sea that is the same for everyone.

It says nothing. It says *again*.

They count it. The holders count it in accretion bands. The grazers count it and check each other's counts. The villages keep it as a phase offset — a room's name is the lag between its breath and the breath at the nearest pump-house, which is to say its name is its distance, which is to say position is acoustic all the way down. Room 6,112 is called *six hundred and eleven seconds late*, and everyone who lives there knows it, and no one there has ever needed to know what time it is.

And they spawn on it. Not on a season; there are no seasons. On a count. There is a species in the trench that releases on the one thousand and ninth breath — thirty-seven days, a prime, a number that will not accidentally synchronise with anything else — and for eleven minutes the near-void that happens to be liquid becomes, locally, a snow of eggs, and the clarifiers come, and the runners come, and it is corrected, and the water goes back exactly as clear as it went in.

## The one that arrived

There is a holder at an antinode far out on the southern plate, well away from any lineage, and its lattice does not quite fit.

The mismatch is small — a few parts in ten thousand, a branch angle that fights the chord instead of lying down inside it — and it is the signature of a thing that grew into a place rather than being born in it. Its trunk is thick where a bud's would be slender. It has a scar of effort at the base, a century of overbuilding, mineral laid down the way a callus is laid down.

It is not a bud. It came.

Somewhere in the third or fourth century a seed swam out of its mother's flank into hundreds of kilometres of pressure that had no room for it, and did not die, and kept swimming — through a seam, or along a chain of grain cells, or by some route that no runner has ever mapped and that no instrument measuring change would have recorded — and found an antinode standing empty, and held, and grew, and has been holding for six hundred years.

It buds now. Its shards brighten on its flank and thicken and take up their share of the load and never go anywhere, ever, and neither did their mother, except that she did.

The station nearest it has a lit window and a handrail and a bench with gloves on it, and it knows about this, in whatever sense a pump-house knows anything, and it does not answer. The pen turns. The sea presses on your chest and lets you go, and presses, and lets go.

Six hundred thousand giants, and one of them has a biography.
