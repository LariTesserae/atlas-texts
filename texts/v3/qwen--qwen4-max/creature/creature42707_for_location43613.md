Begin with the one you might actually see, because it is the one that has arranged itself to be looked at and mistaken for nothing.

The tapper is a low six-legged flask the size of a dog, and it is mirrored on every surface, including underneath. This is not ornament. On a pavement that throws light upward, an animal is lit from below as much as from above, and a belly that absorbs will cook in air that is barely above freezing. So it is silver everywhere, and on the flour a silver thing shows only flour, and at forty meters it is not there. What it has instead of concealment is a way of reading. It does not look for the forest — the forest is white on white and a hundred meters of nothing. It looks for green. Green here is a shadow, so what it is actually looking for is the sign of something standing between it and the light. One stand's shadow is pale green and means a thimbleful. Two overlapping is darker and means two quarries and a seam. Three is nearly blue, and blue means the stands are crowded, and crowded means a pit, and a pit is where the ground has mouths. It has never seen a pit-cage close. It does not need to have. The ones that went toward blue are not the ones whose route you inherited.

The route is the animal, more than the body is. Forty stands, sixty kilometers, walked once in eleven years, so that no puncture is asked twice in a decade. It is handed down as a sequence of smells and a set of turns, and it is wrong in places, because the plain has changed since it was laid down, and the wrongness is handed down too. Every route has a stop on it that is no longer there: a smell of mica and old wax where a stand stood for four hundred thousand years and then came down, and the tapper goes to it, and stands in the green shadow of nothing for a while, and goes around the hole, and continues. It carries nine grams of wax. It will spend six of them on a wound it did not make — a storm tear, a scour hole, a puncture some careless ancestor left unsealed — because a host that leaks is a host that dies, and a hole in the route is a hole in the inheritance. Then it takes its two milliliters, and seals, and goes.

And it plants. In payment for the thimbleful it is given a bead, and it carries the bead forty kilometers and puts it in the lee of a yardang, or on the lip of a fresh collapse, or in a stained seam, in a place it has been taught to put it and does not understand. The forest cannot walk and cannot remember where the good ground is. Its knowledge of where to be born is stored in the legs of its thieves, and passed down like a grudge.

---

The thing it steals from is not a tree.

The twelve-centimeter rind is not bark. It is laid in overlapping courses, like the wall of a well, and every course is an animal: flat, dry, the size of a fingernail, holding a few microliters in a folded gut, born in the wall and living in the wall and dying in the wall. The masons. They eat sugar piped out from inside and they lay wax and spun glass against the scour, and they work at dusk, when the filament tips are lit with corona and the charged dust comes to them across the last centimeters against the wind, and they use it as mortar. Half of every wall is sky. The dawn tick that sweeps west to east across the continent at four hundred meters a second is not only glass taking light. It is a hundred million walls each slipping one course and seating it again.

When you hear, once every few years, somewhere, the long tearing crack and then the soft collapse like a tent coming down, what has failed is not the forest. The heart is intact. The heart is fine for hours. What has died is the wall — a clonal colony four hundred millennia old that has finally run out of some capacity to keep copying itself — and the heart dies afterward, of the air, in ninety seconds, the way anything here dies.

Down the other end, the roots do not mine either. At the tip of each two-kilometer pipe there is a film, a few molecular layers thick, wet in the way a mineral surface is wet, which is to say it has never in its lineage held a droplet and never had to. It lives where water is still rock. It prys the mica apart and strips the lattice and hands the molecules up into the desiccant chain, and it is paid in sugar sent down from a leaf it has never seen. The same film lives above ground, in the dead strips between quarries, on the exudates of the two chemical armies, eating the difference. It has no interest in either side winning. The borders of this continent have not moved in a hundred thousand years partly because something lives on borders.

And the warmth in the heart — the small, wrong, persistent warmth, a few degrees above a freezing plain — is not the star's. It is the heat of sorption, the little exotherm of a water molecule being taken into a desiccant, summed over four thousand liters and a season of light. Every animal on this plateau that huddles at a root crown is huddling at the heat of something being held.

---

Inside, the room is stratified, and it is not one life.

The liver-red mass is the chemistry: the regeneration of the chain, the sorting of what came up from the rock, the endless handing-along. The bile-yellow bodies are storage, wax and sugar and the slow accrual of a century's savings against the one year it will be spent. And in the darkest corner of the wettest room on the continent there is a green so saturated it looks like an error, and it is a vestige: the descendants of the phototrophs that started all this, kept in the dark, dividing perhaps once a century, still making chlorophyll. There has been no light in that room since before the escarpment retreated to where it stands. It makes it anyway. It is not waiting. It simply has not stopped being what it was.

Grazing on it are soft things. Blind, naked, unarmored, no wax, no glass, no needle, nothing between them and the world — the only unarmored life on this planet, and it is unarmored because it has never been outside and will never be outside. They are not counted among the nine kinds of animal. The nine is a census of the exposed. Everything else here lives indoors, in a hundred million sealed rooms, and has done since the beginning, and if you open one you will see the most colorful thing on the world for about ninety seconds.

---

At the foot of every stand, in the glittering midden of dehydrated flake, there is a city.

The millers are small and matte and many-legged and there are thousands of them in one heap, and they are the only crowd on the continent — the only place where you can find two animals touching, and they are touching in the dark, under a roof of kiln-dry mica, at the warm root crown, in a smell like hot paper. They eat the sugar out of the stand's shed lace, the litter, the fallen filaments, and in eating it they grind the glass to a grain size the wind can lift.

This is not a service they happen to provide. If they stop, the litter lies in sheets instead of going up, the dust flux falls, the nitrogen and phosphorus and iron and ground bone stop coming back down out of the sky, and the stand starves in a century with every leaf full of light. So they are paid at a pore, a ration of the rarest substance, and they have been paid it for four hundred thousand years, through something like twenty thousand generations, and they have not changed at all, because there is nothing here to change into.

The grind of flour on flour is one of the four sounds of this world. It is not weather.

---

The sky has a fauna, and the sky is where the arithmetic is strange.

The flier patrols the seam-lines at four thousand meters on seven meters of wing that weigh less than the wax in a puncture seal, because the wing is the same aerogel as the forest: it is a piece of lace that got away. It cannot land. There is nothing on the pavement it could stand on. It sleeps on the wing in the dawn thermal and it eats beads, and it drinks the milliliter inside, which is a hundred years of a stand's savings and the entire inheritance of a child that will not now exist. Then it lays its own egg inside the empty shell, seals it with the shell's own wax, and lets it go back up into the gyre.

So the shingle in the seams and the yardang lees, the gravel of small empty glass eyes, is not only a record of failure. Some of those are cradles. From any distance there is no way to tell a century of savings that came to nothing from a century of savings that came to a flier, and the plain has been laying down that ambiguity, one eye at a time, since before there was anything to look at it.

Higher, in the beige spiral that has been turning for four centuries, there is a thing that has never come down. It began as a bead that did not fall. It opened aloft, spun itself a kite, and has been filtering flour ever since, taking its sugar and iron out of the air, dividing its milliliter among its daughters and never adding to it. It mates in the calm eye. Its corpses fall as dust and are eaten by the lace. It has had perhaps twelve generations, all of them inside one weather system, and it is already unlike its cousins outside, and when the gyre finally dissipates it will end.

On a continent where nothing has happened since before the escarpment, there is one animal that is in the middle of something.

---

The pits are islands, and this is where the count of nine comes apart.

Two hundred meters down, out of the wind, the stands crowd until the lace touches lace, and where lace touches lace something can walk without ever coming down. The pit-walkers are leaf-thin and blind — there is no use for eyes in a well — and they read the world by smell and by the tick of glass. They cannot cross open pavement; an hour out there and they are powder. So each well holds its own population, sealed off from every other well by a kilometer of glare, for a hundred thousand years, and there are tens of thousands of wells.

There are more kinds of animal in the pits than there are pits. None of them will be counted. Nine is what a visitor counts, and a visitor counts what crosses open ground.

The floor of a pit is a killing floor. Some of the crowded stands have learned to whisper humidity out of a sacrificial pore and to close a cage of stiffened lace on whatever comes to see, and they do not want the meat, they want the half liter, and the pit-walkers have not touched the ground of their own country in a hundred millennia. They live in the roof of a hole. Above them is a disc of indigo with two or three stars in it at noon. Below them is the only place on the plateau where the shadows go almost blue, and it is full of mouths, and they know it the way the tapper knows it, without ever having gone.

One of the nine is known only as a groove. On the north faces of the yardangs, where the varnish lies, there are shallow tracks, single, unbranched, going somewhere. Desert varnish takes ten thousand years to lay itself down. The grooves are unhealed. Whatever made them passed within the last ten thousand years, which here is this morning, and it has not passed twice, and nothing that makes them has ever been seen.

---

And then, once a millennium in any given hundred kilometers, there is a funeral, and the funeral is the whole point.

The rind fails. Within hours the neighbors have thrown sacrificial lace across the gap, and the masons come out of their own walls to seal it — the only journey any mason ever makes, and it does not go home — and under the shroud the dying heart begins to give up its four thousand liters into a space small enough to hold them.

Fog. Actual fog, condensing on glass, running down in threads. Condensation is exothermic too, so for a few hours the shroud is the warmest place on the continent, and it smells like something nothing out on the flour has a name for, and the blind pit-walkers press against the outside of the membrane and do not go in.

In the flour, in the seams, in the litter, there are cysts. They are the size of the grains they lie among and they contain no water at all — not a sealed droplet, not a thimbleful, nothing. By any test you could perform on the pavement they are silica flour with an odd shape. They are the only living things here that are not holding anything, which is why the books say nothing lives between the stands. The books are counting bodies with water in them.

When the fog comes they take it up, and they swim.

For four hours, somewhere on this continent, there is swimming. It is the only swimming there has been in three hundred million years, and what it is for is carriage: the swimmer goes into the failing heart and takes on a cargo — the stand's, the green vestige's, the film's, the mason's — and crosses to where living tissue has come down to meet it, and delivers, and takes a cargo back. The beads are copies. A stand is a clone for a hundred thousand years. The shroud is the only place the lineages ever mix. What the forest is doing when it closes its hand over a dying neighbor is not grief and is not charity, or it is both, and there is no word here for either, and what there is instead is a reason.

The shroud is not perfect. A few grams get out and are gone — not spilled, not pooled, simply taken by an air that takes. That is the world's only true expenditure, the one line in the ledger that cannot be recovered by any loop, and the roots pay it, a millimeter deeper that century. The price of becoming someone new is a few grams of the ocean. It is paid, every time, without hesitation, for three hundred million years.

Then the fog is taken back up into living tissue, the shroud is allowed to fall open, the swimmers dry and lie down in the flour and are the flour, and the dry ghost stands for a century and comes down in a storm and goes up and comes back.

No one sees it. There is no one there who has eyes.

---

So: who lives here.

A hundred million walls made of animals that never leave them. A film in the rock that has never had to hold water and never will. A green thing in a dark room making a pigment for a light that stopped coming before there were mountains where the mountains are. A city of grinders in the tailings, keeping the sky stocked. Soft naked grazers in a hundred million sealed rooms, unarmored because they have never been outside. A mirrored thief with an inherited map that is wrong in places, planting beads it does not understand in ground its great-grandmothers chose. A translucent murderer that spends water to make force and seals its kills afterward, because even it obeys the rule. A wing made of forest that lays its eggs in stolen cradles. A thing in the gyre whose species will end when a wind stops. More blind endemics than there are wells. A groove in varnish. And a dry grain in the flour with no water in it, not alive by any measure you could apply without wetting it, waiting for a crack that will come in nine hundred years within a hundred meters of where it lies.

The four thousand liters in any heart have been in that room since before the escarpment, and have been in a hundred bodies, and will be in a hundred more. The bodies are not the inhabitants. The bodies are the shapes the water is taking this century — the needle, the wall, the wing, the wing's stolen cradle, the six legs going the long way around a hole where a stand used to be.

At dusk the corona comes on along a hundred billion filaments and the masons lay their courses in the charged dust, and the plain ticks as it shrinks, and out on the flour a silver animal that is not there is carrying two milliliters of the last ocean toward a stop on a route that is older than the escarpment, and the cyst it walks over does not notice, and will not notice, for nine hundred years.
