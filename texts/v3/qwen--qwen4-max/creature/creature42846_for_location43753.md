Nothing lives in the Kettle, and that is not an absence in the record. It is the record. The sterility is not a condition the room suffers; it is the room's output, the thing it makes besides stone. And still there is one resident, and it is not an exception to the emptiness. It is the cause of it.

The crews never named it. They had a sign for it, though — flat hand, palm down, lifted twice, fingers loose — which on the slate got written as *still making*, and which was used at the top of the ladder in place of a greeting. You came up soapy to the wrists and you gave the man your disc and you made the sign, and he nodded, because of course it was. Nobody thought of it as a creature. It was a rate. A centimeter a year is not an animal; it is a line item. But it has a body, a boundary, a metabolism, a direction of travel, a way of responding to weather, a method of reproduction, and an age, and it is dying at exactly the speed at which it grows, and if that is not a life then the word is doing less work than we ask of it.

---

Its body is a wall, and the wall is moving north.

The drinking face is where unslaked lime meets the wet sand, and it is a meter or so further along every decade, and it is the only part of the thing that could be called a mouth. It does not bite. It wets. The heat comes off it in a slow exotherm that has no peak and no trough, fifty-odd degrees at the working surface, hotter in the core, and the heat is not a symptom of the process, it *is* the process, felt from outside. Behind the face, the fill is spent: cool, sweetening, already becoming ordinary rock, already forgetting it was ever hungry. The survey bolts set in the hot wall are four meters behind the front now. Two of them are inside the deposit. They were not removed. They were included.

Above the face is the fracture network, which is its vasculature, and it has no heart. Steam goes up it because steam goes up. At the top is the lid, which is its skin and its condenser and its only cold organ, and on the underside of the lid the water beads and grows and lets go, and the rain falls back down through the fog to the floor and wicks down through the jigsaw to the sand and goes to the face again. One circulation, closed, unassisted, older than anyone's employment here. The same water, over and over, getting hungrier each pass and then a little less hungry, forever.

And out of the ceiling, the needles: thousands of hollow straws, all lengthening at once, each one a receipt. This is the part that looks like growth and is actually spending. Every centimeter of translucent calcite hanging in the dark above the fog is causticity that will never be caustic again. The thing builds its skeleton out of its own appetite. It cannot make a gram of itself without becoming a gram less able to.

---

It responds. Slowly, but it responds, and this is the closest it comes to behavior.

Rain on the apron vanishes downward and does not arrive for days. When it arrives the face drinks harder and the heat climbs a fraction and the drip chorus thickens — a lag of about ninety hours between weather on the salt flat and a change in the chord under the floor. Nobody who worked here ever heard the whole event. You would need to stand in the room for four days to hear one sentence of it, and you get eleven minutes.

The drumheads are its doing. Where the drip strikes a hollow it lays down a sheet, and the sheet thins and tightens as it grows, and its pitch rises as its thickness falls, so the floor is an instrument that tunes itself continuously in one direction, very slowly, toward a note it will reach and then break through. The rain plays it. The rain has played it every minute since before the works closed and has never once repeated a bar. There is no listener. There is one pocket near the west wall where the shriek falls away and a man could hear the small crystalline tick of the crust getting thicker, which is the sound of the thing doing the only verb it has, and men ate their lunch there, eleven minutes of it, with a wall of sound standing a meter off like a held door.

The vent is not part of it. That matters. The air main charged in 1974 is the one resident of the room that is not alive by any stretch — it does not gust, does not vary, does not answer weather, does not tire. It is a hundred-decibel constant, a material, and the living part has grown around it the way a tree grows around a fence post: the tipped slabs make nodal shadows, the sheets tune themselves in a room whose fundamental is a shriek, the quiet pocket exists because of where the sound is not. The Kettle has arranged its whole interior around a noise it cannot feel and will one day strangle.

---

What it does with objects is the part that people photograph, and the part that is most easily misread.

It does not preserve. Preservation is a subtractive world's idea — you keep a thing by stopping the rot, and here there is no rot to stop. What it does is copy. Water arrives, dissolves, climbs, condenses, falls, and lays the dissolved load down on the nearest surface, over and over, a centimeter a year, until the surface has a rind and the rind has the shape of the thing and the thing itself is gone out of the middle of it. The glove's leather did not survive. The glove's *outside* survived, in stone, perfectly, down to the frayed cuff and the stitching, and inside the perfect fingers there is a hollow the exact size and shape of an absence.

That is the whole of its memory. It remembers only the outsides of things. It has no inside to remember with — one room, two pockets you cannot get your shoulders into, needles hollow as drawn glass, a wheelbarrow that is now a smooth hill with two rust blisters for legs. Everything here is a rind around a void. Five objects, and five voids, and a great deal of bare stone between them, and the emptiness is not the background. The emptiness is the collection.

It has one blind spot. Aluminum it eats without copying — quietly, fizzing hydrogen — and the west wall ladder is gone entirely, leaving a line of steel bolts standing out of a white scar and a pale ghost of an outline where the runoff stained. That is the only thing in the room it ever forgot, and it forgot it by digesting it, and the shape of the forgetting is still on the wall.

It has no favorites. It cannot tell a glove from a hand. Given a hand it would render the knuckles and the cuticle and the small scar somebody got in 1981 with the same flawless indifference, and it would not know it had done anything, because knowing requires an inside. The soap on your skin at the threshold is not an attack. It is the beginning of a very good likeness. The rule is not *don't get eaten*. The rule is *don't get included*.

---

It reproduces the way a drip reproduces: by making more places for itself to happen.

Every straw that lengthens establishes a new drip point on the floor beneath it, and every new drip point starts a mound, and every mound starts a sheet, and the sheets bridge and the bridging makes new hollows and the hollows make new drumheads and new pockets of quiet. Behind the front, the fill settles and the void migrates; the slab tips another few millimeters and the slot widens and more ordinary rain gets in, and more carbon dioxide with it. It opens its own door wider as it fills. It has been doing this since the last truck tipped in 1998 and it will keep doing it after the last person who knows the hand sign is dead.

And it is aging in the only way available to it. Carbon dioxide takes the bite out of the alkali and lays the excess down as stone; every needle, every rind, every fluted rung is spent appetite. In a century the vent will have choked on its own deposit and the shriek will drop to a hiss and then to a drip, and the silence will be the loudest event in the room's history. In three hundred years the pH falls past the point where a moss can hold, and the moss will hold, and the contour that has stood two meters above the floor for as long as anyone has measured will start to climb down the wall, and the queue that has been waiting outside the slot the entire time — cyanobacteria, saltbush, three ferns, the fig with its roots in the sweet lens, and behind them everything with legs — will come in.

The Kettle is not a place where life failed. It is a place where life has been postponed by chemistry, and the postponement is the resident, and the resident is spending itself to keep the appointment.

---

Here is the truest way to say it, and it took the whole survey to arrive at.

The thing that lives in the Kettle is the thing that will fill it.

Not a creature in a room — a casting in a mold, where the mold and the casting are the same body and the room is only the temporary hollow in the middle of an object still being laid down. Every year the hollow is a centimeter smaller in every direction. Every year the ceiling comes down a centimeter and the floor comes up a centimeter and the walls close in, sharp-edged, unworn, nothing subtracted, all of it added, and one day the hollow will be gone and there will be no room at all under the apron, only a solid white block in the shape of everything that was ever left down there: a wheelbarrow-shaped void, a bench-shaped void, a glove with perfect fingers and nothing in them, a standpipe, a pump, a ladder that is a fluted column, and up at the top, fused into one loaf with eleven bumps, the count of the men who went in and came out.

That is the adult form. It is growing toward it at a centimeter a year, in the dark above the fog, thousands of tips at once, and it has no inside to want it with.

Until then it does what it does. Hot rain in a closed room. A bar of ordinary sun crossing a white glove at the usual speed. A shriek that has not paused since 1974. And six hours apart, eleven minutes of a pump moving water nobody needs moved — the only pulse in the place, and not its own, a habit still running in a body that stopped needing it, keeping time for something that has never once looked at the clock.
