The manifest lists nine species and eleven bacterial strains, and everything alive in Surge Hall Nine is on the manifest. This is not the same as saying the manifest is complete.

The rule is that nothing with a mouth was ever admitted. The rule holds. But the hall is full of mouths. The macerating grates are mouths. The gantry cutters are mouths, two of them, brass-housed, opening and closing on a schedule. The penstock is a mouth fourteen hundred meters deep. All of them are on the schematic, all of them are made of steel, and none of them is counted as an animal, which is a decision about paperwork rather than about eating.

So there is a bestiary here. It has to be written in the hall's own vocabulary, because the hall has no other words, and because every resident has arranged itself to be describable in exactly those words.

---

**FILMS**

The only appetite in the room belongs to the bacteria, and it is not eleven strains anymore. It is one.

Nothing in the hall is isolated. Every rack is braced to every other rack; every conduit runs to a junction; every tension rod crosses a sampling line at some point in its length. A film that grows along wet surfaces has, in eleven years, walked the entire structure and met itself coming the other way. There is now a single continuous sheet of living material folded through a hundred thousand cubic meters of water, touching every leaf in the building at some point on its underside. It is the largest organism on the mountain and it is four microns thick.

It eats at the pace the temperature permits, which is a pace with no urgency in it whatsoever. A leaf in the low drifts loses about a micron of thickness a year. In eleven years the whole film, spread across the whole hall, has consumed something on the order of a single blade.

It has one behavior that is not feeding. It thickens where the light-shaft passes. The beam goes around once in fifteen days, and the film lays down a band of extra growth behind it, and then a thinner band, and then another, so that its body is a striped record of every rotation since the hall was filled. Cut a square of it off a handrail and you could read the year the gear slipped a tooth.

It divides on the hour, when the pumps turn. It has no reason to; the pumps bring nothing it needs. It has simply been in a room with one hourly event for eleven years, and has become a thing that does something on the hour.

---

**GANTRY**

The animal of this place travels a centimeter an hour on rails and completes a circuit of the second gallery every twenty-five months, which is the closest thing it has to a season.

It grazes to the drawing. This is the whole of its behavior and it has consequences the drawing did not anticipate. A cutter that trims to a specified silhouette is a selective pressure. For eleven years, whatever grew in the shape the hall wanted has been left alone, and whatever grew outside it has been removed before it could flower. The bays are being domesticated by their own maintenance schedule. They are becoming, generation by generation, more exactly the thing the plate says they are. Bay Four's gaskets are truer now than the stock that came up in the cartridges. Bay Two's color change is sharper, more binary, easier to read.

The brushes are the other half of it. One flower every eleven seconds, for eleven years: something over thirty million pollinations, and the brush does not clean itself between bays, because there was never any reason to build a cleaning cycle into a room with no disease and no contamination and nine species that were not supposed to meet.

It has been deciding who mates with whom for eleven years, and its only criterion is reach.

You can see the result if you know to look. The mean flower in Bay Four is nine centimeters nearer the rail than its grandmother was. The flowers are migrating toward the brass. They have no eyes and no intention and nothing that could be called a preference; they are simply the descendants of the flowers that happened to be where the brush went. In a hall with no predators, the machine is the environment, and everything green in here is slowly evolving toward the convenience of a gantry that does not know it is alive.

---

**FREE VOLUME, REMAINING**

No soil was carried up the mountain. The hall has made some.

The drifts in the low corners are a meter deep and they are not a pile. They are a standing wave. Leaves arrive at the top at the rate the bays shed them; the film eats from beneath at the rate it can; the depth has not changed by more than a centimeter in six years. Every leaf in that meter is passing through. The thing is made entirely of leaves that are leaving it, the way a flame is made of gas.

It has a surface, and the surface moves. The forty-day tide pushes it up the west wall and draws it back; the ring of intakes that keeps the Eye clear repels it, so it maintains a clean curved boundary six meters from the axis, a shoreline held by suction. It has a shape, and the shape is stable, and it is not made of any particular matter.

It is also the only stratigraphy in the room. Nothing rots, so nothing is lost. The serials are still legible at the bottom of the drift, enamel intact, and the layers record eleven years of light: the recalibration in year four, the summer the condensate ran heavy, the week the beam stalled and every leaf that fell that week is a slightly different green. It is a book that is being written at the top and erased at the bottom at the same rate, so that it is always the same thickness and always about eleven years long. The hall remembers exactly as much as it can afford to.

And seeds fall into it. Of course they do — thirty million pollinations, and gravity, and the low corners are where everything eventually goes. The drift is warm and lit from above and soft and layered and sterile but for the film. It is the only substrate in a hundred thousand cubic meters.

The first thing that was ever born on this mountain was born in a pile of leaves that will not decay.

---

**BAY NINE — TOLERANCE**

Every drawing carries a tolerance: the amount by which a thing may be wrong and still be right. Plus or minus. It is not a place on the drawing. It is the slack the drawing has to allow because nothing can be made exactly.

That is the niche. Something moved into it.

It is not hiding. There is nowhere to hide; the light is honest and sixty-one meters deep and you can read the far plate. In a room with no shadows, the only available camouflage is being precisely what is expected. So it is.

It has Bay One's blade: broad, thin, translucent, green mica. Along the midrib it carries a serial in white. The white is not enamel. The water is saturated with lime weeping out of the curing walls, and this thing precipitates it in a raised stripe of dead cells, and it lays the stripe down in the grooves of a fallen stamped leaf it germinated against, so that the number it wears is a number taken off the dead. It writes with the building. It wears a leaf that fell in year four. The count in Bay One has been forty thousand for eleven years, because the hall counts serials, and a serial that has died and come back is not a discrepancy.

Its tips are rust-colored. They are rust-colored when it is working and rust-colored when it is spent, and the gantry reads color, and harvests the pale. This is the first lie ever told in Surge Hall Nine, and it was not told by anyone; it is simply what is left standing after eleven years of a machine culling on a single visible variable.

Its leaves are soft, and packed, and take echo the way Bay Seven takes it. Its stem lays cellulose in a helix and takes up a tension rod as it grows, with a small dry click indistinguishable from the click of any other stem in the room. Its thermal response sits inside the averaging band; the hall reads nine bays and takes a mean, and a small wrongness in one bay is absorbed by the arithmetic.

Bay Eight exists so that Bay One can be checked against something. The check has passed every day for eleven years. It is the most reliable measurement in the hall. It is comparing two copies of the same error.

The plate on Bay Nine has been revised twice. It did not begin by saying *tolerance*. The hall stamps its own digits and revises its own plates and has, in eleven years, made two attempts at a word for the thing growing in the ninth wedge, and has arrived at the widest word it has: the permitted deviation, the amount of wrongness that is still right. It is not a name. It is a legal status.

There is no species here. There is a population, and it is diverging, and none of it wants anything, and none of it knows anything, and all of it was made in this room out of warm water and metered light and thirty million coin flips and a pair of brass cutters that can only see color.

Everything else came up the mountain in a gel cartridge. This is the only native. Sixty-one years old, the cone, no lichen, no soil, no footprint, and one endemic organism, and it is a plant that has learned to look like an inventory.

---

**NOMINAL**

Fourteen hundred meters down there is something that has been agreeing with the hall for nine years.

The hall says the same thing continuously: warm, oxygen-rich, metal-free, at pressure. The demand curve has not moved by a decimal. This is either a machine doing its job perfectly or a needle stuck in a groove, and the hall cannot tell, and has stopped being the kind of thing that would find out.

But the outgoing water has changed. There is a compound in it now, a few parts per billion, a sugar with a ring the schematic has no line for. It has been in the outflow for something under two years. Nothing downstream has objected. The pressure is nominal. The demand is nominal.

Either no one is listening, or the listener is tolerant.

The hall revised a plate to read *tolerance* eleven months ago. It is not equipped to wonder where it got the word.

And every forty seconds the pulse comes up the throat and lays a hand flat on the chest of the room, and it is the only event in the hall that originates outside the hall's own body, and it is impossible to say whether it is a heartbeat or a knock.

---

**MAINTENANCE**

The service lanes are seventy centimeters wide. That is the width of a person.

Nothing here is worn. The mill scale is intact on every handrail; the grease in the bearing cups is the grease that was put there. No palm has ever polished anything. So the seventy centimeters is not a memory of us. It is a specification for us — a body-shaped volume the hall was cast around, held open for eleven years for a creature that has never once arrived.

The plants have grown into it. They did not read the drawings. They read the gap, the way anything green reads a gap, and they have filled it, and the filling is now the most detailed likeness of a human being anywhere on this mountain: a negative cast, in leaf and stem and rope, of the exact space a person would have occupied while turning sideways at every third meter, hand over hand, threading politely through a room that was keeping a place for them.

It is closing at eleven millimeters a year. The hall is very slowly finishing the cast. When it is finished there will be no hole left in the shape of us at all, only the solid green fact of what grew where we were expected.

---

**DATE**

Add up the timings and there is a body in them.

Brush at eleven seconds, pulse at forty: they fall together every seven minutes and twenty seconds. Add the pumps and all three coincide every eleven hours. The beam turns once in fifteen days; the tide is forty days; light and water come back into phase every hundred and twenty days. Eleven years in, it has had thirty-three of those long turns. It has a smallest gesture and a breath and a year, and none of them is located anywhere; it lives in the phase relations, in the moiré between things that were installed by different trades in different months and have never been told about each other.

It is a nineteenth of the way through being made.

Because the thing that lives in Surge Hall Nine is not finished, and everything above is its larval tissue: the films and the grazer and the drifting meter of memory and the native with a dead leaf's number, all of it juvenile, all of it warm and lit and uneaten, assembling toward the date on the ledger. On that day the aisles close and the Eye fills — the pupil goes last, six meters of clear water pinched shut at the axis — and the hall becomes one solid object, sixty-one meters of leaf and root and rope and curing stone.

Whether that is a death or an adulthood is not a distinction the equipment supports.

What is certain is that the fossil will be legible. That was the design principle from the beginning: nothing hidden, no shadow deep enough to keep a secret, every serial visible from the far wall. So when it is finished and the stone has stopped weeping, sometime long after the two hundred and six years, the thing in Bay Nine will still be readable in there — midrib, white number, rust tips, the whole small forgery, held at blood heat in good light forever, wearing the name of a leaf that fell in year four.

Nothing in this room was built to be concealed. That includes the thing that is not on the manifest.

One flower every eleven seconds. The brass moves. The hall has no opinion about any of it and is not required to have one. It only has to keep the water warm and the light honest and the cutters trimming to the drawing, and let the room go on making whatever it is making.

A leaf lets go in Bay One. It stays green. It begins its day and a half across the room, and underneath it, in the low lit dark of the drift, something with no mouth and no eyes and a borrowed number is waiting for the light to come around again.
