## The Residents

Start with the felt.

It is the least of the room: a millimetre of the same pale stuff pressed flat into the laminated silt, going under the blocks and up their sides and over the oak stubs in the stair sockets, unbroken, unremarkable, the colour of the skin that forms on milk and is lifted off with a spoon. Nobody looks at it. It is what the floor is wearing.

It is also the whole animal, lying down.

Everything above it is the same tissue standing up. The bootlace stalks that hold the metre-wide fans come out of the felt without a join. The curtains are the ceiling's felt, hanging. The tiers are the wall's felt, folded out into the room and folded again, each fold a hand's breadth clear of the last, and at the margins where the ceiling comes down to your chest they all run together into a single continuous ruffle with no seam anywhere in it. Put a lamp behind a sheet and the veins show; follow the veins back and they take you into the wall, into the floor, into the next fan, into the felt, into everything.

And where two of them touch, they fuse. There is no bark here, no cuticle, no boundary tissue, nothing that says *this is me and that is not*. Contact is union. So the answer to *how many things live in the larder* is one, and the answer is not a poetic convenience. It is one because everything in the room has, at some point in the last three thousand years, touched everything else.

---

But one is wrong too, and wrong in the other direction.

The fissure in the basin floor is two centimetres wide and goes down and away, and the water that leaves through it carries fragments — a scrap of sheet, a spore, a thread. Above, the same pale tissue lines the grikes in the pavement under the sheep grass, thin as a breath, and the bedding planes for a hundred metres around, and the wet cracks under the drystone wall, and the inside of the solution pipe that was a stair. It is in the hill the way damp is in a wall. Where the gradient is poor it is a film you would scrape off with your thumbnail without knowing you had done anything. Where the gradient is good it stands up.

The larder is not where this thing lives. The larder is the best room of something that lives in the hill, and it is extravagant here for the same reason a tree is extravagant at a woodland edge: this is where the difference is. Forty cubic metres of interface, more surface than the field above, all of it a single edge held open against the dark. The rest of the organism is a rumour in the cracks, going about the same business at a tenth of the rate, and will still be going about it when the hawthorns are gone.

---

What it is like to be it:

Three cells thick. That is the whole body, everywhere — the great fan, the curtains, the felt, all of it a membrane three cells deep with plumbing let into it, and the plumbing is the veins and the veins carry sulphide up and iron down and nothing else, because there is nothing else to carry. It has no wood. It has no bark, no rind, no pith, no heartwood, no core. Every cell in it is within a hair's breadth of the water or the air. There is no inside to this animal. You cannot stab it. You cannot get to the middle of it, because it is all middle, or all edge, and the distinction has never come up.

It holds itself up by being cold. The tissue is stiff at four tenths of a degree — stiff the way the fat on a dripping-tin is stiff — and its whole architecture, the stalks, the flutes, the brackets, the metre-wide fans on bootlaces, is a bet that the cold will continue. Warm this room by five degrees and it does not die dramatically. It sags. The fans come down onto the tiers below them, and the curtains lie along the floor, and the room becomes a heap of wet paper, and stays a heap of wet paper, because nothing here eats and so nothing here clears up.

That is the entire available apocalypse. It is very mild, and it has not happened.

---

It has six verbs.

*Lean.* Toward warmth, along the isotherms, at a rate that is visible in an hour if you know where to look and unmistakable in a week. The lean is the whole of its behaviour and it is not a decision; it is differential thickening on the warm side, four millimetres a year distributed unevenly, and it is as much a choice as a river's bend. But it is *directed*, and direction is the beginning of everything we usually mean by attention. Hold your palm a hand's width from a frond and it will come to you. Not for you. Toward the two or three degrees you are leaking into the room. The distinction matters and cannot be felt from inside an hour.

*Thicken.* Where the gradient is steep. This is why the seam has a crowd along it and the dead corners have nothing but felt.

*Split.* When a fan gets too heavy for its stalk, or the silt under it settles a millimetre, or a block that has been finished becoming itself for a hundred thousand years shifts under the weight of the ice. The great fan by the eastern wall has split and healed eleven times. The heals are the thickest tissue in it, ridged, pale blue, like the seam on a badly mended sail.

*Heal.* Slowly, from both edges, without a scar that anyone would call a wound.

*Sporulate.* At the seam, when the seam swells. Naked cells in a wax coat, going down with the water, out through the fissure, into the cracks of the massif. Almost all of them arrive somewhere with no gradient worth the name and simply stop, and stay stopped, and are still there.

*Stop.* Which is the sixth verb and the only ending on offer.

There is no seventh. Nothing here has ever had a mouth, so nothing here bites, or sucks, or digests, or hunts, or flees, or calls. Six verbs, and one of them is *stop*.

---

The dead are load-bearing.

Nothing eats. This is the fact that takes longest to arrive and changes the most when it does. When a sheet loses its gradient — overshadowed by a fan above it, cut off from the seep by a shift in the silt — it stops, and it does not rot, and it does not fall, and it stays exactly where it was, cold and stiff and pale, holding up the tier above.

You can tell them apart with your hand and a minute of patience. Push a living sheet and it gives with that dry papery sound and comes back over sixty seconds, unhurried, like something remembering. Push a dead one and it stays pushed.

Most of the room is dead. Nine parts in ten of what you are standing among stopped before the Romans, and is still doing its job, which is to hold the living at the height where the warmth is. The forest is a reef of itself. It grows on its own retained corpses because there is nothing here to take them away, and the corpses are not a tragedy, they are the architecture. There are no graves in the larder. There is no ground that is not also somebody.

And in the eastern corner, under three tiers, a wood pigeon, feathered, intact, whole. It fell down the shaft sometime in the last century and the room has done to it what the room does to everything: put a coat on it. It has not been eaten and it has not been absorbed and it has not fused, because it is not the right stuff and never will be. It is the only object in the chamber that is still entirely itself. Everything else in here has, by touching, become everything else. The pigeon is a foreigner in a country with no immigration, dressed for a winter that is not going to end, and it will be a pigeon when the tenth mark is cut.

---

The only thing in the room that moves at a speed you can watch is the frost, and the frost is not alive.

It comes out at night on the coldest surfaces, feathers a centimetre long, and goes back off them by what passes for morning, and the moisture it releases is taken by a frond a hand's breadth away and given back to the wall the following night. Wall to frond to wall, back and forth, a slow tidal grazing that never leaves the room. In the dry weeks of late summer this is the entire water economy and the entire visible activity of the place: a white bloom that walks around the chamber at a centimetre an hour, cropping the cold, and is gone by noon.

If you want an animal here, that is the one you get. It has a range, a round, a season, and a habit, and no more inner life than a shadow.

The other commuter is the water, and the water is eleven days late and unreliable. Three drops in a minute, then nothing for an hour. The forest's whole shape is a portrait of that timetable. The fluting on the brackets is guttering. The fans are umbrellas that catch a drop and spread it to their rim, where it lets go onto the fan below, which spreads it further, so that a single bead from the ceiling seam is divided and subdivided through four tiers and ends as a film on the felt across a square metre of floor. The hand's-breadth spacing between the shelves is not courtesy and it is not decoration. It is the distance at which a sheet can steal warmth from the layer above without being robbed of the drip, and it took a very long time to arrive at, and nobody arrived at it. Two scarce things, two geometries, one compromise, four millimetres a year.

Above the canopy the winners hold their surfaces in the warm upper air. Below, the felt lies in the cold shadow of everything and grows at a tenth of the rate and does not complain, because complaining is a thing animals do.

---

There is one wound.

High on the western wall a hairline fracture lets in a film of water that has been to the surface recently enough to still be carrying oxygen, and where it runs there is a freckle of ochre — the only colour in the room that is not white, blue, grey, or the weak tea of the basin — and around the freckle a bald patch the size of a spread hand where nothing has grown for a long time.

It is closing. The fracture is sealing itself with flowstone at the rate flowstone seals things, which is a hair a year, and it has most of a centimetre still to go. Somewhere in the twenty-third century the last of the oxygen will stop, and the pale tissue will come in from the margins at four millimetres a year, and the freckle will be overgrown and held, like the pigeon, like the oak stubs, like the initials. The room does not repair itself so much as incorporate. It has no other method. It has never needed one.

---

Of the people, what lives here is geometry.

The shelf above the basin is cut square and true, and the growth on it is stunted — a low dense cushion, cropped, the oldest tissue in the room outside the pin's collar, and it is stunted because for a hundred summers hands came down and scraped it off the shelf so the crocks would sit flat. It grew back at four millimetres a year and was scraped off again. That patch is a coppice. It has been coppiced by a family whose surname is on the deed box in a county archive, and it is the only part of the organism that has ever been managed, and it is doing better than any other part of it, because the scraping kept it in the gradient.

Beside the shelf there is a hollow in the growth. It is the shape of a crock: a rounded negative, a hand deep, with a smaller notch at the top where a bail handle sat. Nothing has been in it since the last summer anybody bothered. It is filling from the rim inward, slowly, because the isotherms in that corner are slack and there is little to farm, and at the rate it is going the last trace of the shape of a milk crock will be gone in six or seven hundred years.

That is the human residence here. Not ghosts. A groove worn by a bail, two polishings on the lip of the slot where the rope ran, forty-one knife strokes in fives, the initials T.H. and a date, and a crock-shaped absence that will outlast the language it was made in.

The flowstone cannot see any of it. The flowstone's resolution is a thousand years. Everything anyone ever did in this room, all the evenings, all the milk, the whole century of setting the lid down — is below the floor of what the wall records, a single unresolvable smudge, less than a hair.

Two archives, in the same room, at right angles to each other. One remembers a summer and cannot see a millennium. The other remembers millennia and has never once noticed us.

---

The pin is not an object in the room. It is where something outside the room is touching it.

That is the reading that fits the seam best, and the seam is the evidence: the bedding plane bends around it, three-hundred-million-year-old laminae curving aside and closing without fracture or inclusion, as if the seam had been in the schedule when the sea was laying down its floor. Things that are put into rock make holes. This does not make holes. The rock was arranged around it, or grew up accommodating it, or was never in the way.

So call it a fingertip. Something with a hand elsewhere has put a finger through into this chamber and is holding it still, and has been holding it still for longer than the ice can count, and the holding-still is what the room experiences as four tenths of a degree above freezing. The cold-burn on your fingertip, the clear glaze instead of feathers, the texture your skin cannot report because there isn't one — these are the properties of a contact, not of a thing. You are not feeling an object. You are feeling the place where the room is being *held to a value*.

And along the seam, where the slope between the held cold and the arriving seep is steepest, the forest is thickest, ranked and crowded and tier upon tier: a hundred generations of pale tissue farming the edge of a finger.

Which keeps what? The boring reading says the growth is lint on a standard, the fluff that collects on a weight left in a damp place. But the lint has become a flywheel. It drinks the warmth of every lamp and every August and every falling pigeon and every fool who stays a week, and it drinks it *smoothly*, over its whole enormous area, and it gives the room a thermal inertia the bare rock never had. The standard is steadier because of the mould. Nobody appointed it. It has simply, over three thousand years, become staff.

---

Nine marks, and the ninth is fresh.

Whatever attends to the pin does not come down the shaft. There is no print in the silt, and a print in that silt stays legible for years. There is no scrape on the scallops, no disturbance in the moss on the lid, no measurable warmth, no displacement of air. It is present at the pin and it has not crossed the room. So *comes* is probably the wrong verb, and the wrongness of the verb is the whole difficulty. The far end of a thing does not travel to the near end. It simply reads.

The marks are not writing. They are the flowstone's record of nine moments when its own crystal grew differently — nine hiccups in the deposition, each about a thumbnail wide, spaced a little over a thousand years apart. The most economical reading is that they are not visits. They are *corrections*. Nine times in nine thousand years the value in this hole has been found to be off, and put right, and the wall flinched.

They are not all the same depth. The first is a groove you could catch a nail in. The ninth is barely a change at all, a thing the frost takes differently and nothing else does. Whatever has been wrong here has been getting less wrong, by a factor of about two every millennium, and the ninth correction was very nearly nothing.

The ice in the corner has nine hundred and forty bands and more below it that nobody has reached. It has seen one mark. It is not old enough to have seen two. It does not know there was a before.

And the great fan's eleven splits are not the nine marks and cannot be, because eleven splits in two centuries and nine marks in nine millennia are not the same series, and the arithmetic refuses the correlation before the sentiment can get started. The fan splits because it is heavy and the silt is soft. That is all. It is a relief.

The error bars on the ninth mark are about eighty years either side, which swallows 1782, and the last evening the lid was set, and the war, and the deed box, and everything with a name in it. It is not about us. We are under the resolution. There is no reading of this wall in which anybody down here was thinking about the milk.

---

Two clocks, then, running in the same forty cubic metres, neither of them aware of the other.

One is the crock-shaped hollow, filling at a fraction of four millimetres a year, six or seven centuries from gone. The other is the series that halves every thousand years, which is either converging on a tolerance or on a decision, and there is no way from inside this room to tell those apart. A weight that has stopped needing correction is a weight that can be left where it is forever. It is also a weight that can be collected.

If it were collected, the room would warm by four tenths of a degree, and over the following century the fans would come down onto the tiers below, and the curtains would lie along the silt, and the frost would stop walking its round, and the seep would go on arriving eleven days late into a heap of wet paper that nothing would ever clear away. The pigeon would still be a pigeon. The initials would still be there. There is nobody in the chamber to be bereaved, and the organism extends for a hundred metres into the cracks of the hill in every direction and would go on being a film in the dark, thinner, poorer, unperturbed, farming what gradient was left.

That is the worst available future and it is not a disaster. It is a change of address.

---

Meanwhile the room is occupied, densely, by one thing with forty thousand faces, and it is doing the only thing it does.

Hold your palm a hand's width from a frond and wait. It will take an hour to answer and the answer will be a millimetre, and it will be the entire contents of what it has to say. It is not reaching for you. It is reaching for the difference you make in the air, which is a few degrees of slope, which is food, which is the same thing it has been reaching for since before there was anything on this hill with a spine.

But you will not be able to tell the difference from the inside of an hour. And there is nothing in the room that could tell it either — not the frost on its round, not the water eleven days out of the field, not the pigeon in its coat, not the finger holding the value still.

They all lean the same way. That is the whole society down here. It is enough.
