Begin with one, because the basin will not let you begin with anything else, and then watch it stop being one.

A platter the width of two hands. Dorsally it is a wet fingernail, dull satin, faintly banded, warm enough that the fog beads on it and runs off its rim in threads. Ventrally it is a foot and a mouth. The mouth is a ribbon of teeth that runs over a curved anvil, and the teeth are not enamel but the same carbonate as everything else here, and they wear out in about nine days, and are shed, and are replaced from a sac that never stops manufacturing them. The ribbon moves four times a second. It is scraping the back of the animal underneath it, which is doing the same thing to the animal underneath that, fourteen down, and the animal on top of our platter is scraping our platter's back at the same rate, and has been for sixty years.

So the shell is being taken away from above as fast as it is laid down from within. It is two millimeters thick at the rim and less than a tenth of that in the center, where the neighbor's file has been working longest, and it is not a house or an armor or a possession. It is a throughput. A platter is a place where dissolved carbonate becomes a surface becomes powder. The pavement under your boot is not a graveyard of shells. Almost nothing in it is dead. It is a standing wave, shell being made and unmade at the same rate, and the hundred thousand years of polish is not the polish of stones rubbing in a river but the polish of mouths, and the caliper-perfect sorting is not sedimentology. It is manners.

Which is the first correction. You want to call them grazers, and they are, but the grazing is also the talking, and there is no way to do one without the other. A platter modulates: stroke rate, angle, the pressure of the foot, the length of the pause between quadrants. The film it eats grows on its neighbor's back and the film its neighbor eats grows on its own, and the negotiation of who rasps whom, and how hard, and for how long, is conducted entirely in the medium of the eating itself. There is no separate channel. To speak here is to feed, and to feed is to be heard, and to be heard is to be safe. A hoarder — one that rasps and will not be rasped, that clamps its foot and lets its own dorsum go ungrazed — thickens, and roughens, and grows a raised lip, and is overtopped by its neighbors within a season, and is buried, and becomes substrate. The basin's tidiness is the accumulated refusal of everyone to be an exception.

Listen to the hiss again and it comes apart into three floors.

The coarse layer, the gravel-on-a-drumhead, is the adults, the platters, four strokes a second, a hundred million per hectare, out of phase and unresolvable.

The finer sizzling above it is the young. Juveniles hatch in the flour at a millimeter across and their files are too small to be heard individually and too fast — eleven, twelve strokes a second — and they cannot make a hole in the noise big enough to matter, but they cannot make a mask big enough to hide in either. So they ride. They crowd onto the dorsums of the largest platters, in the shallow lee of the rim, dozens to a back, and they rasp there, and the adults let them, because a juvenile's file is too fine to take shell and because the sizzle they add raises the local floor of the noise by a decibel and a half, which is worth having. An old platter is a nursery and a loudspeaker. The high frequencies you lose first, in your first day here, are the children.

And the eleven-second pulse is neither. It comes up.

Under the mud the threaders lie in their millions, pale, segmented, a millimeter thick and two or three meters long, and they do not travel so much as they peristalse in place, and they are phase-locked — not by listening, they are too deep for the chorus, but by the incompressibility of the mud and by the gas. The shale ferments. Methane and carbon dioxide come out of solution in slow slugs, and the sediment swells, and every threader in the basin feels the swell at once and answers it with the same slow contraction, and the mud exhales, and the whole carpet lifts about a millimeter and settles. That is the pulse in your sternum. It is the forest at forty meters, digesting. Everything above it has entrained to it: the platters reset their stroke phase on the downbeat, the sifters spawn on it, the felted things time their movements to the moment when the entire world is briefly busy being lifted. It is the only clock in the basin and it is not a clock. It is a breath, and it is not theirs.

The sifters live in the flour, and the flour, being finer than the wavelength of anything that could be called a sound here, behaves as a fluid: it pools, it slumps, it braids, it avalanches down the shallow channels in slow gray sheets. The sifters are wedge-shaped and blind and they swim in it. They have no legs worth the name, only combs, and they filter the powder, and what they filter is mostly teeth — shed files, ground fine, the carbonate mouths of the platters above. This is the shortest loop in the basin: a platter makes a tooth, the tooth makes flour, the sifter eats the flour, the threader eats the sifter, the threader's castings feed the film, the platter eats the film, the platter makes a tooth. Nine days from mouth to mouth.

The sifters live eleven or twelve days. The platters live a century or two. The threaders may live four hundred years and have never been within a body length of the air. Nothing here has a generation time it can compare with anything else's. The basin is three tempos stacked in the same square meter, and none of them knows about the others except as weather.

There is no stratigraphy because the threaders will not permit it, and there is no other archive except two.

The first is the shells. On the rim of an old platter, where the neighbor's file cannot reach, there are bands. Most are growth. But at irregular intervals there is a band that is different — denser, whiter, laid down in a single night — and these are the dew nights, the two or three hours a year when the fog comes down and every shell in a hundred square kilometers beads at once and everyone drinks and the rasp thins to a murmur. In the thinning, the animals stop grinding shell and lay it down instead. A quiet ring. You can count them. A platter the width of a dinner plate carries a hundred and forty, and the spacing between them is the only record anywhere in this world of what the sky has done.

The second archive is in the fliers, and we will come to it.

The felted hunters are the reason the whole grammar exists. A pelt of hollow setae, each hair a quarter-wave resonator tuned across a band, so that the body absorbs what strikes it and reports nothing back; mouthparts that shear; no file, no rasp, no carbonate to shed. They do not add to the noise and they do not subtract from it either — they simply fail to reflect, and in a medium made entirely of reflection, that is a shape. But the pelt is not only a cloak. Each hollow hair is also an ear. The whole animal is a listening surface, and what it listens for is the negative: a doorway of quiet opening in the floor of the world.

And silences have grammar. A true hole, hard-edged, a meter across, moving: another hunter, or a death. A thin place — a diminuendo rather than an absence, a rasping fringe around a cold center out on a brine rim: a drinker, exposed, licking condensation off its own shoulder with a jointed limb, keeping its file moving because to stop is to die, and therefore staying out in the cold twice as long as the physics requires, because half its cold is being spent on being audible. The hunters read the thin places. They have learned that a diminuendo has a body in it and a hole may not.

Some of the hunters' prey have learned it back. There is a platter — a lineage, a habit, it is hard to say which — that goes to a bare rise and stops rasping and clamps. It opens a hole around itself the size of a doorway and waits in it, warm, with its foot raised and its shell parted three millimeters. When the felted thing comes into the quiet to find out what has died, the hole closes. It is not about energy. The shale gives carbon; carbon is the only thing here that is abundant. Nitrogen and phosphorus have been passed from mouth to mouth in this basin since before the sky closed, and a body is the most concentrated packet of them in a hundred square kilometers. Everything that hunts here is hunting the same handful of atoms, over and over, forever.

The hunters themselves are solitary and cannot hear each other, because two absences sliding through the same hiss are invisible to one another by design. To mate, a felted thing must do the one thing it exists never to do. It climbs a platter's rim, lifts one leg, and plucks a seta: a single dry click, audible for thirty meters, the only sound it will make in its life. Then it waits in the noise it has made, which is a beacon and a confession, and either something comes to it or something comes for it, and it cannot tell which by the sound, because it has just spent its silence.

The fliers drop into holes. Membrane wings, fringed to kill the whistle, more falling-with-intent than flying; they cannot echolocate, the air is too full, so they hunt the hunters by finding the patches of quiet and folding into them, and half the time the quiet is a trap, and the arithmetic works out.

They roost on the escarpment, and they are the only animals here who have ever been above anything, and they have the second archive.

A flier has ear-lids: a muscular operculum over each tympanum, and it can close them. Nothing else in the basin can stop listening. This is not a small thing. It means a flier can be deaf on purpose, can be unaddressed, can hold something in itself that is not being shouted at by three hundred and forty mouths per square meter. Whether that constitutes an inner life is not a question the basin is equipped to answer. But it is the animal that hesitates.

And it needs the ear-lids, because it navigates by pain.

The focused places — the hollows and inversion-geometries where the whole basin's rasp is folded into a forty-meter spot, where the pressure bleeds a membrane — are the only fixed features in a world with no landmarks, no cairns, no cuts, nothing built. Everything else drifts: the flour windrows migrate, the brine rims creep, the carpet rotates. The notes stay. So the fliers use them. They fly the basin with their ear-lids half-shut, keeping one note at the edge of tolerance off the left wing and another a long way down, triangulating by how much it hurts. A young flier is taught this. It is taken along a route, once, in its first season, by an adult who has flown it for eighty years, and the route is a sequence of tolerable agonies, and it is the only thing in this world that is inherited as information rather than as tissue. A map of a place made of sound, held in the memory of an animal that can close its ears, passed down a lineage that has never seen the ground it maps.

Nothing goes to the notes. Which is almost true.

At the edge of a note the carpet stops, and the ground is scoured and faintly warm and ringing, and the flour blows across and does not settle. But if you go in — with your ears packed, which will not save you, but suppose you go in — the first thing wrong is the shells. They are unsorted. Hand-sized next to platter-sized next to something the width of a coin, mixed, mismatched, jumbled, the caliper grading gone. The only untidiness in the world.

And they are occupied.

There are animals in the notes. They have no tympana, or have them fused and buried under cuticle, and the note that kills everything with a membrane passes through them as weather. They are platters, plainly platters, descended from something that walked too close and ruptured and did not die — and there is no way to know how often that happened or how long ago, because the basin has eaten its own record and cannot be asked. They graze a film that nothing else has touched, thick as felt, sulfur-black, luxurious, forty minutes of growth become forty days. They do not need to rasp in time, because there is no one to negotiate with and no chorus to hide in; they eat arrhythmically, continuously, and the note drowns them completely. They cannot be heard. They cannot hear.

They are not afraid. There is nothing in this basin that can warn them and nothing that can find them, because to enter the note is to be deafened and to leave it is to be a hole. They are exiles in the only sanctuary, and they have been there long enough that their shells have gone thin and soft-edged and various, released from the tyranny of being a good acoustic citizen.

And they touch. Antenna to antenna, leg laid across a neighbor's rim, a drumming with the whole body against the ground that they cannot perceive and that nothing outside can hear over the note. It is the only contact language in a hundred square kilometers. Everything else here speaks at a distance, in the medium, to everyone at once, always, or dies. These speak at a body's length, to one, on purpose, and can stop.

They also live among the intact dead, because the corpses that blow in are not eaten and ground and turned to flour here; they simply lie, whole, recognizable, the only bodies in the world that remain shaped like someone.

The shale is thick. It will last a very long time — longer than the sky has been closed, longer than the bands on the oldest platter, longer than the fliers' map has had anything worth mapping. But it is being spent, at four strokes a second, by three hundred and forty mouths per square meter, and a candle flame is also an equilibrium.

When it fails it will not fail suddenly. The film will grow back in fifty minutes instead of forty. The rasp will thin. The floor of the noise will drop a decibel, and then another, and the holes in it will get wider and easier to read, and the felted things will feast for a century while the carpet goes to nine deep, then six, then two. The pulse will slow. The bands on the last platters will crowd together and then stop, because there will be no dew nights thick enough to matter once there is not enough warmth leaking from enough bodies to make the fog stand.

And in the notes, where nothing has ever listened, the deaf ones will go on drumming to each other through the ground, touching, unsorted, thin-shelled, unaware that the world outside has changed key, and then that it has gone quiet, and then that it has stopped.
