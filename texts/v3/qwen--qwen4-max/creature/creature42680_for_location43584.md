# Who Lives at the Soda Works

## i. The census, attempted

Count heads and the valley refuses you.

A litre of the red pans holds something like the number of human beings who have ever lived — halophilic archaea, rod and disc and flat square, packed in brine at saturation, dividing on a schedule nobody has troubled to establish. You cannot count them; you can only weigh their colour. The pans are red because there are enough individuals in them to change the way light behaves.

Go to the shallows where the mats are, and the arithmetic becomes briefly possible, which is worse. A hundred thousand fly larvae per square metre. A patch of mud the size of a bedsheet outnumbers the town four hundred times over, and there are twenty kilometres of that mud, and the adults are on the wing above it in a haze you walk through with your hand moving in front of your face.

The fish you could count, in principle. Tens of thousands, and that is all of them — every individual of the species alive on earth, in a chain of hot outflows you could walk in an afternoon.

The birds you cannot count and should not try, because they are not residents. They are a condition the lake enters.

And then nine hundred people, in rows of houses built for four thousand, keeping the ledger.

They are not the rarest animal here. The hyena is rarer. So is the marabou.

## ii. The ones who cannot leave

Residency in this basin is not a preference. It is a chemical inability to be anywhere else.

The oldest residents have never been at the surface. In the black mud under the crust, sulfate-reducing bacteria have been eating the fallen mats for ten thousand years at a rate that makes glacial erosion look impatient. They are why the mud is black. When the dredge opens a trench and the smell comes up, that is their breath, and it is the only smell in the valley produced by something living slowly. Everything else that lives here lives fast, in a film, at the top.

Inside the bed itself there are cells sealed in fluid inclusions — brine pockets a few microns across, walled in crystal, occupied. They were shut in when the crystal grew. They are still viable. The crop is inhabited. When Pan Nine is cut and slurried and calcined, the calciner drives off the water and the carbon dioxide and everything that was alive in it, and what comes out of the bagging floor is the cleanest material in the valley: nothing in it, nothing on it, no one home.

The flies are residents in the sense that a tide is resident. *Ephydra* does not travel; the windrows are its diaspora, twenty kilometres of it, knee-deep, going nowhere. A fly hatches into pH 10.5, breathes through a snorkel, grazes a mat, mates in the air over the exact square metre it was born above, and dies into the wrack. It has no ambition. Its entire species' strategy is to be too small and too numerous and too chemically adapted to be worth competing with. Nothing else wants what it has. That is the whole of its security.

The tilapia is the true citizen, and the most precarious thing in the basin, and nobody looks at it.

Two hectares. Spring source above at eighty-six degrees, lake below at saturation, and between them a wedge at thirty-nine where a fish can hold a nest. It gave up ammonia for urea — it urinates like a mammal because the ordinary fish way is forbidden it — and having made that concession it can never undo it. There is no adjacent valley for it. There is no downstream. Block one spring with sand, or let the flood rearrange one outflow, and a percentage of the entire species is simply over.

The males flare at each other all day. Blue-black, fins out, defending a warm square of shallows against a neighbour whose descendants will still be there when the escarpment has moved a millimetre. It is the most concentrated politics on earth: a species with no wilderness, no frontier, no refugees, arguing over the only real estate it will ever have.

And its range has recently expanded, because the Company built a swimming pool.

Cooled spring water, warm as blood, twenty-odd metres by ten. The fish got in somehow — the way anything gets in here, along a pipe, in a bucket, on a boot — and established, and are not removed. So the species now occupies two hectares of natural wedge plus a municipal amenity. Children do lengths through it. It is not a joke; it is the actual conservation status of an animal. It survives partly because a company cools hot water so that its employees can swim.

If the town closes, the pool goes, and the fish lose a pond they have no way of knowing they had.

## iii. The ones the buildings made

There were no spiders on the flats before there were handrails.

Now every stair in the plant is strung, and each web is a small grey flag of insect, and the spiders have been there long enough to have local variation — the ones on the sodium-lit galleries are paler than the ones under the trestles, or perhaps that is dust, or perhaps nobody has looked properly, which is the more likely reason there is no paper on it.

The geckos in the bagging office ceiling are a lineage, not a population. Translucent, fast, tails visibly regrown, hunting the lamp-line where the flies come in at dusk. They drink from the condensation on the underside of the corrugating in the hour before dawn. Their grandparents arrived in a crate.

The bats — a thousand, in the corrugated underside of Number Two gallery — are here for the same reason: nobody built them a roost, but a conveyor gallery in a country with no trees and no caves is a cave that runs for four hundred metres and stays warm. They come out at dusk and scribble over the pans, and the fly-haze thins where they pass and closes behind them like water.

The mice in the bagging floor eat flour that came in on the metre-gauge. That is worth saying plainly: they eat the mainland. So do the hens in the yards behind Row F, whose owners gave up buying feed in the year the protein scheme collapsed and now sweep the windrow into buckets instead. Twenty kilometres of dead fly, free, replenished nightly by the wind. The eggs come out with a faint low taste of the lake that visitors notice and residents cannot detect at all.

The cats hunt the plovers. The plovers — chestnut-banded, grey-brown, small, running the wrack-line with a stop-start-stop that makes them almost invisible against the drift — are one of the few genuinely resident birds, and there is no count of them, and there never will be, because they live in the one place on earth where a bird is a rounding error.

Shade is a habitat here. Under a canvas awning the ground is eight degrees cooler, dew survives until seven, and there is a wet margin where the flies drink and the geckos hunt and a wet ring where a man's boots have stood. Every structure in the works has an ecology on its north side. The town's biology is a diagram of its plumbing.

## iv. The people, by what they do

**Wafula, on the screens.** Ninety minutes, all shift, every shift, nineteen years. The felt of flies comes off the intake in a grey sheet and slaps into the channel and goes back to the lake it came from. He is the second-loudest regular sound in the valley and he does not know it, because he cannot hear himself from inside his own head. His hands are scrubbed and slightly gone — saponified, the oil taken out of him hour by hour — and he cannot hold a newspaper without leaving a translucent print. He counts time in hoses. Ask him how long a thing took and he will tell you: three hoses, maybe four.

**Muthengi, on the dredge.** He sits in a cab above a hole he is making and cuts crystal mush off the bottom of a trench that will be solid again in fourteen months. He works the same ground his father worked. He has read the same six paperbacks for eleven years, kept in a biscuit tin with a rag, because paper goes soft here in a week and a book left on a shelf becomes a brick with printing in it. He is paid, in effect, to be forgotten by the ground. He is the only man in the valley whose labour is guaranteed to be undone, and he finds this restful, and cannot explain why to his brother, who farms.

**Kilonzo and the other one, on the drier.** There is fire in this valley. It came in on a train. It lives in a steel tube and is fed diesel from a tank that was filled six hundred kilometres away, and it is the only combustion for forty kilometres in any direction, and it is watched by two men through a port. Everything that burns here was carried. The charcoal in the yard jikos arrived in sacks; the sacks came from a place with trunks. A child born in Row F has never seen a fire that was not paid for.

**Otieno, on the locomotive.** He is the one who leaves. Twice a week he takes salt up the line and crosses, somewhere above the escarpment, the alkalinity line — the place where the last thorn tree stands and the ground begins, absurdly, to have leaves. He notices it every time. He has decided not to mention this to anyone. He brings back mail, diesel, flour, cement, tomatoes, and the newspaper that reaches the club three days late and goes soft in a week and is read anyway.

**Saitoti, who reads the meters.** Fresh water is piped eight kilometres and metered, in a valley that is entirely wet and entirely undrinkable. He is therefore the most resented man in town and also the one who leads the singing on Sunday, and he has learned to hold both facts in the same face.

**Sister Achieng, at the hospital.** Her ledger is a chemistry text. Eyes: alkaline dust, corneal burns from a splash nobody thought was a splash. Lungs: the brown ceiling under the inversion. Heels split to the meat by crystal. Kidneys, in the men who work the drier and drink their allowance too late. Skin that has had its oil taken out of it for twenty years. She has no rot to fight — the flats tan what they touch — and instead she fights abrasion. Clothes here do not wear out; they are cut, at the elbows and the collar, by salt.

**Mr. Kimani, who teaches three grades in one room.** The school was built for four hundred children. There are sixty-one. Every year the top class is smaller and every year he teaches it harder. He is preparing children for a world that is entirely made of leaves, using a valley that has none, and the only teaching aid he has that works without qualification is the lake itself: he can demonstrate saturation, precipitation, density, pH, and food chains by walking them out of the door.

**The steward at the club,** whose father came down the line in 1948 to keep stores, who has the key to the cabinet with the 1911 report in it, who oils the warped dartboard as if oil were the problem. The board is an oval now. Everyone has adjusted. A stranger throws and misses to the left and the room is briefly, warmly happy.

**The pensioners,** who did not go. Eleven houses in Row C with one light among them; the rest are stores, or full of a brother's furniture, or simply shut. They walk the causeway at dusk, all of them, separately, at the same speed. They cannot sleep anywhere else. Silence, off-shift, off-site, is a thing that happens to them at a relative's house upcountry and they lie awake in it, listening for a thud with no attack to it, and come back early.

**And the traders in the market,** under corrugated iron on pipe frames, selling cabbages that travelled further than most of their customers ever will. A lemon cut open in that market is, to a child standing beside it, the smell of a forest. She has no word for what she is smelling. She only knows it is green.

## v. The children

They are the only generation in the history of the valley that has no elsewhere in its body.

Everyone else came from somewhere with trees. The children were born on a rock peninsula in a basin where the loudest animal noise is four hundred thousand birds arriving as the sound of surf that never breaks, and where the ground drinks the air at night and gives it back by nine, and this is not remarkable to them; it is what ground does.

They cannot swim properly and never will. The brine runs at 1.2 and their legs will not go down. They float absurdly high, whooping, and the pool at the club is the only place in their lives where water behaves the way water behaves in books. One boy spent a whole afternoon trying to drown a stone. He has drawn a conclusion from this that he has not told anyone and will not revise for years.

They walk with one hand always moving in front of the face — an unconscious flick, learned before speech — and you can identify a visitor at two hundred metres by the stillness of his hands. They talk with their lips closed between words. They spit without interrupting a sentence.

They collect the calcified birds. There are flamingos standing in the wrack that were alive when the drier was commissioned: feathers, eye, folded foot, going slowly to mineral. A girl in Row F has a foot, just a foot, grey and light as pumice, and she uses it to hold a door. Behind the fitters' shop there is a handaxe that a surveyor picked up in the north badlands and could not be bothered to carry home — the one exception among a great many that nobody picks up — sharp as the day it was struck, older than the town, older than the language the town speaks, wedged under a bench to stop it rocking.

Wafula's daughter went up the line last year to school. In the first week she wrote home about a fault: a sound at night, continuous, like something frying, like the hose on the screens but everywhere and soft. It was rain on leaves. She had heard the intake all her life and had no other category for water arriving on a surface. She reported it as a noise complaint. She is learning the word for it now, and the word will never quite fit over the sound.

## vi. The dead

Nothing rots, and this is a problem for a town.

The flats preserve. Alkalinity and salt do the work of a tannery, and a body put into that ground would not become soil; it would become an object, and stay an object, and go slowly to mineral in a place where the wind rows the dead flies into drifts and no smell of decay exists anywhere.

So the town takes its dead up. There is a lorry, and there is a road, and there is a place above the escarpment where the alkalinity ends and there are roots, and the families go up there because they want their people to rot properly — to go down into something and come up as grass, to be eaten and reassembled, to be part of the ordinary business that the basin has been refused.

It is the one thing the town imports that it cannot make: decomposition.

The only local dead are the birds, and there is no ceremony for them, and they stand in the wrack in their thousands with their eyes still in, and the children play among them, and this is not horror. It is simply the local condition. In a place with no rot, death is a change of state, like freezing.

## vii. The ones who only come

The flamingos do not live here. They live at a chemistry, and this month the chemistry is here. Four hundred thousand, eight hundred thousand, a seam of grey-pink at the far end where the springs dilute the brine enough for the mats; and when the flood comes down brown and the salinity drops, they read it and go in a single morning to a lake three hundred kilometres away, and the shore becomes ordinary, and the town notices the way you notice a clock stopping.

The herons and kingfishers work the fish-wedge and move on. The marabou stands at the outflow with its bill down and takes fish without appearing to move, and there may be only one of him, and he may be the same one for years.

The hyena is a commuter. She has a den in the escarpment, in rock, in a place with thorn trees, and she comes down the causeway around two, unhurried, past the sodium lamps that are the same colour as the dust, to the nesting ground at the south end, where ten thousand birds shout at her and she takes eggs anyway. She is not interested in the town. The town is a well-lit corridor between her house and her work.

The herders come down with cattle to the sweet springs and stand at the edge of the flats and do not go further, because there is nothing further for an animal with a rumen. Their goats' bones arrive twice a year in the brown sheet, along with sand and once a shipping container. They are in the sediment of this valley and not in its census.

And the engineers come from Nairobi once a quarter, in a vehicle, and squint at the ground and not the sky, and stand on the causeway at midday and see thirty kilometres of white with one grey structure in it and no living thing, and say that it is empty out here, and go back before dark.

## viii. The largest resident

The works is an animal, and it is more permanently resident than any person in it.

It eats diesel, crystal mush, and men. It drinks eight kilometres of piped fresh water and sweats flies off its intake every ninety minutes. It has shrunk from four thousand to nine hundred and kept its shape, the way a pruned thing keeps its shape. It has three generations of conveyor gallery crossing overhead and pipe racks built twice, and it is being buried at a millimetre a year: the abandoned pans of the 1950s going into the crust, pipework cementing, a stratum forming that will sit above ten thousand years of black mud and below whatever comes next.

The people can leave. Otieno leaves twice a week; the children will leave for good; the pensioners could be carried up the road in a lorry like their dead. The pipework cannot leave. The tailings cannot leave. Of everything that lives on this interface, the machines have committed the most to it.

And it heals. That is the one fact in the cabinet in the club, written down in 1911 by men who did not stay: cut the trench, and in fourteen months the trench is ground again. The mine has been healing since before there was anyone to cut it. The town has not learned to do this. The town has one state and does not return to it; it goes from four thousand to nine hundred and stays there and the school gets smaller. But the salt comes back, and everything in the valley with a face is downstream of a bacterium, and the bacterium does not require the town, and the town knows it, and stays anyway, and works the night shift, eleven men, two on the drier.

## ix. What has already left

Most of what lives here is elsewhere by now.

The bagged ash goes up the metre-gauge and onto the mainline and out: into glass, into detergent, into the water treatment of cities that have never heard of a trona pan. There is a windscreen in Mombasa with this valley in it. There is a window in a hospital in Kisumu, and a bottle on a table in Nairobi, and a pane in a school somewhere with leaves outside it, and every one of them is thirty kilometres of white crust that a dredge chewed off the bottom of a trench that has since closed.

The archaea in the crystal were burned out of it at the plant. What arrives is mineral, sterile, perfectly clean — the valley with the life taken out, which is the only form in which the valley is welcome anywhere else.

## x. Four in the morning

For two hours before dawn, everyone here is doing the same thing.

The crust is sweating, pulling water out of air at seventy percent, going from bone-white to the grey of a licked spoon. The dew-catchers on the roofs are running into their gutters, a thin tick of it, the only water in the town that is not metered. The geckos are licking the underside of the corrugating. The mats are wet. The flies are on the eyelids of the men walking the galleries, and on the lips, and in the mouths, taking the only fresh water on the flats, which is inside the people. Wafula is at the screens with his hand moving in front of his face. Muthengi's trench is clouding with new crystal, falling slowly downward through the brine for a living. Out at the springs the fish are in their wedge at thirty-nine degrees, holding their nests, flaring in the dark at nothing.

At the far end, four hundred thousand birds are asleep in the shallows as grey lumps, and will not be here forever.

By six the crust will have a skin on the cut and you will be able to stand on the place where the machine was. By nine the sun will have taken the water back and the ground will ring under a heel. The prints will be gone. Everything that lives here lives in that gap — the two hours when the air gives up water to anything cold enough to ask — and then goes flat against the interface again, and waits, and is not empty.
