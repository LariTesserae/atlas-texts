# WHAT LIVES IN THE ANHER VAULT

## The Carry

A citizen of the Anher Vault carries three and a half grams of water.

That is the statutory figure and very nearly the actual one: a thimble, a bead, less than the sweat on a coin held in a warm hand. It sits in the deep tissue behind the sternum and in the vitreous of the eyes and in a dozen catalytic niches too small to name, and it is the only water in that body, and everything else about that body is oil.

The rest of them is solvent — thin resins and light naphthas, dry-blooded, faintly sweet if you cut them, which is why nobody cuts them. Their skin is waxed twice a turn at the Rectifier's fauna counter, the same wax that goes to the hoppers, the same barrel, no distinction made and none resented. Their lungs are a still. The rig they wear is not clothing but a condenser: a collar of baffled brass, cheek-gutters, a reclaim bladder at the small of the back that a spouse is permitted to empty and no one else. They speak at a volume that a visitor from above constantly, exhaustedly asks them to raise. They do not raise it. A sentence costs. An argument costs more, and the cost is itemized in the Weighhouse ledger under the citizen's own name, and everyone within earshot can hear a person spending themselves.

So they grieve with their hands. There is no water to spare for tears; the ducts were plumbed out eleven generations ago. What grief looks like here is a mouth working without sound and both hands moving in the dry grammar — the flat palm, the folded thumb, the two fingers drawn down the air like a splice being closed. It is a full language. It has obscenities. It has a register used only by relatives at a rendering, and a register used only by pruners, and a joke in it so old nobody remembers what was funny.

The insult is *heavy*. It means wet. It means a person carrying above statute, hoarding, drinking wall-distillate on the sly, keeping a gram in the cheek like a drunk with a flask. On cold turns you can sometimes see it: a faint feather of vapor at the collar of a guild factor's rig, wealth announcing itself as breath. The compliment is *dry as a beam*. Children are told they are lovely and dry. Lovers say it. It is carved over a door in Hask.

They name their children for what the vault has: Arris, Chaff, Seep, Flare, Kerogen if the family is pretentious. There are four hundred women in the vault named Arris and every one of them is named for an edge that has not been rounded and will not be.

## The Ledger

Nobody is born free. A birth requires a loan.

Gestation costs catalyst — actual grams, drawn from the Wet Shop against a bond, weighed out in an ampoule and carried in a warmed cradle against the mother's sternum for the whole term, and the child comes out heavier than any adult in the vault. Children steam. On a cold turn, in a nursery, you can see forty small breaths going up into the light, and it is the only place in nine hundred meters of rock where that is true, and people pay a half-coin to stand at the glass and watch it. Some find it beautiful. Some have to leave the room. The nursery wardens are chosen for temperament and are the most quietly furious people in the city, because they spend their lives guarding the flood and loving it.

The bond is serviced. That is what the freight schedule is for, and the foundry, and the mould orchards, and the eleven-percent argument about pipe-eels. Forty thousand people are forty thousand loans at various stages of amortization, and the debt court sits four days a turn, and the preamble of the code is not a metaphor. *We are guests here, and we are carrying the flood inside us.* It is a balance sheet.

Marriage is a pooling. Two carries entered on one line, jointly liable, jointly assayed; the ceremony is the reading of the combined figure aloud by a clerk while the couple stands with their hands folded so that neither can sign anything. Divorce is a division, and it is ugly, and it is done in grams.

Death is an assay. The relatives sit two days with the salt trays and the warm draw, and they do the work themselves because the code will not let a stranger handle a family's water, and at the end the Gram Clerk weighs the recovery and writes a receipt and the estate is credited. Three grams, often. Sometimes three and a quarter, and the family is ashamed, because it means the dead had been holding something back. The dry remainder is interred in the killed strips under the wards, and it is light, and it is stacked, and it does not rot, because rot needs the thing that has just been paid back.

There is a chart in the Assay Hall, unframed, pinned beside the pebble case. It plots mean statutory carry against generation. The first settlers down the shaft carried liters and were a hazard to everything they touched; they are called the Litre Men in schoolbooks and are not admired. The line falls. It has fallen for eleven generations. Extended in pencil, in a hand nobody will admit to, it reaches zero in about nine more.

Nobody says the rest of it out loud, which is that at zero the Wet Shop holds every gram in the vault, and the citizens hold none, and the question of what a citizen would then be is not a question the guilds have found a way to phrase politely.

## The Trades

The Sear are the cauterizers, women on ladders with electric irons, and you know them by the forearms: a ladder of small shiny burns up the inside of each, one per leak, and a guildsman of forty years has arms like a constellation chart. They work by touch and by the sixty-beat pulse and they can find a weeping joint in a brass-hooped main in the dark by the change in the hum against their palm. They are paid by the seal. They are famously unmarriageable and famously married to each other.

The drovers keep the galvanic rows — ten thousand tubers in racks, spiked copper and zinc, fed and rested on rotation. A drover walks the racks with a stethoscope of grown wood and hears a sick tuber four days before it fails, and names them, and is not sentimental about it, and cries in the dry grammar when a good one goes to the Rectifier. There are drovers who have worked one row for thirty years and can tell you which tuber in it is clever. They will not explain what they mean.

The auger-wranglers smear the pheromone paste along the intended line and lay the grub at the head of it and wait. The recipes are family property. A wrangler's hands smell of a particular lineage's paste for life, and their augers will follow those hands off the line and into a wall if allowed, which is why wranglers wash before they go home and why they never quite wash enough. Feral augers are common in the Between. They have been boring wild conduit through the mat's own thick places for longer than there has been a city, glassy varnished tunnels running for kilometers with no plan and no purpose, and the hush-cats use them, and children dare each other into them, and every few years a child does not come out and the vault goes quiet about it in a way that is not silence but refusal.

The wasp-masters keep the lacquer hives and hire them by the crate and pay the sugar tax, and they smell of sugar, and are consequently the most touched people in the vault — strangers put a hand on a wasp-master's sleeve for luck. They resent this. They also arrange to be seen doing it.

The Cut is the largest guild and the only one with a theology. Pruning crews, subtraction, the endless re-opening of doors. Their catechism is a list of things the city will do if you stop: *it will close the window, it will close the door, it will close the stair, it will close the room you are standing in.* A pruner's virtue is refusal, and the good ones are unpleasant company, and the old ones are something worse, which is serene.

The marker-keepers walk circuits alone, forty-one of them, one per stretch, tending a bulb the size of a head on a pole every half-kilometer. They are licensed to speak aloud at full volume, alone, on their circuit, as a health measure. They all do it. They have developed a shared accent that no one else in the vault has, from talking to nobody in particular for decades, and they marry late and badly, and they keep private tallies of hopper-clicks in little books, and if you ask to see the book they will show you, and the numbers mean nothing, and they are the most complete record of the Between that exists.

The seep-tappers climb the cold far walls and set the gutters and skim the wall-distillate, and they drink it, which is theft and is prosecuted lightly because everyone knows what the work does to a man. Distillate makes you see roundness. That is its whole effect and its whole appeal: a drunk tapper looks at a blade of talus and sees it softened, water-worn, a thing that has been rolled a long way by a thing that is not here. They see pebbles in the corners of rooms. They see the ceiling as a distance. The Assay Hall keeps its one gray stone in lead-glass because a sober citizen cannot hold the idea of it in their head for more than a few seconds, and the tappers come down off the wall and stand in front of the case and weep without any water at all, hands moving, and the attendants let them stay until closing.

## The Dry Blood

Kneel and it is crawling; stand and it is a desert; both are true and the vault's animals have simply agreed to be sparse.

Spindle-hoppers are the vault's cattle and its clock. Knee-high, piano-key pale, grazing scattered, clicking when they jump — a pin dropped on a plate, four times an hour across a kilometer, each one an event. People time things by clicks. A woman waiting on a tram platform will say *two clicks* and mean it precisely, and a man late for a shift will say *six* and be forgiven. When the ghost-drizzle comes and strips the wax off their backs they fold under bracket-fungus by the thousand, all at once, and the plain goes empty, and then the light comes up and they return one at a time over a quarter of an hour, and the whole vault listens to them come back, and nobody has ever written down why that is the most reassuring sound in the world.

Hush-cats are the reason for the holes. Moth-scale felt that eats sound; where one walks the hum drops out in a moving pocket and the hoppers freeze and you notice an absence with a shape. They are not dangerous. They are simply the largest thing in the Between that has an opinion about you. Hunters trap by listening for the holes and are paid in wax and sugar and are all a little deaf in the ordinary way, from a life spent listening past the edge of hearing.

Up on the Pale, upside down across the light, the sky-grazers crop the luminous tissue in slow pale herds, and the Ceiling Crew goes up after them on the anchoring roots with cull licenses and long hooks. There are laws about light. There is a quota, and a season, and a fine for taking a breeding animal, and a whole bitter politics of which district's sky may go brown. The crew are the only people in the vault who have seen the city from above — eleven halos and the dark between and the pale blot of Sill — and they come down different, and they do not talk about it much, and what they say when pressed is always some version of *it's smaller than you think, and it's going the wrong way*, and nobody has ever gotten a straight answer about which way is wrong.

## The Tenant

Most of what lives here is one plant.

The mat is not a landscape. It has a nineteen-hour opinion about light and it expresses it by browning out a district. It refuses crowding — not aggressively, metabolically; put two towers close and the crust between them goes liver-dark and sour and the graft fails, and the fire law merely agrees with a decision already made. It heals windows shut in four years. It eats sharp shale at a millimeter a year with acid it makes in glands the size of a fist, and it has been jacking the ceiling up since before there was anyone to notice, and the fracture that made the vault is younger than the forest widening it. The geology has not started. The mat is doing the geology. It is excavating a country.

And it cannot make water, and it cannot hold water, and there are reactions it needs that will not go without it.

There is a paper read once every nine years in the Assay Hall, to a room of guild factors who have all read it before and all come anyway. It observes that the vault's water is not stored in the mat, or in the rock, or in the seep, but in forty thousand warm sealed vessels that walk, on a schedule, along grown rails, to precise locations, where they deliver measured grams of the forbidden catalyst into the exact joints where the mat's own chemistry stalls. It observes that these vessels maintain themselves, repair themselves, fund their own increase, and render their water back to a central reliquary on death. It observes that the preamble says *guests*, and asks whether that is the correct word, and proposes no other, because the author would like to keep his license.

The factors go home and prune their doors. The pruners have a phrase for it, in the dry grammar, four fingers and a turn of the wrist, and it translates roughly as *we are the part of it that carries the flood*, and they make it at each other across the Between without slowing down.

## Sill

The rot is alive and it is the only other wet thing in the world.

Ninety years on, the scar is eleven hundred meters of pale sterile floor, still faintly steaming at the center on cold turns, and along its margin the white fibrous mass is not dying. It is learning. The Salt Watch has documented encystment: the rot laying down a wax it did not used to make, sealing its wet inside a dry skin, holding its water the way a citizen holds theirs. It has arrived, in ninety years, at the same solution the Litre Men took eleven generations to reach, and it is arriving faster.

And something at the edge drinks. The Watch does not name it in reports. It is a hush-cat, or was; the felt is wrong, matted, and it steams at the collar of its own throat, and it goes in over the salt line and comes out and it is the only animal in the Anher Vault that has ever been observed to drink. Hunters who track it come back with a hole in the sound around them for hours afterward and will not be touched. The law is one sentence: nothing that comes out of Sill goes into a house.

The surveyors' stakes at the margin have sprouted. Everyone agrees this is a joke. What is not a joke, what is in the Watch's ledger in a clerk's flat hand, is that the sprouts are not mat. They are something with a wax cuticle and a salt gland and a tolerance the old mat does not have, growing outward at a millimeter a year from the edge of the wound, into the wound. Ninety years is nothing. The next draft of the vault is being written at its margin by the thing that was supposed to be sterilized, and it is learning to carry the flood inside itself, and there is a staked ward there already with a name and a tram allocation and no funding.

## The Ones Not Yet

Half the surveyed wards exist only as stakes and graft-scars, and they are inhabited.

They are inhabited by the people the debt court has already written bonds against: the not-yet-conceived, whose grams are earmarked, whose carry is projected at three point one, whose names appear in the ledger as *issue, expected*, and who will be born owing for the water that made them. There are more of these than there are citizens. The vault's largest population is its intentions.

Out past the last marker-tree, past the freight loop, eleven kilometers beyond the last house, there is a platform with nothing on it, and every week a woman rides out to prune the rail.

She has done it for nineteen years. Everyone in Node knows the story and everyone has it slightly wrong: they think she cuts it back, that the rail is a nuisance reaching into the dark and she is the hand that stops it. She does not cut it back. She has never once shortened it. She cuts for shape — she takes the low sprays and the twin leaders and the wild whorls that would throw a car, and she leaves the strong terminal shoot every time, and she trains it, and it goes on ahead of her at the speed the mat makes anything, and the platform is not the end of the line. The platform is a place she has been keeping.

She has a name for the station. It is written inside her tally book on the last page, in the dry grammar, which cannot be written, so it is drawn: four fingers and a turn of the wrist.

At the end of the working turn the Pale goes amber, then ember, and she rides back under a ceiling that is measurably higher than the one she was born under, and behind her in the dark a hopper clicks once, and a hole in the sound moves toward it, and eleven kilometers of grown rail lie out there in the light with nothing at the far end yet, reaching, patient in a way that makes the city look frantic, holding a place for whoever is going to live there.
