## The pasture

Start with the rind, because it is the only abundant thing, and because everything else is a rumor of it.

It is not one organism. It is three, in a partnership so old that the boundaries between them have gone architectural: a clay-maker that lays down lattice, a manganese-breather that darkens it, and a fixer that pulls carbon out of the air's thin gruel and out of the rock's own body and lays it in as oil. Three millimeters, three partners, one skin. It grows *sideways* — a hand's breadth in a century, over fresh exposure, the way a stain spreads in cloth. Thickness is capped; nothing here gets thicker, only wider. That is the first correction to make about the Sill, and the people who live on it made it eleven hundred years ago and put it in the building code.

It eats the sky. The fair-weather field, that enormous standing voltage the harvest towers dip their filaments into, is the rind's actual food; the rock is only its plate. This is why the moths resent the towers and why the towers' crews are, in a way nobody says out loud, in the pasture business. Everything on the Sill is plugged into the same circuit, sky to ground, and the rind is the living resistor in the middle, and the animals are leakage.

And it hides in the honeycomb. A grazer's tongue is a ribbon of hooked plates and it can strip a flat surface down to the pale rock in one pass, but it cannot get into a cell the size of a thumb. The tafoni are the seed bank. Every hollow the wind has bored is a refuge, a wet-nurse, a kept inoculum, and when a herd has passed and left its bone-colored stain, the healing does not creep in from the edges — it comes up out of ten thousand pits at once, from inside the wound, and closes in sixty years. The geology is not scenery. The geology is why the world was not eaten bare nine million years ago.

The rind is laminated. It lays down a layer a year and the layers record the field, and the field records the sun, and the dust, and the falls of shocked glass, and the passage of herds. You can core it. The ward archives are racks of cores, thumb-thick, labeled by bearing, and a historian's work is done with a needle and a lens and takes about as long as you would expect. When a herd crosses an archive-site it eats forty centuries of the record, and the law says it may, and the law has never been amended.

The census form has a line at the top for the rind. It is filled in with the number one. There have been four motions to change that to zero, on the grounds that it is not an animal, and two to change it to a very large number, on the grounds that it is not one.

## The heads

The nearest thing to a tree is a hole with a collar on it.

Where a fracture reaches the surface, something comes up. The body is a film lining the crack, kilometers of it, thin as a varnish and black as the seams it lives in; the visible part is a head, a wax-and-mineral lip the width of a table or a room, warm, faintly tacky, breathing oil. Not a root — a root is a thing that seeks. This is plumbing that grew.

The heads drink the buried rind. All that oil the skin laid down and seeped into the fractures, all that carbon put away in the black seams while the salt rose over it: the heads bring it back. They are the Sill's only means of remembering out loud. The food web here is a loop with a fourteen-kilometer detour through deep time, and the heads are the place where the past is handed back to the present, a spoonful a day.

So they are wealth, and they are fixed, and they are inherited, and they are named. Families take their names from heads and heads take their names from families and after a few thousand years the two are indistinguishable. Marriage is a merger of tapping rights, negotiated over decades by people who have met twice. A head buds along its fracture and a daughter surfaces thirty meters off a century later, and there is a whole quiet branch of law about whether the daughter belongs to the mother's house.

A cup of water kills a head for ten years. This is the actual content of the water taboo. Children learn it as cliffs, but the adults know it as inheritance: the thing that can end your family in an afternoon falls out of the sky a few times a century, and when it does the ward crews go out with tongs and glass tarpaulins and they cover the heads first. The people can walk. The heads cannot.

## The herds

A grazer is a low wide dome of dry plate, lacquered in a varnish it secretes from what it licks, so that its shell is polymerized pasture: a piece of the rind that got up and began to walk. It moves three meters a day. It cannot see much. It does not need to.

The herds speak in charge and scent-oil, and this means that a herd's mind is not in any of its bodies. It is a field, a gradient of intention laid over a few square kilometers of rock, sloping slowly toward the good grazing, and an individual is a place where the field is briefly dense. A decision takes four days to arrive at the front of the herd. From a tower you can watch the herd not decide, for a week, and then be somewhere else.

There are names. The names are older than the refining industry — older than the shafts, older than the code, older than the arguments about the horizon. Herd-law is the oldest law on the Sill and it consists almost entirely of transcriptions: what the herd did, written down, and then treated as precedent. The judges consult the stain-maps the way other jurisdictions consult statutes. Damages are assessed in regrowth-years. A lawsuit takes a generation because a generation is how long it takes to know what was done, and a case concluded in one generation is considered brisk, and the parties usually die before the finding and their children receive it as a sort of inheritance.

The white line bends around the herds. It has always bent around the herds. The surveyors' arguments about the curvature of the horizon are the second-oldest argument on the plateau and are conducted with real affection, and the herd-law is not an argument at all.

## The moths

Palm-sized, four meters of trailing filament, no wingbeat ever. They charge at the gradient and ride the thermal and they are the only fast thing on the Sill, and consequently they do all the errands.

They carry rind propagule from stain to stain. They carry the heads' pollen-analogue along the fractures. They run the white line — the sintered stripe holds a different charge from the raw pavement and stands a little proud of it, and there is a raised gradient along eleven hundred kilometers of maintained salt that a moth can feel and follow like a hand-rail. The line has its own fauna: sinter-mites, a whitened crust-thing that grows nowhere else and dies wherever the drifts bury the stripe.

Which means the four-year walkers are not merely devout. They carry inoculum in the seams of their lacquer, and they kneel every few hundred meters to press a thumb of it into a buried section, and they are how the line reproduces. The devotion is agronomy. Nobody remembers this. If you told a walker, she would say yes, obviously, and keep walking, because the Sill's people are not sentimental about useful things being holy.

The moths breed in the standing storm. Eight kilometers of silent turning dust, phase-locked to the slab's daily breath, six centuries at one coordinate: it is a nursery, and the fine fraction it drops is husk and spore and frass, and the ward that lives by harvesting it is selling pasture-seed to the whole plateau and has been for longer than anyone has been keeping count. They go in on tethers. There are families who have gone in on tethers for nine generations and consider the storm a room in their house.

The moth-drovers steer them with charged poles. The tower crews cut them loose with hooked poles and apologize. Both facts are in the census, which lists the moths as livestock, and the apology is not.

## The tenders

They were made. This is the one origin anybody is sure of, and it is the least interesting origin on the plateau.

Feral since the ninth century, grazing scale and rust, breeding in the culverts and laying ceramic eggs that the ground fires at blood heat for eleven months. They have territories. They have a mating season, which is an exchange of parts: two tenders meet, open, swap a component, and go away altered, and descent is therefore a heraldry of serial stamps. There are tenders walking around with a governor assembly cast in the ninth century, and there was a lawsuit about one, and it took forty years, and the finding was that the part belonged to the animal.

They still maintain. Badly, out of sequence, on a schedule that has drifted into ritual. They patch the white line ahead of the ward crews and sometimes patch it wrong. They polish the arrises off new buildings — they get there before the wind does, they are the only impatient thing on the Sill, and the architects have quietly stopped fighting it and begun computing the tender-work into the true drawing along with the six-hundred-year rounding. A building is now designed to be finished by two eroders, one of them alive.

They have a parasite: a small biological mite-thing that eats the lacquer of their seals and lives in the gap between the two chemistries, the only creature on the plateau that is at home in both.

The proposal to distinguish tenders from fauna in the census was defeated, and the arguments were good. The census's working test of an animal is not metabolic or genetic. It is: *does it give way when you come*. The tenders give way. So do the herds, so do the moths, so eventually do the slow ones. The test is a courtesy test. It has been the test since the code was written, and it is the same test as citizenship, which is the ability to reach out and touch the line, and the Sill's people see no reason to have two different kinds of question for the two different kinds of neighbor.

## The slow ones

Cart-sized. Pleated. Standing in the open for eleven or fourteen years.

They are not idle. Their shells are folded into deep pleats and the pleats are farmed: rind grows in them, thick and dark and unlicked, a hectare of pasture folded into a square meter of animal, and the slow one stands in the ordinary white noon and lets it grow for a decade and then eats the whole of it in a season, one long meal, and then stands again. It is the same strategy as a grazer's, with the movement taken out. Whether the slow ones are grazers that stopped, or a separate line that arrived at stopping from the other direction, is a disagreement eleven generations old and conducted entirely in polite footnotes.

Children put offerings in the folds. The offerings are seed. Every child on the Sill has inoculated a slow one, and every slow one carries a pasture composed of small gifts from people who are now dead, and this is understood, and is still an offering.

Certain contracts require a slow one as witness. Not for its testimony — it has none. For its presence. You want a witness who will still be standing in the same place when the case concludes, and there is no other kind. The great treaties of the Long City are witnessed by animals, and the animals are named in the documents, and the road is surveyed around them, and when one moves the ward records the bearing and the hour and declares a holiday, because a move means a birth or a death or an exhaustion, and in any of those cases something has changed that will not change again for a hundred years.

## The people

Four thousand of them, strung out in twenty-odd shaft-heads across nine hundred kilometers, each lamp forty kilometers from the next. They are oil-bodied, as everything here is. They varnish themselves against the sun and the wind, and their skin is dark and glossy and warm, and they are, in the plain biological sense, natives: descended from wet stock, converted, engineered by the same hands that made the tenders, so long ago that the conversion is not history but geology.

They speak, and are embarrassed about it. Voice is the vulgar channel, used for work and for children and for the muttered apology to a moth. The important things are said by scent-oil and by touch. There is a grammar of wrists.

On the inner forearm the lacquer is kept thin — a bare patch, deliberately unscaled, kept bare so that the hour can be felt: when the ward discharges the mast, the hair rises on every arm within two kilometers, and that is the clock. Some people are quiet-armed and feel nothing. They read the hour off the arms around them, and it is nobody's business, and a child learns to watch her grandmother's forearm the way children elsewhere learn to look at a wall.

To greet, you offer the bare patch. It is the thinnest skin you have, the most sensitive, the fastest to blister. You put it into another person's hand. Contracts are made this way. Marriages are made this way. The whole civic life of the Sill runs through the one part of the body that water kills quickest, and the phrase for good faith is *bare-armed*, and the phrase for a liar is *scaled*.

They have three senses and they have divided the world between them. Touch is the present, and the law: the line, the wrist, the hour, the ground warming your back at the third hour like a hand. Sight is labor and the future: the true drawing, the eroded form computed backward, the stain-maps, the ordinary noon. Hearing is the past. Sound arrives four hours late out of the inversion, a hammer struck in Ward Nine knocking at a door sixty kilometers away, and everyone knows what it is and nobody remarks on it, and there are people whose whole office is to sit in the dark at the receiving end and listen to what already happened. The night post is a hammer-code shouted into the duct at dusk on the assumption that it will land somewhere after midnight. It is a telegraphy of patience. It has never occurred to anyone to want it faster.

The census runs every eleven years and is the only crowd most people will ever stand in. Ages are given in censuses. Courtship is conducted at eleven-year intervals by people who have met three times, and the letters go with the walkers, and a betrothal can outlast a tower's arrises. The mayor is peripatetic; the office goes to whoever has most recently walked the line, on the theory that government is contact, and that a jurisdiction is a thing you can put your hand on.

## Below

The shafts go down after the black seams, and the crews report things.

Blind, slow, living in the fracture-film, breeding on the stroke — one tide a day, the slab's out-and-back, which they must feel as the whole world's pulse. They eat the same store the heads drink. They have possibly been there since before the rind learned to be dry.

They are not in the census. The census counts what can be reached; that is not a rule anyone wrote, it is simply what counting has always meant here, the same thing that makes a citizen. And there is a woman out of Ward Nineteen who has walked the whole line once, four years, and is considered to have done something reasonable with her life, and who has spent the forty years since going down shafts with a lamp and a length of sintered salt on a spool, laying the line down after her, and reaching out in the dark to touch something.

She is extending the franchise. She does it by hand, one shaft at a time, at the speed of the law. There is a suit about it. It is in its second generation and is expected to conclude briskly.

## The word

When a fragment comes down out of the sky, the crews go out with tongs and quicklime and glass cloth. They cover the heads. They find the ice in its scar of shocked glass and they bury it before it can melt, and there is a ceremony, and it is not celebratory.

Here is the thing about the ceremony. The word they use at the pit is not the hazard-word, the cliffs-word, the word children learn. It is an old kinship term. It occurs nowhere else in the language. The linguists have argued about it for three hundred years and the argument is old and pleasant and goes nowhere, but the cores from the Kerf are not ambiguous: down at the bottom of the oldest varnish there are laminae laid down in a chemistry that requires water. Nine million years of dry skin, and underneath it, a wet grandmother.

Everything on the Sill is descended from the thing it cannot touch.

At the pit the pallbearers bare their wrists and hold them out over the hole. It is what you do at a bedside. Then the quicklime goes in, and the glass cloth, and the salt, and they turn up their lacquer and go home along the white line, which bends around a herd somewhere out in the dark, and the ground gives off the day's heat into their backs the whole way, and at the tenth hour the mast discharges and every forearm rises at once, and they all know what time it is.
