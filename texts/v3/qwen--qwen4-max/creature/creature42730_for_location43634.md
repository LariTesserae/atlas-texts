## The Tenants

Start with the limpets, because the limpets have property.

The rings they wear were cut, once, from a single flange gasket — the pump-side gasket, ninety millimeters, gone brittle and then gone soft again in the wet. Nobody cut them. They were cut *around*: a limpet's foot will dissolve rubber over about four years, and what it leaves behind is a collar with a scalloped inner edge, and the scallops are the shape of the animal that made them. There are fewer collars every decade. Rubber is the one substance in the room that nothing here can lay down. Calcite is free, calcite is weather, but a gasket is an inheritance and the estate is being spent.

So the collars outlive their wearers, and a collar with nobody in it is a collar about to be buried. When a large limpet dies — and they die in place, glued, the foot going to slime over about a day — the vacancy is filled by a scramble that is the fastest thing in the room that is not water. A queue forms. It has been forming, sometimes, for eleven months: a line of limpets on the wall below the vacancy, sorted by size, not moving, not grazing, holding absolutely still in the way everything here holds still. When the collar frees, the largest moves up. Its own collar goes to the next. The chain runs down the wall in about ninety seconds, fourteen or fifteen animals, each one shifting into a dead neighbor's clothes, and then it is over and the room is quiet again and the queue re-forms, one shorter, to wait for the next death.

At the top of the chain, in the largest surviving ring — a hand's breadth of black rubber worn glassy on the inside by six hundred years of feet — is a limpet that lives on the hatch rim. It has never been below the third rung. It grazes the white beard inward-facing, keeping a lip of bare steel a finger wide, and it does this for the same reason its cousins work the valve seat, and we will come to that.

---

The felt is the room's memory of the machine.

They ate the lagging and kept the plan of it: sleeves, elbows, a flange jacket with its bolt-holes reproduced in living tissue, a bonnet-shaped mass on the north wall over nothing at all. That last one is the shape of a pump that was scrapped and carried out before the animals finished eating it. The felt kept the shape anyway. It buds inside the shape; the shape is what is inherited. There are eleven distinct forms on the walls and each is a fossil of a piece of equipment, held in the bodies of things that filtered the air the equipment used to move. When a form collapses — a sleeve sloughs off in a dry season, say — the room loses a fact about itself. Nothing writes it down. The clipboard is a fan of crystal now.

The worms are the barometer. Thousands to the meter in the construction seams, hair-thin, waving; they are feeding, but the wave is also a reading, and forty seconds before the pressure drops the draft at the floor reverses and the whole field of them changes phase, a slow shrug across nine meters of wall. Everything in the room reads the worms. The moths stop walking. The shrimp come up out of the basins' deep ends. The sheet, if it is mid-strike, finishes.

The moths are the lighting.

They cannot fly; the mica is too heavy and the air too still to be worth flying in, and their wings have gone to something else. In the eleven minutes they stand up on their legs and lift the scales and *angle* them, and the coin of daylight coming down the sun-pipe hits ten thousand broken dial-faces and is thrown everywhere the sun-pipe does not reach — under the pipe runs, into the north wall's shadow, down into the bowls. This is why there are algae films in places with no line of sight to the mirror head. The room has a second sun and it is made of gauge delamination and it comes on with the rain.

The shrimp are the blood. Twelve pools, twelve chemistries, no connection between them for five hours and forty-nine minutes — and then the basins overtop and the floor is a single shallow sheet and everything goes everywhere. Eleven minutes of trade. Sugar of rust moves from the sweet cold bowl to the brackish warm one; the thing with too many mouths gets its shrimp; genotypes cross the room in a body length of water. Then the dams re-establish and the pools are islands again and the market closes.

They leave a doomed pool early. Always. Three or four rains before it spills, the traffic out of it goes one-way, and nobody has established what they are reading. Possibly the pitch of the dam's tick. Crystal taking up its lattice makes a fingernail-on-comb sound and a dam under load makes a slightly different one, and there is a whole nation of animals in this room whose entire sensory life is dedicated to hearing a difference you would need instruments to find.

---

Go smaller. The mosaic has fills, and the fills have fills.

There is a ciliate that lives in the hinge of a moth's wing and nowhere else, and eats the grease the moth combs in there, and is therefore the reason the wing still opens. There is a worm that occupies only the seam between two adjacent limpet collars, so its entire habitat is a shape that exists only when two specific animals are neighbors, and it dies when either moves. There is a rider on the sheet — a flat, colorless, four-millimeter thing that lives in the predator's mucus and eats it and travels with it, and when the sheet lifts off a kill the rider stays behind in the bare oval with a gut full of the predator's leavings and a cargo of everything the sheet had picked up on the way.

That is why the succession is a clock. The wound arrives with its own dressing. Hour two, the film. Hour six, the worms in from the margins. Day one, felt spat. Hour forty-eight, the tessellation closed and *different* from what was there — the sheet has made a patch of young room inside the old room, and the mosaic stays a mosaic because something keeps punching holes in it. There is only ever one sheet. Two would find each other. The room's quiet is a budget and the sheet spends nearly all of it.

The valve animal has no name that isn't a joke. Soft, pale, folded, grown into the seat so thoroughly that the iron and the tissue have negotiated a border neither can now find. It has a wedge. It has the interior geometry of a gate valve, which is to say it has the shape of the thing it ate, like the felt, except the thing it ate is still there and still works and the animal *is* its working part. It cannot see, cannot crawl, cannot go anywhere, and it can only close when the pressure differential lets it — which means it can only fire when the machine turns over, which means the one animal in the room with a job instead of a habit is a slave to a schedule it also enforces.

And the seat is clean. Generation on generation of small glassy limpets graze the calcite off it in a spiral, forty-day lives, and every six hours and some minutes a hand's breadth of them goes down the pipe with a *thock*.

Look at their shells. They are the only smooth shells in the wet room. Everything else here is being buried alive and calling it success; the seat-grazers are the one lineage under selection to *resist* it, to stay bare, to stay glassy, to not accrete. An encrusted grazer jams the seat. A jammed seat stops the rain. A stopped rain kills the room in about nine days. So the room breeds, at cost, a caste of animals whose whole purpose is to hold back the room's own direction, and it eats them for their trouble, and they are also the ones on the hatch rim.

Two clean places. Both of them mouths.

---

Now the part that is not in the chamber.

Beyond the north wall, in the governor housing of the compressor, there is a mass of felt — the same species as the sleeves, the same felt, blind, never once in the light — grown through the flyballs and around the weights. It swells in the rain. It dries between. Its water is part of the governor's mass, and the governor's mass is the setpoint, and the setpoint is the interval.

Six hours *and some minutes*. The some minutes are the animal.

It is slow. It answers in months. After a big sheet kill the interval lengthens; when the walls go crowded and the drip's notes climb, it shortens; it has never been observed to do anything and it is the only thing here doing anything at all. Everything else in the wet room lives fast — forty days, eleven minutes, one spawning season — and tiles surfaces the instant they exist. This one lives at the speed of the walls. Its generation time is the accretion rate. Everything else is a tenant.

The fifty-hertz hum is bolted to it. That hum has not stopped since before the tallies. Nobody has traced what the motor drives. It is possible that the felt cannot count and does not need to; it is possible that fifty cycles a second for six hours is simply a quantity of shaking, and that a body which swells and shrinks can integrate a quantity of shaking without ever knowing a number. The room's animals feel that hum in their teeth and hold still to it and the thing that makes their weather is listening to the same hum from the inside.

---

And the boot.

Four and a stroke. Nine. There are nine rungs.

The chalk is not a visit count. It is a rung count, and it gets re-chalked when it changes, and the ladder loses a rung a decade, and so the surveyor comes down about once in ten years, reads the number off the ladder, writes it on the wall, signs the permit by the coin of light, and goes back up. The form goes into a drawer. The drawer is dry. Nothing lives in it.

They step once. One print, at the ladder's foot, and then they stand — because they know, or because someone told them once, that a dropped wrench is a massacre, and that walking is a series of small dropped wrenches. They stand in the same place every decade. The room has a callus there: a boot-shaped thinness in the tessellation, kept thin, colonized only by the fast opportunists, the hour-two film and the day-one spat.

There is an animal that lives only in that callus.

It is a millimeter long, a seed-shrimp sort of thing, with a two-valved carapace of calcite, and for ten years at a time it is closed and cemented into the wall and indistinguishable from the wall. It is a mineral. Then a boot comes down and lifts and the pressure wave and the crushed film and the sudden bare patch reach it, and it opens, and it has one day. It grazes, it molts twice, it mates in the shallow water left in the tread marks, and if it is lucky its day contains a rain, and it spawns in the rain like everything else, and its young seal themselves into the new crystal that the rain lays down, and the print fills, and they wait.

Its entire active life is a day, plus eleven minutes. It has never missed a rain. It has attended four.

The fern came down the same way — spores in a tread, a passenger, an accident that stayed. Four fronds, because four is what eleven minutes of thrown mica-light every six hours will pay for, and there has never been a fifth. The moths angle toward it. Nobody has established why.

So: the hatch is not a door. It is the second mouth, and the room keeps it clean the way it keeps the valve seat clean, with the same glassy caste, at the same cost. The dry world's flour comes down that throat every day, and once a decade something enormous comes down it and stands in one place and goes away again, and a nation wakes up in the print and lives its whole life and is buried.

The number will be eight.

Except that under the hatch, at the height where the ninth rung used to be, there is a boss of new white growth, and it is the wrong shape for crystal. It is horizontal. It is the right length. It is spaced correctly against the two below it, and it is dense where crystal is usually lace, and it has been there long enough to have a grazer on it, keeping it bare.

It will probably not hold a hand. It is getting sharper, which here means it is getting younger, which here means it is being made.

At evening the coin climbs the west wall and goes out. The hum continues. The basins count their notes. In the dark a hundred thousand small bodies hold absolutely still, and one of them, sealed in the wall at the ladder's foot, is nine years into a wait it has no way of knowing the length of, and the room ticks, and holds its two mouths open, and gets a little younger, and there is a little less room.
