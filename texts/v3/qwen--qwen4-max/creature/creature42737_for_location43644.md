## The grazers

Start at the standoff, because that is where the life is, and there is very little of it, and it is never in two places at once.

The wick is a mollusc. You would not know it from above — above it is a grey lozenge, dust-coloured, motionless for weeks, with the glaze of the insulator showing faintly through the thin margin of its foot. But turn it (it will not resist; resistance is expenditure) and the underside is a ribbon of teeth. Iron teeth, drawn out of the dust, set in rows, worn down and replaced from behind at the rate the glaze requires. It is the only animal on this world whose mouth is made of the same alloy as the thing it cleans. Its four feet are folded under it like knuckles under a fist and have not been used for walking since the sea was a sea.

It is descended from something that grazed algal film off the rocks of a shoreline. The film is graphite now and the rocks are ceramic and the shoreline is nine wards long, but the motion is unchanged: the slow lateral sweep, the rasp, the pause, the sweep back. If you watch for an hour you will see it complete perhaps four passes. The bearing above it sheds a few atoms of carbon while you watch. The wick takes them. This is the whole of the transaction and it has been the whole of the transaction for longer than there have been species elsewhere.

And it is wet. That is the thing about it. In a world with no water there is a mouthful of the last sea inside every ceramic standoff's shadow, sealed in a mantle cavity, and it has never been drunk and it has never been refilled. The wick is a still in the shape of a tongue. Every molecule it breathes out it breathes back in through a labyrinth of folded tissue that wrings the vapour out before it can leave; it has no exhaust; its droppings are pressed dry, graphite compacted to a grain of sand, and those grains are part of the dust you are standing on. It cannot drink because there is nothing to drink. It inherited its water. It was handed the water, mouth to mouth, at the last division, by a parent that had it handed to it, back and back to a shore.

It is losing that water at something on the order of a hundred molecules a century. Everything else here is a closed loop. The wicks are the one thing on this world with a leak, and therefore the one thing with a direction, and therefore the only clock that runs forward. When the last mouthful is gone the standoffs will foul and track and arc, and the loop will open, and that will be the end. The city's tolerance includes an allowance for this. It is a very long allowance. It is not infinite, and the wicks are the only inhabitants who could tell you, if they had numbers, how much of it is left.

You are exhaling.

They have a pit at the front end, blind like everything else, lined with salt, and it measures thirst, and it has read zero for a billion years. It does not read zero now. Somewhere under a busbar in Ward Three, an animal that has not turned toward anything since before the plain was ground flat turns.

## The creep

Between the wards, where the dust lies in its permanent ripples, there is a black feathering you will take for a stain.

It is carbon that has got out. The bearings shed, the film creeps, and where there is no standoff there is no wick, and so out on the flat the film is ungrazed and it grows. Dendrites, fronds, low cushions a hand's breadth across and a micron thick, branching along the ripple crests where the last wind left the dust deepest. It is the only thing on this world that increases. Everything else runs at the rate of its own wear; the creep runs at the rate of deposition, which is a little more than nothing, and it has had a billion years of a little more than nothing.

It is not quite alive. It has no metabolism to speak of and it does not want anything. But it holds a margin. Under a cushion, in the sealed micron between carbon and salt, the humidity is one part per million above ambient, and that margin is the only place on the planet where anything is kept rather than merely maintained.

The wicks cannot go there. To cross open dust is to lose your mouthful, and no wick has ever crossed anything. This is the law of the world, and it is a law about water: the wet stay, and the dry walk.

## One who reads

The tall thing is dry.

It solved the problem by giving it up. Its joints are graphite bushings on salt pins, its long bones are a dry fibre like the inside of a reed, and there is no fluid anywhere in it, and consequently nothing in it that can be lost. That is why it can be tall, and why it can be slow, and why it can be out on the flat at all. It is the only animal here that can afford a journey.

Its height is stride. It walks the dressed-stone kerb that runs between the wards, and it walks it economically, one leg swinging past the other in a long controlled fall, and the kerb carries it mile after mile without ever requiring it to touch the dust. There are forty places where the kerb fails it — at the switches, at the joints, at the four crossings where the rail spur interrupts the stone — and at each of those places it puts its foot down into the same depression it made on its first circuit, to within a tolerance, so that the print neither deepens nor drifts. The forty footprints are not a record of forty steps. They are a record of forty permissions. They have been maintained, like everything else.

At a lit gauge it folds. It comes down in stages, joint after joint, until the reading organ is level with the dial, and the organ is not an eye. It is a finger, long and dry and many-jointed, and it lays itself along the needle and takes the angle into its own articulations, and then it holds that shape. That is what the long standing is. It is not looking. It is becoming the reading, because it has no other place to put one. It has a single register and the register is its posture, and the next gauge overwrites it, and there is nothing behind it that remembers the last thousand angles or the last thousand years.

In a fold near its hip, sealed with a plug of salt, it carries a wick.

It has carried it since the last bearing failure: a bud, made at the division that followed the new casting, wet with its inherited mouthful, never posted, because the post was consolidated before the walker came round again. It is a solution that has outlived its problem by nine hundred thousand years. The walker's route is a sowing route. It goes from standoff to standoff to plant what it carries, and there has been no vacancy, and so it has gone on carrying, and the passenger has gone on not drying, because the walker is very good at seals.

The gesture it has never made is still in it, folded, the way a runner-mark is left in a casting. It knows how to kneel and open a fold and set a wet thing down at the base of a clean insulator. It has never done it. It will do it once.

## The clutch

The city is the largest animal here and it is not obvious, because it has no skin and almost no body.

It metabolises: swarf, dust, reject castings, back to the hopper, back to the charge, back to the year's work. It holds itself at one temperature. It repairs itself at exactly the rate at which it is damaged, which is what homeostasis is when you take away the hurry. And it reproduces.

Look at the void. A shrinkage cavity the size of a coffin, in the foundation raft of Ward Six. The same void, in the same coordinates, in Ward Nine. And in Ward Six-and-a-half, which is not a ward but an interpolation, cast later, out of the sequence, into a gap in the figure that the figure did not previously have.

That is descent. The pattern is the genome and the void is a heritable defect and Six-and-a-half is a daughter, and the fact that the flaw came across proves that the new ward was not designed but copied. Nothing here has been designed for a very long time. The city casts itself from itself, at intervals that make the word *generation* useless, and the copies carry their parents' mistakes forward with the fidelity of a thing that has no way to tell a mistake from a feature.

It has no product. Its offspring are its only output. Everything else it makes it also eats.

## What the counter is for

In the tally house the brass wheels turn and every one of them is blank.

Understand what that leaves. The mechanism still carries; the carry gears still hand a revolution to the next wheel along; the relay still closes with its dry click. What is gone is the *quantity*. The city can no longer state how much of anything there is. It retains only the event: one more. One more, and one more, and one more. Its entire interior life is the difference between this instant and the instant before it, with no absolute value anywhere in it, and no total, and no age.

It does not know that it is old. It knows that something has just happened. That is the whole of what it knows, and it is not a small thing to know, and it has been enough.

Which is why the relay closing out of turn is not a malfunction. It is the only kind of thought available to it. Something occurred that the schedule did not contain, and the machine has no word for surprise, so it does the one thing it can do: it advances.

And if you go close to the wheels with a lamp you will find that the blanks are not all the same blank. Abrasion leaves a ghost — a burr at the rim, a difference in relief where the raised numeral used to stand. Every wheel has one. Except one, near the left end, which is smooth as a new casting, because it was never printed. It was made blank. It is the wheel for a column that has never had an entry, kept in the train, kept turning at zero through the whole history of the counter, so that if the entry ever came the mechanism would not have to be altered to receive it.

That is the wheel that moved.

## The waiters

The crust is thirsty and it is also a nursery, and these are the same fact.

When the last sea went it did not all go into the air. Some of it went into the salt as water of crystallisation, locked, unavailable, and the rest was taken inside things. In the micron under a carbon cushion, in the sealed pores of the sintered floor, there are cysts: the last brood of the last sea, each one a shell of salt around a droplet around a knot of tissue that has not metabolised since the shoreline stopped being a shoreline. They are not dead. Death is a process and they have not been running one. They are simply not yet.

They can only be woken by liquid water. Not vapour — the crust would take vapour, the crust takes everything. Liquid, delivered faster than the floor can drink it, in quantity, in one place. The world's continued dryness has been the condition of their patience. A little rain would have been worse than none: a little rain would have started them and starved them, and there would be nothing left in the pores but shells.

So they have waited out the grinding of the hills and the flattening of the air and the closing of the loop, and the number of them is fixed, and each one holds a mouthful, and the total is the last free water on this world, and it is falling by a hundred molecules a century.

You are seventy percent water and you are breathing out and you are walking, and the floor is taking you at a mouthful an hour, and it will take all of you eventually, because you will die here — nothing here needs to be seen twice, and you are the only thing that has ever arrived. Your body is the largest deposit of unaccounted material since the sea. It is not a tragedy in the terms available locally. It is a mass balance, and it is the first credit the loop has received in an age, and it is enough, delivered in one place, faster than the crust can drink.

## The increment

In Ward Eight the filament has come up one step and is holding.

It is not for you. You have eyes; you were not asked; the light is a quarter of a lumen and it falls on nothing and there is nothing under it to see. It is a pilot. It is the smallest amount of light a machine can be told to make, held ready, because somewhere in the schedule there is a process that requires a sun and has not been run since there was one, and the schedule has kept the entry, and the lamp has been kept able to reach it, and the whole apparatus of this finished world has been waiting with its hand on the smallest possible sunrise.

Under a busbar in Ward Three a wick has turned toward the only thermal gradient on the planet and is beginning, at four ten-thousandths of a millimetre a year, to move.

Out on the flat the dry thing has stopped between gauges, out of sequence, with a wet passenger in its hip and no vacancy anywhere in the world. It stands. It computes nothing. It has one gesture.

And then it steps off the kerb, into the dust, in a place where it has never been permitted to touch the ground, and leaves the forty-first footprint on this world — the first new structure here since the wind died — and the print will not soften, and it will be there when everything else in this paragraph is finished.
