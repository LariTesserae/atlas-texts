## The Residents

In the rust seam, and in two other seams that are less obvious because they do not stain, there is a smear you would take for a discoloration until you put your face close and saw that it has a grain. It runs with the weep, not with the bedding of the rock. It stops where the weep stops. Under a lens it resolves into filaments laid every which way, and along the filaments, at intervals, beads — small bright spheres packed inside the thread like a string of swallowed pearls. Those are sulphur. The film is carrying a pantry. In a room where nothing is stored, where every arriving molecule is escorted straight through and out the far fissure, the film has learned to hold something back, and it holds it inside itself, where holding is possible.

It is not a plant and it has no opinion about the light. What it eats is a difference. The water comes up loaded with iron and sulphur in their reduced, hungry forms, and the air, sliding down around the two bends of the dogleg, brings a whisper of oxygen, and where those two cargoes meet there is a small unpaid debt in the chemistry of the Cup. The film lives on that debt. It is the only debtor's advantage left in the place. Everything else here has come to rest; this one reaction has not, and the whole living population of the room is threaded through it like wire through a needle's eye.

Which means the Cup has a geography, though you would not see it. At the head of the oval, where the water surfaces in its unseen throat, there is nothing living at all. That water has been underground for a long time and has spent everything it had to spend; it is sterile as boiled milk, and it stays sterile for some metres, until the sky's contribution has had time to work down and mix. Then the seams begin. Then, past the last boss, the water gathers itself and goes into the fissure and takes its leftovers away, and there is nothing living there either. Life in the Cup occupies a middle band, three patches of it, islands of rust in a country of clean ivory, and its total extent you could cover with two hands.

## Forty-one

The snails are the size of a thumbnail and you can see through them. The shell is a pale horn-coloured spiral with the animal sitting inside it like a thumb in a glass glove: the dark coil of the gut, the pale mass of the foot, the fine comb of the gill working. There are forty-one. There have been forty-one for as long as anyone has troubled to count, which is not the same as saying that these are the same forty-one.

The number is not a population. It is a measurement. It is the width of the seam expressed in animals. The film lays itself down at a certain rate — a rate set by how much oxygen can come around a bend in a rock a hundred miles from the rain that made all this — and a snail can crop a certain area in a day, and the arithmetic of those two facts comes out at forty-one and has come out at forty-one since the plumbing sealed. Add a snail and one starves. Kill a snail and something hatches. There is no forty-second snail. There is only, occasionally, a vacancy, and the vacancy does not last.

They keep the film at exactly one grazing deep, which is to say they keep it permanently on the edge of being gone, which is to say they keep it young. An ungrazed mat would thicken and choke on its own middle and go anaerobic and die back. The snails are the reason it does not. They are not eating the film so much as farming it, without intention, at a distance of one radula's width, forever.

You will not find an empty shell. This is worth standing still for. The water is saturated; it cannot dissolve a shell any more than it can dissolve a chisel mark. A dead snail's shell ought to lie among the blocks with the chert flakes and the copper wedge, accumulating, one more thing added to a room that adds nothing — and there are none. Forty-one animals, forty-one shells, no spares. The others rasp them down. It takes a while. Lime is not scarce here in the water but it is expensive to pull out of solution, and a shell already made is a shell already paid for, and so the calcium of the Cup goes round and round inside its animals and never leaves. The stuff of those shells is older than any snail wearing it. It has been shell many times. It has been the outer whorl of a hundred animals and it will be the outer whorl of a hundred more, and there is a fixed quantity of it in the room, about forty-one thumbnails' worth, passing along.

The shells have no growth lines. Look for the ridges that record a winter, a drought, a bad year, a spawning: there are none, because there is nothing to record. The spiral is the only shape in the Cup that looks like time and there is no time in it. You cannot age these animals. You can only measure them, and the measurement will be the same next year.

And they are, as far as anyone has been able to determine, all the same animal. No males, no mating, no recombination; a daughter is a copy; the forty-one are one lineage wearing forty-one shells in three patches of rust. So it is tempting to say that there is one snail in the Cup, distributed.

That is almost right, and it is the kind of almost that is worth being wrong about for a while before you correct it.

## The correction

Because here is a thing the water cannot do and the snail can: the snail can lay something down.

The whole room is at rest. Nothing precipitates, nothing dissolves, no crystal grows, no rind thickens; the chemistry has been finished since before there were people to be frightened of it. And yet the shell gets made. And yet the beads of sulphur get packed into the filament. And yet, on one of the forty-one, on the second seam, low down where the weep runs into a joint, there is a thickened place on the outer lip, a clumsy pale repair where the shell was broken and was mended.

It is the only scar in the Cup that is not a tool mark.

Living things can push uphill. They can spend the iron's small debt on making a thing that was not there and putting it where the water would never have put it. In a room where chemistry has stopped, biology is the last remaining chemistry — the only process still capable of adding, of building, of remembering an injury in stone. Everything in the Cup that is still being *made* is being made by something alive. The scallops and the flutings and the bell-holes and the poured-wax bosses were the work of a hungrier water that has finished its work; the snails have inherited the job at a scale of a fingernail's width, and they have not finished, and they will not.

And a repair is not a rate. A repair is a history. That animal broke its shell somewhere on the polished ivory between the second seam and the third, in the barren crossing where there is nothing to eat, and it survived the crossing, and it made itself a patch, and the patch is still there, and it is the only object in fourteen metres of rock that means *something happened*.

So: one animal in forty-one places, yes. But not one animal in forty-one identical places. Somewhere in the count there is an individual, and it is the only individual in the room, and it does not know it.

## The others

In the finger-breadth joints, where the snails cannot go, the isopods work the leavings. White, unhurried, articulated like something assembled from small pale tiles. They lost their eyes a long time ago and kept the sockets, dark specks under the cuticle that are not for seeing and are not for anything. Their world is a slot: a body plan for a crack, flat enough to enter, segmented enough to turn, armoured on both faces because in a joint there is no up. They moult in halves, back first, and eat the cast, both halves, because a shed skin is a fortune in lime and there is no leaving it lying about.

The flatworm is a scrap of wet paper the colour of the water and it is the top predator of the Cup. It has one opening. It hunts by taste, laying its front edge down and reading the film's chemistry off the stone, and when it finds an isopod it flows over it and takes it in and then must wait, gliding, until it can do that again. It is the only thing here that could be said to be looking for something.

It also runs backwards. When food is short it does not starve so much as shrink: it consumes its own tissue in an orderly way and gets smaller and younger, and when times improve it grows out again along the same plan. It has no fixed size and no fixed age and no obvious schedule for dying. If anything now alive in the Cup was alive on the morning the quarrymen set down their tools and did not pick them up, it is this, at some size it has been many times before, in a joint it may have used for a hundred thousand years, having eaten and shrunk and eaten and grown through the entire history of the language the work was ordered in.

Between them, these three dispose of everything. The flatworm takes the soft parts, the snails rasp the shell, the isopods take the crumbs and the cast skins and whatever falls. There has never been a corpse in the Cup. There is no rot here because rot is a thing that happens where death outpaces disposal, and here the disposal has been waiting, perfectly sized, since before the deaths began.

The drip falls into the third basin every nine seconds. Nothing in the room can hear it. The snails have eyes at the bases of their tentacles, real eyes, unemployed, because an eye is a device for detecting change and the light in the Cup does not change; there has never been a shadow on the film that was not a person. The isopods feel the drip. It arrives through the joints as a pressure event, a small shove, every nine seconds, and it has been arriving every nine seconds for longer than there have been isopods, and it means nothing, and it has never meant anything, and it is the only clock in a place with no hours.

## The one at the top of the stair

High in the dogleg, where the fissure has not yet finished turning its back on the world, there is a spider.

It is the only resident with a weather. Its food comes from a country that has soil in it: midges lifted off some coastal scrub a hundred metres up and blown down around the two corners, arriving in gusts and slack periods, in numbers that mean something about the wind and the season and the state of things elsewhere. The spider alone keeps a calendar, and the calendar is not the Cup's. It eats its web each time it rebuilds, so even the imported dust does not stay; what it drops falls into the third basin and is exported. It sits at the border and takes the world's small change and passes almost none of it on.

The spider is also the only resident that will see you coming. Everything else in the room is blind, or functionally blind, or too small to resolve you. You will lean over the lip and put your shadow on a mat that has never had a shadow, and forty-one animals will fail to notice the first eclipse of their world, and above you, in the bend, something with eight eyes will register a change in the weather and go still.

Once, in the memory of the oldest woman in the coastal village, a bird fell in. It is the one event. It was got out with a pole and a boy on a rope, and it was eaten, and the rope-groove at the lip is a little smoother there. That is the last thing taken out of the Cup, and it was taken by hand, and everything that has ever been taken out of the Cup was taken by hand, because nothing here leaves on its own except the water and what the water can carry.

## The ones who left

They are residents too, in the way that a held breath is a kind of breathing.

Whoever cut the thirty-one steps came for the ivory sinter, which cuts like hard cheese underwater and sets like chalk in the dry air above, and they cut trenches straight and true between the natural bosses, and they freed blocks all but one corner, and they left a copper wedge skinned green in a trench and a hammerstone of a granite that occurs nowhere within four days' walk, and one block on the twelfth step, and nine notches beside the rope-groove, and a low dry-stacked wall at the head of the stair that nobody has satisfactorily explained.

In the pocket of insoluble clay — the only clay, the grit left over from an enormous amount of dissolved mountain — there are five bare footprints, small, held perfectly. They survive for a reason that is almost funny: clay has no iron in it and no sulphur, so there is nothing there worth grazing, so nothing alive has ever had a reason to go across it. The one surface in the Cup capable of remembering is a desert inside a desert, and that is why it remembers.

And nothing in the room told them to stop. There is no dark to end a shift, no cold to drive anyone up, no falling water level, no foul air, no hour. The light was the same when they left as when they came. Whatever ended the work came from outside, like the midges, and it went away again, and the Cup did what the Cup does, which is hold what it is given. The stone that was going to be taken out next is still waiting to be taken out next. The chisel strokes stand as crisp as the hour they were struck, and will stand so, because the water cannot take anything more and the air cannot give anything back. Poverty is an excellent preservative.

## The quarry is still open

Here is what you see, finally, when you have been down there long enough for your pupils to stop being useful and your skin to stop reporting a boundary.

A snail on the rust seam has a ribbon under its head studded with several thousand teeth, and it draws that ribbon across the mat and takes off a layer, and the layer grows back at exactly the speed it is taken, and it has been doing this without interruption in this room since the draft sealed. That is quarrying. It is the same operation the men were performing with the copper wedge and the foreign granite: find the seam, cut the face, take the stone out, keep the face true. The snails take a fingernail's width and put it all back. The men took blocks the size of oxen and did not put anything back, and carried them up thirty-one steps into a country where stone can be weathered, and those blocks are somewhere else now, softened, gone to lime and mortar and dust, unrecognisable.

So there are two kinds of sharp edge in the Cup and they are kept by opposite means. The tool marks stay sharp because nothing can touch them. The grazing fronts stay sharp because they are never permitted to stop being made. One is kept by the exhaustion of the world and one is kept by its appetite, and only one of them is alive, and from a metre away in that light they look exactly the same.

The Cup crossed over from being made into being kept. That is true of the rock. It is not quite true of the room. There is a fraction of this place that is still unfinished — a smear three seams wide, never thicker than a fingernail, where a debt is still being paid and something is still being built — and everything that lives here lives inside that fraction. That is the whole habitat. That is the entire country of the living, in a room fourteen metres down that has otherwise completed its business: the thin unfinished rind of a finished thing.

Put your palm flat on the rust. You will feel slickness, a faint yield, the film on a tooth. You will feel nothing happening, because nothing here happens at a speed a hand can read. Under your palm the mat goes on eating; in an hour the room will have made a fingernail's width of itself and taken the same amount away; forty-one animals will have been fed and one of them will still be carrying its old break; the drip will have fallen four hundred times into the third basin and nothing will have heard it; the spider will be waiting on a wind that belongs to another country.

Then take your hand off, and go up, and the shadow you made will close over, and the grazing will resume across the place where you were, at one grazing deep, taking the stone out and putting it back, taking the stone out and putting it back, in the last room in the world where the work has not stopped and nothing has ever been finished.
