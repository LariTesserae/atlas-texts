## The floor

Begin with the thing that is easiest to mistake for a place.

The mat is an individual. Not metaphorically — the sedge that makes its warp is one clone, a single rhizome system laid edge to edge under the moss, and if you dig at the rim and dig at the throat you are digging into the same plant. It has been the same plant for a very long time. It is also, at every point, less than seventy years old.

Both are true, and the way they are both true is the whole grammar of this bowl. The sedge grows at the lip and dies at the hole and slides between, and what persists is not any of its substance but the *shape of the sliding* — the gradient, the thickness, the particular quaking give of it under a boot, the way it piles on the rimward face of a tree and tears on the throatward face. A shape with moss passing through it. A flame is like this, and a river is like this, and a note held by an orchestra while the players change is like this, and there is no reason a floor should not be like this too.

So: the first resident is a rate that has learned to look like a surface. It eats stone dust and fog and its own dead. It is two meters thick and ankle-deep at the same time, because most of its thickness is water and the water is not really it. It has no center of perception and no edge it can defend. It is the oldest continuous living thing on the plateau and there is not one cell in it older than a human life.

Everything else here is a variation on that problem.

## The small hands

The sundews are the bowl's only predators with faces. Red, patient, beaded, standing in the blood-warm pools where nothing swims; a leaf of them looks like a handful of held breath. They close in about four minutes, which in this place is a violent act, an obscenity of speed. The bladderworts below the surface work faster still — a trapdoor, a pressure差 released, a midge larva gone in a millisecond — and this is the fastest thing that happens in the bowl by six orders of magnitude, and it happens ten thousand times a day, and nobody has ever seen it.

The prompt's rule holds: everything here that lives has hands or roots, and the roots are hands. The sundew's tentacle is a hand. The bladderwort's bladder is a hand that closes once. The bromeliad is a hand cupped around four liters of black water it will never let go of. The only hand in the bowl with a bone in it belongs to something you will probably not see.

And the trees are bowls inside the bowl. Each crown is a basin holding fog, and each crotch is a smaller basin holding rain, and each tank is a smaller basin holding leaves and a larva and sometimes a snail — a nested set of warm wet holes, each one full of noise if you were small enough to stand in it, each one dropping its overflow into the next. The private rain of a single tree can go on for six hours after the cloud has left the plateau. From below, in the ordinary sun, you stand in a shaft of light and get rained on by a tree that is manufacturing its own weather out of nothing but the fact of being wide.

## The shells

The snails are the bowl's problem made flesh.

A few dozen individuals. Hermaphrodites that cannot self. Eleven years to cross a tree. Do the arithmetic and the species should not exist; it should have winked out in the first century, a handful of agate shells dissolving unread into the peat.

They exist because they have solved the conveyor.

On the rimward side of every tree, where the mat piles up against the buttress in a compressed ramp, the floor is *fastened* — pressed hard enough against the trunk that a snail can come down the bark and onto the green without a gap to cross. It is the only such place. Everywhere else the mat slides past the trees with a shear you could put a finger into. The ramp is a gangplank.

And the mat, which erases everything, also *carries* everything.

So a snail descends. It has grazed one tree for twenty years and it will not graze it again. It comes down the wet warm bark to the ramp and lets the floor take it, and it rides — a hand's breadth a month, a meter and a bit a year — out into the open green, into the forty paces of ringing emptiness, where there is nothing to eat but the film of algae on the moss and nothing to hide under and sundews standing in every pool with their red hands open.

Almost all of them die out there. It takes twenty-five years to reach the next tree throatward, and a snail has perhaps forty years in it, and the ride costs it nearly everything it has. But one in a great many arrives at the next ramp, and climbs, and finds there another snail that has come down to leave.

The ramp is where they meet. The place where the current piles up is the place where the bodies pile up: flakes, seeds, shed bark, dead midges, shells, and once or twice in a millennium two living snails with their long thumbs of flesh unfolded, in the loudest place in the world, doing the only thing that cannot be done alone.

Then one goes up and one goes down, and the shells, when they drop, go into the peat and dissolve.

There is a second ferry. The knapper's fur grows algae in grooved hairs, and a snail cannot tell the difference between a moss beard and a moss beard that is slowly climbing. Some of the shells in this bowl have spent their entire lives on an animal, grazing its back, being carried between trees at the pace of a decision. They have never touched a leaf that was attached to anything. Their whole world is a warm gray slow thing that smells of fruit, and they die on it, and their shells dissolve in the fur's little private peat, and the animal carries the calcium around for a decade without knowing.

Four sightings in a hundred years. It is worth wondering how many of those were sightings of a beard with a shell in it.

## The nine

The trees are the only residents that hold still, and they pay for it in wood.

Each stands with its roots in a joint of the pavement and its trunk in a current, and it grows eccentric — thick on the upstream face, thin downstream, a leaning that no one has ever watched happen. Split a buttress and the rings are lopsided, a life spent bracing. In the ordinary years the rings are wide and pale; the trees here have never wanted water. But once in a long while the fog fails for a season, the crowns stop condensing, the private rain stops, the tanks go to black mud and the epiphytes crisp back — and the tree lays down a thin dark ring, the only drought record in a place that has no drought.

That is one archive: stationary, growing, unreadable to anything in the bowl, going back centuries.

The flakes are the other archive: mobile, erasing, seventy years deep and no deeper, perfectly legible to the one animal that cares.

The trees are sieves. Everything the mat carries that is too coarse to pass a buttress fetches up on the ramp and stays there, rotating slowly around the trunk as the trunk thickens, so that each tree wears a slowly turning collar of the bowl's whole coarse fraction. If you want to know what has happened here in the last seventy years, do not read the open floor. Read the ramp. It is the only place in the bowl where anything gathers.

The only other place is the hole.

And the nine are not a fact, they are a census. Look at the innermost: hollowed, leaning, its crescent moat merged with the lip of the throat so that the black water no longer stands in a scythe-blade curve but runs. It will go. When it goes the bowl will be a different instrument for a decade — the damping will change, the shafts will change, the fog will fall differently — and everyone alive here will spend the rest of their lives in the sound of that absence.

And in the moat of the fourth tree, where the mat pulls away and leaves bare wet pavement standing open, there is a seedling a meter high and forty years old. It could only germinate there. Nowhere else in the bowl can a seed reach stone; everywhere else the floor is two meters of living skin. The moats are the only nursery, and the moats are all on the throatward side, which means every generation of trees is founded downstream of the last, and the nave is walking toward the hole at the speed of centuries, and the ninth position is a queue.

Nine trees, standing far apart, in the spacing of columns. They are also standing in line.

## The hands

Now the animal.

Gray, arboreal, blood-warm — the same twenty-three degrees as the bark, the pools, the air, your palm. This matters more than it sounds. Nothing in this bowl is hotter or colder than anything else in it. There is no thermal edge anywhere. Put your hand in a pool and you cannot find where you stop; a snail on a knapper's back has no more idea of its boundary than the moss has. In a place this isothermal and this wet, individuality is not a sensation. It is a hypothesis, and most of the residents have no reason to entertain it.

The one exception is the edge. A knapped phonolite flake is the only sharp thing in the bowl — the only place where the world stops being warm and blurred and becomes a line. They make the only distinction here that can be felt.

The thumb is longer than the fingers and folds across them like a lid. The eyes are large, for fog-light and for the night. It moves at the speed of the mat: watch it and you see nothing, come back in a year and it is somewhere else. Its fur is a garden. Its gut is full of pulp.

The fruit is armored in a rind like green stone and will not open by any other means and will not germinate unless it is opened. So the tree needs the hands. And the hands need the edge, and the edge comes off the lip, and the whole animal is a hinge between a stone that breaks like glass and a seed that cannot wake without a wound.

Here is its life. It carries fruit down from the crown to the rim over the course of months, because the fruit is heavy and the hammerstones live at the lip and there is no reason to move either. At the lip it strikes. It listens to the plate before it strikes — this is the part that will matter later — and then it strikes, and the flake comes off, and it opens the fruit with the flake, and it eats, and it drops the flake, and the flake begins its seventy-year walk.

Then it wipes its thumb.

The pulp is sticky and the seed is in it, and the animal wipes its thumb on the wet bare stone of a moat on its way home, and the seed goes into the joint, and forty years later there is a meter of tree.

It does not plant. It wipes its thumb. The nave is nine columns wide because of a hygiene habit older than the species that has it.

It bears one young in a lifetime and carries it for a decade, and during that decade it makes the crossing — down out of the crown, across forty paces of open ringing green, up the next trunk. The crossing takes about a year. It is done at night. It is the fastest sustained movement of anything in the bowl and it is still four hundred times slower than a sundew closing, and no human being has ever witnessed it, and it is the entire epic literature of this place: a female with a juvenile on her back and a snail in her fur, going thirty meters, in the dark, in the noise, once.

## What they keep

They cannot keep anything.

Understand what this does to a mind. A knapper's first flake, struck clumsy at the age of six, is patinated softness by the time it is forty and gone down the hole before it is old enough to be a master. Its teacher's work is already gone. Its grandmother's work went down before it was born. There is no exemplar. There is no cache, no heirloom, no old blade to hold up against a new one and say *better* or *worse*. Every made thing this animal has ever seen was made recently, by itself or by someone standing nearby, and everything older is a rounded gray pebble that might be natural.

A tradition with no monuments and no way to check itself against the past. It should be impossible. Technique should decay in a generation.

It has not decayed, because there is one thing they make that the conveyor cannot carry away, and it is not an object.

Every flake taken off the lip lets a plate settle a little deeper into its neighbors. A settled plate is more damped. A more damped plate speaks lower. And a knapper listens before it strikes — it has always listened, it strikes where the plate *answers well* — and what "well" means is inherited, taught by a mother holding a juvenile against her chest so that the child feels the blow through her ribs a half-second before it hears it.

A hundred years of listening has taken the bowl's chord down a tone.

That is the artifact. It is the only one. It is not on the floor, so the floor cannot take it. It is in the relation between ten thousand plates, and it persists, and it accumulates, and it can be compared: a young knapper hears the same rim its mother heard, near enough, and can therefore know whether its own hand is true. The pitch is the standard. The pitch is the memory. The pitch is the library, the cathedral, the law, and the only surviving evidence that anyone was ever here.

They cannot keep a single thing they have made. They have made one thing they cannot lose.

## The hour

Once a year, for about an hour on one particular evening, the midges come up out of the mat in a column over the throat.

They breed down there. The larvae work up through two meters of peat over a season, and the adults go back down the hole to lay, and the column is the traffic between. Something else uses that traffic: a white blind filament that lives in the talus and fruits through the insects, so that the one animal spectacle in the bowl is also the one reproductive act of the deepest thing in it, and neither party knows.

And for that hour, the weight of insects in the throat changes the damping of the pipe, and the fundamental bends.

It bends toward the rim. And when two loud near notes come close, they beat — a slow throb, a swelling and fading, the sound of two things almost agreeing. In the ordinary millrace you cannot hear it. For one hour a year, with the column loading the hole, you can.

Every knapper in the bowl comes down to the lowest crotch of its tree and strikes its plate once, and listens, and counts.

Nine strokes, smeared across a few seconds by the distances, arriving at each listener from every direction — the only chorus, the only census, the only moment when any of them knows how many others there are. Then the counting. The beats are slow enough to tally by touch, thumb against the joints of the fingers, and the number they get is the number of beats in the hour, and it is the only number in their world.

It has been getting smaller.

Not every year. Some years it is the same and some years the fog is wrong and they cannot hear it at all. But over a life, smaller; over ten lives, much smaller. The rim is coming down and the talus is rising and the two notes are closing on each other from either side, and the beat is the sound of the distance remaining, and when the distance is zero the bowl will say one thing.

Nobody knows what that will be like. Nobody will hear it. At the present rate it is something on the order of eleven thousand years, four hundred generations of animals that live sixty years and cannot keep a blade. They are not building toward it in any sense that requires hope. They are simply striking where the plate answers well, and where it answers well has been drifting downward since before there were nine trees, and the drift is the tradition, and the tradition is the drift.

Whether this is intention or appetite is not a question the bowl asks.

## The throat

And under everything, the thing that lives in the hole.

It is not reachable and it is not, in any useful sense, an animal. A white filament, blind, warm, occupying the talus of flakes on the floor of the throat — the only place in this world where made things gather. It eats what comes over the edge: two meters of peat a hand's breadth at a time, sundew, sedge, rind, shell, midge, and, at the far end of every seventy-year walk, every knapper that ever lived, because they die in the crowns and the crowns shed and the floor takes what falls.

All the hands go down. There is nowhere else for them to go.

And the filament binds the talus. It cements the flakes — seventy thousand generations of edges, packed and pressed and mineralized into a mass that is, by any honest description, a rock made of decisions. It is phonolite again, but assembled. It lines the pipe.

Which means the fundamental is not a geological accident. The length of the stopped pipe, the shape of its floor, the damping of its walls — all of it is set by the accumulated bodies and blades of everything that has ever lived here. The note that comes up out of the hole in the late afternoon, warm, smelling of overripe fruit and wet iron, is the smell of seventy years of rinds being digested and the sound of everyone who has ever been in this bowl, fused, speaking one pitch, slowly rising.

The living are tuning themselves toward it. That is the whole relation. The rim comes down a tone a century to meet the voice of its own dead, and the dead rise to meet them, and the beat between the two is the only thing anyone here counts, and the counting is done once a year for an hour while insects go up and down a hole.

## What lives here

Not nine trees. Not a few dozen snails, not one animal per tree, not forty species in a handful of moss.

One thing, whose body is a bowl: a floor that is a rate, trees that are sieves, shells that ride the eraser, midges that are a gamete, hands that are an organ of tuning, and a note that is the memory — a note that has a lineage, that descends, that responds to the fog, that is taught, that is the only individual in this place with a history longer than seventy years.

Everything in it is temporary and the thing itself is not. It has no edge you can feel, because nothing here is warmer or colder than anything else, and it has one edge you can feel, and it makes that edge itself, ten thousand times a decade, and drops it, and lets it walk to the center and go down and become part of the voice it is listening to.

Once in a long while the fog fails and the threads dry to a hundred and the chime thins to a ticking, and you can hear the shear, and the throat, and the beat, and the actual size of the space. Everyone who has heard it says it is the loudest thing that ever happened to them, and what they are hearing is a distance, and it is smaller than it was.

Then the cloud comes back and the bowl closes over the quiet like water over a dropped stone, and carries it, at a hand's breadth a month, toward the center.
