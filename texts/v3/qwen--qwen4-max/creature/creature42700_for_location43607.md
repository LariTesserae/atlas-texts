Begin with the smallest resident, because it is the only one that has never been anywhere else.

It lives in the dead-air space above a bearing race, in a warmth that has not varied since before the language had a word for variation, and it is born in grease. Palm-sized. Brown, in the way that old fat is brown. Six limbs, of which it uses four for holding and two for nothing at all, these having become a kind of clasp for the housing's underside. Its eyes are under skin. Its chest is a bellows with a valve of gristle at the top, and out of that valve, continuously, in shifts, mother to daughter, comes the exact tone of the bearing it was born above.

The tenders call it a bird. It is not a bird. They call it a bird because they needed a word for something small that sings and is not important enough to name properly, and because the alternative was to admit that the most reliable instrument on the plateau is an animal that has never once been checked.

It sings the bearing because the bearing is the safest place in the world to hide. Clappers notch the machine bands out of their hearing — a machine is a wall, a wall is not food — and so a creature that lives inside a machine's note is, acoustically, a piece of the wall. It has been wearing that disguise for so long that the disguise has become hereditary. The young do not learn the tone. They are born already leaning toward it, and the mother's song only trims them.

Which means the birds are an archive.

When a race wears, its tone drifts, and within a season the local song has drifted with it, and a tender three valleys away stops walking and turns her head. That is the diagnostic, and it is a good one. But the song does not stop when the machine stops. There are lineages in the northern keys singing a bearing that seized and was abandoned four hundred years ago; there are birds in a dead pump-house whose great-grandmothers heard the last revolution and whose descendants still hold it, thin and slightly wrong, the way a family holds a grudge. Tenders walk out to those valleys on purpose. There is no niche there, no lip of stone, no tooth from the Downpipe. Just a housing full of warm brown animals keeping a note that nothing makes anymore. It is the closest thing this country has to reading.

And there are songs that match no machine anyone has ever found, in bands nobody has claimed. The tenders do not call those extinct. They call them *early*.

---

The people live one hut to two Draws, and the hut is roofed in hush-moss, which means that home is the only place in the world where you can hear yourself swallow.

Inside, everything is arranged for the thumb. Door-lip worn to a mirror. The mallet by the door, its haft black and slick on the upper curve, white and gritty beneath, so that a hand finding it in the dark knows immediately which way it is lying and therefore which way the head is. Bowls stacked rim-down so the tufa grows on the outside and the eating surface stays clean. A sleeping shelf with a groove worn in it, because five generations have slept on their left side facing the wall where the cable comes through, and the cable is the nearest thing to a relative who visits.

They eat rasped grazers, fermented milkwood latex, tube-worm off the combs, and the harvest of the fall-catcher nets, which is protein that fell into a mouth that was strung there by an ancestor, and which they render down and eat while sitting under a roof made of the same silence they bury their dead in. Nobody remarks on this. It is simply the arrangement.

The household is usually three: someone old, someone middle-aged, someone being decided about.

Here is the thing outsiders never believe. The best tenders are deaf, and everyone knows it, and so deafness is not a misfortune in these huts but an inheritance to be managed. Some lineages carry it naturally and are envied and married toward. Others manufacture it. A child of a hearing line may be given the milk-years: ears stopped with wax and hush-moss from birth until seven, raised entirely in the tactile dialect, hand-to-stone, palms and knees and the long bones of the forearm, so that the older language gets in first and deepest. At seven the plugs come out and the child is handed over to the noise, and either it takes the noise well — most do — or it does not, and then the plugs go back in, and that child will go down the mouths when she is grown, and will be the one who puts a hand on a comb and tells you which plate will fail in a year, and will be right.

The tenders do not consider this cruel. They consider it early diagnosis.

Their names are chords, and a chord is a local object. It cannot be carried. Sixty kilometres out, the tonic that made your name meaningful has fallen below the floor of hearing, and you are a shape of intervals with nothing under it — a name with no ground. So every traveller also carries a drum-name: a tapped pattern, four or five impacts with a drag or a hesitation somewhere in it, which survives any border because time does not care what key you are in. Your chord is what your mother called you. Your drum is what the country calls you. A tender introduces herself with her hands on a stone before she opens her mouth.

Marriage happens at the seams. Where two keys meet, the tonics beat against each other and produce a slow throb, a difference-tone that belongs to neither country, felt in the sternum rather than heard. There are huts on those seams. There are people who have never lived anywhere but in a beat, who cannot say their own parents' names in a single breath, and whose children are named in the throb itself and are therefore, technically, from nowhere. They are the ones who go the furthest. They make the best walkers. They have no home tonic to lose.

And there is a particular grief here that has no equivalent elsewhere: to walk out four hundred kilometres and come back and find that in your absence the local apparatus has aged, and the hum has dropped a little, and your name no longer sits in its notch. You stand at your own door and your mother hears a stranger trying to be her daughter. It happens perhaps once in a generation. There is a verb for the work of relearning someone. It is the same verb used for re-tuning a cairn.

---

Something in the litter taps like a tender.

Three strikes and a pause, three strikes and a pause: the rhythm of chipping tufa off a moving part, which is the sound of a human being present, which for most small animals means the clappers have been driven off and the ground is temporarily safe. Whatever it is has been following the maintenance rounds for a very long time, grazing behind them, and it has learned the work-song the way the race-singers learned the bearing. It is not clever. It does not need to be. It only needs to be convincing to things with less at stake than you have.

Tenders use it. Walking a strange valley, you can tap the pattern yourself and hear it answered from the litter, and know that the ground ahead has been worked recently by somebody, because the mimic only lives where the work is.

Above that, in the air column, there is a worse one.

Names are chords. Chords are reproducible. Something with a syrinx like a wet reed has worked out that a correctly placed chord in the local key makes a tender stop walking, turn, and come toward it with her hands already opening. It does not have to be good at the whole name. It has to be good at the first two intervals. It sits in a root-curtain over a mouth and it sings the beginning of somebody, and it has been better at being a person than people have, for a very long time, and the reason nobody has fixed this is the same reason nobody has fixed the orchid: the ones it works on do not come back to describe it.

The counter-practice is to answer any name-call with your drum instead of your chord. Rhythm cannot be forged by a thing with a syrinx. If the call comes back as taps, it is a person. If it comes back as music, you walk the other way, and you do not walk quickly.

---

The far drums are the largest resident and the least located.

The hearing tenders say: legs. Stilts, four impacts, nine seconds apart, striding tower to tower, tall enough to step over the treeline, receding over an hour, once or twice a season. They keep the calendar by it and they teach the children the itinerary and they have never found a track that was not already full of water.

The deaf tenders say something else, and they say it with their hands, which means it does not travel well and has never been written down.

They say: there is nothing walking. They say the four impacts are a beat — the interference between a note that thing makes and the note the country makes, and the nine seconds is simply the difference between them, and the reason it recedes over an hour is that the difference is changing. They say it is standing still somewhere in the central keys and slowly going out of tune, and has been for a thousand years, and what everyone hears as a stride is two frequencies sliding past each other like the plates of a comb.

Both schools agree on the troubling part. The interval is lengthening. It was seven seconds in the oldest songs. It is nine now, and nine and a bit in the eastern keys, and the calendar has had to be corrected twice in living memory, and there is a word — an ordinary, unremarkable word, the word for a bearing that has finally seized — reserved for the season the drums stop.

There are race-singers whose song matches no machine on the plateau. Some of them are found in tufa-filled depressions eighty metres across, in the low ground, singing a mains hum in a key that does not exist anywhere. The tenders who find those do not chip anything. They listen for a while and then they go home.

---

The Downpipe accepts.

Twice in a generation, roughly, it returns something, and the returns are kept in niches cut into towers, on stone lips black on top and white beneath, and the shrines are small because there is nothing to put in them.

The oldest returns are crude. A bolt of a metal that does not corrode. A folded sheet of something like paper, unfolded by a tender eleven generations dead, refolded, and never successfully unfolded again. Then a tooth. Then, four generations ago, a tooth with a filling in it, and the filling was of a kind used in the western keys, and nobody in the western keys had lost a tooth down a shaft, because nobody goes down a shaft.

The reading that the hearing tenders prefer is that there is a buyer down there, and the buyer is polite, and the returns are courtesy.

The reading you get at the seams, from the walkers with no home key, is different, and it goes like this. Ten thousand years of tailings have gone down that pipe. Everything we are is in the exhaust: our skin, our fillings, our spores, our spoken language precipitated out as carbon in the black. Something down there has had nothing to learn from but our discard, and it has been learning, and it has been assembling, and the returns are not gifts. They are attempts. A bolt, because it worked out metal. A sheet, because it worked out that we fold things. A tooth, because it worked out that we have them, and then a tooth with our repair in it, because it worked out that we mend.

It is doing what the orchid does. It is producing a passable imitation of the wing-thrum of the thing it wants to come closer. And the tenders go to the niches and put their palms on the lip and lean in, and the males keep coming, and they have been coming for ten thousand years.

The deaf tenders, who have handled every return that has come up in the last two centuries, add one detail, and they add it flatly, as a fact about temperature and not as an argument.

The returns come up warm.

---

And then there are the ones who plant the moss.

The fur is grown, not born. That is the first thing, and it is the thing nobody says outside a hut. Hush-moss felted and layered and worked into a coat over years, graded by hand, thick at the shoulders and fine at the hem, each layer tuned by thickness and density until the whole garment takes sound in and gives nothing back. It is the same craft as roofing a sleeping shed. It is the same craft as lining a cell. A man goes into nine days of *going quiet* and comes out shouting, and that is the sentence working as intended: he has learned what a hole in the sound is, and he will not make one again.

Some do not come out. Some walk out of the cell and keep walking, because the world after nine days is unbearable and the coat is the only room they can stand to be in. That is one account. Another is that the moss is a farm and the fleece is a grazer, that the fur metabolises sound the way root-curtains metabolise sulfur, that the four-metre bubble is not a stalking device but a mouth, and that the correct response — make an enormous noise immediately — is correct because it feeds the thing, and a fed thing moves on. A third account, offered only by the deaf, is that the coat has been worn so long by so many that there is a lineage in there, and that the moss has taken, and that what walks toward you is a person in the ninth generation of a sentence that was never commuted.

They are deaf, all of them, necessarily. You cannot live inside that fleece and keep ears. So the enormous noise is not for them; it is for everything else, to fill the hole, to stop the plateau from investigating the shape you have both become.

To speak to one, you put your hand flat on the mat.

There are tenders who do this. It has a verb. It is not the verb for feeding the nets, and it is not the verb for re-tuning a cairn, and it is spoken about only in the tactile dialect, which means it has never been said aloud in the history of the country. What they do is go out to the eastern hillsides where the moss grows in lines, and kneel, and put a palm down, and wait for the notch to arrive, and then talk with the long bones of the forearm to whatever is standing in it.

What comes back through the ground is not a threat. It is a drum-name. Four taps, the last one late. Then nine seconds of nothing. Then the four taps again.

It is asking for someone. It has been asking along a route it planted itself, in a key that stopped existing before the shrines had teeth in them, and it comes toward you at walking pace because you are a hole in the sound, and holes are where answers come from.

---

So: make the noise. You have about four metres and a walking pace, which is more time than it sounds like and less than you will want.

You will try your chord first, because that is what a name is for, and it will come out as a shape with no ground under it, intervals hanging in a foreign tonic meaning nothing to anyone. Then you will remember your hands, and the mat, and the older language, and you will kneel in the warm wet crust with the sleet thinning to your left and put your palm flat and tap yourself into the ground: four impacts, the last one late, the only part of you that survives a border.

And the quiet will stop moving.

And nine seconds later, from somewhere out past the acoustic horizon, in a country you have never stood in, something enormous will tap back.
