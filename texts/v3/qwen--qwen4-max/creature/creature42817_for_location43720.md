## The population, counted honestly

Most of what lives in the basin is a film, and most of the film is rust.

Weigh it and the animals are a rounding error. The iron sheaths are the citizenry: orange felt in bands a finger wide, laid along the pressure nodes of each column where the water can settle and the sound will not shake it loose. A mat does not spread. It occupies. It holds the same node on the same pipe for as long as the node stays put, which is centuries, and it thickens there and lays down its own laminae interleaved with the mineral's, so that a sectioned column is a ledger with two hands writing in it — cream, rust, cream, brown, cream — and the two hands agree because they are both being dictated to by the same standing wave.

This is the oldest continuous habitation in the basin, and it is not passive. A fat mat is mass, and mass flattens a pipe. The mats have been detuning the colonnade downward for six hundred years by a few hundredths of a cent per century, and the great work of the animals and the people, all of it, every plug and cairn and wedge, amounts to a correction applied against the slow opinion of the rust.

The mats are also the only food. Everything else here is a mouth moving across them.

## The grazers

The rasps are the reason the treble stays up.

A rasp is a fist of banded accretion with a wet bore down its middle and a foot underneath, and it is best understood as a piece of the colonnade that has decided to leave. It crops the felt off a column at about a meter a day, and by cropping it removes mass, and by removing mass it sharpens the pipe very slightly. In the great bores this is nothing; a grain silo does not care what a fist thinks. In the reed-fields, where the pipes are finger-thin and there are a hundred to the square meter, the grazing is the only thing keeping the hiss from sagging into the middle register and muddying the chord. Take the rasps out and in forty years the top of the basin goes dull and flat, and the mats thicken, and the whole instrument coarsens like a throat filling.

The shell keeps bands. A rasp of four thousand bands is eleven years old and carries its entire biography on its back in cream and iron, legible to anyone who can cut it, which is nobody.

In the nulls they stridulate. A null is a second and a half, and a rasp can get six ticks into it, and six ticks is the whole width of the language: names, positions, the two or three things a grazer needs to say. They are rare enough that most rasps spend their lives broadcasting six-tick words into a colonnade with nobody in it. So they walk toward the sink. It takes years, at a meter a day, from anywhere. The sink is where the two great bores cancel most nearly, where the null is deepest and the relief is widest, and the rasps arrive there in their slow trickle from every quarter of the basin and meet and tick and lay eggs and die.

When a rasp dies it stops moving, and the accretion takes it, and it seals, and it becomes a small stopped pipe. It goes on making a thin high note for a while before it fills. The reed-fields are full of these. A good part of the hiss is the recently dead.

## The counterfeit

The wader hunts by lying.

It is pale, eyeless, long-legged, and it works the mid-colonnade where the pipes are wrist-thick enough for its body to damp. It lays itself along a bundle and kills their voices and makes a hush perhaps two meters across. Then it waits, because a hush is exactly what a rasp is walking toward, and a rasp that feels relief will tick, and a tick is an address.

It is a false null. It is also a very good one, if you have only ears.

The rasps that survive it have a test, and the test is the whole epistemology of the basin: **wait for the bones.** A wader can quiet a wrist of stone. It cannot quiet a grain silo; nothing short of a slab can. So in a true null the bass eases too, and you feel it in the long bones and the sternum and the water of the eye, and in a false one the ears are relieved and the bones are not. The cheap silence is local. The real silence is everywhere at once and costs mass to make.

There are waders now that work in pairs and damp across a wider front, and rasps that hold their tick through one full cycle and give it in the second, and the argument between them has been running long enough to have changed the shape of both animals. The wader's forelegs have gotten longer. The rasp's foot has gotten slower. Neither is winning, which is what a long argument looks like.

## The ones with no clock

Inside a great bore there is no beat.

The eleven seconds is an interference between two pipes out in the sink. Inside a single pipe there is only that pipe's own voice, held, with no partner to cancel it and therefore no null, ever, at any depth. Things live down there anyway, at the pressure nodes on the wet throat wall, pale mats and small blind grazers and something longer that moves like a hand feeling for a step.

They have no rhythm and no window and no reason to have either. And after twenty minutes with your eyes shut in the dark of a split bore you begin to see them glimmer, green, faint — and if you count it, and people have counted it, it comes at eleven seconds.

They are keeping a beat that has never existed where they live. Their ancestors came in from outside, from the pans and the nulls, and brought the interval down with them into a place with no use for it, and have been ticking it off in the dark for longer than anyone can estimate, in light, because light is the only channel down there that the sound has not taken. In the loudest place there is, the deepest residents abandoned hearing and invented a lamp to count with.

## The vertical animals

The feeder shafts have their own fauna and it goes the wrong way.

Water falling thirty meters drags air down with it, and in the bubble stream there are small transparent things that ride the entrainment: larvae carried down in the throat of the fall, delivered into the pressure chambers, spending their juvenile life in squeezed air at the bottom of the world. The adults climb back up. They climb the wet wall against the falling water, a millimeter a second, for weeks, and mate at the top, and lay at the lip, and go down again inside a bubble.

Their whole world is a loop thirty meters tall and a hand's width across. They cannot hear anything but the shaft. They have never been in the basin, though they live in it.

And above the lid, occasionally, something crosses going somewhere else. The crossers navigate by the chord — it is audible for two days' travel in every direction and it does not stop, which makes it the best landmark in that part of the world. It also drifts. A tone in a few centuries is nothing to a pipe and a great deal to a route, and the oldest inherited bearings now lead a little off, into country where there is nothing, and there is a thin scatter of failure out beyond the rim that nobody has ever gone to look at.

The crossers swallow grit where they breed and carry it in their gizzards and drop it here. That is where the foreign stone comes from. Every dark wedge in every bore mouth, the only rock in ten thousand hectares that did not grow in place, arrived in a bird's stomach.

## What happens to the dead

Nothing rots in the basin. There is no decay here, no rot, no return; the films are eaten or they are buried, and burial is the default. The stone is the decomposer, and it does not give anything back. Iron that goes into accretion is out of the living world permanently, which means the whole biosphere runs on what the seep brings fresh, and its carrying capacity is set by the water table, and has been falling by a hand's width a decade for as long as anyone has been counting.

The dead do not vanish. They become instrument. A boot is a swelling by the third night. A rasp is a thin note for a season. A wader, which is large, is a bore the width of an arm and sounds for a century, and there are places in the west where a dozen such notes stand together and make a small sour chord that is nothing but a killing ground's accumulated dead.

You can census them by pitch. The higher and thinner the new voices in a quarter, the more recently something was alive there.

## The ones with hands

They came in following the sound, and they have been here long enough that the basin has begun to write on them: a banding at the wrists and the shins, a grey tide at the nails, skin that stays cool and slick and comes away wet.

They cannot shout. In the reed-fields no voice carries at all, and elsewhere a null will let a word go three meters, once every eleven seconds, and that is the entire budget of public speech. So they have two languages. The public one is a lexicon of single enormous words, one per breath of the world, used for names and warnings and numbers. The other is touch: palm flat on the sternum, fingers spelling against the bone, continuous, unrationed, inaudible to anyone not touching. It is the only thing in the basin that is not metered out in eleven-second allotments. You have to hold someone to say anything ordinary to them.

A message that must travel goes down a chain of bodies, hand to chest to hand to chest, five to nine people long, and arrives changed. This is their politics and their postal service and their principal source of grief.

For anything longer than a word they use the basin itself. A plug is a sentence. You drive clay into a mouth and the pipe goes silent and the pressure finds another way down and somewhere else a new voice opens, and the change takes eighty years to finish arriving, and everyone hears it, and it cannot be taken back except by another eighty years. Their arguments are conducted in mass, at the speed of the water table, by people who will not live to hear the reply. A family's holdings are not land. They are a configuration of quiet, and it moves, because a stopped mouth seals and a sealed pipe dies and the silence migrates — and so they walk, following their own sentence as it drifts across the basin, and their genealogy is a route.

They call themselves a word that also means *true*, and it means, literally, **heavy**. It is the rasp's test made into an ethics. A silence you did not pay for in mass is a lie; the bass cannot be faked; anyone can quiet a wrist of stone.

There are two things to do with a life like this, and they disagree about it.

The setters keep walking and keep plugging, and their great work — the thing under all the small tunings, the reason for the cairns and the wedges and the eighty-year patience — is that the two bores in the sink are converging. The beat has lengthened; the oldest legible tallies, read against a fixed water-mark, imply something nearer nine seconds, and the reading is contested and the contest is the most important argument they have. If it is right, the nulls are getting longer and rarer, and in some thousands of years there will be a window a minute wide, and then an hour, and then a day. And two pipes close enough in pitch may lock — may cease to beat at all and hold one relation forever. Locked in cancellation, the sink goes quiet and stays quiet, and everything that lives in the nulls inherits a world. Locked the other way, the null closes and never opens again.

They are trying to steer it. Every plug is a move in a game whose end is thousands of years out, played by people who get one word every eleven seconds and cannot shout at each other across a room.

The sitters say: that is not a life, that is a bequest. Make a small quiet now, for one body, and sit in it.

The cell in the folded quarter is a sitter's house. Its doorway is cut to a wavelength — a duct narrower than half a wave will not pass it — and when it was built the chord was higher and the door let the bass straight through and the cell was a failure, an expensive stone room as loud as the corridor outside. They cut it for a note that had not arrived yet. They knew the chord was falling. They built a door for the pitch the basin would reach in four hundred years, and kept the house, and waited, and the world went flat enough, and the door shut, and someone went in and sat down on the bench.

The accretion is over the threshold now and climbing. And the shape on the bench has a bore down it, and is sounding, faintly, high, the way the recently dead sound.

This is not an accident and it is not a tragedy to them. A quiet place is a place where a body can stop, and a body that stops becomes a pipe, and a pipe ends the quiet. Every cell they have ever built is now a small choir of the people who sat in it, and each one had to be abandoned to the singing, and the route across the basin is strung with them like beads. The setters carry their dead. The sitters leave theirs where they sit. Both are ways of admitting that the only thing you can make here that lasts is a change in the note.

There are plugs in the folded quarter newer than any living hand. Nobody claims them. Either there is a chain out there that has not been met in three generations, or something without hands has learned that mass makes silence and has begun to use it. The setters argue about this in their one-word public language and at length by touch, and no one has gone far enough into the folded quarter to settle it, because it takes days, and you must keep moving, and the water is always working somewhere inside.

## Now

Somewhere in the mid-colonnade today two of them have come around the same column from opposite sides and stopped, which is permitted for a moment, and one has put a palm on the other's sternum.

It is not a word about the water table or the drift or the lock. The commonest sentence in the basin, said by touch, in the roar, at any hour, needing no null and no permission and no eighty years, means approximately: *still walking; still here.*

Then the tone comes back up, and they take their hands away, and go on, at a meter a day, in the direction the quiet has moved.
