Two kilometers down there is something that has never been up.

It is a mat, pale as a fingernail, wet, warm, spread in sheets over the trunks of the drowned forest — trees that were trees before there were grasses, still standing where they stood, still with their branches, held up now by the honeycomb rather than by themselves. The mat eats them. It has been eating them for forty million years and it is in no hurry, because a generation of it takes about a century, and because there is nothing else down there to want anything.

It has one other income. Things fall.

They arrive all the time: adzes, a stilter's footpad, a hail-killed acre of the night tide, a drummer that got old, a child. The mat cannot go and get them, so it has done the only thing available to it, which is to grow thickest where falling is likely. Under the lanes. Under the drum-bosses. Under the lips of the tanks where everything comes to drink and some of it slips. Its distribution is a map of the surface's carelessness, drawn from below, in the dark, by something with no eyes and no opinion. It gives off a gas that rises through two kilometers of gallery and comes out of the vents at night, and that is the meat smell under the turpentine, and every person on the plate has smelled it every day of their life and none of them knows what it is.

It also eats the floor. Not the living skin — the deepest dead strut-work, the oldest galleries, at a rate that has not varied in a million years, and that rate is exactly the rate at which the surface lays down new cuticle and dust and frass and bodies. The plate is two thousand six hundred meters tall because this thing is precisely as patient as it is. The equilibrium has an appetite.

What arrives at the bottom is not a body. By then it is a wet place, and a smell, and the mat turns toward it over the course of a year.

---

Above the mat, the nations.

There are the mastic-packers, who carry resin in a crop and set it with a spit of their own, and who will find a hail-hole the size of a hair and close it in a night. There are the pruners, who cut the vascular bundles in a pattern no one has ever mapped and who, if you remove them, kill the plain in eleven years. There are the cullers, who farm one rot-fungus and murder all others on sight, and who have been fighting a war along a front four hundred kilometers long since before the ice. There are the grafters, who go along the clonal seams and knit them, and who are the reason there are six or seven clones on the plate instead of a hundred, and who therefore, without knowing it, decide which animals can speak to which.

And there are the ones that come up.

Take one: a pale six-limbed thing the length of a hand, with a fan on its back that it does not open for display but for warmth, because the surface at night is six degrees colder than the gallery and it is going to be there for two hours. It has no eyes worth the name. It rises when the shaft opens and the cooling comes, and the cooling moves across the plate at the speed of the shadow, and so the tide is not an event but a wave — a standing wave in the dark, going around and around the world, and it has been going around for twenty million years, and every individual in it is a brief local disturbance in something that does not stop.

They mate on the skin. They lay in the shafts. Most of them do not make it back down. At the second hour of light the plain has a gloss on it, and the gloss is them, and the things that came to eat them are already gone.

Riding the leading edge of the wave there are flat wide hunters, three meters across and four centimeters thick, that surf the cooling front and take the tide as it rises. They have no point load at all. They are the plate's answer to the problem of being heavy: don't be. They flow. You cannot hear one and you cannot outrun one, because running is a point load, and the only defense anyone has ever found against a sheet is to be picked up, or to roll, or to be already lying on something that isn't the floor.

---

The tanks are islands, and there is one road.

A stilter's footpad is a hectare of wet callus and it comes out of the last tank带 with mud on it, and in the mud are eggs. Cysts. Spores. A shrimp's whole future. This is the only bridge there has ever been between the two hundred thousand islands, and it is a bridge made of dirt on a foot, and it works about once in a thousand years per tank.

So a tank's species list is a record of traffic. Tanks on the old lanes have cosmopolitan faunas, cousins everywhere, nothing older than the last glaciation. Tanks off the lanes — and most are off the lanes — have things in them that have not been disturbed since the Eocene. Walk two days off a polished lane to a four-kilometer black lake with a callus lip and put your hand in the water and a frog-analog will come and sit on it. Not because it is tame. Because nothing in its lineage has ever had a hand happen to it, and the whole architecture of fear has never been built there, and you are the first weather it has ever had.

Some tanks are mouths. The lip is smooth and slightly warm and hums, and the hum is not decoration: it is a trigger. The digestive lip is keyed. It responds to a resonance, and the resonance it responds to is the one that the local large animals make when they come to drink, because the plant is nitrogen-poor and has had thirty million years to work out who drinks here. A tank in the D-plain is tuned to D. A stilter from the plain a fifth above can stand in that same tank all afternoon and drink and leave, because its footfall does not say the word the mouth is listening for.

Children are lighter. Their footfalls are higher and finer and they do not say it either.

Which is why, in every band on the plate, the water is fetched by children between about six and about thirteen, who can walk out onto the humming lip of a hungry tank and fill a gourd and walk back, and who are watched from the callus by nine adults who cannot follow them. And why adulthood, on the plate, is not a ceremony. It is a morning when you go out with the gourd as usual and the tank hears you, and the lip goes slick under your heel, and you find out with your whole body, once, that you have become the kind of thing this place eats.

Nobody who has been through it talks about it much. There is a sign for it. You make it at children sometimes, to be kind, and it means *not yet*.

---

A drummer is born sharp.

The horn is stiff when it is new and the note is high, and a young male's territory is a small bright circle inside his father's, and he is tolerated. Then the horn wears. Everything here wears. The note descends over about thirty years, a slow glissando through the local scale, and the plain thickens under him as he drums because drumming thickens wood, so that by middle age he is standing on a boss his grandfather began, a meter of dense resonant callus that his family has been building by making noise for two hundred years. Territory here is not land. It is an instrument, inherited, that you finish tuning and hand on.

And then he goes flat past the bottom of the local band.

His sons answer him as a stranger. Not out of cruelty — they cannot help it; the note is wrong, and wrong is a species. He drums the challenge and gets back the response you give to something that has wandered in from nowhere, and he keeps doing it, for a season, sometimes two. There is a word for this sound. People use it about weather, and about the very old, and about a certain hour of the evening. Some flat males walk to a seam and cross it and become briefly, catastrophically intelligible somewhere else, and are killed there. Most stay and drum into a country that has stopped recognizing them, on a boss their own bodies made.

The sap-wells open at the base of the boss when the chord is right, and the sap is sweet and slightly narcotic, and a drummer will stand at it for eleven hours past thirst, drumming badly, which is fine, because the plant does not need him accurate. It needs him loud, and it needs him there, and it has found the cheapest way to keep him.

Others come to the wells too. Which is the whole reason for the next thing.

---

The answerer has no key.

It is squat and wide and about the size of a dog, with a belly-file worn to nothing and a membrane in its throat it can tension to any pitch it has ever heard. It lives on the clonal seams — the places where the wood changes key, where a drummer crossing over becomes unintelligible. It lives there because the seams are where confusion is manufactured, and confusion is its food.

It answers. A male in D throws a challenge at his boundary; the answerer, sitting three hundred meters away in the seam, gives him back a perfect D challenge from the wrong direction. He goes to fight it. It is not there. It is at his sap-well, drinking. It does this in eleven keys, and it has heard all eleven drummers, which no drummer will ever do, and it has no species-mate it can recognize, because the thing it recognizes is a sound and it can make all of them.

The plant has been in an arms race with it for a million years. The chord gets more complicated; the throat gets better. In a bad decade the answerers take a whole plain's wells and the mast does not open and everything that eats sap and pollen and the things that eat pollen starves through the following year, and the drummers drum into a silence that is entirely their own fault and not their fault at all.

There are two things on the plate that can speak every key. One of them opens the masts.

The people's sign for the answerer is the same sign they use for a stranger, and it is not an insult, exactly. It carries the two meanings that a stranger carries here: *you are not one of us*, and *you are the only new thing that will ever happen to me*.

---

The trotter's home is a line.

It cannot stop, so its range is not an area but a circuit: a loop about two hundred kilometers around, threaded through the shallow margins of six tanks where it can drink at speed, mouth low, one lap per stride, without ever breaking the nine kilometers an hour that keeps it from going through. It runs the loop in a day. It runs it again. It has never been inside the circle it draws. It was born trotting, in the middle of the loop, and got up in eleven seconds, and inherited the route from its mother, who inherited it from hers.

Its whole life is the outside of a shape it will never enter.

When it slows, it dies. That is arithmetic and it is not negotiable and every trotter arrives at it. But there is one substance on the plate that will hold a point load, and it is the dead callus of a burn scar, dense and dumb and non-resonant, and old trotters find it, and walk into it, and stop.

They stand there. For the first time in their lives they stand still. Nothing lives in the scars — nothing drums, nothing rises, nothing hunts — so there is no food, and they do not eat again, and a big one can take four or five years to finish dying, and it spends those years motionless in the quiet with its eyes gone milky and its feet, at last, resting.

They are not tame. They will kill anything that touches them. But they will not come, because coming is the one thing they have finished with, and so the safest neighbors on the plate are a dozen starving monsters that have finally stopped moving.

This is where the people build. In the black scars, in the few hectares of true quiet, among the standing ones. They bear children there. They speak there — out loud, in air, with the voice, which is a thing you can only do in a place where the ground is deaf, and which no one born on the plate can do anywhere else without being, in the most literal sense, public property. Lovers walk three days into a burn to say something to each other that the floor will not hear. It is the only privacy in the world.

And when the standing ones finally collapse, their weight concentrates, and the callus takes a day to fail, and they go down into the dark; and the scar records a dull patch; and the people navigate by it, and name it, and pass it, and sign *here the great one went down, in the time of the grandmother before the grandmother*, and do not stop, because it is two more days to water.

---

Stones fall from the kites, and you have an hour.

A kite takes grit from the rim cliffs four hundred kilometers away because it must grind its food, and the grit wears round and small, and when it is too small the kite casts it, on the wing, at altitude, the way any bird casts a pellet. It comes down through the haze. If it lands on open skin it begins to sink, and the skin takes about an hour to fail under something that size, and getting to it means going out flat on your belly across a sighing floor toward a pebble, and most people do not, and most stones are lost in the first sixty minutes of their arrival on the plate.

The ones that survive land in tank margins, where the callus is thick.

A woman on the eastern plain owns three. One is banded gray and white and came from her mother's mother. One is the color of a tooth. One is the size of a thumbnail and is not really useful and she keeps it anyway. With them she boils water, and boiling water is not a convenience here, it is the difference between a sick infant who lives and a sick infant who does not, and so people bring their sick to her, from four days out, from six, lying flat on wide sledges and dragging their children.

She has not moved in nineteen years. Her sledge has healed to the ankles. She breaks it free at dawn when she can and she cannot always, and everyone who comes to her can see the living cuticle standing up around the runners like a lip around a wound, and everyone knows what she is doing, and nobody says it, because she is the only fixed point in a world of routes.

Bands navigate to her. She is a name that is a place instead of a schedule. She is the closest thing on the plate to a town, and she is sinking, and in eighty years she will be a knot with a name, and the sign for her will be a flat patch under the ear-stick somewhere east of the four-tank lane, and children will be told what it means and will not slow down.

---

The people do not own places. They own times.

A band's property is a circuit: a year-long loop through tanks and lanes and bosses, with a date attached to every stop. Two bands can use the same tank a month apart and never meet and never quarrel, and this is the ordinary arrangement, and it requires no negotiation and perfect calendars. The calendars are knotted cord, and drummed sequences, and songs nobody hears in air. Trespass here is not crossing a boundary. It is arriving early.

Their houses are sledges — four meters wide, woven fiber over a wax-sealed frame, wide enough that nine people and a coal and a child and a season's dried pale ones spread out on it do not make a point load anywhere. You do not carry a load on this plate; you drag it, spread. The sledge is the house and the boat and the bed and the stretcher and the drum-platform, and every morning it has to be broken loose from the skin it has begun to heal into overnight, with a long crowing crack like a knuckle in another room, and that sound is the first sound of every human day, and it is the sound of not being eaten.

Fire is carried by one person, who carries nothing else, who does not run, who walks last. The scars are named for the ones who dropped it. That is the only monument this world permits: a silence the size of a country, with somebody's name on it, audible for centuries under a cheekbone. The oldest scars have no names. Those are the ones the sky dropped.

Hearing migrates inward across a life. Ears in air until about twenty, useful only for hail. Then the cheekbone, on the ear-stick, and the callus that grows there is the mark of an adult, and children touch it. Then the teeth. Then, at the end, the palms, flat on the planed membrane of a listening house, an old person lying on their stomach reading two hundred kilometers of wood through their hands, which is also the posture for waiting out hail, and for sleep, and for love, and for dying. There is one posture here. It takes a lifetime to learn all the things it means.

The dead are given to the humming tanks. You cannot dig, and you must not cut — cutting is the one wound the plant cannot afford — so the only graves are the plant's own mouths, and you carry your dead out on the sledge and lay them on the warm smooth lip and the lip takes them, and this is understood to be the other half of the arrangement. The ground eats your dead. You make its chord. Neither half is a metaphor.

And you meet a stranger twice in your life.

The first time you are nineteen and there is a mast preparing and your band walks eleven days to a chord-ground where four other bands are already setting up their sledges, and you find someone from a circuit you will never walk, and you learn their name — which is not a word, it is a rhythm, six taps and a pause and two — and they learn yours. Then everyone drums, and the mast opens, and the kites come down out of the haze screaming, and you go home.

The second time is years later, at another mast, and it is the marriage. In between, it is a correspondence: you drum their rhythm into the floor at the appointed hour on the appointed day of the year, and two hundred kilometers away someone puts a cheekbone to a stick and feels a stranger's name arrive in their face, and drums yours back. That is the whole of it. That is a marriage on the plate: two people who have touched once, keeping a rhythm for a decade.

The children go to the other band at the next chord. You hand them over on the callus among a hundred strangers and the noise, and you do not see them again, and they are thirteen, and they have just found out that the tank can hear them.

Nine is the fewest hands that can hold the chord against an answerer. Twenty is the most a tank lip will carry through a dry season. Every band on the plate is a number between those two facts.

---

At the chord-ground the sledges are laid out in a ring and the platforms are planed thin and the old ones lie flat with their palms down.

Nine people drum. Not to be heard by each other — they cannot hear each other, the air is full, the whole world is a hundred and fifteen decibels of animals doing the same thing — but to be heard by the floor. The interval is old and specific and it is not theirs; they learned it the way the answerer learned it, by listening at seams, and they can lie in any key on this continent, and once every few years they choose not to.

Out at the boundary an answerer gives back a flawless challenge from the wrong direction, and nine pairs of hands bury it, and the mast opens: three hundred meters of green scaffolding coming apart in the haze, pollen going up in a column you can see from four days' walk, sap standing in the wells, kites dropping out of the sky toward it with their wings folded like thrown knives.

The ground cannot tell the difference. That is not a figure of speech. The plant has been listening for forty million years and it has no mechanism for distinguishing a drummer from a band of people who have made themselves into the correct noise, and that indistinguishability is their entire religion, their entire ecology, and the reason they are tolerated on a surface that eats stillness.

Two hundred kilometers away, a band that will never meet them feels the plain change under its cheekbones and knows the year is good, and gets up, and breaks the house loose, and goes on.
