## Bodies

Nobody stands in the middle of a room. Rooms don't have middles here; the phrase is a curse, or a joke about the dead. A native entering any space puts a hand on stone before putting weight on the floor, and keeps some part of the body in contact — knuckle, hip, the outside of a forearm — with a frequency that outsiders find frantic and locals cannot perceive in themselves. The hand is called the wall-hand. It is the left, by convention, so the right stays free for work. Lovers hold the right hand. To take someone's wall-hand is a threat, and the gesture survives in the language as an idiom for betrayal.

They lean. Everything in the Hold falls very slightly toward the nearest wall, and the inner ear of anyone born here has spent nine hundred years of inherited instruction learning to correct for it. Natives stand at an angle no one can see. Visitors from the sill stations stagger for three days and then get it, and then go home and stagger for three days there. Old people who have spent a lifetime leaning sometimes lose the correction on their deathbeds and spend their last hours insisting the bed is tilted.

Their bones are dense. Their jaws are heavy and their molars are worn flat, because the poor of the antinodes speak by pressing a bone-stick to the mandible and clacking, and a talkative woman of sixty has teeth like a much older woman's. The rich have long vowels in their names. The poor have rhythms.

Fire behaves. A candle flame in the Hold does not stand up; it lies over toward the wall like a dog pointing. Smoke leaves a hearth and is gone, instantly, deterministically, delivered to a specific antinode where it settles into the rind and is eaten. So there is no haze above a kitchen, ever, and no smell of cooking anywhere but at the stove — but on the wall three hundred meters away there is a black stripe, and the stripe is layered, and a district's cooking can be read in it like tree rings. Nine centuries of dinners. The soot-cliffs of Four are protected. There are people whose profession is scraping them in millimeter lifts and telling you what the poor ate in a year when the tuners were young.

Nothing airborne carries disease. There has never been a coughing plague in the Hold. There have been four terrible hand-plagues, and the quarantine laws are all about touch, which is why the wall-hand custom is also a hygiene custom, and why the poorest and most crowded districts wash their forearms to the elbow eleven times a day.

## The Keeping

In the fertile antinodes a child hears until about eleven and then, over two or three years, stops. It is not sudden and it is not painless. Everyone knows the schedule; there are songs about it, songs you sing while you can still hear them.

So there is a rite. Around the tenth birthday a family spends what it has — a season's fig crop, a dray, a debt — on four minutes in a rented null, and the child goes in with one chosen person and hears one chosen thing. This is the Keeping. What is kept is meant to last seventy years.

Most choose their mother saying their name. Some choose a frog chord, or rain on a cold rib, or a word in a language nobody speaks anymore. A few choose nothing at all and sit in the silence, and those are the ones who become tuners, or trouble.

There is a woman in Bay Two who has said *I am here, I am here, you are safe* to nine thousand children in a wardrobe-sized null off a fig terrace. She is the most reproduced voice in the world and no one has ever heard her outside that room, including her husband, who is deaf, and who reads her hands. She is paid in silence-credit. She has never been rich. She has been in the null so long that she cannot walk properly on the open wall; she stumbles, she grabs, she leans the wrong way.

Because that is the other thing about nulls. In a true null the sweep cancels along with the harmonics, and things fall straight down, and a plumb line hangs like a plumb line. Null-born children learn to walk twice: once for the wardrobe and once for the world, and most of them never learn the second one well. They are graceful in rooms and helpless on terraces. They are carried, by servants, across the surfaces they own.

## Trams

The seams — the hand-wide ribbons of cancellation that run wall to wall through the open volume — carry the eels and they carry the trams, and the trams are the only place where a poor person can hold a conversation.

A tram car is a null dragged along a line. Eight people, maybe twelve, knees touching, and for the length of the ride they can hear each other breathe. This is the entire social life of the antinode districts. Confessions happen on trams. Arguments are timed to routes: you begin a fight at Gutter Nine and finish it at the Junction of the Two Figs, and if you are not finished you ride back, and everyone in the car knows and pretends not to. Proposals are made on the long wall-to-ceiling run, which takes forty minutes and costs a day's wage, and the answer must come before the terminus, because after the terminus you cannot hear the question again.

Drivers are the priests of this. They know the eel timetables; they know which junction will be alive with spawning at which beat; they know that a car stalled at a junction is stalled for a reason and that the reason is usually a boy who has paid them to be slow. There is a whole etiquette of the deliberate delay. Drivers who accept too much are called *long-drivers* and are despised and are always employed.

The knot-hunters are the other seam-people. They sit at junctions with their bodies spanning the ribbon, motionless, sometimes for two weeks, because the eels have no other way to go and no ability to imagine a detour. A hunter's world is as one-dimensional as the eel's, and hunters marry hunters and their children are named for junctions. The oldest hunters are half-grown-into: the rind creeps up a still body, and a man who has sat the same junction for thirty years has moss to the waist and does not consider this a problem. There are hunters who have been buried standing and are still hunting, and whose families bring them food, and who are consulted about the map.

Twice a season the map is redrawn and most of the eels die — expelled from seams that no longer exist, fired into stone at forty meters a second. This is eel-fall. It is the largest protein event of the year. Everyone eats. Nobody is grateful, exactly, but the harvest is taken with a formality that other harvests are not, and the hunters do not work during it. They sit at the junctions that are about to exist, which are currently ordinary lethal air, and they wait, in the noise, with their bodies in the wrong place, for the world to come around to them.

## The flat fauna, and one exception

The shale crabs are the end of everything. Every mote of skin you shed, every moth, every spore, every ash-flake from every meal — the field delivers it to the antinodes and the crabs are waiting in sheets, and they molt with the beat, and the molt is the calendar. Shell-fall is swept up and pressed into board and paper and roofing; the poor write on crab-shell, and a legal contract is a rectangular pale tile that clacks when you flick it. There is no new matter in the Hold but the rising limestone and the brine seep. Everything alive here is made of the same nine centuries of stuff, over and over. It is not a metaphor. The calcium in a child's femur was in a tuner, was in a fig, was in a crab, was possibly in whatever raised the ceiling. People say *I'll see you in the figs* the way elsewhere they say *goodbye*.

The drays are deaf and bone-plated and enormous and they browse the wall-forests on ledges a body has no business standing on. Herders walk them along seams that have names but no maps. A dray wears a bell nobody hears and everybody feels through the stone; a herd approaching is a change in the floor. Nine days before a retune, the drays begin to walk. The tuners announce nine days out. The tuners watch the drays first. There is a heretical school that holds the drays are the instrument and the tuners are the readout, and it is not clear that this is heresy so much as trade gossip that got written down.

The wingless wasps walk. A pollinator's working life is eleven hundred kilometers of surface film, and it navigates by the figure — by the arrangement of its own species' trees, which is the shape of a harmonic. A wasp reads a sound with its feet. Wasp-roads are visible as faint polished lines in the moss, and they are the oldest continuously used paths in the Hold, older than stairs.

And then there is the thing that broke the rule.

You never see a crosser leave. You see it arrive. It comes out of the dim empty volume at forty meters a second and hits a rosette of cushion-moss on the bare antinodal stone with a sound like a dropped fruit, and it lies there for a second with its skull-plate ringing, and then it gets up and eats whatever is on that wall.

Crossers are lizards, strap-bodied, heavy-headed, with a shock organ behind the jaw that is mostly fluid and mostly ruined by middle age. They launch themselves into the void on purpose. They can do this because the trajectories are deterministic and their lineage has been keeping the map for longer than there have been people here. And they *farm*: a crosser carries spores of the cushion-moss in a throat pouch, and it plants the pad it intends to land on, and then it waits a season, and then it jumps. The rosettes are visible from across a bay. They form a graph, like the seams but ballistic, a second subway drawn in green dots on lethal stone.

People learned to cross from watching lizards. The Shot guild fires armored capsules wall to wall along surveyed trajectories into nets of dray-gut: eleven kilometers in under five minutes, when the tram would take three hours around the graph. It is how medicine moves, and treaties, and bad news. Shot-men are deaf by twenty-five and have spines like gravel by thirty and are paid absurdly and are usually the children of knot-hunters, who understand what it means to trust a line you cannot leave.

## Up

There are people on the ceiling who have never seen the floor.

They live seven hundred to eleven hundred meters overhead in the upside-down forest, in the fungal stars, and they tend the light. The green heaven is a crop. It has been bred for nine hundred years by lampwrights who graft and cull and select for color and steadiness, and the particular green of a district's sky is a family's signature, and there are feuds about it that have outlasted languages. The violet of the antinodal seams is wild; the green is domestic. When someone says *the light of home* they mean a strain, a lineage of fungus, a thing their great-grandmothers made.

Ceiling folk are long and thin and prehensile and they think of the floor as a swamp full of crabs and noise. They marry down rarely and reluctantly. Their dead are dropped, which is the only funeral in the Hold that involves the volume, and the fall takes about half a minute, and the mourners watch the whole way because there is nothing in the air to hide it.

Blight happens. Bay Nine went dark for forty years in the last century and its people lived by seam-glow and hand-lamps and are still called the Dim, and still, four generations later, marry each other.

## Down

Under Four you can descend through six swallowed street levels. The floor rises a millimeter or two a year and the ground floors become basements and the basements become cisterns and the cisterns become stories. Down there the field does not reach. The stone stops it.

So there is silence, free, unlimited, and no food.

The buried levels are full of pale things: crabs gone white and slow, geckos with no eyes at all, mosses that gave up light and eat the last heat of the old extrusion front. And people. Debtors, poets, the dying, lovers who want a week of hearing each other and can afford nothing else. Cellarers go down with a sack of figs and come up thin and famous. There is a whole literature of the descent, and its central image is not the dark. It is the dust.

Because down there, alone in the world, dust lies on floors. Nothing sweeps it. It settles and it stays. You can write in it.

The buried levels of Four are the only writing surface in the Hold that is not alive and does not move. Nine centuries of it, layer on layer, floors written over by generations, letters to the dead, tallies, obscenities, a child's hand, the whole palimpsest scraped and rewritten and scraped. People go down to write a name and know it will still be there. Sweeping a floor in the unders is the worst crime in the local code, worse than assault, and the punishment is to be taken up and put out on the loud wall and left there.

## Eleven

The drowned district is not a pool. Water has mass; the field combs it too. The flood in Eleven stands against the wall in a lens, a lake with a vertical face, held there, shivering with the beat, and the drowned lamps burn in it because they are fungal and were never fire.

Something lives in there and opens doors.

For a long time this was read as hunting: doors, then cisterns, then notch-frogs, then dinner. The tuners went and listened and came back and said no. It opens doors in sequences. It closes most of them again. It leaves a particular combination standing for weeks, and the combination is not random, and when the tuners mapped the rooms it had left open they found that the opened rooms were coupled, and that the coupling produced, in one small chamber at the center of the district, a cancellation.

It is building a null. It has been building it for eighty years. It is making a wardrobe of quiet, room by room, in the dark, underwater, and it sits in the middle of it, and the lamps it keeps burning are the lamps around that room.

The tuners have a name for it that is not a species name. They will not say what it is. Two of them have gone down and stayed. Nobody has yet gone in and asked, because to enter the chamber you would have to open a door, and the door is not one of the ones it left open, and everybody understands — without being able to justify it — that this would be rude.

## Marks

The dead are sown. You choose your harmonic while you can still afford the paperwork, and your body goes into the volume, and the field takes it apart and delivers it, deterministically, to a specific antinode of a specific figure, and you become a fig. Families are groves. A grove is a deed, a genealogy, and a shape: you stand in your family's trees on the far wall and you are looking at your own name written in the arrangement of trunks, eleven kilometers wide, in the handwriting of a sound.

The rich are buried in nulls, where nothing is delivered and nothing much rots, and they persist. There are mausoleum wardrobes in Six with nine hundred years of ancestors in them, dry, individual, distinct, still themselves, still with faces. The poor find this obscene. Every few generations somebody breaks in and feeds the rich to the field, and there are riots, and the riots are called compost riots, and the songs about them are cheerful.

And on the bare walls between the rind there are the marks: the stains of everything that ever entered the volume unattached. Birds mostly. Some are people. The trajectories are deterministic, so a stain is a testimony — from its shape and spread a reckoner can tell you where the body entered the air, and therefore which ledge, and therefore, often, whose hands. It is very hard to get away with murder in a place where the wall keeps the receipt. There are old marks lacquered over by descendants and tended like graves, and there is a trade in reading them, and there are marks nobody will read, on certain ledges, that everyone knows about and nobody discusses.

## The hour

Twice a season the fundamental drops out and for about an hour the Hold has weather and a third dimension.

The cysts hatch. They are in the rind everywhere, small hard grains that children scrape into jars, and they have one sense organ, a bone that rings with the nineteen-beat shove, and they hatch when it stops. They are the only animal in the world whose sense is tuned to nothing. The sift comes out of the walls in a bloom, translucent, grain-sized, and falls, and mates in the falling, and dies on the floor, and its eggs are the next cysts. It has one hour of adult life every few months and it spends all of it falling. It is the only creature here that has ever experienced the volume as a volume. Children open their mouths to it. It tastes faintly of salt, of the sea nobody in the Hold has seen.

And the people fall too. For one hour the world is built to the specifications of a wardrobe, and the people who own the wardrobes are the only ones who know how to stand in it: the rich come out and are suddenly, for sixty minutes, graceful, dancing on the open floor in a physics they were raised in, while the poor stagger and grab at nothing and laugh and go up onto the ledges anyway and jump. Nets, harnesses, dray-gut lines, the whole apparatus of licensed falling. Children conceived in the hour are called hour-got and are thought lucky and are teased.

Then the last bell, and everything still in the air is fired into stone, and every retune there is someone who stayed too long, and their mark is added, and their name is tapped on collarbones for a generation.

## What the tuners hear

They are made, not born. A tuner is boned at seven: rods set along the mandible and the shins and the sternum, so the skeleton becomes the instrument. Afterward they hear the whole bell — not the notches, not the crevices where everyone else lives, but the entire hundred-and-thirty-decibel structure, continuously, forever, in their bones. Speech is a scrape to them. They read hands. They cannot sleep in quiet; a null is agony; they sleep in the antinodes, in the loudest places in the world, on the floors of the poor, and the poor leave a mat out for them and consider it an honor and a small ongoing joke.

They know what the field does in exhaustive and beautiful detail and have stopped pretending to know why. They also keep the record: every retune for nine hundred years, every figure, every relocation, knotted cord and mapped wall, an archive nobody outside the guild is shown.

The figures move. Over nine centuries they have moved through a sequence. The guild's private argument — the one that splits every generation of them and is never resolved and is not spoken of in front of the frog-breeders or the tram drivers — is whether the sequence has a grammar. Some say maintenance. Some say search. A few say it is a sentence, very slow, eleven kilometers wide, written in trees on walls, and that the retunes are its words, and that we are inside something that is talking, and that the correct posture toward this is the posture of a lichen toward a conversation.

They announce nine days out either way. The drays walk. The trees spawn toward places where nothing grows yet.

**

And in the loud districts, in the noise, a mother with a newborn does the thing every mother does, which is to tap the child's name on its collarbone with a bone-stick, four beats, over and over, so the child knows it before it knows anything.

She taps it off the beat. Deliberately, carefully, in the gaps between the nineteen shoves a second that are coming up through the floor and through her own legs and into the infant's long bones.

So that the child can tell the difference between its name and the world.
