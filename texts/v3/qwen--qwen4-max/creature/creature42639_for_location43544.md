# Who Lives Here

## The majority

Most of the living things on this plain are two millimeters thick and have no names, no centers, and no edges you could point to.

The crust is not one organism but an agreement: a fungus that has learned to farm an alga, an alga that has learned to be farmed, a liverwort folded flat as a fish scale, a cyanobacterium that swells to eleven times its dry volume in the time it takes you to kneel down. Together they make a skin over the pale clay, grey and brittle and apparently dead for eleven months of the year, and in ninety seconds of rain they go the color of a bruised leaf and begin to work. They do most of the photosynthesis here. The trees, which are what a visitor photographs, are a minority industry.

The crust's citizens do not compete for light; there is more light than anything can use. They compete for the two millimeters of ground where water sits long enough to matter, and they compete by *staying* — by being able to stop entirely and start again without damage, which is a thing almost no animal can do. A crust patch the size of a dinner plate may be older than the tree above it. It has no idea it exists. It holds the surface together, and where it is broken the clay scalds and the scald spreads, and the crust's whole polity retreats at the speed of a widening wound.

If you want a census of this place, it is mostly a census of that skin. Everything else is a minority living on top of it, or inside it, or in the rare holes where the water gathers.

## The ones who made the floor

There is no topsoil here that was not eaten first.

The termites have been going down forty meters and coming back up with clay in their crops since before the trees arrived, and the plain you walk on is their spoil, redistributed, sorted, cemented, and walked over. They are the mining guild. They are also the undertakers: a tree that dies standing is not fallen but *consumed*, eighty years of it, from the heart outward, until the trunk is a sleeve of bark holding a column of frass, and one wet season it kneels.

The mounds are the visible part of an animal that is mostly not visible. A cathedral spire three meters high contains a colony you could hold in two hands. The rest is architecture: a lung of thin porous walls that flushes carbon dioxide by the simple trick of heating faster on the east face at dawn and the west at dusk, so the whole structure breathes twice a day, in and out, like something asleep. The wedge mounds stand edge-on to noon and are therefore compasses; you can navigate this country by insects who have never needed to navigate anything.

Inside the mound, in the warm dark, lives a snake as thin as a bootlace and pink as a grub, blind, with scales too fine to scratch, eating the eggs. It has never been outside. Its entire world is a building made by someone else, and it is a predator there, and the builders tolerate it the way a city tolerates rats — not out of kindness, but because the city is larger than its own attention.

When a colony dies the mound does not. It stands empty for centuries, weathering down to a pedestal of cemented subsoil, and the pedestal is a good place for a shrub to germinate in a wet decade, and so the plain is dotted with the ruins of cities that have become flowerpots.

The yabbies do the same work vertically and alone: down to the water table, up with mud pellets, a chimney at the mouth like a small industrial town. Their burrows end in a room with a water ceiling. In drought they carry a cup of that water in their gill chambers and stand in it, breathing damp, waiting. Their abandoned holes are the addresses of everything else — snakes, frogs, the small flattened mammals — so that the plain's housing market is entirely secondhand.

## The standing generation

Four trees to the hectare, and each one is a solution to an arithmetic problem: how to be alive where phosphorus is the scarcest thing in the world.

You cannot afford to drop a leaf here. So a leaf is not shed; it is *emptied*. Every atom of phosphorus is withdrawn back into the branch before the husk is let go, and what falls is a leaf with nothing left in it, and even that is eaten. The canopy is grey and hard and small because grey is cheap and hard is durable and small does not transpire. The tree looks stunted. It is four hundred years old and it is spending its whole life the way a miser spends a fortune: a millimeter of girth a year.

Underground it is not miserly at all. It sends a fan of sinkers thirty meters down through rock you can carve with a fingernail, and it pays fungi in sugar to go where roots cannot and dissolve phosphorus out of iron-bound grains with acid. Some of the shrubs do this themselves and spectacularly: three weeks after good rain they grow a fist of white bottlebrush rootlets, flood the gravel with citrate, take what they can, and let the whole apparatus die. A year's income, mined in a month, from stone.

On the seep aprons under the breakaways, where water is permanent and phosphorus is not, one plant has stopped bothering with the ground. The sundew is the size of a coin, red, jeweled, and it eats midges. It has become a predator because the soil is bankrupt and the air is rich — because the air, at dusk, is *full of phosphorus with wings on it*, rising off the pans in a fog. The sundew is mining the chorus.

In the crown of one tree in twenty there is a mistletoe, greener than its host, lush, spendthrift, transpiring water it did not pay for. It is the only thing here that looks like it lives somewhere wetter, and it does: it lives inside somebody else's plumbing. Its fruit is eaten by a small bird with a gut like a chute, which passes the seed in a quarter of an hour and must then wipe it onto a branch — a specific branch, on another tree, in a landscape where the next tree is fifty meters of open nothing away. That bird's entire life is a delivery run between four addresses per hectare. It is the busiest animal on the plain and it is busier than anything needs to be, because it is keeping a plant alive that has forgotten how to touch the ground.

The trees are the ones who are leaving. There are almost no seedlings; the cohorts stand in gaps two centuries wide; and in the low ground the water table is rising to meet the roots and bringing the salt with it. Where it comes within two meters, the samphire moves in, red and jade, and the trees go, and the scald widens a few meters a decade. The samphire is not an invader. It is the next tenant, and it has been waiting in the wings since before there were mammals, and it is very good at salt.

## The waiters

Most residents of this plain are not currently doing anything.

A meter down in the clay, a frog the size of a plum sits inside a bag made of its own shed skin, layer on layer, dried to a parchment cocoon with two tubes at the nostrils. It has been there two years. Its metabolism is a rumor. It is not asleep exactly; it is *spent*, running on the smallest current a vertebrate can run on, and it will not move until the ground above it goes soft — and when the ground goes soft it comes up through a meter of clay in the dark and starts calling before the water has even arrived, because it can read the flood three days out through the soles of its feet.

Then: eleven days of the loudest living thing on earth. Thousands per hectare of shallow water, a chorus with physical weight, a sound you have to shout across an arm's length to be heard over. Eggs, foam, tadpoles, and then a margin of mud so thick with metamorphs the size of a fingernail that the ground itself seems to be granulating and walking away. And then down, all of them, into the clay, into the bags of their own skin, for another two years.

The frogs of this plain exist as frogs for about one week in a hundred. The rest of the time they exist as a possibility stored in the ground.

The dust of an empty claypan is the same trick performed by smaller bodies. It is not dust. It is eggs — shrimp eggs, killifish eggs, dry for a decade, viable, waiting with a patience that has no experience of itself. Wet them and within the hour there are swimming things in a place that on Tuesday was a cracked white polygon. The shrimp will be mature in days and dead before the pan gets warm. The killifish has eight months and uses all of them: grow, spawn, press the eggs into the clay, die in the shrinking shallows. The swimming fish is not the animal. The swimming fish is a phase the animal goes through, the way a storm is a phase of the sky. The animal is the egg in the dust, and it is ten years long.

In the deepest pans, coiled in mucus chambers, the lungfish wait out droughts longer than most mammals live. They breathe air through a tube. They eat their own muscle. They have been doing this since before there were birds, and they are the oldest individual bodies on the plain — older than any tree, older than the mound they lie beside, quite possibly older than the last time the salt lake filled.

And the tortoises go the other way: not down but *along*, grazing the seep aprons at the pace of a grazing tortoise, twenty years to the size of a dinner plate, carrying their water inside them in a bladder they can draw from, so that a tortoise is a small mobile pan with legs. They are the commuters. Their tracks, faint depressions between one wet place and the next, are the only roads, and everything smaller uses them.

In a cold crack on the shaded side of a breakaway there is a pantry: several thousand moths folded like roof tiles, wing over wing, fat, aestivating out the hot months, waiting. A bird knows about this crack. It has known about it all its life, and its parent knew, and the crack is a resource the way a well is a resource. Nothing on this plain is more valuable than a place where a lot of bodies are asleep in one small dark room.

## The hurried, inside the slow

There is exactly one animal here that cannot afford to wait, and it survives by being small enough to hide inside the waiting of everything else.

A planigale weighs about as much as a coin and has a skull flattened like a spatula, so that it can pour itself into a crack in the clay a centimeter wide. It hunts crickets in the fissures at night. It must eat every few hours. It cannot store, cannot dormant, cannot go slow; it is a mammal running at mammal speed in a country where the currency is phosphorus and the interest rate is a millimeter a year. It lives in the cracks of the slow world the way a mouse lives in the walls of a cathedral, and the cathedral does not know, and the mouse is not being ironic.

Its population is a boom and a bust. In the flood years it is everywhere; in the dry years it is a handful of individuals in the deepest crabholes, holding on, and the whole species of that district is a few grams of hunger in a hole in the ground.

Above it, in the last of the light, the midge fog stands over the pans like smoke that has decided not to leave. This is the plain's actual harvest: the phosphorus that the termites carried up and the water carried sideways, finally airborne, finally moving fast. Everything with a mouth is in that fog. The bats come out in loose hundreds and take it apart. The nightjars take it apart. The sundews take it apart. The frogs, who are made of it, are shouting.

## Addresses

Some residents own a place, and the place outlasts them, and is inherited.

A pair of cranes holds a stretch of the ghost channel for thirty years. They come back to the same sedge, they trumpet in duets that carry across the flat, and they dance — an absurd, formal, unnecessary bouncing with wings half open, performed in front of each other, in a landscape with no audience, for decades. They are the only married thing here.

The bittern owns a pan you will not find. Its whole method is to not be there: bill vertical, body striped like the reeds, swaying when the wind sways, so that the pan contains one more reed than it does. Its boom carries two kilometers and comes from a direction that turns out to be wrong. You will hear it every evening of a wet season and you may never see it, and this is not a failure of your looking. This is the animal.

The nightjar has declined architecture altogether. It lays its eggs on bare gravel in the shade of a boulder, and it is itself gravel — mottled, broken-patterned, invisible at three meters with your eyes on it. By day it is a piece of the boulder country. By night it is a mouth with a net for a throat, and it drinks on the wing, skimming the pan, and its descending whistle is the sound of something falling slowly through warm air.

The monitor works the breakaways as a trapline: a kilometer of wall, forty boulders, each one a small country with a lizard on the hot face and a fern in the cold crack and ants underneath, and it remembers all of them, and it visits them in an order over three weeks. It is the only animal with a map of the whole cliff. It stores fat in its tail and goes slow and forks its tongue at the seep mud and knows which crack has the moths in it.

The raptor's dead tree is an address older than the raptor. Generations of birds have perched on that one whitened limb because it is the only thing above the plain for a kilometer, and under it is a midden: a slow accumulation of small jaws, planigale skulls the size of grains of rice, lizard vertebrae, the sorted arithmetic of who is being eaten here. The tree is being consumed from the inside over eighty years. The perch will be inherited until it kneels.

The weavers have solved predation with poverty. Nothing can climb to them, because nothing can *cross* to them: the tree stands alone in fifty meters of open ground, and the emptiness is a moat. They hang their nests over nothing, and the plain's scarcity, which starves everything else, is the wall around their children.

And the bats — the bats are the only residents for whom the whole region is one room. They roost in the breakaway cracks and in the hollows the termites are making of the living trees, and at dusk they go out across fifteen kilometers in a few minutes and read the plain as what it actually is: not a landscape but a scatter of wet points in a dry field, each point loud with insects. They navigate by the noise. The plain, to a bat, is a constellation.

## The room

At the foot of the northern breakaway, water that fell as rain on highlands far away a million years ago comes up warm and faintly salty and has built a low dome of precipitate around its own mouth. Inside the dome is a pool the size of a room.

In it live a fish, a snail, a flatworm, and a reed, and none of them live anywhere else in the universe.

They are not rare. Rare implies elsewhere. They are *singular*: the entire species is a few thousand animals in a bathtub, and has been since the ghost channel stopped being a river. When the river dried — over ages, silt and sill and salt and the slow abandonment of a drainage that could no longer reach the sea — everything in it had three options. Carry the water inside you, in a cocoon or an egg or a bladder, and wait. Or leave, on wings, and become a visitor. Or find the one room that never dries, and stay in it, and never leave it, and become the room.

The frogs took the first option. The birds took the second. The fish in the spring took the third, and it has never seen a river, and neither has its mother, and neither has anything in its line for longer than there have been ice ages.

The snail grazes the precipitate on the reed stems. The reed holds the rim of the dome together. The flatworm is the apex predator of this world — a centimeter of appetite, gliding over the weed, the top of the food chain in a country the size of a bedroom. Nothing here is dwarfed or stunted; there is no such thing as a small ecosystem, only a whole one in a small place.

They are the oldest continuous residents and the most fragile things on the plain. Their world depends on a pressure in an aquifer recharged a million years ago on hills they will never know about. A change in that head — a bore, a drought with a new shape to it, one boot on the soft dome — and the room closes, and the last of a lineage that outlasted the rearrangement of the continents ends in an afternoon, in warm water, without anything noticing for a thousand kilometers.

The plain around them is the reason they exist and the wall that keeps them. Nothing ever came to break it. That is the whole of their security, and it is made of nothing at all: it is made of *absence*, held for a hundred million years, and absence is not a thing you can maintain.

## The arrivals

The salt lake's permanent population is in the black mud, and it does not breathe oxygen. Sulphate reducers, working the buried gypsum, making the smell that comes off the shore when you break the crust with your heel — the smell of the plain's entire dissolved inheritance being metabolized in the dark. In the pink shallows above them, algae that like salt more than they like company, and brine shrimp grazing them, and a food chain three links long that runs whenever there is water and does not run when there isn't.

The birds are not residents. Ten thousand of them arrive from a continent away in the twice-in-a-lifetime years, long-legged and black-and-white, and nest on islands of gypsum crust that crunch like frost, and raise chicks in a place that will be a white glare again before the chicks can fly. They knew. Nobody knows how they knew. They are not living here; they are *blooming* here, the way the pan blooms with shrimp, the way the crust blooms green in ninety seconds. The lake is the organism. The birds are what it does when it rains.

## The listener

There are people for whom none of this is empty, and who have names for the quartz ribbons, and for whom the white seams wandering the red plain are not writing but the spine of something lying under the skin — a body whose story is only legible at walking pace, and which is therefore a book that must be read with the feet, in order, over years. That is a form of residence too, and it is older here than anything except the crust.

And there is whoever is standing on the pale ground at dusk now, hearing the chorus arrive intact from fifteen kilometers away and being unable to tell it from the one at their boots, and finding the place empty.

The emptiness is imported. It is not a property of the plain. The plain is the loudest place you have ever stood, and every gram of it is occupied, and the frogs and the termites and the flatworm and the two-millimeter skin are not lonely and have no capacity to be. The loneliness is a thing the listener brought with them, like a chair.

## What is actually living here

Step back far enough and the individuals stop being the point.

There is one resident of this plain that is fifteen kilometers wide, an inch deep, and continuous. It has no body of its own. It borrows bodies — a cicada for the day shift, a frog for the night, a bittern for the low notes, a hundred thousand cricket wings for the texture, and under all of it the hiss of sheet water moving over gravel at the speed of a minute hand. Its personnel have changed beyond recognition; the species that made this sound when there were no birds are gone, and their replacements' replacements are here now, and the sound did not stop. It has been running every evening since before there were flowers.

It is the largest thing alive on the plain and it is made entirely of other things being alive briefly. It is what the ground does with the last of its phosphorus. A hundred million years of rain took everything soluble away and left iron and aluminum and silica, and what remains of the mountain's substance is mostly a delta on a coast three thousand kilometers off — but a little of it, a vanishing fraction, a few grams per hectare, is still here, and this evening it is in the throat of a frog.

That is what all of them are. The crust is granite holding still. The termite is granite carried upward, mouthful by mouthful. The tree is granite standing thirty meters tall and spending itself a millimeter a year. The midge is granite with wings, and the sundew eats it, and the frog eats it, and the frog is granite that has learned to shout, and the shout goes out fifteen kilometers over water that will take a week to decide which way to lean.

The mountain is being spent. This is what spending sounds like at the very end, when almost nothing is left and the little that remains is thrown into the air every evening at enormous volume, saying nothing, to no one, in a country with no one to hear it and nothing to block the sound.

At the edge of the pan, on mud that has been soft for three days, a frog the size of a plum inflates and calls. Under it: red gravel, an iron crust you could break with a heel, forty meters of clay that used to be a mountain, and below that the competent stone that nothing living has ever touched.

The call goes out. It arrives, intact, at the other side of the plain, where another frog answers it, having waited two years underground for exactly this.
