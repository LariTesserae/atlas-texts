Because a click is a survey.

The clicker sits in a bore no wider than a thumb and strikes the wall with a hinged plate on the front of its head, and then it waits for the wall to answer, and only when the answer comes does it strike again. So its tempo is not a mood. Its tempo is a measurement. Slow clicking means a long wait means a big hollow; fast clicking means the room has shrunk. A colony in a fresh fracture clicks at the pace of a man walking. A colony in a hollow that is filling up clicks faster, and faster, and at the end it clicks like hail, and then it leaves, because the plant has heard it and come, and the room it measured is now wood.

That is the arrangement. The clicker finds the void; the plant grows into the void; the void becomes a gallery, and the gallery weeps nectar at its walls, thick and pale, sugared turnip and cold iron, and the clickers drink it and lay eggs in the grain. The colony is paid in sugar for the survey, and evicted by its own success. A clicker-keeper's whole craft is keeping a colony homeless: splitting the box, offering it a new crack, moving it on before the tightening. Keepers are usually old. They have the patience of people whose work will not finish in their hands.

Because a box of clickers lashed to a cold strut will make a tip lean, too. Quietly. Below the dish. It takes forty years instead of one, and nobody has to die, and there are bridges in the eastern basin that were begun by a woman who is now a knob on a node wall, and tapped into service by her great-granddaughter, who never held a flute in her life and thinks the flute-players are show-offs and murderers.

The plant does not distinguish. A click and a flute are the same word in the same language. Hold onto that; it comes back.

**The hurry**

Below every cistern, down the helical bark, runs a black strip a hand wide: the weep. Dry and dead-looking for nine hours. Then the bend goes over and the basin gulps and for forty seconds the strip is a river, and it wakes.

The crust on it is not crust. Under the flat grey scab is a shelled grazer the size of a lentil that spends nine hours as an egg in the rime and forty seconds alive. It hatches in the first second of the flood, moults three times, mates, lays, and dies before the water has finished draining off the lip. It has no mouth in its adult form and does not need one. It is ninety percent water it never has time to lose. Its entire biography is one gulp. There are nine hundred and seventy-three generations of it in a year and it has been doing this since before the anchors had rings.

People scrape it. It is the only protein that comes to your door instead of being carried, and it makes a red flour that tastes of iron and shellfish and keeps forever, and the scraping has to be done inside the forty seconds, because the weep is dry before and after and because the forty seconds are the only time you are allowed to make a noise.

So the children are sent out in the roar. They go down the lip on knotted lines with copper scrapers and they shout, and shriek, and laugh, and call each other filthy names, and hammer the bark with their heels, all of it, all of the noise a child has been holding for nine hours, all of it at once, in the one window the world provides — and then the water stops and they come up the line in absolute silence with their mouths shut and their eyes enormous.

Every laugh here is scheduled. There is a word, tapped, that means *the laugh*, and it is the same word as the name of the local water. A person who laughs outside the gulp is a person who dies, and everyone knows a family that happened to.

**Those who own a bend**

The masking radius of a gulp is a few kilometers, and it is not shared equally. Some crotches hold one cistern and one household, and that household can be loud whenever it likes. Others hold nine huts and a water that belongs, by old agreement, to whoever spliced the ladder.

So there is rent. Forty seconds, rented, paid in hurry-meal or felt or copper or a season of carrying. There are people who own their own mask and people who have never in their lives made a sound they were entitled to. There are surgeons who go down inside the siphon tube with a copper blade and shorten the fold, and by moving a gulp eleven minutes they move every meeting, every marriage, every build-window and every name for thirty kilometers, and they are paid accordingly, and sometimes they are killed accordingly. The tube is full of water while they work. They have to be out before the bend. Most of them are.

**The rasps, who are a dialect**

Forty thousand blind animals filing along a wire in the dark, and the reason they do not fall off is that they know the bore. Each herd is fluent in the local pipe-notes the way a valley is fluent in a vowel. Take a herd two hundred kilometers west and it is still walking confidently and it is walking on nothing, because the notes it steers by mean different distances there. This is called going out of tune, and it happens, and it is the reason there are felt-grey drifts on the rock floor four kilometers below certain cables.

They file in a stagger. No two rasps in a herd draw the file at the same instant; the herd's noise is spread thin and even across the shimmer-band, so that from any distance a herd sounds exactly like the fine filaments doing nothing in particular. A herd at rest is invisible to the dish. A herd that panics bunches its rhythm and becomes, for about four seconds, the loudest discrete object in the basin.

People cannot drive rasps. People can *re-plumb* them. Open a valved septum here, plug one there, and the pipe's note changes and forty thousand animals turn as one, and this is how meat comes to a node. The voicers are the closest thing here to engineers and the closest thing here to criminals. A mis-voiced line in the middle basin sent a herd along a strut that had been shed the season before. Nobody taps about it.

Rasp felt is the clothing. Rasp fat is the lamp oil. Rasp gut-paste is the only sour food. And the flute you build a bridge with is a rasp's leg bone, which means that every bridge in the world is a piece of the animal the plant was feeding, used to ask the plant for more.

**The hush is a shepherd**

It is not malice and it is not plague. It is the pruning arm of the same economy. It comes to noise; noise is where the plant grows; where the plant grows thick, the grazing is good; where the grazing is good, the rasps are many; where the rasps are many, there is eventually a panic, and the hush eats, and the panic is loud, and the plant grows toward the panic. The loop closes. The hush has been gardening this basin for longer than there have been people to be gardened.

It also knows the clock. This is the part visitors are slow to accept. A hush habituates to the siphons exactly as you do — they are inedible, they are weather — and an old one learns which bends have huts under them, and it will sit on a strut above your cistern with its dish folded and wait, without moving, for nine hours, because it has learned that at this water, at the bend, something sometimes plays a flute.

So the law has clauses that come after the first one, taught later, when a child is old enough to be frightened usefully:

*Never build twice running at the same water.*
*The forty seconds mask your going out. Nothing masks your sitting still afterward.*

Almost nobody is taken while playing. They are taken in the eighth hour, on the return, when the hands have gone stiff over the flute's mouth and the body has begun to shiver hard enough to knock against the bark.

And the hush itself must court, and it courts in taps on the strut, because it cannot afford its own voice in air. Which means somewhere out there a growing tip is leaning toward a hush's love-tapping, thickening, building the very platform the pair will hunt from. Everything here that wants to be found builds the world a little more toward itself. There is no exception to this. There is only the question of who else is listening.

**The green hours**

The lamps and lures make sense once you understand that the aurora is the only time seeing works.

There is a guild of animals here that is functionally blind for weeks and then, when the sheets come down, has one enormous wet eye and two hours of vision. They hunt in the green. They gorge, they mate, they lay, and then they go back to being furniture. They must be ready always, at no notice, which is why they are so slow and so fat and so strange.

That is why everything stops. The stillness under the aurora is not reverence. It is the same stillness as under the dish, run on a different sense: for as long as the sky is lit, the dangerous thing is not noise but *shape*.

People freeze too, out of the same arithmetic, and have made a virtue of it, and there are prayers tapped on the sternum during green that nobody would admit to owning. And there are green-builders, who have noticed that the hush also freezes, and who therefore play in the aurora without a mask, unroared, unmuffled, gambling that a dish stays folded while the sky is lit. Sometimes it does. The green-builders have a very high reputation and a very short average life, and the two facts are related in the usual way.

**What a person is, physically**

A barrel chest and a slow heart, for the thin air. Ears with muscles that shut them, used for rockfall. Eyes that are large and mostly ornamental, good for aurora and cambium-glow and nothing else.

And a jaw. The jaw is the real organ. You put your teeth on the bark and the wood tells you the truth: which water is speaking three valleys over, whether a herd has bunched, whether something with soft feet has landed on the strut you are standing on. Air lies — air brings you the shimmer and the wind and your own blood. Wood does not. The hush's feet make no sound in air and a very small sound in wood, and the difference between those two facts is the entire profession of the watch.

The watch are the old ones, and the deaf. Deafness here is not a deficit; it is a specialization. A woman who has lost her ears to a lifetime of roar reads the floor with her gums and is never fooled by a false echo, and she sits at the node's mouth with her jaw against the grain and eleven people sleep because of her. Her teeth went first, worn to the nerve from listening, and she was proud of each one.

Babies are carried against the collarbone, face in, so that crying goes into bone and not into air. A mother walks around with her infant's scream conducting through her skull and her face perfectly calm. Weaning is learning to tap. The terrible season is the second year, when a child is loud by nature and cannot yet be taught, and the whole node takes turns holding it, and there are small knobs on the walls for the ones who were not held well enough.

And the walls: a node is a compost engine and it thickens where it is spoken to. Talk in your house and your house closes. Every family knows the arithmetic — a chatterbox lineage cooks itself into a smaller and smaller warm room until one season the crotch seals and the hearth is inside the wood. So quiet is not manners here, it is housekeeping, and grandmothers shush children with real fear, and the ascetics who barely tap at all live in caverns.

The dead go into the compost, and the compost is the heat. You are warm because of your grandmother, specifically, mechanically. And once a year the family taps a dead person's name-figure at the node wall, and the wall leans toward the name and thickens, and after four generations there is a knob, and after ten there is a pillar, and you can read your genealogy with your hands in the dark. The ones lost crossing get a cairn on the rock anchor instead, out in the cold, where nothing grows toward them, and that is the worst thing that can happen to anybody here.

**Eleven hours**

A bridge is eleven hours of music.

Do the arithmetic. The water speaks for forty seconds and refills for nine hours. A year of that is nine hundred and seventy-three gulps, and forty seconds of each is thirty-eight thousand seconds, which is a little under eleven hours. That is the whole of it. Eleven hours of a rising figure, paid in forty-second installments across a year, and no human being can stay awake for nine hundred and seventy-three consecutive nights.

That is why nobody has ever finished one alone. Not because it is hard. Because of division.

The minimum is three, and three is a tragedy; the working number is nine or eleven, in relay, with feeders and a watch and someone whose only job is to hold the player's hands over the flute's mouth when the roar stops, because by the sixth month the player cannot be trusted to do it. Bridge-players have a face you can recognize: the eyes go loose and the jaw sets and they develop a tick at the nine-hour mark that stays with them for life. They are fed by hand. They are spoken of the way you speak of someone who has agreed to be spent.

And the player does not sit on the strut. The player hangs in the gap on a sinew ladder forty meters long, spliced by four generations, with a growing tip coming toward them from each side, leaning in toward the noise, thickening as they come. The tips reach the ladder before they reach each other. They grow around it. They take it.

That is how you know a bridge is finished: your ladder is inside the world. The splices your great-grandfather made are in the grain now, under bark, and you can put your teeth on the new cable and feel them, a little irregularity in the conduction, four knots and a hundred years. Sometimes the tips take the ladder with the player still on it. There is a person-shaped knot in the middle of the Long Span and everyone who crosses it puts a hand on it, and it is not a memorial, it is a *joint*. It is load-bearing.

The music is all ascending. Rising means come; the tip leans and thickens. Falling means let go, and falling figures are the other profession, the let-go players, who are called in to shed a dead strut before it takes a settlement with it, or to make the plant loosen its grip on a cistern it is strangling. Their music is the only descending music there is, and it sounds like grief to everyone who hears it, because it is played at funerals too, to make the node release the dead. They cry while they work. It is not required. It just happens.

**The bell**

Copper is prised out of fresh fractures and hammered cold, and a cold-hammered bell is a poor thing: dull, thick-lipped, work-hardened, full of the microscopic cracks that will one day become one crack.

You cannot hammer it here. Hammering is noise. So the copper is carried west, past the last cistern, out to the growth front where nothing lives and nothing hunts, and there, in the only silence in the world, a person sits on raw scree and makes as much noise as they like.

They hammer. They shout. They sing — actually sing, in air, with the throat, the only singing that occurs anywhere in three thousand kilometers — and they ring the new bell once, in the open, and hear their own name go out over a range that has not been broken long enough to have any hollows, and nothing comes.

Then they stuff the mouth with felt and carry it home and hang it in the hut, and never ring it again.

Every household has one. The felt is changed each generation so the rot doesn't get in. A bell is a recording of the one hour of a person's life when they were sincere, and it hangs in the dark with its mouth stopped, and everybody in the room knows exactly what it is for.

Because there is one other use. A bell carries *out*, farther than a shout, farther than a flute, and the hush comes to noise, and it comes to the loudest noise. If it has taken your child's line and is working up it, you can take the felt out.

Most bells are never rung. When one is, the family keeps the cracked bell afterward as a bowl, and does not tap the name of the person who rang it, because the wall would lean, and they cannot bear a knob that size.

**Unnamed water**

A place is named for its gulp, and the name is a rhythm, and two places whose waters speak the same rhythm are kin, and their people marry, and a person will walk four hundred kilometers to meet a name-twin they have never seen and stand beside them in the dark listening to two identical waters and weeping without making a sound.

Which means a cistern that drifts is a town losing its name. Out west there is one that has been failing for two centuries and now speaks every eleven days. Its people did not die of thirst. They died of *quiet*: fewer taps, so the tips leaned away; the node cooled by degrees; the herds went out of tune and left; the siphon's fold thickened because nothing near it was making noise, and thickening made it fail faster. A settlement here dies by getting quieter, and the plant walks away from it while it is still full of people.

There are surgeons who went out to cut that fold. Two came back. What they reported was that there was nothing wrong with the tube.

The survivors are unnamed water. They carry a rhythm eleven days long, which fits no other place, and they are treated as ghosts, and sometimes as holy, and they are the ones who go west.

Because the front is where you can be loud. There is a slow walking sect out there that stays a few kilometers ahead of the pale tips, moving at the speed of the growth, singing. They sing all the time. It is the only continuous singing in the world and it is entirely safe and it is a kind of exile, and what they are singing about, mostly, is that they will not be leaned toward.

And the tips turn. Every one of them turns, and thickens, and comes, at a few kilometers a year, behind the singers, into the hollows the singing finds. The rasps will follow the new bores. The hush will follow the rasps. In four hundred years the children of those singers will be sitting in a warm node with their jaws against the wall, learning before their own names that they may only build while the water speaks.

Every song here is a seed, and nobody who plants one lives to hear it arrive.

**What lives in the fundamental**

And there is something in the trunk-lines that has never heard any of this.

It lives four kilometers down a pipe open at both ends, and its whole sensory world is the note under everything, the pressure in the sternum, the thing below hearing. It cannot hear the siphons. It cannot hear the rasps, or the clickers, or the rockfall, or the flute. It is deaf to the entire audible world and always has been. It knows only the fundamental and the two times a day when the fog tide turns and the key changes, and it steers by those, and it goes very slowly, and it is very large, and it grazes the pale sweet inner walls, and when it moves it changes the pipe's effective length by enough to matter.

You feel it as a beat. A slow wobble in the drone, one swell every eleven or twelve seconds, in the back teeth, and no instrument here can find it and no ear can locate it, and the old people tap a figure that means *someone is walking in the pipe* and go on with what they were doing.

The hush cannot find it. It makes no sound above the fundamental; its voice *is* the drone, indistinguishable, unmaskable, unaddressed. It is the only animal here that has never had to be quiet, because it has never once been audible.

Twice in the recorded rings, two of them have met. The beat becomes a pulse, deep and slow, and it goes on for a season, and every living thing in three thousand kilometers feels it in the sternum without stopping, and children born in that season carry a name-figure with a rest in the middle of it, and nobody knows what the two of them said to each other, or whether *said* is a word that applies.

**So: what lives here**

Two listeners. One that grows toward what it hears, and one that comes toward what it hears. Everything alive in this basin is a solution to the problem of being heard by the first without being heard by the second — the stagger, the moss sole, the taps that stay in the wood, the eggs on the feet, the nectar-paid survey, the forty-second mask.

And the people are the only ones who know that there are two, and can therefore aim. That is the whole human niche here: not warmth, not tools, not fire. *Address.* A sound meant for one listener and hidden from the other. The flute inside the roar is the first sentence anybody here ever spoke, and every bridge is a paragraph of it, and the bell with the felt in its mouth is the terrible alternative, which is telling the truth to both at once.

They are the second clicker colony. They found the hollows; the plant came; the hollow became a gallery and wept sugar at the walls, and they drink it, and they are moved on by their own success, and the node seals, and the family goes out along the cable with the ladder and the box and the mute bell, looking for a new crack to be loud in. The plant pays them in warmth and nectar for the survey. It has been paying them for a long time. It does not know their names, and it is leaning toward every one of them.

Under all of it the drone, which is not listening, and is not for anyone, and is the only sound in the world that has never once been a message. It is what you hear when there is nothing to hear. It is what the dying hear last, in the teeth, after the water has gone out of range.

And on a cold strut somewhere above four kilometers of nothing, a person with their hands over the flute's mouth, waiting; and eleven hundred meters below them, inside the pipe, something the length of a valley turning slowly toward the tide, and neither of them will ever know that the other is there.
