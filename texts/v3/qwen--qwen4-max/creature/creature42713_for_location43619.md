## The Citizens

Start with one, because there is no other way in.

It is nine millimetres long and the colour of the moon at the base of a fingernail. Its body is a comma of wet paper, seven pairs of legs folded under it like a hand half-closing, gills combing quietly at the belly, gut a dark thread visible through the cuticle from the outside — you can watch a meal move through it. Where the eyes were there is nothing: no stalks, no pigment, no scar. The cuticle closes over that part of the head smoothly, the way skin closes over a place where a tooth used to be.

The antennae do the seeing, and they do not see. They are pitted along their length with tens of thousands of small wells, and in each well a molecule sits down and is recognised. What arrives is not a picture. It is a chord: *this edge, this curvature, this much of the old scent left, this much fresh, therefore here, therefore press.* It holds between its mouthparts a bead of grit and glue the size of a grain of salt. Its mandibles are worn blunt and square, a spatula, a trowel. It sets the bead into the eleventh course of a tower it will never perceive, presses — never taps — and holds. The glue sets in about the time it takes to walk a body-length. It knows the set has happened because the mortar stops smelling of *now*.

Then it backs down the outside stair, one leg at a time, in a silence so complete that the sound of its own mandible closing does not leave the millimetre it was made in.

---

Understand what it is holding. Not a tool. It has never had a tool and never will; the trowel is its face. The kiln is its belly: sternal glands lay down carbonate as a wet paste that stiffens into curbs, thresholds, the fused galleries where two towers lean together. The line it draws behind it as it walks is mucus, and it hardens, and it is a sentence. Everything in this city was made by a mouth or a belly or a trail, and everything in it was made by an animal that could not check its work.

The plan is a fragrance laid into the mortar by the dead. Fresh mortar carries the imperative — *build here, this high, this curve* — and it burns weak blue for nine or ten days while the bacteria in it cure, and then it goes dark and goes on smelling for centuries, fainter, in the subjunctive. So light and grammar are the same clock here. The glowing suburbs are the present tense. The black centre is the ancient past, still legible to an antenna, still obeyed. A mason working at the roof-edge is standing in the only lit part of the world, and it does not know that lit means anything; it knows only that the mortar there is loud with instruction and the mortar below is a whisper.

Nobody in the room has ever seen the room. There are eyes in it — the anemones in the door-niches have crude ones, the sea spiders have four apiece, the guest who comes four times a season has two magnificent ones — but no citizen has any. Sight here is entirely outsourced. The city is visible only to its predators and its tenants.

---

The trades are not castes. They are ages of the mouth.

It molts. Everything it works with is shed: the trowel, the rasp on the foreleg, the pads that press. For a day and a half the new mouthparts are soft as wet ash and it cannot lay a grain, and in that day and a half it does the soft work — it broods, it wads the galleries with mucus batting that swallows the noise of the young, it carries the dead down the terraces to the shore. Then the face hardens and it goes back up the tower and builds for eleven days. Then the trowel begins to round off, the rasp loses its bite, and it is sent to the throat.

That is the last trade. The gatherers go out into the angular grit under the plug and bring back fresh fracture, and they are all old, all worn, all nearly finished, and they work in the one place in this room where a sound can get out. The room's ear-shape gathers whatever happens near the throat and throws it up through the rubble amplified. So the city's exposed hand is made of its exhausted citizens. Nothing in the arrangement is cruel. Nothing in it is even decided. The odor says *go up when the mouth is blunt*, and the odor has been saying it for a very long time, and the ones who did not go up left fewer copies of the saying.

On the foreleg of every gatherer there is a fine transverse ridge, a file of tiny teeth, ancestral, unmistakable. It is the organ that used to sing. It has been repurposed for trimming grains. It is the only instrument left in a city that was selected, over an unguessable span, for making no music at all.

---

The young are brooded in the wadded galleries and they hatch already fluent. They have never been taught. They come out of the batting with the whole grammar in their antennae and they begin pressing where the smell says press, and they copy, and the copy is imperfect, and the imperfections are the reason this room is so crowded.

Consider the aqueduct that carries water from one part of the room to the same part of the room. It is useless. It is also a sheltered slack channel with a slow throughflow, and it is packed to the roof with drifting comma-bodies who use it as a nursery, and the masons eat the overflow. Consider the chamber four millimetres too small for anything that has ever lived here: it is a perfect brood-cell for a scale-worm, and there are nine hundred scale-worms in this city, all of them in mistakes. Consider the spiral stair built to a body-plan the species abandoned eleven thousand generations ago. Nothing fits it. Sea spiders molt on it, hanging from the treads like scaffolding out of true, because it is the only structure in the room with the right spacing for a leg to catch.

A correct city would have had room in it for nothing but its builders. This one is habitable because it is wrong. Two hundred thousand citizens and perhaps a million guests, and the guests are all living in the typos.

---

The dead go into the lake, and the reason is editorial.

A corpse smells like an instruction. It smells like *build*, in the wrong place, at the wrong height, with the wrong curve — a lie in the only language anyone here has. A body left in a gallery would be read by the next generation and obeyed, and the district would grow a tumour of nonsense, and the nonsense would be inherited. So the soft-mouthed carriers take every corpse down the terraces and release it at the shoreline, and it sinks through the interface into brine with no oxygen in it, and there it stops smelling. There is no rot. There is no false sentence. The lake is a quarantine of the text.

It is also, therefore, the text's other author.

The archive does not leach much, but it leaches. When the tide drives an internal wave along the interface and the whole ordered stack of citizens lifts and turns and settles, a thin breath of very old odor comes up out of the brine and hangs along the shore terraces, and the masons who work there read it and build accordingly. This is why the deep districts are conservative to the point of parody — the same wall, the same gate-curve, the same eleven courses, laid over and over above a lake full of ancestors who all say the same thing. And it is why the roof districts invent. Up there the only smell is fresh mortar and raw dissolving chalk, no dead at all, and the plan gets copied loosely, and the misspellings bloom.

The city is squeezed between an archive below and an eraser above, and the citizens are the copying that happens in between.

---

The eraser is the ceiling, and there is a trade for it.

The sulfide fog off the brine rises and meets the oxygen coming down through the throat, and where they meet on the roof the water goes sour and the chalk gives way — a fingernail's thickness a year, silent, a snow of flour falling for hours before it lands. The plasterers work up in that fog against the raw wound. They are the only citizens whose instructions do not come from the dead. Raw dissolving carbonate has its own smell, sharp and mineral and *unwritten*, and it provokes a behaviour the inherited plan does not contain: thicken here, buttress here, lay paste into the soft place until it stops smelling soft.

They are patching a membrane and they do not know there is a membrane. They are laying carbonate against acid; the city is an antacid the size of a cathedral; it is a scab extending over a wound that will not close, and it is doing this with the same gland that makes the doorsteps. In a few thousand years the roof will go, and the bell will lose its throat and its hush, and the light and the noise and everything that listens will come in. The plasterers are not delaying this. They are, in the plainest reading, keeping the room habitable right up to the instant it stops being a room, which is what everything here does.

---

Four times a season the guest pours itself down through the slot: a pale cold-water octopus, small enough to fit, eight arms and two eyes and a memory.

Two hundred thousand animals stop. Not hide. Stop, mid-gesture, one leg raised, mortar wet on the mouthparts. The city becomes an architecture with nobody in it, and the guest walks the streets for some hours taking six or eleven or forty, and the towers do not react, and then it withdraws and the water carries the news chemically for a day, and the raised leg comes down and finishes its step.

Here is what the guest is, apart from a mouth. It is the only thing that has ever read the whole plan. It has touched every district in a single night; it holds the four gates and the useless stair and the terraces as one connected shape in whatever it has instead of a mind, and it comes back and takes from a different quarter each time. No citizen will ever have that. No citizen has ever had a whole sentence.

And its arms are wet with the fragrance of the plan when it leaves. It squeezes out and crosses a kilometre of grey gravel and squeezes into another bubble in the young chalk, another held room, another two hundred thousand, and it brings the smell of a foreign city in on its suckers, and the masons at that throat read something they have never read and build a district that is a translation. If two rooms in this shelf share a misspelling — the same too-small chamber, the same stair to the same obsolete body — that is probably how. The rooms have never made a sound that reached each other. They have never needed to. Something walks between them, and it is the thing that eats them.

---

The rest of the residents are not under the discipline, and this is managed by zoning.

Sea spiders tick. Brittle stars in the wall-cavities scrape when they reach out with their arms like hands feeling for a dropped coin. The nemertean hunts by taste and is silent, and the scale-worms read the curbs and are silent, but the tenants who make noise are all down low, in the deep districts by the brine, as far from the throat as the room permits, and the waist-high wall with its four mucus curtains is not a defence against anything that walks. It is a baffle. It keeps the clatter of the guests out of the upper galleries where the ear begins to gather.

At the throat itself, outside the plug, there are listeners. They never come in; they cannot fit, or will not risk it. They hang in the open sea with their lateral lines turned toward the gravel and they wait for the room to speak. Almost always it says nothing. Once every few years a pocket of dissolved gas collects under a chalk shelf and lets go, and a plum-sized bubble rises and breaks at the interface, and the room *rings* — one clean note thrown out for a kilometre in every direction — and then the whole city goes flat to the ground for a day and a half. Not from fright, or not only. A ring brings the listeners to the plug and keeps them there, working their mouths at the grit, for about a day and a half. The flattening is the city holding still while its own voice is being answered.

In the plaza stand the four trees, clumps of tubeworm with their red plumes withdrawn to a fringe of dark hair. They are older than every building. They are not citizens and they do not build; they simply drink the seep and stand there, and the city grew around them the way flesh grows around a splinter, and the reason there is a plaza is that four animals got there first.

The anemones in the door-niches are farmed. Their faint flicker draws the suspended throng in; the masons take the overflow and, not knowing they are farmers, bring them scraps. The bacterial mat on the open ground is pasture, grazed to bare chalk in some places, velvety in others. The bacteria in the mortar are the light, the cure, and the calendar. Everything in this room that is bright is alive, and everything that is alive is somebody's food, and the whole two-month feast of marine snow coming down through the plug fattens the plaza districts while the shore districts go on eating cold chemistry and resent nothing, having no capacity for it.

In winter a grey coin of noon crosses the floor. Nothing looks up. Looking up is not a thing any of this can do. But the larvae against the ceiling can, and do — they are the one phototactic stage, and when the rumor of light comes down through the rubble they swim toward it, into the throat, into the outbound tide. The only light in the city that does not come from something alive is also the only thing that ever calls anyone out.

---

Almost all of them die. The number that find another soft place in the young chalk, where water is going down through a plug of angular grit, is one in a very great number.

Picture that one. Nine millimetres of wet paper alone in a new cavity with no lake in it, no wall, no gates, no towers, no trees, no dead. It has the whole misspelled plan in its antennae and nothing to read it against. It gathers a grain of fresh fracture, glues it in its mouthparts, and presses it into the raw floor — and the plan says *here, an open space; here, a plaza*, because the plan has said *plaza* for as long as there has been a plan, and the reason there is a plaza is four red animals that are a kilometre away or a hundred kilometres away or dead these nine thousand years.

So it builds an emptiness for trees that are not there.

And it holds. And the open ground it has left is the one place in the new room with clean flow over it, and tubeworm larvae drift down through the gravel looking for exactly that, and settle, and put out their plumes. The city calls its trees. It has never once known it was doing it.

---

What lives here, then. Two hundred thousand blind bodies, each of them a few weeks long, each of them a face that hardens and wears out and is spent at the throat. A million guests in the errors. Four elders in the plaza. A lake full of every citizen there has ever been, in order, saying the same thing very quietly. A guest with eyes who carries the fragrance of one city into the next on the wet rings of its arms. A wound in the roof and the paste laid against it. And under all of it, the thing that actually resides here and never dies and has never been alive: a habit of pressing, copied by hands that cannot read, from a sentence nobody wrote, in a hundred small white rooms in one shelf of rock, none of which has ever heard the others, all of them glowing at the edges, all of them building against their own exhale.

The leg comes down. The step is finished. The mortar begins to shine.
