# Who Lives in Basin Nine

Ask, and someone will hand you a list, and the list will be wrong in a way nobody local finds interesting.

The register does not count people. It counts shares. Nineteen hundred and six names, four thousand three hundred and something drip-rights, and the mismatch is not an error: a woman can hold eleven undersides of catwalk and one fog-shadow and be, legally, a small rain. A dead man can hold a cistern. There is a share held in escrow by a lawsuit, which is to say that one of the residents of Basin Nine is a disagreement between the Gants and the Pells about a pipe, six years old now, fed and watered and housed, with its own file, its own anniversaries, its own children who have married each other twice. The pipe has corroded through in two places. Both families maintain it. Neither will concede that maintenance implies ownership.

And there is the standing share: an allotment of water assigned to the sump itself, inalienable, unowned, defended by nobody and everybody. Thirty meters of black netted surface with a heron in it. The heron has no legal personhood and has, in practice, a liter a day, and if you proposed to take it you would find out how fast nineteen hundred people can become one animal.

---

Go down first. Under the crust plates the basin is not empty and never has been: sandhoppers in their thousands in a space the size of a hat brim, beetles, woodlice the color of candle wax, mites, the pale gravel mice. By weight there is more life under the flats than on them. The town has known this for nineteen years and still walks carefully, the way you walk over a floor you have been told is thin.

The mice are the important ones. They do not drink. They have stopped needing to; they take their water out of the bodies of insects, and their kidneys have gone to paper, and forty generations in they have begun to be a slightly different animal from the ones that came over the berm in year one — longer in the leg, paler, less interested in depth, living in the crust rather than under it. There is a woman who traps them, measures them, writes the numbers in a book, and lets them go at the exact plate she lifted, and her books are the most accurate instrument in the basin, better than the piezometers, better than the stakes. The drying front descends four centimeters a year and the mice knew it first.

They are also the first natives. Everything else here is a refugee. The mice are the first residents of Basin Nine that could not have lived in the sea, that have no ancestral memory of water, that would drown in a bucket. The valley's citizens have arrived. They are two inches long and nobody has named them properly yet.

---

The refugees are the ones visitors notice.

Shore crabs in the cable trays, pale and slow, carrying a spoonful of water across the dry spans in the roof of the shell, moulting into the troughs, grazing the moss off the underside of the world. They came through the sluice as larvae in the first month and they have never left and they do not know anything has happened. Nineteen years is nothing to a crab. They are still on the shore. The shore has simply been rebuilt above them in iron, and the tide is now a shift schedule, and the moon has been replaced by a low flat B.

Nobody eats them. Ask why and you will get five answers and none of them will be the reason. Because they carry the sea in them. Because they were here first. Because a man at Sump Road ate one in year three and in year six the note broke. Because they are the ones who came back. The children say that if you eat one you will hear the silence, and the children say this without knowing what the silence is, and the adults do not correct them. The prohibition is older than every explanation of it. It came into the basin with the crabs.

Frogs beyond counting in the drip troughs, tuned. Four stages in series, three running, one spare, a note slightly flat of B, and the seams at dusk ring on its harmonics, and you feel it in the sternum before you hear it in the ear. People over forty say the frogs used to sound wrong. People under nineteen cannot hear what they mean by that. This is the first thing the basin has made that is not a copy of something else: not a shore, not a marsh, not a machine, but a chord that only exists where these pumps and these frogs are in the same bowl. It is nineteen years old. It is younger than the town's teenagers. It is the oldest thing the town has that is entirely its own.

Bats in the pipe lagging. Snails coming up the piles at night to graze the hanging meadows from below, which is to say the town is being slowly eaten by things that believe they are on a shoreline, and the town is right to let them. Starlings in the tens of thousands, going up in sheets that change the light, and the peregrine on the stack that has never had to hunt anything slower than a pigeon and has consequently become a kind of aristocrat.

---

The people are organized around maintenance the way other towns are organized around money.

**Dobb Hesk** does not sweep. His shed has wire, stakes, a ledger, and a brush. The brush is nineteen years old, oiled twice a year, and has never touched the floor. It will be passed to his apprentice, who will also not use it. It is the most important tool in the basin.

What he does instead is read. He keeps the cordons at the gate end where the first month's tracks are: surveyors' boots, the wheelbarrow, the crate set down by a man who stood there a while, thinking. He keeps a ledger of soles — the tread patterns of every boot that has been sold in Basin Nine since the pumps started — and he can tell you who crossed the north flats in a given season and whether they were carrying something heavy on the left.

He also keeps the rain. Rain here is patchy, uncommitted, forty days a year and not the same forty days for everybody, and there is no gauge in the basin as fine as a footprint going soft. Eleven rains to erase a bootprint is an average. At the gate end, in nineteen years, some prints have taken four. Dobb's ledger is therefore also the town's rainfall record, its most precise one, and the reason he can tell a widow exactly how much weather has happened since her husband walked to the kiln.

**Bracka and Kettil** hold the note. They have handed it hand to hand something over eleven thousand times. The handover takes four seconds and during those four seconds the tone is doubled and beats against itself, a slow throb under everything, and the whole basin feels it and does not remark on it: watches are set by it, bread is timed by it, a certain kind of kiss is timed by it. They do not particularly like each other. This is considered correct. You should be able to let go of somebody you have no reason to hold on to. They do not eat from the same tin. They do not attend the same funeral. Neither has been out of the basin in nine years, and they have never discussed this.

**The combers** work the rim in the fog, in mesh and fan-noise, and come down at nine with their hair full of the town's drinking water. They tally in milliliters and they are the only people here who are competitive about anything. A good comber is spoken of the way a good surgeon is spoken of elsewhere, and with the same underlying arithmetic: she got eleven liters a day out of a shadow that everyone else had written off.

**The Gants and the Pells** are still suing. Their easement for shade runs across a condenser row that neither of them built, and the case has now been inherited twice. The children of both houses are the best lawyers in the basin and the only people who can eat at either table.

**Tam Nibb** in the assay office revises the plan every decade. Soil in three hundred years, humus, something you could dig with a spade. Her revisions are read aloud at the levelling and argued about like scripture, and the arguments are always about the same thing: whether the last decade's revision was more hopeful than the one before, and whether that is allowed. Her plan also has a schedule of arrivals. Bees, when there is enough flower. Earthworms at roughly year one hundred and forty. Oaks at two hundred and ten. She has written immigration documents for animals that do not exist, and filed them, and the town pays her to keep them current.

**Everybody levels.** Twice a year, at the equinoxes, the whole population goes under its own kitchen with wedges and a spirit level, and the basin breathes a centimeter a season, unevenly, and this is the harvest, this is the festival, this is where you find out who is speaking to whom. Children learn to read a bubble before they learn to read a word. There is a woman at Nine-and-a-Half whose eye is so good that she is borrowed across the whole basin to arbitrate, and she has settled four feuds and started one, all by saying *that's true* or *that's not* about a kitchen floor.

There are no bees yet, so the apples along the condenser frames are pollinated by hand, with a rabbit-fur brush, by whoever is nearest, in the plain generous light that comes up off the white floor into the underside of everything. Two brushes matter in Basin Nine. One is used constantly on flowers. One has never been used at all.

---

The felt room behind the pump hall annex is sixty-two decibels and rented by the hour and there is a waiting list.

The bereaved rent it. So do people who have to decide something. So do lovers, and men who want to hear their own voice, and a boy of eleven who went in on a traded hour to hear his own heart and came out frightened and would not say why. Hours are willed to grandchildren. There is a woman who has accumulated forty-one hours and will not spend them, and everyone knows, and nobody asks.

Once a season the starlings do the shift whistle and half the town stands up, and everyone laughs.

And in the starlings' song there is a gap. Nine seconds of nothing, in the middle of a phrase, in the birds at the north end, passed down through birds that were not alive in year six. They learned the silence when the note broke and the water came up in the low seams within four seconds and every bird in the basin went up at once, and they have kept it, and they teach it, and it is now simply part of the song. The young hear a rest. The old hear the nine seconds. Both are hearing the same thing exactly.

The dry-born — the ones born after the gate, who are nineteen and younger and are now most of the young — sign in their sleep. They do not shout; they have grown up in eighty-four decibels and have simply arranged their mouths differently, and the over-forties find them eerily quiet and slightly deaf and entirely fluent. They cannot sleep away from the note. They go inland to school and come back thin, or they don't come back and write about a bearing they keep humming on the nightstand. They have never been in water over their knees. At a certain age somebody takes them up through the lock and puts them in the sea to the shins and they come down white-faced and are, from then on, a person of the basin. Some refuse. The refusal is respected and remembered.

The over-forties navigate by fathoms. They say *mind the shoal* about a place you could walk across in the dark. They give directions to the Kettle and to Three Fathom, and the Kettle is a low patch of crust and Three Fathom is where the winch drums are stacked, and they are not being poetic, they are being accurate in a coordinate system that has been temporarily withdrawn.

---

And the dead are still walking here.

This is the part visitors cannot be told, only shown. The flats keep a footprint for eleven rains and at the gate end for nineteen years, so the town's dead have not finished arriving. There is a track that goes from Eleven Row to the kiln and stops being refreshed in the winter of year fourteen, and it is still there, and her husband walks beside it. Beside, never in. You may walk with a track. To walk in it is to end it, and everyone knows what that means, and when a man at Sump Road stepped in his wife's print in year seventeen the town did not condemn him and did not forgive him either; it simply understood that he had finished, and left him alone, and was gentle with him afterward in a way he found unbearable.

Grief here has a weather and a geography. It goes soft at the north end first. It lasts longest near the gate. People count rains the way people elsewhere count months, and Dobb Hesk is the one who tells them the number, and he has never once been asked to stop.

---

There is a square of shell flour on the north flats, eight meters on a side, wired at the corners, that nobody has ever stood on. Not the surveyors, not the crews, not the children, who dare each other to the wire and no further. It is the only ground in Basin Nine with no document written on it.

The town will tell you it's a control. A reference. Something to measure the worked floor against. Dobb lets them say that. He walks its perimeter every week and checks the wire and writes nothing in the ledger, because there is nothing to read.

In Tam Nibb's plan, in the back, in the section that gets revised every decade and argued about at the levelling, the square has a different label. It is where the first spade goes. Three hundred years, roughly, if the front keeps descending four centimeters a year and the salt line keeps going home a meter at a time and the note does not stop. Somebody will stand there and dig into actual soil, humus, the thing the mice are already halfway toward, and the town has spent nineteen years and a man's whole working life keeping that ground free of every footprint it could possibly have taken, so that whoever stands there first will be standing on nothing anybody has touched.

That is who lives in Basin Nine. Nineteen hundred and six people, four thousand-odd shares, one lawsuit, one heron, a hundred thousand frogs on a flat B, starlings holding nine seconds of silence in trust, pale crabs that think it is still the shore, mice that have already crossed over, and a valley's worth of residents who do not exist yet — for whom the town is keeping its dead in the flour, and its first ground empty, and the note going, hand to hand, four seconds of beating at every changeover, all day, all night, slightly flat.
