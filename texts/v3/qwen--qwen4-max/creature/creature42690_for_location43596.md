## The People of the Kettle

Meet one on the coast road and you have them inside a minute. They are talking at a volume that makes the salt-seller lean back. They are standing with one hand on the doorframe, not leaning on it exactly, just keeping it there, the way you would hold a rail on a moving boat. Their hair is oiled flat and smells of warm fat and old fruit, and under that, faintly, of ammonia, and under *that* of hen-house. The backs of their hands and a band across the forehead are burned brown; the rest is a green sort of pallor, the colour of a thing grown under leaves. There is lime in the creases of their knuckles and grafting wax under two nails. When you say something quietly they do not hear it at all, and then they come in close, suddenly, and drop their own voice to almost nothing, and it is startling, like being handed something.

They cannot sleep in a bed. Inns on the coast have learned to stop being offended. A person from the Kettle will put the quilt on the floor beside the bed and lie on the boards with their back against the wall, because a mattress is cold and a room without water in it is silent, and silence, to them, is not restful. Silence is what happens before a squall. In the morning they are found on the floor like something dropped, and they wake up cheerful, and they buy gritstone querns and copper pins and go home up the wet road with a snail already on the collar of their new coat.

---

Start with the hands, because the hands are the record.

The right palm — or the left, if they are left-handed, but the town prefers the right and children are corrected — has a shine on the heel of it like the handrails, from the same kind of labour: striking wood, ten thousand times a year, spit and slap, *four hundred and a half*, applause. Old market women's palms are yellow and glossy and slightly hollow. The fingers are another matter: lime-cracked, split at the tips in the cold weeks, healed and split again, so that a woman of fifty has fingertips like the bark of the tree heather on the road, shredded rope. The Tiers have wax permanently in the whorls. The cave crews have hands that are soft and wrecked at once, guano-soft, and a rash on the wrists that never entirely goes, the cave itch, which is a mite, which lives in the guano, which lives on the birds, and which no amount of lime and oil has ever defeated. Everybody's nails are cut short. Everybody's nails are stained.

The feet are the other record. Nobody here walks with a heel. You learn, before you are three, to go along a wet duckboard in the dark with the weight on the ball and the toes spread, a short testing step, and the whole population moves like that, a quick flat cautious shuffle that on dry ground looks like hurry and is not. Children run on the high bridges at a height their mothers will not use, and the mothers let them, because the alternative is teaching them to be afraid of the only roads that are always dry. Feet rot. Marsh foot is the commonest illness in the bowl, treated with oil and lime and a week on the Shelf where it is dry and hot, and old people's feet are a horror, and the old people show them to each other and grade them.

Then the ears. Everyone in the Kettle over thirty has a notch taken out of their hearing, a slice of the world simply gone, and which slice tells you their trade. Beaters lose the top of it. Loom workers lose a narrow band and can still hear a weep. Dyers and tanners lose little and cough instead, ammonia in the lungs, a wet bark that goes on all winter in the workshop storey. The cave crews lose nearly everything, because ten thousand oilbirds clicking in a stone room is not a sound, it is a pressure, and after five seasons a crew-worker lives in a muffled world with a permanent high ringing in it like a weep that isn't there.

This is not a tragedy here. It is a qualification. The crews recruit the deaf and the going-deaf by preference, and there are families in which deafness is expected, planned for, discussed at the same time as a boy's apprenticeship. The town has a whole second language for it, older than anyone can date: knuckles on the board, a flat palm on the sternum, two fingers on the inside of the wrist, a code of taps that runs under a conversation the way the channels run under the floors. Hearing people use it too, across a loud market, in the caves, in bed. And because a deaf person in a flood is a dead person unless somebody is thinking about them, every deaf child is paired at four, by lot, with a hearing child, and that pair is called *second ear*, and it is a bond the town takes more seriously than most marriages. There are second-ear pairs of sixty years' standing who have never lived in the same house and who eat together every flood.

The teeth are worn flat by gritstone flour and snail shell. The lungs of the old are full of lime dust; there is a cough called the stone cough that means you worked the caves in your twenties and it means you will not see seventy. And nobody, none of them, can smell their own town. They think the coast smells of nothing, and find it disturbing, and say so: *it's like being in a room where somebody has died and been tidied away.*

---

A person here is a sound before they are a face.

Names exist — plain ones, Hesk, Ibb, Noll, Sarrow, Tace, Wen — but the name is not what you use. What you use is the call: two rising notes and a stop, a click and a fall, a long low note bent up at the end. Every household has one and every person in it answers to it, with a personal tail on the end if you need one particular member. You are given your call before you are given your name, at about the age when you can first make a noise that carries, and it is chosen partly for how well it goes through wet leaves. Calls with a click in them carry best. Calls that resemble a weep are unlucky and get changed. Blind people are not disadvantaged here in the least; two of the finest grafters in the lattice are blind, and the best eel-catcher on the north side is a blind man of sixty who works entirely by the sound of water under boards and who is insufferable about it.

Nobody has ever seen their own name written. Writing exists in exactly one place, on the Shelf, and it is a list of the dead.

Sound is also the whole architecture of privacy, which is to say privacy is a place you go to. If you want to say a thing that must not travel, you walk to the fourth weep on the north wall, where the rope of water is thick and loud enough to eat a sentence, and you stand shoulder to shoulder with the person and talk into the wet rock. Lovers do this. Debtors do this. People confess things there that would ruin them forty paces away. And if you want it *really* kept, you go down the duckboards to the lip of the Swallow and say it into the *shhh*, which will take anything, which has been taking everything for six hundred years. The Throats, who go down into it twice a year, have therefore heard more of the town's business than any living person, and are famous for saying nothing, and are given free eel for the rest of their lives.

The other side of this is that the town has no secrets in the ordinary register. An insult shouted across the bowl is a public act, with witnesses, and is treated as one. Marriages end over things said at market volume. And in the five or six lean weeks after fruitfall, when the pigeons have gone and the roofs are grey again and the bowl goes quiet enough to hear a single conversation across ninety paces of water, everyone becomes suddenly, unbearably polite. The quiet weeks are when you learn who is not speaking to whom. People say they hate the quiet weeks. What they mean is that they can be heard.

Under all of it, the beaters. Four and a rest, four and a rest, from the bark-cloth benches on the second terrace, apprentices on the mallets and old men with ruined shoulders keeping the count. Everything in the town is timed to that rest: songs, jokes, the punchline dropped into the gap, arguments, the rhythm of the looms, the way people make love. A beater who misses a rest is the nearest thing here to an omen; the whole bowl feels the hole in it and looks up. And when somebody dies, the beater on that hour drops one beat, once, on purpose, and six hundred people stop what they are doing, and forty thousand birds do not care at all.

---

The guilds are not professions so much as kinds of person, recognisable at twenty paces.

The **Tiers** are slow. They have to be; their medium answers them at a hand's width a year. They talk about weather in decades. They are the only people in the bowl who are routinely, calmly patient, and they marry each other, and their households are the ones with three generations in them and a plan. A child who wants the guild is given a non-structural withy at six and must keep it alive and shapely for nine years, and about half fail, and the failing is not shameful, it is simply information. Their signature is a cut and a wax seal on a limb, and the tree heals over it, and in four years the mark is a raised knob you read with your thumb, and in twenty it is gone and the graft is the record. When they vote to let a trunk die they hold something that is unmistakably a wake, with drink, at the foot of it, and then the twelve-year business begins, and the apprentice who ties the first replacement across the gap will be a journeyman by the time it takes weight, and a master by the time anyone trusts it.

There is a woman on the third terrace called Sarrow who cut a structural limb in a bad winter forty years ago. She was thirty-one. Her sister's floor was going and she needed a beam and she took one, and the floor held, and the sister lived another twenty years, and the limb was not dead, whatever she says. She is not forgiven. It is important to understand how mild this is. Nobody spits at her. She is fed, housed, warmed, helped up the stair; her down is good; she has taught the flood drill to two generations of four-year-olds and is thanked for it. Her number at market is answered a half-beat late, always, by everyone, including people who were not born then. Her grafts are checked twice. She is never lent a knife. There is no procedure for appealing this because there is no procedure at all; it is not a punishment, it is a fact about her, like her height. She says the limb was dead. The Tiers say it was not. When she dies her bones will go into the terrace fill like everybody's and the town will keep her, which is the only kind of forgiveness it has.

The **cave crews** are loud, deaf, rich for a season and reckless with it, shaved-headed against the mites, lamp-scarred, and they squint and wear bark shades in the four hours of white sun because their eyes have forgotten how. They click to each other in the caves, a habit from the birds, and they click in the market too without noticing, and you can always tell one. The taking is by quota and by lot: a fixed age, a fixed proportion of ledges, drawn by household. There is a family whose lot has come up *leave* three generations running and who are poor and extremely proud about it. There is another whose lot has come up *take* so often that the coast assumes they own the caves. Nobody owns the caves. The legal fiction, repeated so often it has become sincere, is that the birds own the caves and the town is a tenant, and the quota is the rent, and the whole apparatus of lots and counts and fixed ages exists because you cannot argue with a landlord who does not know you exist and might simply stop coming home.

The **Throats** are small, wiry, long in the arm, with a resting pulse you can barely find. They are taken at fourteen and are finished by thirty-five if they are sensible. They can hold a breath for a length of time that visitors treat as a party trick and they treat as nothing. The rope-hands above are the ones who actually hold the life, and every Throat knows it, and the rope-hands are usually their second ear, or their wife, or their brother. In the two weeks before a descent nobody calls in a Throat's debts and everybody buys them drinks and they hate it, all of them, every year, and say so, at volume. Then they go down on tallow lamps with bone picks into a smooth spiral two people wide with the whole bowl's water coming down it, and they cut lime and root-mat out of the throat, and sometimes they do not come up.

Their names are on the Shelf, cut into fresh tufa, which the water immediately begins to fill. The guild re-cuts them. The re-cutting is done by the newest apprentice, always, so that every name passes eventually through hands that never met it, and the oldest names are no longer letters at all but a raised scar you read with your thumb, and the blind come and read the wall, and this is understood to be exactly the point. There is a running argument, never resolved, about whether a name may be let go once nobody living remembers the person. The Tiers, who think in centuries, say let the stone have it. The Throats cut it again.

On the Shelf there is one woman with a stick, and she is not joking. The office is held for life or until the hands go, and she is chosen by the other guilds jointly, and — this is not written anywhere and everybody knows it — she is chosen for being disliked, because a popular fire warden is a town on fire. She sleeps on the rock. She has the right to put her hand on any surface in any house at any hour. She has burned a man's cloak in front of him for carrying an ember below the line, and the man watched, and said nothing, and the market that morning was very quiet. She is training a girl of eleven to be unpopular, and the girl is coming along.

And there is the **Ear**, who is nobody for most of the year: an old woman who nets eels and complains, sitting with her bare feet on a board over a channel where she can feel the throat's pitch through the wood as well as hear it. She has three understudies who must agree with her, and the youngest is always a child, because children hear the top of the register and the throat's warning lives up there. When she says ring, the bell rings, and for eleven minutes she is the entire government of the Kettle, and then the market is stacked on the second terrace and she is an old woman with wet feet again. In three hundred years the office has been wrong twice. Once for nothing: the town dismantled itself, stood in the rain, watched a pond that never came, and made a festival of it, and the child understudy had been right and was insufferable for a decade. Once the other way. That flood has a name and a height cut into the notch and a great many people in it who did not get up the stair.

---

The children are the fastest thing in the bowl. They cross at a run on routes their parents will not use, carrying hens, carrying a smaller child, carrying nothing; they can click, badly, in the dark, as a game, and go along a passage with their eyes shut, and shriek when they hit the wall, and do it again. They race snails on the moss walls and bet in hands of down. They know the forty weeps in order, north to south, as a counting rhyme with obscene verses. At four they are drilled at flood: your hamper, your rope handle, your hen, your stair, your place on the terrace, and they are drilled at it by Sarrow, who is not forgiven, and who is very good at it. They wear pigeon skin, leather-soft, going shiny at the elbows, and sleep in down at blood heat over the compost pits, and are warm all winter in a wet hole, and their legs are a map of mite scratches. At nine or ten a child is given a knife and a mark, or a mallet, or a rope, and the town has decided something about them, and they know it, and the ones who wanted the knife and got the mallet cry, and get over it, and become beaters, and lose the top of their hearing, and keep the hours for fifty years.

The old go up. The rim gardens are dry and bright and the guano-black soil is warm to sit on, and there is a bench of them at the notch who watch the road and grade the floods. This is an institution. They stand in the rising water and criticise the water — its colour, its speed, its manners — and then, when it is over the boards, they get out and criticise the carrying, and the stacking, and the young. They can name every flood of their lives by height and by what it took. When they are dying they ask to be carried to the Shelf, because it is the only stone floor in the Kettle and they want to lie on something that does not rot, once, at the end, and the woman with the stick allows it and sits with them and says nothing.

Afterwards the marsh takes the body, in the ordinary way, iron and cold tea. The bones are cleaned and hung in the drip-line on the north terraces, on strings, with the baskets and bowls and the child's shoe, and in a year they are stone, pale and glittering and heavy, and then they go into the terrace fill, and the terraces are built up out of them. Children are told the terraces are made of grandmothers. It is not a metaphor and nobody pretends it is. It is why you do not spit on a terrace, and why the tufa is trimmed with a bone saw and not a copper one, and why the wall of names on the Shelf and the ground you stand on are, in the local understanding, the same substance at different stages.

---

The dogs do not bark. Six hundred years of selection in a town made of sound has produced a small hard silent rat-dog that signals by leaning against your leg, a firm deliberate press, twice for *there*, once for *come*, and a hard lean and a stare for *now*. A barker is given away to the coast inside a week. They are allowed everywhere, including the Shelf, including the market trestles, including the beds, and there is one that lives on the Weep Stair and belongs to nobody and is fed by everyone and is called, by all six hundred people, the same thing.

There is an eel in the channel under a house on the second terrace that has been fed by that household for four generations. It is as thick as a thigh and has a scar and is called the aunt. It is illegal to catch and nobody would. There are three others like it in the bowl and their households are quietly competitive.

And the birds are the population. Forty thousand on a slow day, and the town's whole self-understanding is arranged around the fact that the residents who matter cannot be spoken to, cannot be reasoned with, cannot be punished, and are not tame. The oilbirds have their own society in the north wall — ledge tenancies held for decades, a crèche heat you can feel from the cave mouth, chicks stacked fat and silent in the dark, the pour at dusk and the click and the forty square miles of laurel fruit coming home in their guts every dawn. The people go in among them with lamps and baskets and take a fixed proportion at a fixed age and are, in their own accounting, extremely careful tenants who have never once been told the terms. The pigeons are the seasonal tenants who overstay, white on every roof, unbearable, netted, eaten, worn. The bats are the night shift over the marsh. The crickets in the guano are livestock that nobody had to decide about. The snails are on your tools and inside your kettle and in a bowl at your elbow.

And the two largest residents are appetites. Rot, which is eating the town at the rate of everything in nine years, and which they farm under their beds at blood heat and sleep on and love. Lime, which is building the town at the rate of a fingernail a year, and which fills the names and the throat and the terraces and the old men's lungs. The people live in the narrow place between the two of them with a basket, and are themselves slowly encrusted and slowly eaten, and know it, and have made all their customs out of it.

---

Strangers come. Debtors, mostly, and refugees from the coast in bad years, and traders who marry in. You are not from here until three things are true: you can name the weeps, you have a call, and you have carried a hamper up the Weep Stair in a real flood with the water actually coming. The third is the one that matters and everyone knows it, and there is a specific, unkind, affectionate silence in the market when a newcomer is seen going up with a hen under each arm.

Marriage is a graft. Two households inarch a pair of limbs across a gap, non-structural, deliberately non-structural, planted so that it can lawfully be cut, and the couple tend it, and its thickness is a public statement. Ending a marriage means cutting the wedding withy in front of the Tiers, one clean cut, and the stump is kept by whoever is judged to have been wronged, and there is a shelf of stumps in the guild house that nobody talks about. Some couples never plant one. Some plant one and never touch it and let it close over a window, and the Tiers open the window, and everyone can see what happened.

Some leave. They go down to the coast for salt and pins and stay, and are spoken of as *gone quiet*, and about half of them come back within two years, thin, sleeping badly, saying they couldn't hear anything, saying it was like being dead in a tidy room.

---

At night the lamps are hundreds of small yellow smokes at different heights in the leaves, and the pour goes over the rim, and the marsh mutters, and under the boards the compost goes on rotting at blood heat. Somebody is awake in every house. Somebody is always awake, listening past the frogs and the weeps to the throat, checking its pitch the way you check a child's breathing, and then letting it go, and rolling over.

And when the bell has rung and the market is stacked and the whole town is standing along the terraces in the rain shouting advice at a pond, the Ear does the roll: every household answers with its call, down the line, north to south, sixty-odd calls in ninety seconds, and the households with a dead person in them this year do not answer, and the gap is heard, and nobody fills it. She waits one beat longer than she needs to. She has always done this. Nobody has ever asked her to stop. Then she says the number, and the number is right, and the water goes on going down the throat.
