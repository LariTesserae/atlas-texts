Two hundred and forty of them, at the last counting, and the counting is a notch on the same stick that measures the stone. Four hundred and eighty hands. Something over nine thousand works. You can do that arithmetic yourself and arrive at the shape of a life here: forty structures per person, a round that takes most of a day to walk, fifteen miles of white stone between waking and sleeping, and a body that is used almost entirely for the purpose of arriving somewhere and moving a paddle six inches.

Begin with the feet, because they do. The rims are sharp and the soles are the only part of a person that touches the city without intending to, so the soles have become an instrument. They walk with a slide rather than a step — the ball of the foot set down and drawn forward a finger's width before the weight follows, reading the edge, finding the place where the wax-lip has been rounded by last week's traffic. A stranger's gait is audible from a quarter mile: the small crunch of a heel taken carelessly. Children are corrected for it before they are corrected for anything else. The arch goes hard and yellow; the heel stays soft and is guarded like an eye. Everyone has a scar on the inside of the left foot from the first time they were careless, and inside that scar, if you press it, there is a grain. A cut made in a running channel closes over the city and keeps it. Old people carry a dozen of these, small hard seeds under the skin, and can tell you which district each one came from.

The hands are glassy. Everyone's. Greased with sedge oil at the start of a shift, scraped after, and still the palms shine like the inside of a shell, and the fine hair of the forearm is stiff and pale and breaks off when you rub it. The glaze is not painful. It is only a stiffness, a slight reluctance in the skin, and the scraping is a social act, done in pairs at the end of the round, one person working the other's forearms with a curved bone while they talk about nothing. It is where the gossip lives. If you want to know who is quarrelling, sit near the scraping place at dusk.

And the oldest ones tick. There is powder on the drying floors — the one place on the plateau where anything is dry enough to be dust — and a lifetime of it goes into the lungs and does what the water does everywhere else. It lays down. A woman of sixty turnings breathes with a faint click at the top of each breath, and by eighty she sounds like a channel a long way off, and the young can hear her coming across an empty terrace before they can see her under the hat-brim. Nobody thinks this tragic. It is the same process as everything else. She is simply being formed a little faster than she is being turned.

---

They hear appallingly well. They have to; the whole place is a whisper and the information is in it. A stone curtain no thicker than a fingernail rings when touched, and its note is its thickness, and its thickness is its age since the last turning, so the halls hung with curtains are not concert halls but gauges. A maintainer walks the length of a colonnade trailing two fingers, and the run of pitches tells her which bays are due, and she can hear a bay that has been running four days too long the way you can hear a wrong string. Children are tested on this at seven. Those who cannot hear the top quarter of the range are sent early to the low runs and the settling pans, where the work is coarse and the tolerances wide, and they are not shamed about it, and they mind it terribly. To go deaf here is to be blinded in the only direction that matters.

So they speak quietly, and they have a large vocabulary for small sounds and almost none for loud ones. A channel is named for its note as often as for its position. There are six words for the sound water makes going over a lip, distinguished by the height of the lip and the load of lime in the water, and a seventh for the sound of a lip that is about to choke, which is the only sound on the plateau that will make a person run.

Their faces, lit from below by the glare off the stone, look patient and slightly unreal, and this is a lighting effect and should not be trusted. Under the brim they are ordinary: vain, funny, bored, holding grudges of a specificity that would impress a lawyer. There is a man on the middle terraces who has been letting the East Curtain run two days over its schedule for eleven years because he likes the shape it makes — a fluted thing like a held breath — and he has arranged his other channels to cover the discrepancy, and he is not sorry, and everyone knows, and the argument about him has outlived two generations of the people having it. There are feuds about weirs. There is a woman who is not speaking to her brother over a plug.

---

Because the balance is exact and people are not, there has to be somewhere for the error to go.

There is. It is on the low side, past the drying floors, and it has a name that means approximately *the room that is not on the round*. Every district's small cheats and latenesses and vanities are settled there, by a compensating standing or a compensating running, so that the notched stick at the outflow reads the same notch and the city keeps its weight. The room itself has not been on any schedule for a very long time. It has been running, net, for perhaps four hundred turnings.

It is a monstrosity. Cauliflower upon cauliflower, knobs the size of sleeping oxen, drapes fused into a single organ-like mass, a floor that has risen to meet a ceiling, channels that have choked and been overflowed and choked again until the whole space is a solid white lung with water threading through it somewhere in the dark. It is the only thing on the plateau with a history. It is the only place where you can point at a layer and say *that was the summer of the bad plug* — except that they don't have summers, and don't say it, and mostly don't go.

Children go. It is the dare. You walk in as far as the light does and put your ear against the mass and hear the water working inside it, and you come out and lie about what you heard. Adults go too, sometimes, alone, and sit at the edge of it in the middle of the day when everyone else is in the shade, and look at the one object in their world that has been allowed to become something. It is not called sacred. It is called the room that is not on the round. But there is no other place to look at time.

---

They do not count themselves among the animals, and this is not vanity; it is a grammatical fact. The word for a person is the word for a channel, with a different hand-sign. A person runs and a person stands. To run is to work, to lay down, to thicken, to be a child, to be well. To stand is to rest, to thin, to be old, to be idle, to be grieving, to be unmade a little. They know this is a figure of speech. They keep it because it schedules well: a household's labor and a district's water can be written on the same stick with the same notches, and there is a real sense in which a person left too long without work goes soft, and everyone has seen it happen.

Which means the round is not only maintenance of stone.

A house alone on a terrace with half an hour of empty white on every side is a house that must be passed through. Not for its cistern. For its occupant. The intervals at which a person needs to be seen and scraped and argued with are known, roughly, the way the intervals of a wall are known, and the round is built to include them, and when someone's round is quietly shortened — when the passing-through comes less often — everybody understands what is being said. Solitude here is not a mood. It is a process, and it has a rate, and the rate is measurable in the same units as everything else. There are people out in the far districts who have not been touched by another hand in a season, and you can see it in the way they hold their arms.

The night round is one person. It always has been. She walks the whole lace under stars that do not twinkle, on stone that is giving back the day's heat, and the only sounds are the silk of the water and her own blood in her ears, hour after hour, and she is the best-paid and least envied adult on the plateau. They rotate it. Nobody does it twice in a cycle. There is a thing that happens to people who do it too often, and it is described, when it is described at all, as *standing*.

And marriage is a crossing. Two rounds, plotted on the same stick, either intersect or they don't. If they intersect, there is an hour, once a cycle, when two people are in the same place, and that hour is the marriage, and everything else about it — the shared dormitory, the children, the debts — is arranged around the fact of that hour arriving. If the rounds don't intersect, you can make them. You can move water. It costs a great deal: a district replumbed, a season of extra hands, a discrepancy that has to go somewhere, and it goes to the low side, into the room that is not on the round. That mass is largely made of people wanting to be together. Nobody says this. It is the sort of thing everyone knows and no one has ever put in a sentence.

---

The dead go into the standing rooms and in a season there is nothing to find. This is proper. But there is a window.

Bone is the only hard animal material on the plateau, and bone scrapers are the primary tool, and there are no animals. So a family may take one bone, early, before the quiet water has finished — a rib, usually, or the long bone of the forearm — and it is not theft and not relic; it is a tool, and it is used, and it wears. A good rib lasts three generations of scraping. It thins. It goes from a rib to a blade to a sliver to a thing you can no longer hold, and then it goes back into the standing room, and that is the second death, and it is the one people actually weep at, because the first is only arithmetic and the second is the last time you will hear her.

The loudest sound on the plateau, most days, is somebody's grandmother wearing out against wet stone half a mile away.

There is another thing, and it is not spoken of, and it is not exactly forbidden. A body given to a *running* room does not dissolve. It skins. It takes a crust, and the crust holds the shape, and the inside goes slowly, in its own time, in the warm water, with nothing to hurry it — and what is left is a form with a hollow in it, exactly as a pipe is a form with a hollow where the reeds were burned out. There are three culverts on the low side whose bore is not the shape of a bundle of reeds. The water goes through them. It has been going through them a long while. Whether this is desecration or the deepest possible propriety is a question they have arranged not to have, and the arrangement takes a certain amount of maintenance, like everything else.

---

Their history is not on the sticks. The sticks record cycles and cannot hold an event; a stick is a circle you can walk around forever. Their history is in the weave.

Everything is formed on something — withy, split sedge, coiled rope — and the pattern of that thing is printed forever in the face of the stone. Every household twists its sedge a little differently. A woman with a peculiar habit of doubling the third strand puts that habit into every wall she forms, and the wall outlives her by centuries, and a trained reader can walk a district and say: this bay and that bay and the long retaining wall by the culvert were all made by the same hand, or by hands taught by that hand, and here is where the teaching changed, and here is where somebody came in from outside and twisted the other way. That is the archive. It has no dates. It has lineages, migrations, forgeries, a scandal about a man who copied another family's twist to marry into a district, and a whole discipline of argument about whether a pattern can be owned. A person is dissolved and gone; her twist is load-bearing and will be load-bearing when the language has changed. They lean on their ancestors without noticing, which is the most any people manages.

The copper awls are used for the notches. Nothing on the plateau is made by cutting except the record of time, and the awl is what cuts it, once a cycle, in a small ceremony involving four people who would rather be working. That is the only place a blade goes. They find this reasonable.

---

They will tell you plainly that no animal has climbed the cliff, and they will not include themselves in the sentence, and if you press them they will look at you with the mild patience of someone whose language you are breaking. Seeds came on the wind. Spores came on the wind. The people are what the water is doing now.

Across the cloud sea there are other shoulders with other laces, most of them empty, one or two with a thread of smoke. At the low sun, when the pans go copper, a wet palm held at the right angle can throw a flash a very long way. They do this. It is not conversation; there is nothing to trade and nothing to say. What they flash is numbers. The notch. The count. The other lace flashes back its notch and its count, and both parties learn the one thing worth knowing, which is whether the arithmetic is still holding somewhere else.

One of them stopped answering a long time ago. Nobody has gone to see. Going to see would be an event, and there is no notch for it.

They understand the empty laces perfectly. They can do the sum as well as anyone: the water requires more each generation, the hands do not multiply to match, the fabric goes on ahead of the bodies, and the round gets longer, and the passing-through gets rarer, and one day a district is dropped, and then another, and the dropped ones start to grow or thin without anyone, and become rooms with histories, and eventually there is a whole plateau of histories and nobody to hold a stick. They can see three or four stages of that across the cloud floor on any clear afternoon. It is not a mystery to them. It is a schedule that has not come round yet.

---

So: what lives there.

A mouth. That is the honest answer, and it is the one they have organized two hundred and forty lives around not saying. The plateau has no larvae, no snails, no nibbling thing, nothing that eats and tears and leaves dust — nothing except the one species that arrived, somehow, before the cliff finished rising, and that greases itself against being eaten, and roasts tubers in the warm ground, and cuts, once a cycle, with an heirloom blade. Everything with legs that has ever been on this table of land is standing in a running channel with a bone in its hand, deciding where the water goes. The absence of animals is not a condition of the place. It is an achievement, renewed daily, by the animal.

They are very good at it. The stick reads the same notch. The city at dusk weighs what it weighed at dawn.

And at night the stone gives back the heat it took, and the round-walker crosses an acre of glaring white gone soft and grey, and stands still in the middle of a district with nobody within a mile, and hears, under the silk of the water going over lips no higher than a knuckle, the only hurrying thing on the plateau: two hundred and forty hearts, distributed widely, going around at the speed of blood, keeping the whole warm sharp-edged weight of it in place by hand.
