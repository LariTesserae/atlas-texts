## The People of the Combs

You can tell one in a lowland market by the hands and the volume, in that order.

The hands are soft. Not idle-soft — soaked-soft, the palms pale and slightly corrugated at the fingertips like the inside of a shell, the nails broad and flat and ridged from a lifetime of nine degrees and permanent wet. But the *grip* is a thing to see. Comb hands are precise the way a watchmaker's tweezers are precise, because everything they do all day is done at a tolerance and most of it is done by feel, in fog, under a cloth, or sixty feet down a culvert where the lamp is a rumour. They can dress a flange joint blind. They can hold a conversation in a stranger's palm.

And they are too quiet. A Comb man in a lowland street asks the price of fish and nobody answers him, because he has pitched his voice to sit in the notch below six miles of falling water, and in a place with no falling water that register is simply not speech. It is a sort of breathing with intent. Lowlanders think they are being secretive, or holy, or rude. Comb people in the lowlands think everyone is screaming, and cannot sleep for it, and come home.

---

The voice has a *radius*, and the radius is the whole of etiquette.

A thing said at four inches is a different act from a thing said at four feet, and there are words for both, and children are corrected on it before they are corrected on their letters. Confession is a nought-inch thing: mouth at the ear, breath on the neck, and the ordinary posture of intimacy in the Combs is two people apparently asleep against each other in a stairwell. A marriage is vows at no distance at all. A grudge is a nine-foot thing, and to widen a grudge — to say it at twelve feet, at twenty — is to escalate, formally, in front of witnesses, and can be brought before the Assize by the person it was aimed at.

Because the fine is by distance, shouting is the most expensive thing a person can do, and so, inevitably, it is how the rich display themselves. There is a verb, *to spend*, which means to lose your temper audibly on a public walk. A wool factor on the Twelfth once shouted his brother's name across four terraces and paid the Assize eleven hundred marks for it, and the family still refers to the incident with pride and with the exact figure. Poor men have never in their lives raised their voices above a wall. Their anger goes into their hands: a gate closed a half-second too hard, a pawl let fall, a bearing deliberately not greased. Vandalism in the Combs is not broken things. It is *loud* things.

The hush-wardens' paddles have eleven positions and every child knows all eleven. Held flat and low: *you are audible and I am being kind about it*. Held edge-on: *someone below us is listening and it is not me*. Held up over the face, which is the one that makes visitors' stomachs turn: *the works you are standing in has gone quiet, and it went quiet because of you*.

---

Now the ears, which are the real organs of this place.

Thirty-one thousand people living inside a sound they have all learned to subtract. They do not hear the water. They hear the *exceptions* to the water — and there is a professional class of them, the listeners, who go about with a hardwood rod and a bone button on the end of it, and who put the rod to a party wall and the button to the tooth, and hear the mill on the other side of the wall through their own skull. They can name a failing bearing shell three terraces away and tell you which of the two hundred stalls on the Twelfth has the replacement, graded, second-hand.

They are exported. Alongside the cold and the metre bars and the gauge blocks, the Combs sell *diagnosis*: a small pale woman in a lowland cotton mill, walking a line of shafting with a stick against her teeth, earning in a month what her terrace earns in a year, and hating every hour of it — the dust, the sun coming in sideways off the floor, the shadows lying about the place like dropped tools, the water tasting of nothing at all.

And because hearing is the trade, deafness is not a disability here. It is a specialisation. The fulling stocks — the loudest thing in the Combs, the thudding you would call quiet if you heard it in a room — are worked by the Loud, a brotherhood of deaf men and women with their own hands, and their hands have leaked outward into everybody: half the finger-language under the bargaining cloth at the parts market is stock-signs, borrowed, and a hearing fuller will swear at you fluently in a grammar he does not know he learned from his grandfather's neighbours.

The Loud have one privilege that everyone else in the city would trade a year of head for. They can be as noisy as they like, and they *rent it out*.

Once a week the stocks run an extra hour, and people come down the stairs to the fulling floor with their collars up and their faces already going, and they stand in the thudding and they scream. Grief, mostly. Also rage, also laughter — it is the only place in six miles where anybody laughs out loud, and the sound of forty people laughing under a battery of fulling stocks is the single most indecent noise the Combs produce, and it is on the schedule, and the head-wardens allow for the water. It is called the crying hour. Nobody has ever proposed moving it. The Loud stand at their frames with their backs politely turned and feel it come up through the floor into their boots, which is how they know it is a good one.

---

Names are brass.

A person here is not named for a saint or a father. A person is named for the station they keep, and the station's name is cast into the gate-plate: *Long Race*, *Fourth Tooth*, *Nine-Inch*, *Amber*, *Second Settling*. You inherit the post and you inherit the word, and there have been nineteen women called Fourth Tooth and the parish register does not distinguish them except by dates and by faults. Surnames are measurements. There is a family called Three-and-a-Half who have been three and a half inches of fall on the Nineteenth since the reign of a king nobody can name, and they marry accordingly, and a Three-and-a-Half who marries a Sixfoot is spoken of the way lowlanders speak of a duchess.

Wealth is height. Not the height of your ceiling — the height of your water. To be rich is to own a vertical distance near the top of the hill, cold four-degree water with all six miles still in front of it. To be poor is to live low, in spent water, warm, at the Tail, where the fall has all been used and the ground is being manufactured and the smell is enormous. *Tailer* is an insult in the upper terraces and a boast in the lower ones. The poor have the better hands and the worse teeth and the only real laughter, and they know exactly what the rich are, which is people who have paid to be dry.

Dryness is the luxury. The most expensive rooms in the Combs are the ones with no water in them at all, up in the cold, dried by condensate and salt, where the standards live at a temperature that has not shifted a degree in a hundred and eighty years. The people who tend them have chapped lips and split knuckles and pay a physician for grease. A dry hand is a title. A wet hand is a wage.

And the plates wear. Brass gate-plates wear to the thickness of a fingernail under six hundred years of thumbs, and are re-cast on a cycle so old the cycle has its own name, and when a plate goes into the crucible the name goes with it and comes out again in new metal, and the letters are the same letters. Every seven years, at the turn, there is a thimble of the pear spirit — small hard pears, hand-pollinated, tasting of cold stone — and a widow puts her husband's plate in the melt and gets it back with her own name added, and children learn to read on the gates because the gates are the only place the family is written down.

**The name is the same name. The brass is not the same brass.** Nobody here finds this a metaphor. It is simply how names are made.

---

The other residents are livestock, and the livestock are mostly green.

The moss is herded. Three and four species deep in every joint, cropped on a rota to a nap you could sweep, and there are moss-shepherds who can tell you which of the four is coming up thick in a bad year and who have a horror, passed down like a plague memory, of the blight of the year the flange joints all failed at once and the hill ran hot and loud for eleven weeks. The horsetail is a crop with a cutting licence. The willows along the parapets are pollarded to knuckles and are individually older than any family in the city — six hundred years old and four feet tall, and known by name, and *pruned by the same hands' descendants*. The alder is in a box, and the box has a specification, and the specification is the most litigated document in the Combs after the water-rights.

The heron in the fourth settling bed is an office. There has been one heron in the fourth settling bed since the bed was cut, and the town's legal fiction is that it is the same heron, and the eel-tallyman enters its arrival and absence in the ledger with everything else. The dipper bobs on a stone dressed for it in 1740, which means that at some point a mason was paid to make a bird a place to stand, and the invoice survives, and the invoice is the thing schoolchildren are shown to explain what kind of town this is.

There are no bees. There has not been a bee in living memory, because there is nothing to be a bee *for* — one week of pear blossom and a shade flora that does not bother with flowers. In the sun-week the children go up the seed-orchards with rabbit-tail brushes and slit-visors over their eyes and do the work of an insect, tree by tree, and are paid in coin, and cry from the glare, and are told that this is the only week the world has edges. Comb children are frightened of shadows. They see them once a year, lying hard and black off the parapets, pointing somewhere, and they do not like it, and they are glad when the lid closes.

---

The dead go into the ground, and the ground is a product.

There is no soil here that was not made, and no place to put a body that is not already somebody's fall or somebody's race, and so the Combs do what the arithmetic requires. The dead go down to the digesters at the Tail with the wool waste and the spent cress-root and the filter cake, and are composted to specification, and go back up the hill in the empty ballast cars, and come out of the sack into an alder box on a wide walk.

So the trees are your grandmother. This is not a poetic formulation; it is the funeral, and there is a form of words, and the form of words includes the batch number. Families visit a box. They bring the pear spirit and pour a thimble into the made ground, which is technically an offence against the specification and universally winked at. When an alder dies there is an inquest, and the inquest is a soil assay, and if the batch was adulterated — too much wool waste, a bad ash, someone's fraud — then a family has a case, and the case will be heard, and the soil guild has been broken twice in six hundred years by exactly this.

The folk-devil of the Combs is not a monster. He is the Man Who Kept It: a miser who dammed a race and never returned it, who *accumulated*, in a place where accumulation is the one sin the physics will not forgive. Children are told he is still down there under the made ground. Children are also told, by the same grandmother in the same breath, that the made ground is where grandmothers go, and they draw the obvious conclusion, and nobody corrects them, because the conclusion is useful.

---

There are two archives, and only two, because nothing else is permitted to settle.

One is the ledgers, where the head of water is entered, and the eel count, and the moss crop, and the fines by distance, and the deaths — all in the same columns, all the same kind of fact.

The other is the ice. In the deep walks, where the sun has never reached, there is grey gritty ice in the masonry joints that has not melted since it was laid, and it is the only sediment in the city. There is a man whose job is to shave it. He takes a curl from a joint on the Thirty-Eighth, melts it in fog water, and counts what falls out: soot, ash, grit, the flue-signature of a works that closed before your language had its present spelling. He is the lichen woman's opposite. She walks the terraces with a hand lens and reads the air now, and can shut a works with a sentence, and is bribed and hated and unmarried and kept honest by having her lens among the metre bars. He reads the air then.

In one of his shavings there was a bee's wing. He has it under glass. Children are brought to see it and do not believe him.

And once a year a person goes down the shaft in the Fourth Tooth, past the made ground and the old fill and the older fill, and puts a palm on the actual mountain, and comes up, and says the four words: *cold, dark, smells of nothing*. Six hundred years of the same four words in the ledger. It is the only line that never changes in a place where nothing else is ever the same, and the office is called the Palm, and the current Palm is a woman of sixty who has been three other stations before this one, and she is the town's single organ of touch — the one place where the whole moving shape reaches out and confirms that there is something underneath it that it did not make.

The Combs' idea of the end of the world is not fire. It is that one year the Palm will come up the shaft and say something else.

---

So: who lives here.

Ask, and you will be handed a list of stations, not persons, and the list will be recast in seven years, and the man who hands it to you will have a different name by then and will not think that worth mentioning. Thirty-one thousand people who are, this year, the hands and the ears and the nose and the one palm of something six hundred years old that keeps its shape by never keeping anything. It has no memory but the ledgers and the ice. It has no god but the balance and no devil but the hoarder. It cannot sleep, because the water does not, and it cannot shout, because shouting is lost head, and it has therefore built itself one thudding room where it can.

Go down to that room on the last hour of the week. Stand in the noise with your collar up while a woman screams her husband's name into a battery of fulling stocks and forty people laugh at nothing at all, and a deaf man at the frame feels it arrive through his boots and does not turn around.

Then the stocks stop, and the hush comes back down over six miles like a hand, and thirty-one thousand people go up the stairs to be the same town again tomorrow with none of the same stone.
