## The Diet

Begin with the taste, because everything here begins with the taste.

Zinc is the wall. Recipe is the cities.

The air that comes down the returns has been filtered, but filters are rated for particles and not for dinner, and ten million people cooking and shedding and breathing and spilling produce an aerosol that no specification has ever adequately named. Fat, mostly. Skin flour. Sugar in quantities too small to measure and too large to ignore. Salt, cellulose, lint, the volatile fraction of frying oil, the ghost of onions. It comes down the arteries warm and it meets a cold ceiling and it condenses out with the water, because that is what condensation does: it scrubs. Every drop that leaves a drip-groove has washed a little of somebody's kitchen out of the sky-that-isn't.

The deposition surveys put it at about a gram per square meter per year, which across the working floor is thirty-odd tonnes of other people's breath.

That is one revenue. The other comes up from below. Fresh-fractured slag is chemically hungry: reduced iron, sulfides, glassy surfaces that have not yet made peace with water. Wet them and they give up electrons. The cutting heads in the far east are not only making room. They are making food, forty meters a month of it, and the sharpness that water has not yet had time to round is the richest part of the whole basin.

So there are two things to eat here — the exhaust of the made world above and the newness of the made world below — and everything alive in the Sweat lives on the difference between them.

The film that does the eating is not slime. Slime is a thing that happens to a place with time in it. This is a fur, a hand's-breadth of shine where the wet stone should be dull, gray-green in the seams and rusty at the lips of the lifts, and in the nine-degree trenches where the returns run shallow it thickens into mats that the crews call leaven, because they smell like bread and because they rise. Under a work-lamp you can see the leaven breathe: little domes of gas lifting and collapsing on a cycle that has nothing to do with any clock.

There is a rule about not eating it. The rule exists because of a fitter in year six who ate it, and was sick for a week, and told everybody it tasted exactly like it smelled, and then went back and did it again.

## The Small Along

Nothing here goes down. Down is more manufacture. So the whole fauna is a fauna of edges, and it is flat.

Seam-lice first: four millimeters, translucent, shaped like a fingernail paring that has come to a decision. They graze the fur in the expansion joints of the Colonnade, in the moss strips, along the thirty-meter ruled lines of writing on every pillar, and they are so nearly the color of wet stone that the way to find them is to look for the places where the moss has been mown. They cannot swim the sheet-water. Two millimeters is enough to drown them; the film of it holds them the way a meniscus holds a needle, and then doesn't. So they walk the cable runs.

Understand what the cable runs are. They stand off the floor on little cast legs, a hand above the water, dry on top, warm at the core, running for kilometers between pump-houses that otherwise have no connection to anything. They are the only roads in the Sweat. Everything that cannot fly and cannot swim is on them: lice, crickets, the pale hunting things that eat lice, the whole slow freight of the basin moving at a few hundred meters a shift along a ridge of polymer jacket the width of a wrist. When a run is de-energized and lifted out, a road is destroyed, and the maintenance log records the tonnage recovered.

The bolt-holes are nurseries. The shuttering left them in rows like a stave, ten centimeters deep, plugged with nothing, weeping. Lice breed in them. So do the calves.

Sump-calves: hand-length, slow, skin like wet paper, eyes that are only pigment and always have been. They have no lungs. They breathe through the skin of their backs, which means the whole posture of their lives is back-up, belly-down, pressed along a warm seam with the leaven under their mouths and the air on their spines. They do not hunt. They do not hurry. One per hundred meters of old seam is not a density; it is a spacing, and the spacing is enforced.

Two calves in dispute settle it by one lying on top of the other. The one underneath suffocates over about nine hours. The one on top is not eating it, and does not shift, and afterward moves off along the joint at the speed of a minute hand.

They keep the forty-hour cycle in their bodies. When the fan gates reverse and the pressure drops, they go into the joints and stop, and their metabolism falls to something that is not quite metabolism, and they wait out the sideways drips and the dying frost-fern without anything that could be called patience, because patience implies an alternative. They spawn in the flush. They release into the roaring gray sheet at shift change and let it take the eggs south, and almost all of the eggs go to the Shallows and become flour, and a very few catch in a downstream joint and are the next generation. Eleven years is perhaps seven generations of calf. There are seams in the west where the animals are noticeably larger and slower than the animals in the east, and nobody has decided whether that is age or ancestry or simply more leaven.

## Fifty Puddles

Under the Blue Trunk the joints weep on their own schedules, and each drip has worn its crater of frost, and each crater is an island.

The rime shrimp are three millimeters and there are fifty populations of them, one per puddle, and they are separated by a hundred meters of two-degree sheet-water that would kill any of them in the crossing. They live in the thaw halo: a ring about a centimeter wide where the drip's freeze has not yet closed and has already begun to open. A drip every four seconds means the ring freezes and thaws fifteen times a minute, nine hundred times an hour, all shift, all year.

They do not avoid this. Somewhere in the last decade they stopped treating it as an event. The shrimp go solid and come back and go solid and come back, and in the gaps they graze, molt, copulate, and lay eggs that will not hatch unless they have been frozen at least four hundred times. A shrimp's life is not a duration. It is a stutter with a shape.

Between flushes the fifty puddles are fifty worlds. During the flush they are one ocean for eleven minutes, and everything mixes, and then the water walks south and the islands come back. This has happened something like eight thousand times. The shrimp generation is six weeks. Do the arithmetic and you get about ninety-five generations of drift and remixing, which is not enough for species and is easily enough for *difference* — for a puddle at the north end where the shrimp are darker and freeze-tolerant to a degree that the puddle nine joints down cannot match.

No one has looked. Looking would mean sampling, and sampling would mean a request, and the request would have to say what it was for.

## Two Ways of Being Deaf

The fundamental is thirty-one hertz and it is in every surface. This has one consequence that organizes the entire animal life of the basin, and it is this: down here, the ordinary ways of sensing the world do not work.

A spider came down in a crate of insulation in year two and built a web in an antinode off the Cascade Stair, and every strand of it screamed continuously that something enormous was arriving. It rebuilt four times. Then it stopped rebuilding and sat in the hub with its legs pulled in and starved in a place thick with food. A mouse got down in year four and lasted nine days for the same reason. The falls, the crews call them: the steady small rain of up-top things that arrive competent and are killed by noise. The leaven takes them all within a shift.

The trough crickets survived because they were already halfway to deaf. They came down in the same insulation, they eat the sizing on the cable jackets, and they live along the roads in numbers. They call. Their calling is useless. In the roar their song carries about forty centimeters, which is less than the distance between two crickets, and for years they simply shouted into the fundamental and bred by accident, by touch, by stumbling.

Except along the nulls.

Where the thirty-one hertz cancels itself, sound is suddenly worth something. A cricket on a quiet line can be heard ten meters off. So the crickets have found the nulls — not by understanding them, by the plain arithmetic of who got laid — and now they lek along the cancellation lines in loose singing chains, and the chains are the loudest living thing in the Sweat, and you can only hear them if you happen to be standing exactly where the world is quiet.

And the leased hush pockets are the opposite country. A damped bay is a piece of the ordinary world, rented: no fundamental, no stacking, surfaces that mean what they say. Everything up-top that fails out here succeeds in there. Spiders work in the damped bays. Webs in a hush pocket are legible. Crickets breed in them in absurd densities because nothing that hunts by silence can get in. There are eleven leased bays in the basin and they are the only places where an imported animal can live without becoming something else, which means that the property map is also the biome map, and the leases are the only habitat designations anyone has ever issued here.

Some of the leases carry purity clauses. The hushmen go in with slates and hand-picks and clear the troughs, because a man paying four hours' credit for silence does not want an insect being stupid about sex in the corner. Other leaseholders let them stay. There is a trunk supervisor in the fourth ring who has renewed the same bay for six years and has never once used it to talk.

## The Luck

The flier is not a moth. It has four wings, a ninety-millimeter span, a heavy pale body, and no ears at all. Along its abdomen runs a fluid-filled canal with a bed of hairs in it — a spirit level that has learned to listen — and what it reads is the pressure gradient of the standing wave. It does not hear the fundamental. It feels the shape of the room.

Its world is not a space. It is a graph. The nulls are edges; the places where nulls cross are junctions; an antinode is not a loud place but a wall, because crossing one blanks the organ and the animal comes out the other side tumbling and deaf. So it flies straight along quiet lines and turns only where quiet lines meet, and a foraging flight is a route, and a good route is inherited. The maps of nodes and antinodes that the acoustic office updates after every plant modification are, functionally, its range. When a damper bank goes into a bay, three edges are deleted, and the moth that has been flying them for a season flies into a made silence and cannot read anything and starves with its abdomen full of a signal that means *nothing here*. When a lease lapses and the dampers come out, the spiders go blind, the cricket density collapses toward the nulls, and within two shifts there is a moth in a place that had no moths.

It hunts the leks. It lays its eggs in cable troughs within a hand's reach of a singing chain, and the larvae eat cricket eggs, which is why there are only a few hundred of them in nine kilometers by four: junctions are rare, and a pair needs a junction to itself, and courtship is two animals on the same quiet line modulating their wingbeats so that the beats cancel in the space between them. They sing by making a hole in the noise. Nobody has recorded it. There is no recording that would survive the fundamental, and there is no hush pocket a moth will enter.

At shift change, when the flush comes and the whole basin is a roaring gray sheet and every living thing down here is inside a joint, the moth is on the wing above the water. It is the only animal in the Sweat that does not have to hide. The crews see one or two riding the roar at the top of their lamps' reach, and it is considered good luck, and it has never been written into a report.

## The Animal Whose Habitat Is a Line Item

Walk east from the west wall and you are walking forward in time. The floor gets younger under you at forty meters a month. The fur gets thinner and rustier. The seams get sharper. Nine kilometers is eleven years laid out flat, and you can stand with one boot in a mature community and the other in a place four days old.

Behind the cutting heads there is a band about two hundred meters wide, and in it lives a flat pale grazer the crews call a marcher, nine days from egg to egg, that cannot eat anything but the fur on fresh fracture. It needs the reduced iron. It needs the corner unrounded. Two hundred meters back — five months of age — the fur has gone over to the ordinary mat and the lice have arrived and the marcher is outcompeted, gone, absent, not one.

So it moves east. It has been moving east for eleven years, laying ahead of itself, crawling up the cable legs against the pitch of the floor while everything else in the basin is being carried south. Its lineage has traveled five kilometers and has never occupied a place older than five months. There is no refuge behind it and no habitat ahead of it except what the heads make.

If the advance stops — a budget cycle, a rephasing, a decision made in a room with a window — the band stops, and the marcher walks into its own old age and ends. It is the only creature here whose continued existence is a matter of capital expenditure, and it does not know that, and it is very good at what it does.

## The First Soil Is an Animal

The flush is the top predator. It is eleven minutes long, it comes at shift change, and it takes everything that has not found a joint: lice, crickets, shrimp out of their puddles, calves caught in the open, marchers, eggs, the failed falls, all of it south across one-in-four hundred to the Shallows, where the water slows and drops its load.

The gray flour on the bottom of the Shallows is ground stone. It is also everybody.

In the quaking deltas at the margin there are masons: twenty millimeters, segmented, blind, pink where they are thin. They eat the flour. They pass it through a gut that coats it in mucus and extrude it as pellets, and the pellets flocculate, and flocculated flour settles differently — it holds, it coheres, it stacks with a cohesion that raw flour cannot manage. It is mud. Two centimeters of it, in patches, in the deltas east of Cascade Stair, smelling strongly of bread.

Mud banks stand differently than flour banks. Mud on the inside of a swing builds a bar that stays. Mud on the outside lets the water take a slab clean off with a flat slap.

The runnel east of the Stair began to swing last season, half a meter off the cut line and then further, and the survey has logged it twice as a defect, and the survey is right that nobody drew it and wrong about what is doing it. The first curve in this place that nobody drew is being built by worms out of the dead, at a centimeter a season, and it is the first material in the Sweat that anything could root down into.

To name that in a report is to specify a cause. To specify a cause is to open a remediation. There is a form waiting, and the masons are on it nowhere, and this is not an oversight.

## The Natives

Eleven years is the age of the place, which means the oldest human being born in it is ten.

There are nine of them. Their parents hold keeps and leases and trunk watches and have stopped taking leave up top, where the silence is thin and the food tastes of nothing but itself. The children sign natively — not the crews' safety sign, a full language, fast, with the basin's grammar in it: a whole class of verbs that take the fundamental as their subject, a tense for *during the flush*, a way of marking whether a thing is audible where you are standing or only where it is standing. They have never been in a room where a voice carried by itself. They find the damped bays funny, and then, sometimes, not funny.

They are the ones who know the fauna. Not the survey — the survey has the moss, the frost-fern, the leaven, and a category called *biological fouling*. The children have forty-odd animals and they have them in their hands: the calf, flat palm laid over flat palm, held nine hours; the shrimp, a fingertip stuttering; the marcher, two fingers walking east and never back; the moth, two fingers walking a line and then lifting off it into nothing, which is the sign for luck and also, in a different orientation, the sign for a thing that cannot be told.

They have a sign for the runnel. It is the index finger going straight, the way water is supposed to, and then refusing. It has no written form. If you asked them to write it they would write *defect*, because that is the word the adults have, and they are ten and not stupid.

And this is the thing that has actually happened here in eleven years, under the light budget and the cold grade and the forty-hour gates: nothing has become a new species, and almost everything has become something that cannot be anywhere else. The shrimp needs its nine hundred freezes an hour. The calf needs the pressure to fall on schedule or it will not spawn. The marcher needs the heads to keep cutting. The moth needs thirty-one hertz, phase-locked, forever, and would die in a quiet room with its abdomen full of nothing.

Take any of them up top and they die of stillness, of unscheduled air, of a silence with no shape in it. They have not become new animals. They have become animals with no elsewhere, which is the first half of the same thing, and the second half is only time, and time is the one commodity this place has never been issued.

Ask a child what lives in the Sweat and she will not tell you. She has no place to put the words. She will show you, and the showing takes most of a shift, and it is accurate, and at the end of it she will stand at the quaking edge of the Shallows and make the sign for the water refusing, and then she will look at the form in your hand, and wait to see which word you choose.
