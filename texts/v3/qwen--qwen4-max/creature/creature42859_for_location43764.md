## First, the obvious ones

Nine figs. A hundred thousand snails. Something like a hundred million midges, though the number is a guess in the same way the volume of a river is a guess. Swifts in the thousands. Bats. Beetles in armor. Moths the size of hands, flying in grids. Tree frogs beyond counting, in the leaf-axils, each axil a wet green room with a family in it and a mother in the middle of the family dropping unfertilized eggs into the water for her tadpoles to eat, which is a thing frogs do in small held puddles when there is no river and never has been. Fig-parrots, screaming, in the seventh tree only. One lizard. One hawk.

That is the list, and it is a good list, and it is the wrong shape.

Nothing on it is the right size to be the resident. The lizard is four meters long and there is one. The midges are two millimeters long and there are a hundred million and they live, individually, about four hours. If you want to know who lives in the hole you have to stop asking who is in it and start asking what it is that stays.

## The column

The column stays.

It has been there continuously — not as a lineage, not as a habit, but as a single unbroken body of moving air and animals — since long before anyone thought to cut a numeral at the northern point. Its cells turn over twice a day. Its shape does not. It fills the shaft from floor to rim, it rotates at a rate that has not measurably changed, it holds a temperature and a humidity and a boundary, it has a day-face and a night-face, and it breathes.

The swifts are not hunting the column. The swifts are the column's hunting organ. Watch them long enough and the possessive stops feeling like a figure of speech: they fold and pour through the same volume the midges occupy, they turn where the midges turn, they never touch the wall, and they never, ever cross the rim. There is a lid on the hole. It is made of nothing but the place where warm wet air stops being warm wet air, a shear surface forty meters up, invisible, absolute, and to a bird that reads the air with its whole body it is as solid as a windowpane. The swifts live under glass. They sleep aloft. Their feet are almost nothing, used once a season, to cling and lay.

At dusk the flow reverses and the lid lifts, and for eleven minutes the swifts go up into the sky.

Then it settles, and they come down, and the bats take the same volume over, and the column changes key without changing shape. It is one animal with two shifts. It is the only thing in the pit that ever leaves and comes back. Everything else that leaves — the fog, the water at the sump, the smell of fruit going over — does not return.

The figs cannot reproduce without it. The wasps ride it. The midge larvae eat the fruit-fall. The lizard eats what the column drops, and the floor is clean, and nothing is allowed to accumulate, and this is not tidiness. It is a metabolism.

## The wall

The snails are a scale.

They sort themselves the way anything sorts itself in a gradient: the condensate film is thickest low down, where the night air pools and the stone is warmest, and the big shells go where the food is, and the small ones take the thin high scrape, and the bands come out hand-span thick and ochre and umber and bone, largest at the bottom, ruled across forty meters of wall like the graduations on something.

They may not be sorting themselves. Nobody has watched all night. But it does not matter much, because either way the wall is a readout, and either way something reads it. A snail's shell is a log. Every night of its life is in there, the good ones thick, the dry ones thin, a spiral of everything the pit exhaled while that particular animal was alive. Multiply by a hundred thousand. The wall of the shaft is the pit's own history, written slowly in carbonate by animals that cannot move more than a meter in a week, in a place where nothing erodes and nothing is permitted to fall.

Between the bands the stone is bare and faintly glossy. It has been licked clean for two billion years by things with radulae, which are tongues made of teeth, which is one of the several ways this world has of getting its surfaces held to tolerance without appearing to do any work.

## Nine, and the ones inside the nine

Each fig has its own wasp. Not a wasp — *its* wasp, one species, obligate, the only insect on earth that can enter that particular tree's fruit, and there are nine trees and therefore nine kinds of wasp and no overlap and no substitutions.

The wasp lives two days. She has no functional gut as an adult; she does not eat; she runs on what she was given as a larva. She flies out of the fig she was born in, across eighty or a hundred paces of bare warm rock with nothing on it, following a scent that only a receptive fig emits, carried to her by the column. If she finds the right tree in the right hour she squeezes through a passage so tight it strips her wings and most of her antennae off on the way in, and she never comes out again. She lays her eggs inside the flowers, which are inside the fruit, which is inside the hole, and she dies there.

Her sons hatch wingless. They will never see the outside of the fig. They mate in the dark, and then they chew a tunnel out through the wall of the fruit — an exit they cannot use, for daughters they will never meet, who will fly out through it carrying pollen to a tree they have never smelled.

Then they die in the dark too.

Hold that. A door made by someone who will not go through it. There is going to be another one.

## The four seconds

Every hour or so the frogs stop.

All of them, all at once, thousands of axils, a wall of sawing sound in the exact band where human hearing has no defenses — and then nothing, for four seconds, and then it comes back like a door closing.

In those four seconds the pit is audible. Drip. The dry ratcheting of the swifts. The parrots, briefly, giving themselves away. The wind on the lip forty meters up, a bow on a wire. The whump, if the ninety seconds happen to land there. And under it all the tone, which you do not hear, which you feel in the bones of your face, and which is the same tone it was five days' walk ago.

Nobody who has stood in it believes the frogs stop by accident. Whether they stop to listen or stop so that something else can listen is not a question the pit answers, and it is the same question the place refuses everywhere else, wearing different clothes.

At night they go up a fourth. A fourth is three to two. It is not a random interval. The stone has a frequency and the frogs have a frequency and at night the two of them are in a ratio a child could sing.

## The large singulars

There is one lizard, under the third tree, and it does not hunt. It lies on warm rock with its belly at exactly the temperature of the floor, which is the temperature of everything, and it opens its mouth, and the column feeds it. Midge-fall, moth-fall, the swifts that die aloft, the frogs that misjudge, figs, parrots. Four meters of ledger. Everything the pit drops, it eats, and so the floor stays clean, and so nothing accumulates, and the lizard is not a scavenger so much as an organ.

No one has seen a juvenile. No one has seen two. There is a lizard the way there is a rung.

There is one hawk, at the northern point, near the numeral, and it is the only resident that lives on the outside — in the thin cold air, on the flat, in the wind that never changes speed. It goes down into the hole rarely and never far. It sits at the number. When the rim sings at dawn and dusk it shifts its feet, which is the only thing in the world that visibly anticipates the note.

And in the last third of its call there is an exact fragment of the tone coming up through the stone, which is below hearing, which the hawk has never heard, which it sings anyway.

Now put the hawks in a line. One per hole. Eleven thousand holes, or ninety thousand, spaced days apart across a subcontinent, and a bird at each one that can be heard by the next bird over on a flat with no relief and no shadow and nothing to interrupt a sound. A relay. A note passed hand to hand across a finished world by animals that do not know they are carrying it, in a call that also, incidentally, means *this is mine, keep off*.

The plumes are the same network in water vapor. The ticks along the rim are the same network in touch. The world here says everything three times, in three media, and one of the media is alive.

There is also, the accounting insists, exactly one thing that eats lizards. Nothing has been seen. Every so often there is no lizard under the third tree, for a while, and then there is.

## The hands

Somebody braids the rope.

Fig-bark, woven, replaced every few years, and the weave is the same weave it was when the first person thought to write down that it was the same weave. Somebody puts three figs in the bowl on the plinth on the southern rim, fresh, every morning, and by evening the bowl is empty and polished — not licked clean, *polished*, warm, the way a thing is when a broad soft tongue has been over it a long time.

Somebody cut the mason's ticks along the rim, at intervals, so faint that you find them with your fingertips and not your eyes. That is the detail that will not leave you alone. Nobody cuts a mark that fine by sight. That is a mark made by someone working in the dark, or by someone who does not use light, laying a hand along the lip of a forty-meter hole and knowing where the next tick goes by the length of their own arm.

Down at the eastern edge the floor dips and the water goes into the sump and does not come back, and blind things live in it, and turn to face a current in a chamber that has never been seen.

The hollow in the bench inside the hut is the shape of a body. It is a deep hollow. Stone does not take that shape from a person sitting on it for a lifetime; it takes it from a person sitting on it for longer than that, or from a great many people with the same posture, or from one thing that has been sitting down and getting up in the same place since before the mammals were a rounding error.

There is one keeper. There is one of everything large. The world here can afford nine trees and a hundred million flies and exactly one pair of hands, and it has arranged itself accordingly, and it does not say whether the hands grew out of the wall like the rungs, or came up wet from the sump, or are the last of something that used to be a village, or are the same thing wearing the last of a village.

The building four days east never gets closer at the rate it should. Neither do the trees. Nine canopies forty meters wide, eighty paces apart, in a hole a hundred and ten paces across; pace it out and the arithmetic does not close, and everybody who has paced it out decides, privately, not to make an issue of it. Distance is one of the things held to tolerance here. The keeper walks four days east and arrives in four days regardless of how fast they walk, and comes back with bark.

## The lapse

Twenty-two rungs, grown out of the wall, of the wall, as though the stone were asked politely for a ladder and complied.

Then they stop. And at exactly the level where they stop there is a rectangle of stone slightly different in grain from the rest, which is the only repair anyone has ever needed to make here, and which was not made by anyone.

So: once, something happened at that depth. The one failure. The stone healed itself, because the stone maintains itself, because that is what it is for — but it did not remake the rungs, and it has had two billion years, and it never will.

Because the rungs were not part of the specification. The rungs were a courtesy. And courtesies are not covered by maintenance.

Eight meters of fig-bark rope span the gap. It is replaced every few years by something with hands. The machine gave a ladder to a soft thing that could not climb, and then, once, it dropped it, and the soft thing's descendants have been holding the two ends together ever since, out of bark, out of the trees, out of the hole.

That is the second door made by someone who will not go through it, except this one is made by someone who goes through it every day, twice, carrying fruit.

## The actual population

Kneel and look at the grains. They are all exactly the same size. They are not grains; they are units. Scratch one and the scratch is shallower in an hour and gone by morning and the dust is not where you left it.

Whatever those are, there are more of them under a hundred kilometers of this than there are midges in the column by a factor nobody has bothered to write down. They are the residents. Everything else — the snails, the figs, the hawk, the hands — is a rounding error in the census, a film of something on the working surface, a warm wet complication in the ports.

They ate a mountain range grain by grain and kept the heat. That is the whole biography. It took an interval in which the age of mammals is a rounding error, and it was not violence, exactly; it was more like sanding; and when it was done the world was flat and warm and empty and finished, and it has been finished ever since, and it maintains itself, and nothing further is scheduled.

And it breathes through eleven thousand throats, or ninety thousand, and into every one of them life fell and stayed and became enormous the way only isolated things become enormous.

## What stands up

Here is what the hole has that the flat does not: height.

The machine cannot make relief. It gave relief up; relief was the thing it was built to remove; it holds a surface to the width of a fingernail over a hundred kilometers and it will hold it forever. It cannot put a hill back. But it can leave a hole, and a hole full of warm wet air will do the rest for free.

Nine trees, each carrying a hillside's worth of biomass folded into a space you could walk around in a minute. Snails in bands like strata. Frogs in thousands, which is what rain sounds like when it has somewhere to land. Swifts falling and rising, scree in reverse. Roots in the seams, epiphytes in the crotch-work, hanging soil, orchids, curtains — an entire montane province, compressed, kept warm, kept wet, kept loud, in a cylinder punched in the last page of the story.

And every morning and every evening a plume of breath and midges stands two hundred meters up out of the emptiness, leaning in the wind, the only vertical thing for a hundred kilometers in any direction: a mountain made of animals that live four hours, rebuilt daily, visible from ten kilometers away, silver in the moonlight.

The flat has no mountains. It has the *shape* of mountains, eleven thousand times, in a medium that does not erode, maintained by things that do not know what a mountain was.

Whether that is a use of the animals or a courtesy toward them is not a question the place answers. But it grew a ladder once, when it was asked politely. And something is counting, and the swarms are very good at counting, and the count has never once been lost, and the numeral at the northern point has worn to a dimple with a shape in it because someone, very long ago, thought this hole should be counted — and someone still puts out three figs, and something still eats them, and the bowl comes back polished, and the rope holds.

## Three in the morning

The fog lies knee-deep in the basins and the roots come out of it like the backs of animals. The column has resettled. The bats have the shaft. The frogs are up a fourth and the stone is under them at three to two and the whole pit is one chord.

Then the four seconds.

And in the four seconds: the drip, the sump, the wind on the wire, the whump coming due, and forty meters up a hand going along the lip in the dark, finding the next tick by the length of its own arm, and moving on toward the bowl.
