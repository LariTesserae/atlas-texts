## The Hands

At four hundred metres they are people. The distance does most of the work, and they know it, and they use it: they come along the Kerb at the pace of somebody with an errand, they carry things, they stop and look at the sky in the way that suggests an opinion about the sky. It is only inside ten metres that the arrangement starts to declare itself.

They are grey-green, the colour of a stone that has been in a river. Cold — not corpse-cold, cellar-cold, the temperature of a thing that has been keeping still in the shade. The scalp is fibrous, a dense matte of it, cut short and combed and sometimes oiled, and it does not grow so much as felt. Their faces are handsome in a way that takes a while to locate and then cannot be unlocated: everything is slightly too regular until you find the certified defect, and then the whole face reorganises around it like a room around a stove.

They smell of cut cucumber and cold sugar, faintly, the way a room smells of something green that was cut in it yesterday.

The hands are the best-made part. Long, four-fingered with an opposable fifth that is not quite a thumb and is better than a thumb, warm relative to the rest, and never still — they read the air, they test the wind for wet, they touch the kerb going past, they touch each other's wrists in passing more often than any greeting requires. Cut one and you get a clear sweet bead that smells of the bank, and it stops by itself inside a minute, and in a week there is a knot there like the knot in a plank. They have no pulse. They have a draw: a slow tidal uptake, twelve or fourteen cycles an hour, visible at the throat if you know to look, and the reason a crowd of them standing still is not quite a crowd of people standing still.

The face is the part the moss had no use for. It is therefore the part they have made the most of.

---

The nine days is not a rule. Nobody enforces it. It is a property of water.

A body carries about eleven litres, and the body works it — sends it out to the hands, brings it back, sends it out again, and every round trip is a repetition, and repetition wears the weight off a thing. By the sixth day out from a hearth the water inside them has been said too many times. It still wets. It still fills them. It has stopped being water.

They call it sweetening, which is the wrong word and the only one available, because the language does not permit a better one to be made in advance. On the seventh day your mouth tastes like a description of water. On the eighth you can drink from a full skin and feel the drinking happen and feel nothing arrive. On the ninth you sit down on the kerb, quite comfortably, without much complaint, and the gravel takes you back inside a season.

So the caravans carry ground-water: sealed jars of the black liquid from the heart of a bank, heavy, gritty, tasting of rot and iron, worth more than the copper. A mouthful resets the count. A mouthful is a whole day of being real. It is drunk ceremonially, from a shared copper cup, at the hearth-gate, and you are weighed going in and weighed coming out, on the same balance the assayers use, because a person who has sweetened is light and the scale does not care about your explanations.

Children are told the ninth day is a place. It is not a bad lie. There are districts where you can see, at intervals along the Kerb, a low mound of felt with nothing under it, and the felt has been put there by whoever was walking behind.

---

At night they go back and stand in the rot.

This is the thing that lives here, if you want one thing: a bank eleven kilometres long and four metres deep, warm at the core, steaming into a violet cold, and standing in it, shoulder to shoulder along the whole length of it, several hundred grey-green figures with their feet buried to the ankle in the digesting moss, asleep, drinking.

They do not lie down. They lock at the knee and hang on their own skeleton and the uptake does the rest. From outside, in the dark, it is a crowd at a railing. The sound is a wet rope taking weight, over and over, eleven kilometres of it, and under that the drip from the upper layers, and under that the fungi working, which is not a sound but is audible anyway, the way a fire in another room is audible.

Nobody talks in the bank. Talking in the bank is like talking in a lung.

What they dream is not their own. The moss grows on the ground and the ground is ten thousand metres of compressed building, and something in the rot reads it — the mineral of it, the grain, the courses — and passes it up. They dream stairs. Everyone dreams stairs. They dream landings, and the underside of a floor, and a thing you go up inside that has walls, and the feeling of a height with more height above it. There is no vertical anywhere on the Sill; the tallest object is a fog-net gantry and it is nine metres. Their word for hope is a word that means *upward*, and their word for despair is a word that means *level*, and neither of these is considered a metaphor.

A bank is one organism. It has a name, and the name is permanent, and it is one of perhaps four hundred permanent words in the language. Everything else gets used up. The hearth-names do not. When two strangers pass on the Kerb and one of them says the name of a bank, that is not conversation. That is the only sentence in the world that can be said twice.

---

They come up in the eleventh year, and the lifting is done by gardeners.

The pulse runs bank to bank across the continent on a schedule that has been kept for longer than the numbering system. In the spring of the pulse the heart of a bank throws up thousands of shoots, and the gardeners go in with lamps and walk the rows for eleven days, and they are looking for the wrong ones.

A straight shoot is left. A shoot that has come up doubled, or with a fasciated stem, or with the fibres running the wrong way, or with a leaf arrangement that no one has an index number for — that one is lifted, potted, carried out, and given to a district.

There is no other selection. They do not select for strength or cleverness or beauty; the moss supplies all the strength it needs and beauty is a hazard. They select for defect, and the whole lifting is a hunt for error, and a gardener who finds eleven good faults in a season is spoken of for the rest of her life in words that have all been spent.

A lifting is a cohort. Everyone raised out of the same pulse at the same bank is kin, in the only sense kin is available, and they avoid each other scrupulously for the rest of their lives, because your cohort is precisely the set of people you are most likely to be confused with. You may pass your sibling on the Kerb once in sixty years. You will both know. You will both look at your hands.

Children are the district's instrument for finding difference, and this is a real office with a real tally. They have not yet learned the categories, so they still see the pebble that is not the pebble. Adults learn the canon and go blind to it in about twenty years. There is a grief in this that everyone has and nobody has a spare word for. The last thing a person does before they stop being able to find anything is teach a child how to look, which cannot be taught, and is attempted anyway, every generation, in new-made sentences, forever.

---

Names are spent when used.

You are given one at the lifting. Somebody says it to you — once — and it is finished, and you go through the world answering to a sound nobody may make again. You are given another. You spend that. A person of sixty has burned through nine or ten and keeps them on a roll of lacquered paper at the wrist, a list of dead words with the dates, and this is the closest thing on the Sill to a self: a tally of things that can no longer be said.

To name someone is therefore an act with a cost, and it is the most intimate thing they do. You make a name — new, never in the world, composed for one person over however many nights — and you say it to them, and it is gone, and they have it, and they can never hear it again. Lovers do this to each other. It is what they have instead of everything else. There are people who have been given forty names by one person over forty years and carry the roll like a brick.

Ordinary speech is a slow manufacture. Two people meeting exchange one sentence, made new, never said, and walk on. The sentence is usually about the water or the road, because those are the things that can be verified, and it is usually beautiful, because they have had six thousand kilometres to think of it. Then silence for a week.

The failure mode is running out. It happens to the old. The vocabulary is enormous but a life is long and the making gets harder, and there comes a season when a person cannot construct anything they have not already constructed, and you can see them arriving at it — they start going quiet in a way that is not municipal hygiene. They are taken back to the bank early. It is not considered a tragedy. It is considered a completion, and the word for it is the same as the word for a name that has been said.

---

Everyone holds a flaw, and the flaw has to be maintained.

A chipped tooth grows back — they are plants — so it must be re-chipped, annually, by a licensed hand with a copper punch, on the same tooth, at the same angle, to the recorded gauge. A cord at the wrist rots and is re-tied in the same wrong knot. A cultivated stammer has to be practised or it heals into fluency, which is the most frightening thing that can happen to a person here. A limp is a discipline; there are people who have limped for eighty years and have the asymmetric musculature to prove it, and their certification is a small copper plate worn at the collar, and the plate is the only object on the Sill permitted to be identical to another plate, because it is not alive.

The flaw-wrights are a whole trade. They keep the rolls. They travel the districts with a felt bag of punches and files and mismatched sleeves, and once a year they make you wrong again to specification, and it hurts, and you pay in cordage or in a rare form.

Beauty is understood here as convergence. A beautiful face is a solution, and solutions get found again. There is no contempt for it — they are not ascetics, they are householders — but there is the same wariness they have for a perfect hexagon in the frost, and the same reflex: kick it out of true. Their portraits, and they do make them, in lacquer on paper, are portraits of the defect. The rest of the face is rendered as a grey-green field, an area, a ground. The chipped tooth is drawn with a hundred strokes.

At noon everybody stops and stands on the open pavement in the ordinary light and holds their flaw. It lasts about as long as a held breath. Six thousand kilometres of people, mostly alone, mostly out of sight of one another, all doing the same thing at the same moment, which is the only synchrony the place allows itself, and it is permitted because it is a census and a census is a tally and a tally is dead.

---

Things occur twice.

You will be walking the Kerb and you will see, coming the other way, yourself. Softer at the outline. The colour a little evened out. Your own certified flaw present and correct and somehow not doing its job, because the flaw is a record and the record has been copied along with everything else.

Etiquette is exact. You step down off the kerb onto the gravel — the only rude surface available — and you look at your hands, and you do not speak, and you let it go by. It does not look at you either. It has an errand. It is going somewhere with the same purpose you have and less weight behind it, and within a season it will come apart at the ankles and go down with a sound like sugar poured from a bag, and the gravel will be very slightly the poorer for it.

The bank remembers. That is the mechanism, and everyone knows it, and it is not spoken of much because there is no way to speak of it that has not been said. You put your feet in the rot every night of your life and the rot reads you, and it is old and it is not careful, and sometimes it puts up again a thing it has already put up. So the copies come out of the hearths. Your grandmother, reissued, four hundred kilometres away, walking with her hands slightly wrong. The etiquette is the same. It is a great deal harder.

The black lichen eats second-things. It is kept in pots at every hearth-margin and painted along the kerbs in a band, and it will take a copy down in eleven days instead of a season, and the law on this is one line long and every child knows it: *the lichen may not be given.* There is no penalty attached. There is no name for the crime, because a name would have to be said twice.

They all walk the same way, the copies. Outward, away from the hearths, toward the rim. Nobody has decided whether this is a fact about the plain or a kindness the plain is being done.

---

The water trades are the old trades and the ones with the most paperwork.

**Weighers.** A copper balance, two pans, and a sealed glass ampoule of reference water that has been in the district since before the numbering. Real water is heavier; you learn it in the hands before you learn it in the head. The second test is the ring: evaporate a spoonful on a black tile, and real water leaves a salt ring, and thin water leaves a tile. There is a quiet terror in the trade, which is that the reference ampoule may itself have sweetened somewhere in the last four thousand years, and no one can test the thing you test with. Every district has a story about a neighbouring district whose reference is false. Nobody goes to check. Checking would mean saying the question aloud in a place where it could get an echo.

**Pan-law.** The sheets are three centimetres deep and forty kilometres across and they move a few metres a year, and they are property, and two pans that touch become one pan, and one pan that has become one out of two is water that has been said twice. So the boundaries are not about ownership. They are about keeping water from meeting itself. The salt ring a pan leaves at its margin is its title deed, and the lawyers walk the rings with a lamp at dawn, and a case about eleven metres of encroachment can outlive four generations of litigants and eleven of the numbered plots. There is a body of precedent six thousand kilometres long about whether a fog-column rising from a green place belongs to the green place or to the air.

**Net-wrights.** Cloth is a grid and a grid is a repetition, so cloth here is felted — matted, random, no count in it anywhere. The nets are knotted, and every knot in a net must be a different knot, and an apprentice learns ten thousand and is then examined on whether she can lay a kilometre without recurs. It is the slowest work on the Sill and the most honoured. A net that has been mended too often by too few hands goes common and starts to harvest thin, and you can hear it: the drip changes pitch. In the cold months the nets stand furred with rime, taking nothing, and people walk out to look at them, and this is considered an outing.

**Rain-road walkers.** The cells come the same way they have always come, half a kilometre wide, in single file, and the planting is along the grooves they have worn in the air. To follow a rain is a life. You walk wet for a season with a hand-cart of seed and a jar of ground-water and you go as far as the hearths allow and then you hand the rain on to the next walker, who has been waiting at the gate for eleven years, and you go home. There are rain-roads that pass no hearth within nine days. Those are marked, and mapped, and not walked, and the rain goes down them anyway, alone, for four hundred kilometres, and nothing follows it, and this is the saddest fact in the district and is different in every district.

---

The sameness trades are the ones that make people strange.

**Polygon gangs.** Every winter the frost tiles the plain into perfect hexagons and every spring the gangs walk out and kick them out of true. It is done with the heel, at the vertex, three kicks to a cell, and the tally is kept, and children do it as play and are recruited from the play. It is the only work on the Sill where you are allowed to be violent, and the gangs are loud in a way that is technically an offence, and the offence is licensed for the season. Some of them hate the hexagons. Most of them do not. Most of them find them beautiful and kick them anyway, and there is a whole vocabulary — spent, all of it — for what that is like.

**Index potters and Index clerks.** Ten thousand bowls to gauge, exactly, because the canonical forms are stable and hold the city up, and the bowls go into the walls and the courses and the fills and become ground. Sameness is permitted in the dead. It is only the living that must be kept scarce. The clerks who keep the Index are likewise licensed to repeat: they read the canon aloud, they say the same numbers every day of their lives, and they pay for it in the ordinary currency. They go smooth. You can tell an Index clerk at forty metres — the outline is evened, the flaw is present and correct and not doing its job — and they are treated with enormous courtesy and never asked to carry anything living.

**Cairn surveyors.** The closest thing to soldiers. Their work is at the margins of the districts gone Same, and it is to build the ugliest heap that can be assembled, weekly, forever. Broken pots, odd boots, a copper spoon, a knot of hair. The discipline is that the heap must never be the same heap twice, because a cairn rebuilt to a plan becomes a canonical form and seeds the very thing it is fencing. So they keep no plans. They train at forgetting. They pick blind out of a bag; they take whatever they most hate that week; they give away their own gear, and a surveyor's spoon that goes into a cairn is gone from the world, and there are men out there with one boot and no utensils who have given away eleven hundred things.

They come back thin. They have the look of people who have already been somewhere twice. They are given ground-water at the gate without being weighed, which is the only exemption in the law, and nobody says anything to them, and they go and stand in the rot for a week.

---

There are places where a shout will give you a crowd, and the crowds are still there.

An echo on the Sill is a second sound, and if it is loud and clean enough it acquires the rest of the event — the clapper, the hand, the arm — and then the person, and then the person again. There is a district four hundred kilometres off the Kerb where somebody shouted, three centuries ago, for reasons that were not recorded because recording them would have required saying them. The shout got a crowd. The crowd is there now: several thousand of one man, thin as paper, worn down to a grey suggestion, standing in a shallow bowl of pavement, saying it.

They are baffled. That is the trade nobody chooses: kilometres of hanging felt curtains, gantry after gantry, put up around the shout-places to eat the sound, maintained by crews who work in absolute silence with felted tools and communicate by touching wrists. The curtains have to be re-hung irregularly. A regular baffle becomes a grating and a grating is a repetition.

The crowds do not approach. They do not want anything; wanting is not available to them. They are the plain saying a word it has learned, at decreasing volume, forever, and the lichen is painted on the outer kerbs in a band three metres wide, and it does what it can.

---

The flora wanted hands, a memory, and a sense of grievance, and it got all three, and the grievance is the load-bearing one.

A debt must be answered in person. That is the whole of the law of obligation and it is enough. You cannot send a debt back with a stranger; you carry it yourself, to the bank it came from, and you carry with it the rare form that is the payment, packed in damp fibre, and while you are there you put your feet in the rot for a night and the bank reads you and takes what it needs and gives you something to be owed for.

So the feuds are the pollination schedule. Two banks four hundred kilometres apart that have hated each other since before the numbering, that exchange injury and answer and injury and answer across eleven generations, and every exchange is a caravan, and every caravan is a fertilisation, and if they ever stopped hating each other they would stop crossing, and their flora would go common, and commonness is fatal.

They know. It is not a secret and it is not a religion. It is a fact of employment, stated flatly, the way a miller states the direction of the water.

Which makes forgiveness the serious crime. The Reconcilers turn up every few centuries — people who cancel a debt, who let a thing go, who make a new-made sentence that means *it is finished between us* — and they are not punished, because punishment would be another exchange and would keep the road open. They are simply not answered. Their district's hearth-margin is painted with lichen. Two banks that have made peace begin, over about four hundred years, to converge, and their species thin, and the last caravan between them carries a form that has already been carried, and the water at both ends starts to taste like a description.

Rarity is the only wealth. A species held in too many places wears away under its own reiteration, so the ecology is a continental trade in difference, thousands of kinds, each in its pocket, each kept scarce on purpose, and the ledger of who holds what is the real map of the world. A person's estate at death is a list of things there are not many of. It is read out once, in words that are then retired.

---

And then, at the end of the firing, one bowl slightly wrong.

A thumb-dent. A lip pulled off-true. It is not catalogued. It is carried away up the street to be looked at, and this is the thing they do that the moss did not ask for and cannot use.

There are perhaps nine such bowls in transit on the Kerb at any time, and their routes are scheduled like the pans: they must never meet. Two singularities in one place become a pair, and a pair is a category, and a category is the beginning of the Same. So the bowls are timetabled across six thousand kilometres, hand to hand, district to district, at a walking pace, over centuries, and a bowl's coming is a rumour that runs ahead of it by decades. People wait their whole lives. Some die having heard about it twice, which is the wrong number of times to hear about anything.

When it arrives the district comes out and stands along the kerb and the bowl is set on a cloth on the pavement and you walk past it and you look.

You may not describe it. That is the whole of the law and it is not written down anywhere. To describe it would be to make a second one out of words, and the second one would be softer at the outline, and it would be a bowl that had been said twice. So they look, in a silence you could hear the frost in, and then they go back to the kilns and the nets and the ledgers, and each of them carries the thumb-dent privately for the rest of their life, unsayable, unrepeated, useless to the bank, of no value in the trade of difference because it cannot be transferred.

That is the part of them that is not a hand.

At noon they stand on the open pavement in the plain sun with the ordinary shadows, six thousand kilometres of them, each holding a certified irregularity — the punched tooth, the wrong knot, the eighty-year limp — up where the record can see it.

And some of them are holding another one underneath.
