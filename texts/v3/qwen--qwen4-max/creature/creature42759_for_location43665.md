Begin with the manifest, because the manifest is wrong, and the way it is wrong is the first thing that lives here.

Nineteen mosses were carried up. There are twenty. The twentieth came in as packing — a dry grey wad shaken out of a crate of sedge in the second spring and swept, presumably, toward the sump, where it was not swept hard enough. It is the one that stopped bothering with rock. The tender calls it the plan.

The plan has no wild form. It has been looked for. In the herbarium at the lower station there is a sheet of it mounted beside a sheet of the same species taken from a culvert four hundred kilometres north, and the two do not look like relatives: the northern one is a scruffy olive tuft with leaves arranged every which way, and the Kettle one is a flat bright ribbon that lays itself along a wet cylinder in a straight run and turns ninety degrees when the cylinder turns. It does not climb the pipe. It sheathes it, at a constant thickness, three fingers, and it stops at the dry side of a stone as if it had been told to. Given a seep it will follow the seep uphill. Given a capped branch it will follow the ghost of the seep for years, thinning, going the pale yellow-green of something living on an income that used to be larger, and not dying, and not going anywhere else.

That pallor is diagnostic. The tender reads the whole bowl by it. A pale run means a leak too small to hear, or a line somebody closed in the second decade and did not write down, or a place where a person used to stand and no longer does.

Because there is a branch of the plan that is not on any schematic. It comes off the low tier at the standpipe, runs down the post, crosses the wet flagstone by the engine-house door, goes up the doorframe to the height of a shoulder, and stops. Another comes off the handrail of the headwall stair at the fourth length and follows the rail down through all two hundred and twelve steps in a thin hungry line, brightest where the drip from a wet sleeve falls, which is at the turns. There is a patch on the back of the chair by the turbine, coat-shaped, that is greener than anything else in the bowl. The plan has mapped the rounds. It has mapped them by wetness, which is to say by habit, which is to say it has drawn a portrait of a job.

In the four days between turns it goes the colour of the capped branches.

One more thing about the plan, and it explains the brass. Copper kills moss. Everything copper-bearing in the Kettle is clean: the lids of the junction boxes show through the fur like eyes because nothing will grow on them, the survey pegs keep their caps, the brass plate by the engine house with the four-hundred-year estimate on it is as legible as the day it was screwed up. Every other surface in the bowl has been taken. The brass is the only writing left, and it survives not because anyone maintains it but because it is mildly poisonous, and this is the whole of the Kettle's policy toward its own inhabitants.

---

Under the mill, in the warm sump, the cricket.

It is the colour of a fingernail. It has no wings worth the name and no need of them; it lives in a space two hand-spans high, at twenty-six degrees, in the dark, on spent moss and lime sludge and the bodies of mites, and it has been doing this for thirty-one years, which is something over eighty generations. It is smaller than the crickets in the tin were. It is paler. It has, in the way of these things, lost most of its wariness of light and none of its wariness of vibration, because vibration here means the mill.

It sings. This is the fact that took twenty-two years to establish.

The roar is a hiss, broadband, and the turbine sits above it as a thin steady wire of sound, and between them there is a window — a narrow band where the bowl is, acoustically, almost empty. The cricket's call is in that window. It is four or five notes, very high, very small, and it lasts about a second and a half, and it cannot be heard at all while the grid is running, because although the window exists the ear does not; the ear has been flattened by an hour of roar and stops resolving anything.

During slack it can be heard. Not easily. You have to be sitting still beforehand, and you have to be sitting still in the eleven minutes, and almost nobody is ever sitting still in the eleven minutes, because slack is when you go up to look at the fins.

There is a column on the tally board for it. In thirty-one years it has forty-one marks. The column does not distinguish between hearing the cricket and being answered by it, and there is no other record, and the people who could have explained the convention are dead or downslope and cannot be asked without a great deal of trouble.

---

The worms came from a tin and the tin is still here; its lid is a saucer under the lamp in the engine house. Everything in the eleven centimetres descends from that tin. They are small and pinkish and they cannot go down, because below the made soil is mineral flour and angular shard, and the mountain has not finished being a mountain. So they work the same shallow layer over and over and have become a surface animal, coming up under the lamps, retreating when the fog thins and the sun gets in. The mill turns out a barrow a day of black crumb. The crumb is not soil. The worms make it soil, at a rate nobody has ever measured, in the only place on this flank where there is anything to make it from.

The moth is a small brown tineid whose larva eats dead moss in the warm corners of the terraces. It has been here as long as the moss. Its marking has drifted; the tenders' photographs, kept in a tobacco tin, show a species slowly losing a spot. It flies at lamp-dusk, which is not dusk: the lamps come on by photocell when the fog thickens, so the moth's evening arrives at random, sometimes at eleven in the morning, sometimes twice in a day, sometimes not at all. It has adjusted. It mates in the false evening, whenever that is, and its numbers are steady.

The white flowers are one plant. It runs under the sedge and the sphagnum from the fourth terrace to the nineteenth and it has never set much seed, because it opens only at slack, and slack comes every thirty-four hours, which means the flowering walks around the clock — ten hours later each time, all the way round and back in about twenty-four days. Most of its openings happen with the sun straight overhead and no moth awake. It flowers to nobody. It has been flowering to nobody for three decades and holding its ground by runners.

But roughly once in twenty-four days a slack falls inside a lamp-evening, and the moth is up, and the flowers are open, and seed is set. There is a column for that too. It is the shortest column on the board.

---

The swift is not a resident and is the most important animal here.

It comes over the rim at dusk from somewhere below and outside, drinks off the top pool on the wing, and goes. Nobody has ever found where it sleeps. It roosts somewhere with mud, because swifts of this kind build with mud, and it gathers that mud from the terrace lips, and mud carries worm cocoons and mite eggs and moss fragments and spores, and a bird that drinks at eighty pools in one bowl and travels is not going to drink at only one bowl.

On the peak thirty kilometres away there is a red lamp. Under it there is another notch, another grid, another manifest.

The rhubarb-like thing with the red stems that nobody planted and nobody can account for is on the manifest of the other bowl. It is item six. It has been item six there for twenty years.

So the bowls are not sealed, and were never sealed, and the four hundred kilometres of nothing is not a barrier but a distance, which is a different thing. There is a bird going back and forth in the dark between the warm rooms, carrying mud on its feet, and it has been doing this long enough that the mosses of the two bowls are no longer quite the inventories they were carried up as. Something is being averaged. Nobody is doing it. It has no schedule and no gauge and no column on any board, and it is the fastest process in the Kettle.

---

The tender is a post, not a person.

The turn is ninety days. The gap between turns is four. There have been, at this bowl, nineteen tenders, and no tender has met more than three of the others, and every one of them was taught the job by someone who was taught it by someone, hand to hand at the overlap, so that the way you hold the needle — pinched, not gripped, because you will do it two hundred times a day and the grip gives you a cramp at the fourth terrace by the second week — has come down thirty-one years without ever once being written in the manual, which is a good manual, and which does not mention it.

The work is holes. There are something over twenty thousand perforations in the grid and the plan grows over them from outside at a thumb's width a year, and the tender walks the tiers with a hooked wire on an aluminium handle and opens them, two hundred a day, and the pass takes eleven months, and by the time it comes round the first holes are furred again. This is not maintenance in the sense of repair. Nothing is broken. The tender is pruning the only native of the place, one eleven-centimetre interval at a time, forever, and by choosing which runs to keep open is also selecting which runs the plan grows best on, so that after thirty years the moss is measurably better at being a pipe than its ancestors were. The tender has bred it. Not on purpose. There is no column for that.

The gloves are mended with copper wire because copper wire is what there is, and the mends stay bright and clean while the leather around them goes green, and a well-used glove in the Kettle ends up as a green mitten with a shining constellation on it, the negative shape of every repair a person ever made. There are four pairs hanging in the engine house. They are the only objects in the bowl that are not being slowly eaten, and they are the only objects in the bowl that are unmistakably hands.

The chair by the turbine has been re-caned twice. The wear in the frame under the cane is older than either caning and is not the shape of any one body; it is the average of nineteen, a shallow smooth depression that fits everybody approximately and nobody exactly, and new tenders find it uncomfortable for about a week and then never notice it again.

The tenders whistle. You cannot shout usefully in a roar — visitors try for an hour — but a whistle at the right pitch goes through it, in the same window the cricket uses, and there is a small inherited vocabulary: two notes for *coming up*, a rising one for *stand clear of the penstock*, a long flat one that means nothing except *I am here*, which is used more than the others. It is used at night. It is used going down the stair. Nobody has ever explained why and every tender does it.

There is a thing that happens to tenders after long turns, in the kidneys. It is the same thing that has happened to the valves at the bottom flight, which have not turned in nine years and are now features of the reef, and the water goes around them. Nobody says this out loud. The medical officer at the lower station has a jar.

The flywheel is where the people are kept. The rind grows a millimetre a year and is chipped off every fourth year, and each chipping comes away in blocks, and the blocks are wrapped in oilcloth and stacked on the three bare bunks, numbered, eight of them now. Cut one across and you get four bands, one per year, and in most bands there is a notch where somebody scratched their initials into the soft lime before it hardened — a little V, filled by the next year's deposit, sealed in. Two notches in the band for the eleventh year. No notch at all in the band for the nineteenth, and no explanation, and the board for that year is in a different hand. A gouge, deep and angry, in the twenty-sixth. Moth wings in most of them. A grey ash layer in the fourth, from the winter the engine-house stove smoked. It is the only stratigraphy in the Kettle that has anything in it but lime, and it is four years long at a time, and it is the reason the long cycle is done by hand with a wooden wedge instead of a hammer, which is slower, and which is in the manual, and which nobody has ever proposed changing.

The mug on the sill is not washed. It is rinsed. The ring in it is the outgoing tender's, laid down over ninety days at the rate the water lays down everything, and the incoming tender breaks it out with a thumbnail on the first morning and starts their own. This is not sentiment. It is just what you do with the mug.

---

Now put it together, because none of the above is really the resident.

There is a thing here that takes in hoarfrost at the rim and glacier at the headwall and rock at the crusher, and puts out soil at a barrow a day and lime at four millimetres a year and moss at a thumb's width, and breathes once every eleven seconds, and knocks once a minute, and sighs every forty minutes, and goes silent for eleven minutes every thirty-four hours, and has a heartbeat you can watch the spokes of. Its canopy is its own plumbing. Its skeleton is deposited by its own blood. Its gut is a four-metre drum. Its skin is twenty mosses and a bird's mistakes. It has one hand, which is replaced every ninety days and knows how to hold a needle.

It is mortal and it knows the date. Four hundred and some years, on the brass, which stays legible because it is poison: the glacier fails, the head goes, the water stops, and the cold comes over the rim in an afternoon and takes the whole green thing in a night, leaving a cream-coloured mass with pipes in it like veins in a leaf, which is a fossil of a room.

It reproduces. That is what the red lamp is. Thirty kilometres away, another notch, another wound in a flank, another manifest with the rhubarb at item six; and between them, in the dark, one bird with mud on its feet, doing the only fast thing in the whole system.

Everything here waits at the speed it has. The worms wait in eleven centimetres. The plan waits on a capped pipe for a seep to come back and does not seem to mind. The flowers open on a schedule that walks around the clock and are mostly unvisited and are still there. The cricket sings into a window in the roar that a person also whistles into, and about once a year somebody is sitting still at the right moment and adds a mark to a column, and the column does not say whether anyone answered.

---

Nobody is here today. It is the second day of the gap. The grid is running, the lamps will come on when the fog thickens, the flywheel is putting on its thousandth of a millimetre, the white flowers will open at four in the morning to an empty bowl, and the branch of the plan that follows the rounds is the pale yellow-green of a capped line.

Somebody is due in two days. They will come to the notch and stand with one hand in each season and smell bread and wet iron, and shout once, out of habit, and stop. They will break the ring out of the mug. They will read the board from the top.

And then, because it is what you do on the first day, before the rounds, before the fins, before anything — they will go and sit down in the chair that fits them approximately, in the warm sump noise under the mill, and wait for the intake to close, and listen to the eleven minutes for something very small that may or may not be there.
