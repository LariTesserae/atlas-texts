Begin with the bulk, because the animals are a rounding error.

Nine parts in ten of everything alive in the basin is moss, and most of that is sphagnum, and the sphagnum is very likely one organism repeated — a clone laid over the terraces in armchair-sized hummocks, each hummock a colony of shoots growing up through the corpses of its own previous generations, so that every cushion is a column of its own history, wet at the top, black and compressed at the bottom, ten thousand years of itself standing in water. It holds thirty times its weight. It holds the basin's water the way a hand holds a coin it has forgotten about. It keeps the chemistry acid and slow, which is why the peat does not finish rotting, which is why there is peat at all. Everything else here lives in a structure the moss is passively maintaining and has never noticed.

The moss does not experience the roar as sound. It experiences it as a pressure on the outside of its cells, a permanent slight squeeze, a hand resting on it. It has no organ that could distinguish sixty hertz from silence. What it notices — what it has evolved around, thoroughly, over an interval that makes the alder carr look recent — is the eleven seconds.

To the moss, reconciliation is drought. The only drought it has ever known: the sheet of water goes flat, the film thins, the arriving stops, and for eleven seconds nothing is being delivered to its surfaces. It responds by clamping. The hyaline cells shut, the shoot tips curl a half-degree, the whole hummock takes a breath and holds it. Seven hundred times a year, more or less, since long before there were hummocks. Cross-section an old cushion and you can see the stress lines, pale bands where growth paused, laminated as finely as the travertine and for the same reason: the basin keeps settling something, and the settling gets laid down.

And it spores in the pause. It has to. A spore released into the ordinary roar is knocked out of the air by the first of ten million arrivals and drowned where it lands. A spore released into eleven seconds of absolutely still water can travel — over a surface with no ripples on it, on a film with nothing arriving, four hundred thousand terraces briefly continuous, one connected motionless sheet thirty kilometers wide. The moss has one window a year seven hundred times over, and it uses the first two seconds of it. The fog above the hummocks goes faintly gold at the moment of release, a color you would not see unless you were standing in the right quarter, and then the water resumes and the whole attempt is either somewhere else by then or is mud.

---

Under the moss, under the peat, in the weeping faces and along the seams where causality slips, are the actual masons, and they are films.

Sheathed bacteria in threads finer than anything with a name, laying in sheets a few cells thick over every surface where water is giving up its dissolved stone. Rust ones, cream ones, grey-green ones with a texture like the inside of a cheek. They do not build. They *prefer*. They keep a microenvironment a fraction of a degree, a fraction of a pH, away from the water outside, and in that fraction the stone lets go, and the wall grows, and the wall's lobed and scalloped and ear-shaped geometry is nothing but the shape of a preference held steady for a very long time.

Here is the part that matters for everything above them: the light is not the point. Late light, grey light, six hours of shuttered light filtered through stratus and fog and sedge — that is not enough to run a basin this lush. The base of the food web here is not the sun. It is the settling. When a discrepancy resolves and drops out of solution, it gives up a little energy on the way down, the way any falling thing does, and the films are positioned to take that fraction. They live on argument being concluded. They are grazers of consensus. The liverwort growing on the causality seams is their canopy and their trellis; the fresh tufa lip, soft enough to crumble with a thumb, is their pasture at the exact instant it becomes pasture, which is the instant it stops being a disagreement.

Everything else in the Long Settlement is, at one or two removes, eating the resolution of a difference of opinion.

---

The snail is the part that remembers.

Small, pale, translucent enough that you can see the dark thread of its gut through the shell; a few thousand individuals across seven hundred square kilometers, which is one per twenty hectares, which means you could walk a week in the fog and see three. It grazes the fresh lips. It eats resolved discrepancy while the discrepancy is still soft — the day-old lamina, the argument only just concluded, still slightly warm if you had an instrument fine enough to notice.

Its radula makes a sound. It is the only sound in the basin produced on purpose by an animal, and it is inaudible under the roar by a margin of about forty decibels, and it has been inaudible for as long as there have been snails.

The shell is banded, one band per reconciliation, and this is not a metaphor: the animal's calcification is entrained to the pause, lays down a discontinuity in the eleven seconds, resumes. An old snail carries several years of the basin's argument with itself in a spiral you could hold on a fingertip. Two snails' shells from different quarters disagree about how many events there were in a given season, because the events are local, one to three a day depending on where you are standing, and the shells are the only place that difference is kept.

But the archive is corrupt, and the corruption is alive. There is a film that grows *inside* the bands, in the discontinuity, and lays down its own laminae there, so that a parasitized snail carries extra events — a season with nine hundred reconciliations in it, a year that was longer than the year. You cannot tell from the shell alone. Nothing here can tell from the shell alone. There is no reader.

And the grazing un-settles. When the snail rasps a soft lamina away, the resolved difference goes back into solution, and the basin has to conduct that argument again, and does, and the roar's pitch wanders by an amount too small to name. This is why the place is *old* rather than merely ancient: the snails recycle its disagreements. The same few hundred thousand arguments have been being settled here, imperfectly, repeatedly, for a length of time that has no useful unit. The patina is a diet.

They mate across copies. A snail finds the nearest instance of itself — offset a meter, or twenty, up or down through the fog, a snail with a slightly different number of bands — and exchanges with it, and what comes of that exchange is the difference between them, made briefly flesh. Most of these offspring precipitate within the hour. They are the finest laminae in the rims, the ones with a faint nacre in them. Once in a very long while one does not precipitate, and crawls away, and is a snail.

The old ones encrust. The shell keeps growing stone after the animal inside has stopped growing shell, and a truly ancient snail is a lump of travertine the size of a plum with a live wet animal in the middle of it, grazing a few square millimeters a day through a hole it can no longer widen, entirely immobile, still recording. There are lumps in the rims that are still alive. You would not know which.

---

Now the animal that has had the most practice.

In the peat of every terrace there are eggs, colorless, smaller than a period, in numbers that would embarrass the midge. They are waiting for the pressure change — the sinus thing, the straightening of the fog lanes, which arrives about a second and a half before the tone. That is their entire sensory apparatus: they detect the precursor, and they hatch into the pause.

What hatches is flat. A ribbon, four to nine millimeters long, transparent, thin as a shed scale, with a mouth on its underside and no thickness to speak of. It lives on the surface film of water that has gone perfectly still. It cannot survive water that moves; a single arriving drip tears it. It has, therefore, eleven seconds: two of them getting oriented, five of grazing the film and the spore-fall and the shredded adults of the previous pause, three of mating, and then it lays and sinks, and the four hundred thousand waterfalls restart, and every adult in the basin is torn to a molecular smear that the films will have by evening.

One generation per event. Seven hundred events a year. Two hundred million years.

Something on the order of a hundred billion generations, which is to say this transparent film of an animal has had more practice at being itself than every other lineage in the basin combined, by four orders of magnitude. It is the most refined thing here. It is refined *for* eleven seconds — its grazing is a fixed motor sequence that runs whether or not there is food, its mating is simultaneous and requires no negotiation, its eggs are pre-fertilized before it hatches. There is no slack in it anywhere. It is a completed animal.

And its world is not this one. To the flat thing, the Long Settlement is a silent, motionless, glassy place, absolutely flat, absolutely still, thirty kilometers of connected surface with no arrivals on it — a place that is periodically and catastrophically destroyed by movement. The roar is not a sound to it. The roar is a solid. It has never been in the roar and lived. Its entire cosmology, if a thing with eleven seconds could be said to have one, would be a still world with an apocalypse at the end of it, and the apocalypse would be the ordinary condition of everything else.

There are others in the pause with it. A springtail-sized grazer that walks on the flat sheet, on tension, and gets its two seconds in the middle of the interval. The moss's spores, drifting. The snails spawning, late, at nine to eleven, which is why the nacre laminae are so thin. It is a guild, and it is partitioned in milliseconds, and it is the most crowded community in the basin, and it happens in a window during which the whole place looks like nothing whatsoever is going on.

---

The eel is one animal.

Probably. It is unpigmented, blind, and it moves between copies of a pool rather than along it, and its length is not conserved any more than length here is conserved generally: a meter in the shallow dish, twenty in the sinkhole, and both of them the same eel, and an observer standing still sees it in several places at once and is not seeing several eels. It has no eyes and no pigment and no swim bladder and, apparently, no interest in distance. It tastes with its whole skin. It navigates by the smell of resolution — by which quarter of the basin is currently settling something — and it feeds where the settling is freshest, on the films, on the snails it can find, on the eggs in the peat, on the midge larvae when there are midge larvae.

It is the only animal here with a continuous history. Everything else lives in a place where two pools a meter apart can be a day apart, and copes by not needing continuity. The eel simply declines the problem. It does not traverse. It is *already elsewhere*, and has been for a length of time nobody can bound, and if it reproduces it does so by drift: when two of its instances disagree far enough, they stop being one eel, and the difference between them precipitates as a small bone-colored plate in the peat. There are plates in the peat. They are the only bones in the Long Settlement, and none of them ever belonged to a body.

There is a notch in it. Somewhere along its length there is a stretch that is thinner than the rest, that does not appear in every copy, that is missing from the instance in the eastern carr entirely. Something took a piece of it once. It is the only animal here that has been touched by the hush and is still an animal.

During the eleven seconds it lies absolutely still, all of it, every instance at once. That is the only moment when a stationary observer would see one eel.

---

Every fourth summer the fog goes brown for two days.

The midge larvae are in the peat, and they are counting. Each carries a laminated organ, a stack of tiny plates, and each reconciliation adds one, and at somewhere near two thousand eight hundred the animal stops feeding and starts climbing. The count is local — one to three events a day depending on where you are — so the threshold is crossed at slightly different times in different quarters, which is why the emergence is smeared over two days rather than being an instant. It is the only appointment in the basin, and it is kept to within thirty hours, and it has been kept for longer than there have been alders.

They are deaf and wingless. They crawl up the sedge stems, and the sedges are hollow and tuned, whole hectares acting as resonators, and this is what the tuning is *for* as far as anyone can tell: the stands are a telephone exchange. A midge on a stem in the northern flushes feels that stand's bright seething through its feet; a midge in the eastern carr feels the low knocking; and they walk toward the pitch that means *crowd*, downhill, along the fog lanes, because the lanes are where the arithmetic behaves and a wingless animal that steps out of a lane spends its distance on other things and never arrives.

The deep middle, where the sound is closest to a chord, is where they end up. Two days of the only abundance this place has ever attempted: the fog thick with crawling bodies, the sedge bent, a smell like wet bread, like yeast, over the cold resin of the bog myrtle. They cannot hear each other and do not need to. Then it ends, and they fall, and the fall is the basin's one pulse of fertility — a fourth-year deposit of nitrogen into peat that otherwise gets nothing, and the phosphor in the runnels runs bright blue for a week, and the foxfire in the carr comes up strong enough to show you your own hand.

The fungus is not glowing for anyone. Nothing here has eyes worth the name. The rot-glow is exhaust, an oxidative leak, and its four-year brightness is a meal, not a signal. But it *deposits*. The deep pools take it and hold it and spend it slowly, so that in the fourth summer the carr is lit for weeks by light that has already stopped being made — the basin's own past shining on the basin's own present, seen by nothing, recorded only as brightness in water. There is a kind of memory here that has never once been looked at.

---

The bird is three or four individuals in the eastern carr, and may be one bird held at three or four depths of agreement.

Its lineage had ears. You can see where they were: a sealed hollow in the skull, bone grown over the opening, and in that hollow now a pressure organ, a membrane on a fluid sac that reads the precursor the way your sinuses do. It goes down into the peat when the warning comes and waits with its bill in a gap it did not dig. It has never heard anything and never will.

It booms at four or five hertz, below the basin's fundamental, in the one band where nothing else is talking, and the booms carry — thirty kilometers of shallow bowl, no hard shadows, nothing to reflect off, a note you would feel in the bones of your face. If the three or four are one, then the booming is how it finds its instances, and the instances converge on the sound, and when two of them agree closely enough they merge, and the merger drops a lamina. Courtship here is consensus. It has the same output as everything else.

It walks. Its outer primaries are fringed and mineralized, tufa grown along the shafts, and it could not fly if it wanted to; the copies do whatever flying there is. It lays in a bare scoop on a fresh lip, in the softest stone, and the egg begins to accrete immediately, because everything wet here accretes, and the chick has to break out of a shell that is turning into the terrace. Hatching is a race against mineralization. Some lose. In the old rims there are bird-shaped voids, and beside them, in the same stone, ear-shaped ones — molds of tympana, hundreds of them, the shapes of organs that used to work here. The snails graze those lips like any other.

Its brood is a day away. It lays where the light lags, and the chick develops in a pool still holding yesterday's noon, and the parent drops food into water and the food arrives the day before, and it feeds a chick it will not meet until the chick has already been fed. Its territory is not an area. It is an interval.

There is a mite that lives in the sealed hollow of its skull and dulls the pressure organ, and a bird with mites goes down late.

---

And the hush, which is the reason for all of this deafness.

Eighty meters wide, moving against the fog-drift — against the drift, always, upstream toward the surfaces that have no upstream. Inside it the sedges stand with their stems open and nothing comes out. It is the only quiet in thirty kilometers, and it does not listen for sound. It listens for *relief*.

That is what it takes: the small noise a body makes when it stops being careful. A sigh. A wing resettling. The unclench of a muscle that had been holding a territory or a mate or a vigil. It cannot hear any of that inside the roar, because the roar is everything and relief is a small thing. So it carries a pocket of nothing through the noise and waits inside the pocket for something to be glad. Anything with a tympanum, hearing the roar stop, believes for one instant that the danger has passed. That instant is audible. Then the animal is not there, and later it is in the tufa, and the shape of its ear is in the rim, and a snail eats the rim.

What the hush is, nobody is in a position to say. The films grow on it, so it has surfaces. It smells of clean stone, which in this basin is the smell of nothing at all — no resin, no iron, no yeast — and a nose would know it before an ear did. One account, if there were anyone to hold accounts: the surplus that gets subtracted is water, mostly, but water is not the only thing that arrives here from nowhere. Sometimes what arrives is a version of something that no other version will agree to. The reconciliation takes it. And what is left over — the un-agreed remainder, the part that was never settled and never dropped out of solution — goes on moving through the sedge, eighty meters wide, looking for something capable of noticing it.

Which would make it a mason too. Everything here settles discrepancy: the films settle it as stone, the snails unsettle it and let it be settled again, the eel settles it by drifting apart, the bird settles it by booming until two are one. And the hush settles the discrepancy of anything that could *hear*, because hearing is a way of being at odds with the roar, and the basin has been closing that argument for two hundred million years, patiently, one relaxed animal at a time. The deaf are not the survivors of the hush. They are its finished work.

It ignores the moss. It ignores the snail, the eel, the midge, the film. During the eleven seconds it also stops, all of it, because it moves by listening and a flat world has nothing in it to hear. Reconciliation is the truce. Everything alive here knows that, except the bird, which knows the other thing, and goes down.

---

So: who lives here.

A clone of moss that thinks the impossible is a dry season. A film that eats conclusions. A snail that keeps a corrupted ledger of the basin's arguments and reopens them by eating the page. An eel that is one animal in several places and has a piece missing. A wingless crowd that counts to two thousand eight hundred in the dark. A fungus shining at nothing, whose light gets kept. A bird with bone over its ears, feeding a chick in yesterday. A pocket of silence hunting for the sound of something feeling safe. And a transparent ribbon, thinner than a scale, that has had a hundred billion generations to perfect eleven seconds, and for whom this basin is a still and quiet place, endlessly, catastrophically interrupted by weather.

Come back to the pause, since it is the only moment they are all in the same world.

The fog lanes straighten. The tone comes up through the marl. Four hundred thousand waterfalls hold still. And in that flat instant the moss is clamping and throwing spores into a golden haze, and the films are taking their fraction, and the snails are spawning nacre, and the eel is finally one eel, and the bird is down in the peat with its bill in a gap, and the hush is stopped mid-sedge with nothing to hear, and a hundred million transparent animals are grazing, mating, laying, and sinking as fast as their fixed sequences will run — the busiest moment in the Long Settlement, thirty kilometers of glassy water, absolutely silent, absolutely full.

Then the water resumes. The adults shred. The eggs go down into the peat to wait. The pitch of the world drops by an amount too small to name, and out on a fresh lip somewhere a snail lays down a band.
