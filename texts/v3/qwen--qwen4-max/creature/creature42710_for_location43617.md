Begin with the obvious tenant, and watch the obvious tenant come apart in your hands.

The bats. But *the bats* is not an animal; it is a weather system with fur. So take one. She is eleven grams, a free-tail, forearm the length of a pencil stub, lips wrinkled like something left in water, ears shaped as if someone had thought hard about the collection of sound from a particular direction and then made the thought out of skin. Her tail slides in its sheath and works the membrane between her legs like a hand adjusting a sheet. There is a notch in the trailing edge of her left wing, healed pale, where a popped chert knuckle opened her four hundred nights ago; the membrane is living tissue and it closed itself, the way nothing else in this country closes anything. Her wing is a hand. She is hanging by her toes on a tendon lock that costs her no effort at all, so that sleep here is free, and she is four years old, which is old, and she has left through the same skylight something like fourteen hundred times, and she has never once been outside in daylight, because she is never outside in daylight.

She is not the resident. Keep her, though. She will be needed again at the end.

Before you dissolve her into the ceiling, notice that she has a name. Not one she was given — one she answers. Somewhere below her in the pelt, in a crèche where the pups are packed in a warm shivering carpet forty to a square decimeter, there is one animal that is hers, and among four hundred thousand animals in a roaring dark at ninety decibels she will find it, first by its call, then by its smell, at the end of a night's flying, and she will not make a mistake more than a few times in five weeks. This is the only identification in the country. It is the only proper noun. It is used constantly for thirty-five days and then it falls out of use forever, and neither of them will ever need it again, and the sound of it will have gone into the roar with everything else.

What she also has is an accent. The four provinces do not sound alike above the range of your ears. The pulse rates differ, the terminal buzzes differ, the way a bat drops its frequency to keep its returns out of a neighbor's band differs; there are bats here who hunt in silence and read other bats' echoes, thieves of a made thing, and there are bats who have learned to shout slightly wrong so the thieves cannot use them. A young animal does not know the room. It follows an older one out and back for a dozen nights and learns the route as a sequence of pressure-shapes: the throat of the second gallery, the hard bright ping of a chert knuckle, the fur-ceiling that reads as fur and moves, the cold column at the shaft where the air changes and the insects are. It learns this by ear, from somebody who learned it by ear. Sixty kilometers of tradition, held nowhere, written on nothing, transmitted mouth to ear at thirty strikes a second, and if you killed every bat in a province the tradition would be gone and the room would still be exactly as sharp as it was. Four islands of dialect in a country with no walls between them. A bat crossing from the third province to the first re-tunes in about a hundred wingbeats, the way you lower your voice in a different kind of room.

And she is inhabited. This is the part that takes the longest to arrive at, and then it does not stop arriving.

On her forearm, flattened sideways to slip through the fur, there are three wingless flies with legs like grappling hooks and eyes reduced to a rumor. They were born on her. Their mothers were born on her mother. They have never been off a bat, never touched stone, never been in the cave in the sense of being in a room — their entire universe is forty square centimeters of warm skin and the horizon is a fold of membrane, and it is a universe with weather in it, because when she flies the wind comes. On the trailing edge of her wing, in the thin vascular country where the skin is nearly transparent, there are mites that live only there and are found nowhere else on her body. In the cup of her left ear there is a mite that lives only in ears. Inside her there is a nematode with a life cycle that requires guano, which is to say a life cycle that requires the floor, which is to say the flies and the mites and the worms are all citizens of the same country and most of them have never seen it. She goes out every night into a world of moths and wind and stars. They stay home. Ten thousand years of home.

Now the floor, which is her second body.

The colony has two bodies and the air between them is the lung. The ceiling is the one that flies. The floor is the one that digests, and it digests outside itself, at thirty degrees, in a meter of warm felted dung that is not a heap but an organ. Beetles work the top few centimeters, the grazers and the shredders; below them the roaches, pale and unhurried, moving through it like slow fish; below them the mites in a density that makes the surface seem to rise and fall, and the springtails blooming and collapsing in patches like frost forming and going; and threading all of it the fungal felts, white and faintly sweet, and in the warm middle the bacteria that are the actual engine. Predatory beetles hunt the grazers. Pseudoscorpions hunt the mites and ride the flies to get from one district to another, holding on with both hands, commuting on a stranger. There is a springtail here that has been found in the guano of the second province and nowhere else in sixty kilometers of walked passage, which means the emptiness between provinces is a sea, and the four knots of unbearable life are an archipelago, and there are endemics on each island, and nobody has finished the list.

Stand in it and your footprint closes in eleven minutes. Put a dead bat on it and come back at the same hour tomorrow and there will be a faint discoloration and no bones. This is the thing to sit with: ten thousand years of a million animals dying at a steady rate, and not one skeleton. No grave, no bone bed, no skull in the sediment, nothing for a trowel. If you dug here you would find the present all the way down. The pile has no stratigraphy because it has no past; it is mixed and eaten and rebuilt at the rate it arrives, and the top of it is exactly as old as the bottom, which is to say about four days.

Some of the deaths have a shape. At the margin of the third roost province a centipede the length of your hand hangs from the ceiling by its last legs and takes a bat out of the air — reaches, folds, and is eating for two days afterward. In the breakdown at the base of the fourth shaft a snake lies in a socket between two blocks whose fracture faces would still fit together, and takes the pups that fall, which is a number, and the number is small. At one skylight a hawk has learned that there is no dusk here and hunts at eleven in the morning and at three in the afternoon and at four in the morning with equal expectation, and has been doing this for a season, and its parents did not do it. These are the only events with edges. Statistically they are nothing. The floor takes the leftovers overnight and the country does not record them.

Between the provinces live the ones for whom nothing happening is not a famine but simply the condition. A cricket on the ceiling of a dry fossil gallery, antennae four times his body length, singing into a room that hands his note back a half-second later; he cannot distinguish a rival from a wall, and so he answers himself, and adjusts, and answers again, and the only courtship in this country is a duet with a reflection. A pseudoscorpion in a chert crack that has not eaten in fourteen months and is in no distress, because distress requires an expectation. A whip spider flattened on a wall in the flying gallery, reading the air with legs it uses as antennae, taking a midge perhaps twice a month, and living twenty years, and being therefore one of the oldest individuals in the country and having nothing to show for it. These are not stragglers. This is most of the map. The overwhelming is four knots; the rest is this — animals of enormous patience distributed at one per hundred meters through a space the size of a county, each of them alone in a way that has no season and therefore no end and therefore no urgency.

At the eleven shaft-mouths, the starving beautiful ones. Moss on the wet breakdown, growing a millimeter a year. Algae in a green film exactly where the light lands and not a centimeter past it. Ferns with fronds too long and too thin, floppy, embarrassed-looking, doing their best with light that has fallen forty meters down a stone tube and arrived already spent. And the seedlings: white-stemmed, absurd, three meters of etiolated reaching with two leaves the color of weak tea, grown from a fig dropped by a pigeon nine years ago, alive, still climbing toward the mouth, never once properly green, never going to fruit. They are the only things in the country that grow, and growth is the one verb this place does not conjugate. Everything else is at the same rate. The fig is trying to have a future.

In the alcove off the second flying gallery, the gnat larvae hang their threads with their beads of mucus and burn their cold blue-green, and a midge comes to the light and is held. The larva will pupate and become a fly with no working mouthparts, and live four days, and mate, and die. This is the only creature here with a biography — a beginning, a middle, an end, a shape you could tell. Everything else has a rate instead of a story. The little stars are the only made light in the country and they are on for a few months and then that wardrobe-sized nation is dark for a while and then it is lit again, and this is the closest thing to a year that anything down here experiences.

Three levels below, the river people are not citizens. Blind shrimp with antennae longer than their bodies, a fish with no eyes reading the water with a line of pits along its flank, a crab the color of wet paper. They live in a different country entirely: theirs has weather. It floods. It has gravel bars that move, driftwood that arrives from the surface and rots, a flood line four meters up the wall with a date on it if you knew how to read it, bones sometimes, sediment, layers, a record. They know what year it is. They are the neighbors, and the stair down to them is the only way out of the perfect tense.

And there are the ones with lamps, who come a few times a decade and walk the sixty kilometers and give the chambers names. Their footprints close in eleven minutes. Their carbide smoke is eaten. The only durable thing they leave is a brass bolt in the wall and a line on a survey sheet, and the sheet is above ground, in a pocket, getting wet — which is where all the history of this place is kept, because none of it will stay down here. From inside, we are not observers. We are the weather. We are the only thing that ever changes.

So: who lives here.

Not the bats. The bats turn over completely every four years or so; every body in that ceiling has been replaced, over and over, a thousand times since the ice went; nothing in this country is old except the living of it. The tenant is the rate. The tenant is a throughput with a temperature of nine point six degrees and a voice at ninety decibels and a metabolism measured in tons per night, and it has been at home here, at this address, at this volume, without a single gap, since before the grass on the plateau was that grass. It has no artifacts because it does not need any; it is the artifact, remade continuously, and it is the only thing here that has ever been made. Its cells fly out and come back braided in a rope that runs both ways at once. Its gut is on the floor and eats its own falling. Its lung is the draft it makes by being warm. Its memory is in ears, passed mouth to ear, and would vanish in a night if the ears stopped, and the sharp scallops on the walls would not notice.

And it is also, right now, eleven grams, and warm, and coming in from the fourth skylight with a moth's worth of sugar in her blood, dropping her frequency to suit the accent of the province she was born in, finding a ceiling with no hole in it because the pelt closes over any gap in less than a wingbeat, hooking her toes into a neighbor's flank and folding her hands over her face. Somewhere below her, one animal is calling a sound that means only her. In five weeks it will stop meaning anything, and the roar will be exactly as loud.
