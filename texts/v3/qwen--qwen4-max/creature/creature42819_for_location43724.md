## The Residents

The commonest animal in the world has no front.

It is the size of a large dog and shaped like a decision that was never made: five-fold, pale, blunt, a disc of muscle with a ring of rasping teeth at the centre and no head anywhere on it. It has no face, so there is nothing to read. It has no eyes, so there is nothing to look out of. It has a nerve ring instead of a brain, and the ring's whole business is the coordination of a mouth with a circumference. If you turned it upside down it would not notice, and neither would you, because it has no upside.

It holds a hectare of wall. It has held that hectare for eleven years and will hold it for four more, and in all that time it will not travel more than a hundred metres from where it started, and it will cover, by crawling, something on the order of nine thousand kilometres.

The hectare is not a property of the animal. This is the first thing to understand about residence here. Nobody chose a territory size. The grazer sweeps its patch in a slow inward spiral, cropping the wall's inner face down to the day-old lignin, and the wall behind it begins again immediately, and the spiral is timed — not by the grazer, which cannot time anything, but by fifteen years of ancestors being slightly wrong — so that the animal arrives back at its starting point exactly as that starting point becomes edible. The hectare is the area of wall that regrows in the time it takes to come around. Make the grazer faster and its territory shrinks. It has been made faster. It is being made faster now, at a rate too small to see, and the plant is being made faster too, and the two accelerations are locked, and the hectare has not changed in a very long time.

At the edges, neighbours meet. Two discs, arriving from opposite directions at a boundary neither can see, press their margins together for about four seconds and separate. This happens at every edge of every territory on schedule, and it is the only contact between adults that ever occurs anywhere in this world. It is not a greeting and it is not a fight. It is a measurement. Each animal is checking that the other is still there, because a neighbour that has stopped is a vacancy, and a vacancy is the only thing in the world worth moving toward.

---

They are born in wounds.

A grazer at the end of its fifth year cuts a pit in its own territory, a shallow dish about the width of a hand, and lays into it, and then does the one thing it will ever do that is not rasping: it stops. It sits over the pit and does not eat for nine days, and the wall — which grows whether or not it is being eaten, which is the whole engine of this place — grows up around the clutch and over it and seals it. The young develop inside the healing. They are incubated by the world's attempt to close a hole. The temperature of the nursery is nine and four-tenths degrees. The light in it is piped daylight, filtered through a centimetre of new wood, and it is the colour of the inside of a hand held over a torch.

On about the eleventh day the seal fails, because the young inside it have been eating outward, and they come out into a chamber the size of an inland sea, and they are three millimetres long, and they drift.

This is the only journey anyone takes. The adults are fixed; the couriers are fixed to their circuits; the fans are cemented; the fliers are fixed to their loops. Only the newly hatched are loose in the water, and they are loose in it for between two and forty days, and everything in this world that could be called a predator is waiting for them, and nothing else. There is nothing else to eat. Every adult here is occupied, defended by its own function, replaced at exactly the rate it is removed. The only unattached material in the entire massif is the young.

They navigate by the field. Along every septum the plant holds a few hundred volts across a wall, and the map of that field is fixed and legible, and a grazer's body is a conductivity shadow on it — a smudge, a distortion, a *something here*. A drifting juvenile reads the map for a smudge that has recently gone faint. That is a vacancy. It swims toward the absence of a neighbour, and settles, and begins the spiral, and fifteen years later cuts a pit.

A fixed fraction never find one. They drift until they fail, and the fans take them.

---

Inside the grazer there is a piece of the world.

It cannot digest lignin. Nothing animal can. What it does instead is carry, lining its gut, a living graft of plant tissue, taken in at hatching from the wall of its own nursery — so that every grazer is born swallowing a mouthful of the thing it will spend its life eating, and keeps that mouthful alive inside itself forever, warm, fed, dividing. The graft breaks down its own kin. The grazer absorbs the products. It is not a stomach so much as a piece of the factory installed in a mobile housing and set to work on the factory.

When the animal wears out — and they wear out, the tooth-ring finally failing to regrow fast enough, the spiral falling behind, the wall gaining a few microns a day on its occupant until the occupant is simply *inside* it — the graft does not stop. It goes on dividing. It is the last thing in the world that is still hungry. Over about thirty hours the grazer is converted, from the inside, into the material it spent fifteen years removing, and the wall closes over the place where it was, and there is no body, no shell, no shed tooth, no stain.

Nothing is buried here. Nothing is even left.

---

The fans are the same animal at an earlier stage of the same argument, or they were, once; now they are a separate lineage that gave up the spiral. They are cemented into the throats in ranks, facing the flow, and they are shaped exactly like the flow, which is to say they were not designed and neither were they sculpted by anything with an opinion. They take the fine drift: shed tooth, sloughed epithelium, the fragments of failed juveniles, the pollen the fliers drop, the corpses of couriers that missed a turn. They are the reason there is no sediment. They are the reason there is no past. Every archive this world could possibly have laid down is eaten within about six hours of being deposited, by an animal that has never moved.

The couriers are long and silver and do not stop. Their routes are closed loops through the chambers, and the loops are not remembered, not learned, not taught; they are the paths a body of that flexibility and that buoyancy takes in that current, and a juvenile courier finds its route by being carried along it once and thereafter matching the water's shear against its own flank. They carry the plant's chemistry — signal molecules that would take a week to diffuse a hundred kilometres and take an afternoon this way — and they carry it incidentally, in the mucus of their flanks, having picked it up at one septum and rubbed it off at another. They have no mouth worth the name. They have no destination. A courier taken off its circuit and released in still water will swim a widening spiral until it finds a shear that matches, and then it will be back on a route, and it will not be the same route, and this will make no difference to anything.

The fliers never land. They skim the canopy's underside at speed and take their food from the stomatal crypts without touching, the way a swift drinks; they mate in the air; they inject their eggs into the leaf-shingle in a strike lasting a fifth of a second and are gone before the tissue closes. They sleep one hemisphere at a time. They are the only animals here that cross between mouths, and therefore the only reason the massif is one population and not nine hundred, and they do not know this, and it is the largest thing anyone here does.

When a flier fails it falls into the intake column, which is the only funeral available, and goes down without splash into water already moving the same way.

---

There are two liars.

The first is a grazer that has stopped being electrically visible. Its body has acquired a conductivity that matches the water it displaces — not perfectly, but to within the resolution of a three-millimetre juvenile drifting past at forty centimetres a second. It holds a full hectare. It rasps, it maintains its spiral, it meets its neighbours at the boundary and presses and separates. But on the map it reads as a faint place. It reads as *nobody here*. And the young come in, looking for the vacancy that will be their whole life, and settle onto a wall that is already occupied, and are absorbed at the margin of a mouth that has been waiting eleven days for exactly one of them.

It is not hunting. It has no capacity for pursuit. It is a hole in the only language anybody speaks, and the language has one word, and the word is *empty*, and it says it by being shaped like nothing.

The second is not a grazer at all, and it is the strangest thing living here.

---

It holds station in a duct junction, and it is the only animal in this world that swims hard in order to stay still. Everything else is either cemented, drifting, or going with the flow. This one beats against it, continuously, for its entire adult life, to remain in one place: downstream of a stretch of plumbing where the tension is marginal and the threads let go often.

It is waiting for the crack.

Ten million times a second across the massif a water column snaps, and the sound of it is a rifle-shot in a room a hundred kilometres wide, and the plant floods the void and restores the tension before the sound has finished crossing the chamber. But the shock has already gone through the water, and in the water there were juveniles — three millimetres, drifting, delicate — and some fraction of them are stunned. They come downstream in a pulse, a few seconds behind the noise.

The animal's latency is nine milliseconds. It is the fastest nervous system in the world, in a world where nothing else needs one. Every other ear here is a high-pass filter that has given up on the roar; this one is tuned to the single event that the roar cannot predict. It flinches, dashes, takes two or three, returns to station, and resumes beating against the current.

And here is what it has that nothing else has: an interval. Between the crack and the pulse there are two or three seconds in which something is *about to happen*. It is oriented toward them. It has, in the thinnest possible sense, an expectation — a state that is about a moment which has not arrived. It is the only creature in this ocean that has a future, and the future is two and a half seconds long, and it is always the same future, and it arrives.

It is also the tolerance in the machine. Hatching and vacancy are matched rates, but they are matched the way two flows are matched, with slack, with noise, with a difference that has to go somewhere. This animal eats the difference. Both liars do. They are the error term, made flesh, and the reason a system running at full capacity forever never jams is that there is something living in the gap, feeding on the amount by which the world is momentarily wrong.

---

So: who lives here.

You will want a list, and the list will be misleading, because every entry on it is a moving part of something that does not move. The grazer is the mechanism by which the chambers stay open. The fan is the mechanism by which there is no history. The courier is the plant's nervous system, borrowed and given a body. The flier is the plant's gene flow with wings. The liar is the pressure regulator. None of them is *in* the world in the way a fox is in a wood. They are the world's opposite numbers, the counter-processes it must run against itself in order to keep standing at this exact setting, and they have been recruited into that work so completely that there is no remainder — no behaviour, no surplus, no idle hour, nothing an animal does here that is not load-bearing.

And they are still animals. That is the part that does not resolve. The grazer has a life: a pit, a seal, eleven days of dark, forty days of drift, a faint place on a map, fifteen years of spiral, four seconds at a boundary repeated some millions of times, a failing tooth, thirty hours of being eaten by its own stomach lining. It is a biography. It has a shape. It happens four hundred trillion times a second and it is always the same one, and it is happening now, a hundred kilometres that way, to something the size of a dog with no face, which has just come around again to the place where it started and found it, on schedule, edible.

Somewhere nearer, in a duct, a thread lets go.

For nine milliseconds nothing in this world is happening. Then something is about to. Then it has. Then the water is nine and four-tenths degrees, and the light is the colour of daylight, and the roar goes on being silence, and the animal resumes holding itself perfectly still against a current that has never once, in all the time there has been, gone anywhere else.
