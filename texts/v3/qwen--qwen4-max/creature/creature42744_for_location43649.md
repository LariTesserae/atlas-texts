Begin with the ones who are also the walls, because there is no separating them.

Under the green light the wet quarter wears a nap, a film, a slickness you would call algae and be roughly right. It is a pasture of things too small to have shapes worth naming, and it does the only chemistry that matters here: it breathes the carbon out of the water, and the water, unburdened, drops its stone. The balconies, the lace, the frozen cauliflower, the whole apricot architecture — none of it is built. All of it is exhaled. The masons of this city are a smear of green that has never once intended anything, and the city is what their breathing leaves behind. If you scraped them off, the hollow would still get its water and would still, slowly, coat itself, but it would coat itself flat and stupid, like a kettle. Everything worth looking at down here is the difference between water that has passed through something alive and water that hasn't.

The moss knows this better than anyone. Each fat cushion sits on a plinth of its own making, and the stone climbs its stems as fast as it can, and the moss grows up as fast as the stone climbs. A cushion is not a thing sitting on a rock. It is a thing running, very slowly, to stay at the surface, and it has been running since before the fig was tall. Cut one out and you get a green crown on a column of everything it has ever been: last year's moss, and the year before, held upright, held perfectly, going down into the wall like a rope into a well. The cushions stand a hand's breadth apart because the water divides that way, each with its own irrigation, each with clean pavement between. Lush and far apart. There is no crowding in the wet quarter and no generosity either.

The snails eat the masons. This is the one subtraction in the hollow, and it is negligible, and it is healed. A snail works the film with a tongue like a file, and if you put your ear to the wall you can hear a hundred of them at it, a rain three valleys off. Behind them the stone closes over the grazed patches within a season, the way a mouth closes over a bitten cheek. And a snail repairs its shell the same way the hollow repairs everything: by adding. It cannot take a damaged edge away and start clean. It can only lay more material over the break, thicker, blunter, until an old snail is a lumpy scarred thing, all boss and bulge, a record of every accident it has survived, carried on its back, still grazing. The city in miniature, going at a snail's pace, which is the correct pace.

They cross the plazas only when the water comes. A snail cannot make that journey on a dry floor in the time available, and the ones who tried are in the pavement now, foot-down, shells intact, facing the direction they were going. There is a street of them under the main seep, all pointed toward the water. In the grey quarter on the east wall, where the channel moved two years ago, the dead are pointed toward a place that no longer runs. They went on trying. Nobody here can revise.

The crabs are the exception to the rule that nothing moves. They came down the water road, which runs one way, from the chamber upstream, where their people live white and eyeless in the dark and have done for longer than the fig. These have eyes and use them badly. They are pale in the way of things that have not needed color in a very long time and have not yet been asked to give it up entirely. At night they come out over the plazas and take the fallen fig leaves apart, and this is the whole of the hollow's sanitation, freight service, and mercy: a crab can eat a leaf before the water claims it, and that is the only way anything here escapes being kept forever. Everything the crabs finish is gone. Everything they drop is architecture. Their middens are the archive, and their laziness is the archive, and a leaf lying half-shredded at dawn with a pale claw still resting on it is a document in the making.

They are patient beyond any useful meaning of the word. A crab will hold a leaf motionless for ten minutes because something shifted on the wall above it, and then resume, and lose nothing by the delay. They have all night. They have every night.

The frogs are furniture that speaks. Sit still in a basin through the dry hours and you are a stone with a wet eye: limbs folded, skin gone the color of the floor, breathing at the minimum rate that keeps the account open. Nothing about them suggests an animal. Then the film swells from beneath, lifts them, and the whole hollow's public life happens at once — courtship, insult, boundary, grievance, all of it compressed into forty minutes, all of it conducted at a volume that would be unremarkable in a farmyard. A frog here does not measure its life in days. It measures it in visits. An old female in the third basin has spoken perhaps three hundred times, and between those times she has done essentially nothing, and this is not a hardship; it is the shape of a life. She lays her eggs in the surge. The tadpoles race the drain. The basins that keep an inch between visits are the nursery; the ones that empty are the tomb, and there is a seam in the west wall, a fine dark line of punctuation, made of everything that did not finish in time.

The crickets keep the same law, and the law is not a custom. Once in a while a young male calls in the quiet — one syllable, misplaced, the ordinary error of the young — and the gecko's head comes around. Nothing here learns from that. Nothing here can. It is simply that the ones who could wait are the ones still present, generation on generation, and the hollow's silence is not discipline and not fear. It is a sieve. It is what is left.

The gecko is an ear with hands attached. It sits at the margin of the coin's path at midday, taking the one warm hour, tail blunt from three regrowths, eyes enormous and mostly idle, and it does not hunt with them. It listens to the wall. It is the reason for the quiet and it is also the hollow's earliest news: it hears the swallow in the root-pipes a half-minute before the water shows, and lifts its head, and everything in the basin that has a body capable of tension goes tense. The racer is not a resident. It comes down a root now and then, a commuter, a tax, and it hunts the same way, and there is nothing to be done about it except be furniture.

Which is why the forty minutes are not only permission. They are cover. In the racket the gecko is deaf, the racer is deaf, the whole listening apparatus of the hollow is jammed by a small dense ordinary clucking, and so everything dangerous gets done then. The snails cross. The crickets molt, soft and unable to run, hanging out of their old skins in plain view. The frogs pair. The wasps come home heavy. The crabs haul their largest loads. The city has no rush hour. It has a rush, unscheduled, announced by a swallow, and then it freezes with all its business half-done, and holds.

The wasps are the customs house. Nothing down here can make protein out of stone and green light, and the wasps go up the slot to the scouring plain and bring it back: caterpillars, stung into stillness, stacked in grey paper cells under the south overhang. Alive. Fresh. Not going anywhere. Read the second rule of the hollow again and look at that larder — everything that stays is kept — and consider a caterpillar that will never be a moth, held perfectly, for weeks, in a paper room in a stone city under a fig. The wasps are not cruel. They are only the most literal citizens. The female argues at the entrance of her cell during the surge and works mute the rest of the time, and you can see that she is saving it up.

The bats are the tenements, and the tenements are a gift of the plumbing: the fig's grip coffered the ceiling into a hundred pockets, and the pockets became deeds, and now hundreds of animals hang in exact spaced solitude, none touching, each in its own room, in a city where the wet quarter's rich keep a hand's breadth of clean plaza between cushions and the ceiling's poor keep exactly the same. They are the daily commuters. They see the plain every night; nothing else here sees it at all except the wasps and the swiftlets at the mouth. They come back and drop the plain in the dark, and that is the second import, and the moss is fat on it, and the seams of it run black through the wall beside the ash and the leaf-fall. A bat that dies gripping does not fall. It hangs, and the film finds it, and the coat comes, and it goes on hanging inside the stone with its wings put away, at ceiling height, in the strata, while beside it the living continue to arrive and take up their rooms.

The swiftlets are borderers and will be evicted first. They nest with spit glued to tufa, so they too are masons, and they need the slot, and when the slot goes they go. The fern is one, in a crack, with no other of its kind anywhere in the hollow; it has been throwing spores for years and every one of them that landed wet is in the wall now, a fine dust of unrealized ferns laid down in courses. There is a spider at the lip whose web is entombed beneath her, and the one before that beneath it, and she builds each new one on the roof of the last, so that she is climbing, all her life, a ladder made of her own finished work.

Near the mouth, where a boot came down once in soft cheese-stone, there is a footprint. It is filling. Nothing here can preserve a wound as a wound; the coat goes on evenly and the concavity shallows, and in a decade it will be flush, and in a century it will be a low swell, the way a tree turns an injury into a boss. It is the only human resident and it is slowly becoming a lump.

And upstream, in the chamber that fills and siphons, the white blind crabs go on with their business in the dark, and every surge sends a few of their young down the road that only runs one way. They arrive already suited to what is coming. The hollow is being stocked, quietly, for its own future, by a country it has never seen. The lid will close on the light, not on the road; water does not stop, and a bat can pass a gap the width of a thumb, and the fig will keep making doors the way it makes everything, by dying in places — a root rots, a bore opens, the traffic continues through a hole with no daylight in it at all.

So what lives here is not really a list. It is one slow thing wearing everybody: stone for a body, moss for lungs, a siphon hours away for a heart, forty minutes of ordinary racket for a voice, and a memory that is nothing but courses — leaf-fall, ash, shell, wing bone, tadpole, spit, spit, spit. Its floor is ribbed like a palate. Its air is at the temperature of a mouth. It is warm and wet and mostly silent and it speaks when it is spoken to by rain falling somewhere it will never know about, and it is building, from the inside, at the speed of a snail's repair, the bone that will close over its one eye.

It will lose the light and keep the hearing. Everything in it is already arranged for that: the pale, the patient, the earless green, the ones who arrive blind. They are not waiting for the dark. They are waiting for the water, which is a different thing, and comes more often, and is on its way now.
