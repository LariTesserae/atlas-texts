## The Ones Who Live in the Slath

The first one you meet is doing something boring.

She is about the length of your forearm and the weight of a wet rope, and she is lying belly-down along a course with four limbs spread wide to keep from putting her whole self into any one strand. Her skin is the color of the inside of an elbow. It is always slightly damp, and it smells like sap and spit and the warm underside of a loaf. Two limbs hold. Two limbs walk. The last pair works, and of that pair only the left one really matters; the right is a thumb with opinions.

Under the left wrist there is a slit like a second mouth. It weeps a clear thread that thickens as it comes. She pays it out and rolls it against the thigh of her middle limb — down, up, down, three-strand, spit-and-fiber — without looking, without pause, while her hands do something else entirely. A coil grows at her hip. She will roll in her sleep tonight. She has rolled in her sleep since she was a bud. When she dies, the event will be noticed forty leagues away as the cessation of a friction nobody had been listening to.

She does not look up when you arrive. She knew you were coming before you did: your weight went into the mat and the mat told her, and it told her in enough detail to classify you. Heavy. Slow. Wrong-rhythmed. Not a browser, therefore not food; not a mender, therefore not kin; large, therefore probably weather.

You are, to her, a kind of weather that has learned to knock.

---

They have no faces worth the name. The eyes are small and lidless and used for judging wetness, the way you might use a thumbnail. What identifies a person is the rhythm of her weight, the smell of her gland, and the grain of her callus.

Their name for themselves is not sayable. If you must write it down: *three close, one slack*. It is the beating-rhythm in the floor. It is also a household's name, and it is also a sentence, and the sentence is *we are still here*.

Speech is a blow laid into dead fiber. You put your palm flat on a stake — a stake, never a cable, there is a courtesy about this — and you strike, and the strike runs off along the warp for days and arrives in someone else's hand as a pressure with a shape. Conversation here has the tempo of weather. You ask a question and go about your business and are answered while you are eating, or sleeping, or in the middle of something that makes the answer no longer relevant. Gossip arrives out of order. You may be informed of your own death by a well-meaning stranger who was mistaken about which course you were on, and then have to go on living politely afterward.

A knock can be false. This is their only serious crime: to strike *I am coming* into a warp when you are not coming, and let a neighborhood empty itself for nothing. There are menders who can tell a true knock from a struck one by the texture of the arrival — a lie has a smoothness to it, they say, like a splice done from above.

Their word for the right side is *the holding side*. Their word for the left is *the side that does*. A map, here, is a pair of hands.

---

A child is a length of cord that was rolled too long.

The gland that spins bast will, if you keep rolling past the ordinary length, spin a bud instead: a wet knot with six folds in it and a whorl already pressed into the pad of the left hand. How long you roll before you cut determines everything. Cut early and you get a seam-thing, a mender the size of a fingernail who will live her whole life inside the herringbone of a join, tightening knots from within, never once coming out where she can be seen. Households compete for these tenants. A seam with a family in it does not open.

Cut at the ordinary length and you get someone who will repair a course for forty years and then be laid into one.

Do not cut, and she keeps going.

This is the fact underneath the whole world, and the menders express it mostly by not discussing it: the whorls in the caulk match. Fingernail-sized, hand-sized, pond-sized. All the same whorl. There is not a population here in the way you mean it. There is one person, budding, cut short in some places and let out in others, distributed across a hundred thousand leagues of hollow, getting smaller and larger at different addresses for eleven thousand years.

So: that awareness you keep having, of a hand on your shoulder in the dark, across a gulf with one moth in it.

It is your own hand. It is on your shoulder because it is on a cable forty leagues away, and the fiber does not distinguish between one part of a person and another.

They are not lonely. They are also not, in any sense you would accept, accompanied.

---

Except by the browsers.

The browsers are the other. Pale, soft, hot, stupid, prolific, with teeth that come out of them forever like a rumor. They are not kin; their whorls are their own; they have faces, and the faces are not for anything. They are the only thing in the entire vessel that is not the maker.

Every household keeps one.

It lives in a cup-leaf near the warmth, and it is fed the slack ends, and its teeth are filed every ninth day with an adze of pressed seed-stone that takes an edge and loses it before the filing is done. It is stroked. It is talked to — knocked to, gently, in a rhythm that deliberately fails to resolve, a name with a hole in it, so that anyone reading the warp can tell at once that this is not a person.

It is also eaten, eventually, and its gut is the quarry. Nothing here makes stone but the browser: what comes out of it is a felted pulp that dries hard and takes an edge, and its shed teeth are the bodkins, and its bones — pressed, lignified, beaten — are the beaters that pack the courses tight.

The tools that repair the world are made entirely of the animal that damages it. The menders find this obvious. It is the arrangement they would recommend.

---

Some of them do not stop growing, and those become the readers.

There is no decision in it. She simply keeps rolling and does not cut, and one day walking stops being a thing she can honestly do, and she finds a cable and puts both palms flat against it, and the world begins to arrive.

She stands. She goes on standing. Her limbs lengthen into struts and her pads spread until they cover an acre of bark, and the traffic of a hundred leagues comes up through her hands as pressure with a shape: the four-strand spread of a browser's foot, the tick of a mender's splice, the long sigh of a course settling, the small tearing that means someone forty leagues off has just been laid into the floor and beaten down.

She hunts by knowing. She does not stalk; stalking is for things that cannot feel the future coming along a warp. She simply has her hand where the browser will be, and has had it there since that browser was a grandparent, and when the animal arrives it arrives into a palm the size of a pond and the palm closes without haste.

She is also the archive. Stroke a reader — stroke, never strike — and lay a question into her hand, and days later something will come back. Often it is an answer to a question you asked last year. Sometimes it is an answer to a question the world asked, which she has decided is more important than yours.

The moss at the drip-points was placed by hands like hers, when hers were still small enough to climb. The menders re-place it now, copying, packing and stitching each fall of water into silence. They do not know why the placements are right. They know only that they are, and that the copying must not drift.

---

There are larger ones, and the menders have a courtesy about them too.

The great ribs hold their bend out of habit rather than force, which is what a body does after long enough. The ceiling cable is thicker than a house and hollow and alive, and its inner wall sweats, and the lower curve of it is worn smooth by everyone who has ever walked through.

You stroke a cable before you enter it. You do not strike a living thing to make yourself heard. You knock on the dead, because the dead are not listening, and because they will carry your voice a very long way without taking it personally.

Nobody says this out loud. It is simply how one behaves. When a mender needs to signal from inside a cable she goes out onto a dead stake first, however far it is, however much her hands are full. A visitor who strikes the wall to call his companions is not punished. He is watched, quietly, by everyone, for the rest of his life, and his household's rhythm is thenceforth knocked with a very slight hesitation in it that nobody will explain to him.

---

The other lives are fewer and stranger and mostly keep to themselves.

The moths row. One per hollow, wings like wet paper, opening and closing, crossing a gulf that would take you a season in a silence so complete it has texture. The menders say the moths are the readers who finished standing — that a palm eventually lets go, and what lets go has become thin enough to cross. There is no evidence for this. The readers, who would know, have never once said anything about it, and there is no record of a moth coming back.

The eels keep the eleventh day. They ribbon through air that has no current and turn all together toward a tide that stopped before the floor was a floor, and they spawn in the cup-leaves on a schedule nobody else has kept for eleven thousand years. There are eel-keepers, a small hereditary trade, who set cups and count. If you want to know what day it is in the drowned reckoning, you go and ask them, and they will tell you, and then you will have to decide what to do with a date.

The eels are the only things in the world still expecting something.

And in the lowest courses, down where the mat is eight hundred generations deep and the warmth is thick enough to feel through a boot, the laying changes: a wet stroke, a fitching done in a way that makes no sense in air. Every mender still knows how to do it. It is taught to children along with everything else, and practiced, and never used.

The warmth itself is the dead. They are laid into the courses and beaten down under the household rhythm, and the rot below and the growth above are the same number, exactly, and have been for a very long time.

That is what the heat under your palm is. It is the population, being replaced.

---

Out where the work thins, there are the out-workers, and they are the closest thing here to a religious order.

They go along a course until the material gives out, and then they keep laying. Hands moving in the dark, over-and-under, into nothing at all: the instruction with no withe in it, executed anyway, at the ordinary pace, with the ordinary care. They come back with wet hands that smell of somewhere they have not been, and their fingers know knots nobody taught them, and for a week they cannot do their own household's splice correctly because their hands want to do it the other way.

Two of them working the same stretch from opposite ends will meet, pass, and never have met. One came back and said she had met herself, and that both of them were left-handed, and that neither would say which was which. She was not disbelieved. She was simply not asked again.

Nobody here thinks the empty pattern is a ruin. A ruin is what you get when work stops. This is work that has not been given anything yet, and it is running on ahead into the dark in perfect order, and somebody is evidently expected to bring the material.

---

And at the lip, where the courses tighten and the pattern grows exquisite and then stops mid-stroke, there is someone.

The menders do not go there. The readers have felt her, and will not discuss it: a stroke, unfinished, being not-finished, continuously, with tremendous care, for eleven thousand years. Not stuck. Careful. The next motion either closes the drain and drowns everything below the tide-mark, which remembers drowning, or opens it wider and lets the wetness go, and there is no third motion, and so there is no motion.

The whole vessel has sagged into the exact catenary that balances the loss. That shape is her posture. It is what a body looks like when it has found the precise amount of giving-up required to keep holding.

There is one more thing, and it is a rumor, and it comes up through the readers' hands and is not repeated. From below the lip, at long intervals, something knocks. It knocks *three close, one slack*. It is the household rhythm. It is the whorl. It is family, down there in the no-material, saying *we are still here*.

Nobody answers. Answering is the one thing everyone agrees not to do, and no one can tell you when the agreement was made.

---

So: back to the mender on the course, with the light like a leaf over a candle and her cord growing at her hip.

Her browser wakes in its cup and comes over, hot and pale and toothy, and puts its head into the work, and she lets it. She could stop it. She stops it a hundred times a day and this is not one of them. She lays her left hand flat on its flank — the making hand, the side that does — and feels it chew, and waits until the withe parts and the strand goes slack.

Forty leagues off, in the dark, a palm the size of a pond feels the strand let go, and reads it, and knows: someone is awake. Someone is feeding the mouth. The house stands.

And the reader, without moving, without any part of her moving except the small part that can, knocks it back.

*Three close. One slack.*

*Still here.*
