Begin with the smallest, because the first claim about this place — that it is empty — is a claim made at the wrong scale.

The joints will not take a knife blade. They will take a thread. Between the packed grains of the flour there is pore space, a labyrinth of channels a twentieth of a millimeter across, running down through the fill between cobbles for thirty, forty centimeters before the fill goes anoxic and sour. It is not shelter in any sense a fish would recognize. It has no rooms. But it is a volume, it is saturated, it is full of surfaces, and it is inhabited by something on the order of a hundred thousand animals per square meter of pavement, all of them together weighing less than the diatom film they are crawling under.

Threadworms, mostly. Ostracods like animated punctuation. Harpacticoid copepods built like grains of rice with opinions, and the small bristled things that have no common name because nobody has ever needed to point at one. They do not swim. At their size water is not a fluid but a syrup, a medium with no coasting in it: stop pushing and you stop instantly, in place, forever. They climb the labyrinth grain by grain, mowing the bacteria off the silt, and when the film above them is stripped they eat the flour itself, or rather they eat what is living on the flour, which is always something.

And here is the thing that revises everything: the storm does not reach them.

A benthic storm re-lays a hundred thousand cobbles the size of fists. It does not excavate a pore twenty microns wide. The avalanche passes over their heads like weather over a cellar. Down in the joints the great violence of the plain arrives only as a change of menu — a fresh delivery of oxygen, a burial of the old film, a sudden abundance of dead diatoms ground into the fill. They experience the ninth day as a meal. The residents of this world are sorted not by strength or by armor but by whether they are small enough to be beneath the notice of the thing that destroys everything. There is a size below which the plain is gentle, and almost everything alive here is under it.

---

Above them, in the flour itself, there is a population that never touches the floor at all.

This is the second revision. The nepheloid layer is not weather with animals accidentally in it. It is the largest habitat on the plain, twenty meters thick where the ground is a single skin, and it is where the amphipods are — the uncountable ones, the invisible ones, drifting and swimming in the grey, eating the falling snow while it falls. They intercept the bloom from four hundred kilometers north *in transit*. They are the reason so little of the snow reaches the bottom intact: it has been eaten twice on the way down, once in the clear water and again in the dust.

They live in the ceiling. When the storm thickens the layer and drops it onto the stones, they go down with it, and for a night they are benthic, crawling in the dark among cobbles bigger than their whole world, and then the layer lifts and they rise with it and are pelagic again, and none of this is a migration to them because they never moved relative to anything. The habitat moved. They stayed in it.

They are the plain's true nomads and also its most faithful residents, and the distinction turns out to mean nothing here.

---

Now the ones who own something, and can therefore lose it.

Under the two or three boulders that leave a genuine void, the brittle stars. A void is a lease. It exists because a boulder is heavy enough to have the right to remain, and it will end when the boulder is undermined or the scour fills its shadow, and until then it is the only address on the plain that a body can put itself *inside* rather than onto. The brittle stars know this in the way that animals know things: they are not sedentary. They walk the mosaic at night, arm over arm, testing, and when a void fails they go looking, and sometimes they find another and sometimes they are simply out on the open pavement in the returning light, flat and visible and mottled and gone by morning.

On the big stones of the western sector — the meter-wide ones the megaripples do not reach, that shift once in a decade instead of once in nine days — there is a bryozoan that has solved the rolling problem by refusing to have an up.

It encrusts the whole stone. It grows around the equator and over the poles and underneath, a continuous crust of tiny boxes, and when the stone is overturned the colony does not die; the part that was feeding is now against the ground and suffocates, and the part that was against the ground is now in the water and begins to feed, and the colony resumes at the same size. It has been doing this for a long time. Cut it open and it is banded: pale growth, a dead interruption, pale growth, another interruption. Forty bands. Forty overturnings. Four hundred years of storms, recorded in order, with their sequence intact.

It is the only stratigraphy in three days' travel. It is a document, and it is written on the one thing in the region that keeps its position long enough to be written on, and it will be destroyed by the storm that finally moves that stone, and there is nothing here that reads.

At the scarp lip, the coralline. Dull rose, a few square meters, a millimeter out and a millimeter back. It is not growing; it is *persisting*, which is a different verb and the correct one. It has the same relation to its own body that the plain has to its mosaic: an enormous expenditure that produces no increase, a form held at the exact rate at which it is being taken away. It may be a thousand years old. It is the only resident with an age worth stating. Everything else here is either too small to have a history or too recently arrived to have earned one.

---

Most of what lives here does not live here. It arrives.

The diatoms fall out of a bloom that happened in sunlight, in a country the plain has no access to; the film on the cobbles is not a local product but a delivery, and it is guaranteed, and it will come again after every stripping, and this reliability is the only reason anything else is possible. The kelp comes from a coast sixty kilometers away and lies in the swale for a week, and in that week the flour boils. Amphipods come down out of the ceiling in numbers that make the water above the blades look granular. Snails arrive from nowhere. A town forms — a real one, dense, noisy in the chemical sense, mating and eating and converting a sunk forest into bodies — and at the end of the week the blades are lace, and the town disperses upward into the dust, and the swale is scoured flat in a night and there is no deposit, no midden, no bone, no evidence.

But there are more amphipods that year. The manna is not stored in the world. It is stored in the population, which is the only ledger the plain permits.

The crab is a resident in a way nothing else is: it has a bearing. It crosses the plain in a season, walking into the flow at an angle it does not reconsider, over megaripples and gravel trains and the old river channel, and the mosaic re-lays itself under its feet twice a hundred times in the crossing and it does not notice, because a route is not a thing on the ground. A route is a thing in an animal. The crab is carrying the only map in the region and the map is of somewhere else.

The shark passes high above the ceiling, in clear water, in light. Nothing on the pavement sees it. It cannot be seen through twenty meters of flour; it is an event in a neighboring country. The plain's biggest animal is not a resident and not even a rumor. It is weather with a spine.

---

And the fish, which is the only thing here that has to *find* something.

Seventy centimeters, heavy-headed, thick-lipped, mottled in liver-pink and smoke-grey so exactly that it is invisible until it moves and then it is only a movement. Its lip is worn pale on one side. Two of its crushing teeth are broken and the replacements are coming in behind them, crooked. It is old — older than the position of the anvil, certainly; possibly older than the last three positions of the anvil.

It rises sometimes. Not to the surface, but to the ceiling, and hangs beneath that grey lit undersurface where the snow is visible as snow, where there is distance and direction and a source. It is the only resident that has ever been in the light rather than under it. Then it goes back down into the dark and quarters the mosaic in lanes.

The lanes are the part that matters. When the corner is where it was, the fish goes to it directly, and the whole business takes a minute: the clam set against the edge, the sideways jerk, three knocks, a pause, two more, the meat, and the shell dropped open on the stones. But after a great storm the corner is gone — buried under a hand's breadth of re-laid pavement, or turned, or shifted three meters into a company of identical stones — and then the fish searches. It goes over the ground in parallel runs. It picks up a rounded cobble and strikes it and gets nothing, and drops it, and tries the next. It will do this for a month. It eats urchins and gets thin. And then one day it turns a stone over with its mouth and there is an edge under it, and the plain has one sound again.

There may be another fish in the western sector. The plain is three days across and a fish is one fish wide; two could spend their lives here and never meet. If there is another, it has the same habit and nobody taught it, and when it dies the habit dies with it and no one is informed.

This is the last thing to understand about who lives here. Everything else on the plain is renewed from outside — the film from the north, the amphipods from the water column, the algae from its own patient margin, the voids from the next storm. All of it is re-supplied. Only one thing on this entire surface is generated locally, and it is not a body or a shell or a deposit. It is a way of doing something, held in a single animal, requiring a specific stone that the world will not hold still.

The coralline can be chipped to nothing and grow back. The bryozoan's four hundred years can be erased and another stone will be encrusted. The threadworms are beneath the notice of violence and will outlast every storm that ever comes.

The knock can stop.

---

After the front passes, the flour settles over four days. The ceiling lifts. A grey non-light comes down on a mosaic that is new in every particular and identical in every respect, and in the joints the threadworms find that the menu has changed, and on ten thousand stones the film begins, sent as always from a place with sun.

And somewhere out in the lanes, a heavy head moves over the cobbles, turning them, one at a time, looking for the only corner in the world.
