## First, the ones you see

The carriers are the least strange thing here, which is why you notice them first and understand them last.

Surveyors called them *camels*, which is wrong in every particular except the silhouette. They are not related to anything you know. They have six legs and use four for walking and two for standing still, which they do often, in the middle of a street, for no reason anyone has been able to determine. The hump is not fat. It is a bone-and-cartilage saddle that keeps the throat sac from swinging when they walk, because a swinging sac sloshes, and sloshing wastes energy, and energy is heat, and heat is vapor, and vapor is loss. Everything in this basin has been designed by that sentence.

The sac itself is a folded organ under the jaw, translucent when full, and you can see the water move in it. Forty liters. It is not a stomach. Nothing is digested there. The water enters through the mouth and leaves through the mouth and in between it sits in a living bag being carried down a glassy tunnel at four kilometers an hour, and the animal doing the carrying has no ability to vomit. It cannot empty itself. It can only be emptied — by a receiving mouth at a transfer station, or by a predator, or by dying.

An empty carrier is a terrible thing to watch. They do not panic in the way you'd expect. They *search*, methodically, insanely, going along the walls with the tongue out, tasting the plaster for the ghost of damp, and then they go to the sleeping stacks and stand over the grazers and lick the corners of their eyes until the grazers wake and shove them off. There is a word in the drum for a carrier that has been empty more than a day. It is not the word for *thief*. It is closer to the word for *hole*.

They live about ninety years. They are gentle, stupid in the way that a bucket is stupid, and the whole city loves them the way you love a bridge.

## The hands

The masons are the size of a spaniel and shaped like a question. Blind, six-legged, chalk-colored, with a head flattened into a mallet and a mouth that is not really a mouth anymore — a mill, a gizzard turned outward, gypsum teeth in a rotating ring, and behind them the enzyme gland that turns chewed rock into plaster that sets in ninety seconds.

They begin as swimmers.

This is the fact that reorganizes everything. Mason eggs hatch in the nursery, and the larvae — pale, eyed, finned along the back, three centimeters long — are placed into the throat sacs of carriers. And there, in forty liters of warm dark water, they swim. For eleven days they are fish. They are the only fish in the world. They hunt other larvae, they molt twice, they school in the slosh, and their ocean has a heartbeat and walks. On the twelfth day they stop swimming, lose their eyes into their own heads, and are passed out at a transfer station into the hands of the ward, and they never touch free water again as long as they live.

So the builders of the city arrive inside the water they will spend their lives learning to keep from spilling. Every mason in every wall has, in its body, the memory of an ocean, and the ocean was a throat.

The adult castes are not really separable animals. There are the **mouths**, which lay plaster; the **eaters**, which chew old plaster and re-lay it, and in doing so read the ward's history by taste and re-inscribe it, so that the archive of this civilization is a thing being continuously digested and rewritten by its own librarians; the **counters**, which do nothing but walk the galleries touching everything with their antennae, a slow tide of census, the ward's proprioception; and the **drummers**, big-headed, short-lived, whose entire existence is to strike the floor and be struck back.

A ward holds nine hundred million. It has one temperature, one humidity, one appetite, one grief. When you ask *who lives in the tower*, the honest answer is that the tower is the animal, and the nine hundred million are its cells, and the plaster is its skeleton, and the fact that the cells walk around and occasionally eat each other is a detail of implementation.

Except that they also defect. There are mason lineages that lay bad plaster — thin, porous, sweet-tasting — and cheat their way into the warm districts, and there are wards that have been quietly rotting from the inside for four hundred years while the census reports full strength. The city is not a utopia of cooperation. It is a very large body with a chronic autoimmune condition and an excellent public relations department.

## The landlords

The bladderwoods are not trees.

Cut one — carefully, at the edge, where the wound can be plugged — and you find no heartwood, no rings, no pith. You find chambers. You find muscle. The pads up in the light are full of a green slurry that is not leaf tissue but a tenant: a photosynthetic organism, distantly related to the valve mosses, which lives inside the pad and pays rent in sugar. The tree is a landlord. It is a sessile animal that farms light by keeping algae in glass houses, and it moves — slowly, contractile tissue in the petioles, adjusting the pads through the day so that the coins and bars of light on the floor slide and pool. If you stand still for an hour you'll notice the light has been arranged around you. You are being given shade, or being denied it. Something has an opinion about your temperature.

The gasket vines that fill every joint are the same lineage as the veins under the streets, or were, before they diverged: one branch went down and became plumbing, the other went up and became caulk. They are not parasites and not mutualists. They are the immune system of the lid. A gap opens — a pad falls, a branch cracks — and within two days a vine has grown into it and swollen and gone waxy, and the gap is a scar.

The valve mosses beneath them open in cool and clamp in heat, and they are the reason the air under the lid is dry rather than merely still. They are breathing. All of them, in unison, twice a day, a hundred billion small mouths opening at dusk and shutting at midmorning when the oven-wind comes off the salt.

And in the seam where a vine meets its host: a mite. And on that mite: a smaller mite, which does not eat the mite but eats the wax it secretes, and which cannot survive anywhere else, and which has been found in every seam sampled across three thousand kilometers, and which nobody has ever seen do anything at all.

## The plumbing, and what it wants

Under the streets, the veins.

They are the oldest continuous bodies in the basin. Not the oldest lineage — that honor goes down four hundred meters to the pumps — but the oldest *individuals*. A vein is a tube of muscle and membrane, valved every few meters, pale as a peeled root, and it has no mouth, no gut, no brain, and no end. The one that runs beneath the eastern carrier road has been traced for six hundred and forty kilometers and has not been found to terminate. It grafts to itself. It grafts to other veins and the graft takes and the two become one accounting.

Its inside is the only outside water ever gets.

The beat you feel through your boots — one squeeze every eleven seconds — is not the city's rhythm. It is the vein's, and everything else has been enslaved to it. The escort files step in time with it. The census is timed to it. The nurseries humidify on it. When a vein's beat drifts, the district drifts with it, and the drummers spend their short lives correcting the tempo, and there are things that live in the pacemaker nodes and make the heart skip, and a skipped beat is a stumble, and a stumble in a file sounds like lunch. There is a predation pathway in this world that runs from a parasite the size of a comma, through a rhythm, to a dead carrier. Nothing here has to touch anything to kill it.

Here is the thing the surveyors got wrong for two hundred years.

The veins cannot breed in brine. Their gametes rupture in salt; they require dilute water, free water, water *outside*, and there is no such thing. So they wait. Some of them have waited nine hundred years.

And while they wait, they work. A vein that runs against the base of a cistern tree will, over a decade or two, thin the seam. Not tear it. Thin it — a slow enzymatic courtship, a millimeter a year, patient as the salt. And then one night the wall lets go and forty tons hit dry air and the sound goes out across the felt city like a bell, and every animal under the lid stops.

And then everything moves toward it.

The veins move fastest. In the four hours before the mud is eaten they spawn into the flood — a white cloud in a gully, the only broadcast spawning on the continent — and the larvae have perhaps ninety minutes to find a host, a wound, a drinking mouth, anything, and almost none of them do, and the handful that succeed are carried into the plumbing and grafted and become another few meters of pipe.

So: every gully in this world is an accident, and every accident is a wedding, and the half-born rivers stitched across the basin are not wounds. They are birth canals. The infrastructure is sabotaging itself, slowly, at the rate its lineage can bear, and the masons come out and eat the mud and seal the seam, and the whole thing happens again in eighty years, and neither party has ever known anything else.

## The doors

In the nursery entrances there are animals whose job is to be architecture.

They hatch mobile. They spend a week crawling the frames, fighting each other for position — a good doorway is a fat living, ten thousand passages a day — and then the winner cements its tail into the jamb and grows into an annulus of muscle and wax, and after a month it is no longer an animal in a hole. It is the hole's ability to close.

Doors live sixty years. They have two ganglia: one for hunger, one for the drum. They can shut in four tenths of a second and hold a seal against a pressure differential for eleven hours. They are fed by toll: everything that passes brushes a paste against them, a smear of sugar and enzyme, and a door on a busy street grows fat and glossy and a door on a dead gallery goes gray and thin and is eventually replaced.

They know the census as touch. Ask a door what a ward is and it will tell you, in the only language it has, the pressure of nine hundred million bodies going by.

And there are false doors. A parasite, flat and wax-colored, that cements itself into a frame just like the real thing and looks exactly right and does not seal. It lets a whisper of vapor through and drinks it. It is the worst creature in this world, worse than any hunter. A hunter takes forty liters and the district recovers. A false door takes a hundred milliliters an hour for sixty years and the ward does not notice until the nursery fails, and by then the thing has been standing in the doorway being a wall for two generations, and everyone has walked past it and touched it and fed it and thanked it.

## The corners

The lickers are palm-sized, many-eyed, and fastidious to the point of mania.

Their tongues are fringed with capillary hairs and can strip four milliliters from the corner of a mouth in nine seconds. They ride the carriers' humps. They dress the tree-plugs. They clean wounds. They work the eyes of the sleeping grazers nightly, under a contract so old that the grazers' tear ducts have partly atrophied — tears here are routed inward, to the gut, and the only weeping that reaches the surface is the weeping of an animal in extremity, which is why grief in this basin is a hemorrhage, and why there is always a licker nearby when something dies, waiting with its tongue out, being useful, being paid.

They are the only citizens who go outside on purpose.

Out on the pan they wear a wax coat they secrete and renew, and they can last four hours in the oven-wind, and what they do out there is read the dead.

The windrows ring the city for a hundred kilometers: carcasses carried out by the undertaker caste and laid in rows to leather rather than rot, because rot needs water. They do not decay. They cure. They lie in the salt for centuries with their skins drawn tight over their frames, sorted — genuinely sorted, by year and by ward — into a dry museum that is also a savings account. A carrier that died in the drought eleven hundred years ago still holds three milliliters of bound water in the socket of its eye, and a licker can find it, and taste it, and know from the isotope signature of the deep pumps which decade it came from.

In a bad decade the city mines the museum. The undertakers go out in files and bring back the ancestors and the lickers work them over and the water goes back into the veins, and the drum that season carries a register that no other season carries, and the surveyors' notes on it are illegible because the surveyor was crying, which is an expensive thing to do here, and someone had to catch it.

## The lungs, and the roof

The filter-flyers are the size of a wren and they fly with their mouths open all day.

They cannot close them. The jaw is fused. They quarter through the sleeping stacks and the galleries and the transfer stations catching the moisture of exhaled breath before it can drift to a gap, and their gut is a condenser, and at night they dock — a needle at the tail, an ostium in a vein — and void into the plumbing what they took from the air. They are kidneys with wings. They are why the air under the lid is dry enough to feel strange on your tongue in a place made entirely of water.

They are also, at eighty percent water by mass, the wettest things in the world, and therefore flasks with a heartbeat, and there are canopy hunters called wringers that do nothing but catch them and squeeze.

And above the lid there is a country nobody underneath knows about.

The high fliers never come down. They cannot: their wings are too long, their legs too weak, and the underside of the canopy is a ceiling they have no behavior for. They live in the dusk plume, the only cloud, drinking the city's exhaled breath at four hundred meters, and they are pale and hollow and enormous and they see the stars every night of their lives.

When they die aloft they dry as they fall, and they come down onto the top of the lid as flakes, and there is a whole biome up there — dust-eaters, crust-lichens, sun-white beetles, a roof-garden of things that live on a desert three hundred meters above a crowded warm city — whose entire water budget is the bodies of animals that drank the city's breath.

It never rains. On top of the lid, it snows corpses.

## The listeners

The hunters are long and low and ear-less, with a jaw built like a seismograph, chin flat to the floor, and they do not eat what they kill. They drink it. Forty liters takes a hunter from a wire to a barrel, and a full hunter cannot run, and there are things that hunt full hunters — sumps, bloated, patient, sitting in a gallery for a month waiting for something rich to walk past.

But the interesting predator here is not a body. It is a sentence.

Somewhere in the last few thousand years a lineage learned to drum. Not to make noise — to make *meaning*, to strike the floor with a ward's signature and the phrase that means *this ward is dry, send*. The files come. The files come into a gallery that has been empty for a season, and the floor closes over them.

The city's response has been the whole cultural history of this place: ward signatures that rotate with the season, escort files that carry a stutter-check, a rhythm within the rhythm, an inherited tune that lives in no animal but only in the *habit* of the file, passed from old carriers to young by walking beside them. Tunes go extinct. There are roads nobody uses anymore because their tune died with a generation of carriers in a bad fire year, and the file cannot march without it, and a file that stumbles sounds like lunch.

And there is one worse thing.

A hunter that drums the leak-word.

Because everyone must go to a leak. It is not a decision. It is the deepest reflex in the basin — the veins to spawn, the masons to eat the mud, the lickers to salvage, the ruderals to hatch, the carriers to drink and be filled. And so the false leak-word empties a district into the dark, and the masons must choose whether to believe, and if they disbelieve a true one the nursery dries and the ward is finished. There is a threshold in the drum, a credulity, tuned by ten thousand years of murder, and it is the closest thing this world has to a philosophy.

## The four-hour people

In the crust, everywhere, there are eggs.

They have been there for centuries. Some of them are older than the ward that stands above them. They wet in ninety seconds and hatch in four minutes, and for the length of a leak they own the world: flash-swimmers, shrimp-things, a mud-grazer with a shell like a fingernail, an alga that turns a gully green for one afternoon — the only green that has ever touched the ground in this basin — and a leak-lily that germinates, blooms, sets seed, and dies in six hours, and whose pollen is carried by lickers who came for the water and stayed for the sugar.

Forty minutes a generation. They mate in the shallows of a dying accident, lay drought-eggs in the mud, and go to leather.

And then the masons come and eat the mud.

Which means the eggs go into the plaster. Every wall in this city is a seed bank. Every tower is packed, floor to flue, with the dormant bodies of the only organisms here that have ever known free water, waiting inside the architecture that exists to prevent it. When a ward falls — and wards fall — the rubble wets, and the four-hour people come up out of the ruins of the thing that swallowed them.

There are fire-versions too. Scorch-seeds. Smoke-beetles that arrive in the column with their eggs already developing. And in the wards, at intervals, along the mineral firebreaks, there are the bladder-organs: sessile animals, fat as barrels, filled with a year of a district's savings, whose entire life is spent waiting to burst. They have no other function. They cannot feed themselves; they are fed. They are buckets that were once something else, and when the heat comes they rupture and flood a chamber and die, and the ward rebuilds them from the nursery stock, and the drum has a word for them that translates, approximately, as *the ones who are spent*.

## The count

So who lives here?

Not the carriers, which are containers. Not the masons, which are hands. Not the trees, which are landlords, or the veins, which are plumbing with an appetite, or the doors, which are architecture that gets hungry.

There is one body of water in this basin. It is a sea's worth, and it is older than every species currently holding it, and it has not been outside since before anything here had a mouth. It goes down four hundred meters into the black rock and comes up sweet through the pumps — which are the oldest lineage, the ones that went *down* when the sea went up, and which take a fraction of every liter as their fee, and are therefore the only things in this arrangement that grow. It rises through the veins on an eleven-second beat. It crosses the dry districts in throats. It sits in pads and bladders and sacs and the sealed damp of the nurseries and the eighty percent of a bird. It goes around once a year and it has been going around for eleven thousand, and everything you can see under the lid is a temporary shape it is passing through.

The census is it counting itself.

And the census never balances. Every ward, every season, across three thousand kilometers, the count comes up short by a small consistent fraction, and the counters have carried that discrepancy for as long as there have been counters, and there is a drum-word for it that means something like *the part that is aloft*.

Because at dusk the plume goes up, and the high fliers drink it, and they never come down, and they die above the lid and fall as dry flakes onto a roof of dust-eaters under the ordinary stars. A few tonnes a night. A rounding error. A tax the city pays and cannot refuse and has stopped, in any sense that matters, noticing.

The sea left before anything living had a mouth to notice it. It is still leaving. The whole crowded, silent, gasketed, eleven-thousand-year argument down here is not a wall against that departure. It is a negotiation about the rate.

And when a cistern lets go and forty tons hit dry air and a continent stops mid-step to listen — that is not the argument failing.

That is the water, once every eighty years, getting a word in.
