## The Occupants

The largest living thing in the room is four microns thick and you cannot find its edge.

It is a film. It runs from the slab up the glazed brick, over the arrises, along the cable trays, around every flange face and bolt head and handwheel spoke, down into the leached mortar joints and back out again, unbroken, fourteen hundred square metres of it, and it is not one organism but a consortium with a division of labour. On the outside, where the current brings oxygen, the iron-oxidisers sit in their twisted stalks like ferns in a greenhouse. Twenty microns down, where the oxygen has been used up, other cells are breathing sulphate instead. Between them they have a working arrangement, and the arrangement is this: they eat the pumps.

That is what graphitic corrosion is, from the inside. It is not only chemistry. The iron left the cast iron because something wanted the electrons, and the something had a membrane and a metabolism and made more of itself. Every atom of iron that went out through the draft tube went out through a cell. The pumps were not dissolved. They were *metabolised*, over a century, at a rate too slow to watch, and the graphite skeletons standing in the centreline are the part of the meal nobody could use.

So: nothing in this hall is preserved. Everything in it is being cleaned.

---

The posture of a facility awaiting a shift change is a real observation and it has a cause, and the cause has a mouth.

Snails — small, dull, thousands — work the brick with a ribbon of two hundred teeth, rasping the film off in strips a tenth of a millimetre wide and leaving a track you can see if the light is right. Amphipods pick at the higher growth. Caddis larvae graze the flats and net the flow. The crayfish sweep their aprons with their maxillipeds, brush the grit out of the doorway, carry it three centimetres and drop it, over and over, all night. Between them the whole grazeable surface of Valve Hall No. 2 is cropped about every eleven days in September and every forty in February.

That is why the foundry marks are legible. Not because nothing is growing on them. Because something is always taking it off.

The census of a hundred thousand is a census of what a lamp can show you. Under the silt there are tubificid worms at densities that make the number meaningless — millions, heads down eight centimetres in the black, tails waving up in the water, a meadow growing the wrong way. There are chironomid larvae in the top centimetre with haemoglobin in their blood, which is red. There is rust in the lamp halos, which is red. Those are the only two reds in the hall, and one of them is inside an animal, and the light that would show it to you does not reach that far down.

---

The spacing is not mostly temper. It is hydraulics.

The flow crosses the hall in sheets, laminar, orderly, carrying a fine constant rain of detritus, and anything that filters or grazes takes what passes over it. A neighbour upstream is not a rival in the social sense. It is a hole in your food supply. So the bryozoans sit on the flanges at intervals set by the shadow their neighbours' feeding currents cast, palm-sized antlers of jelly spaced to the centimetre, and the amphipods hang in the column scattered like sparks because clustering, here, buys nothing at all. The crayfish cells along the wall base are spaced by the reach of an arm.

Three economies, then. Most of the room eats the current. The dead zones behind the pumps eat what falls, and have a different fauna — sponges, grey and fingered; the worms; the burbot; a slow sediment of everything that has ever been alive upstream. And three dinner plates of the room eat light, and are the only place in a hundred square kilometres where anything is making sugar.

Everything else is downstream of a mouth.

---

The pondweed has been trying to flower for six years and it is not going to.

It is one clone, anchored in a mortar joint under the middle lamp, and it sends up a pale etiolated filament with four leaves arranged along it like a hand held open. It grows eleven centimetres and a snail crops it. It regrows from the same node. It has done this something over forty times. Flowering in that family is a matter of day length and warmth and a signal from a sun, and what it gets instead is forty watts of tungsten at a distance of a metre, forever, at eight degrees.

It is not dying. It has a starch bank in the joint and it is patient in the way that only a plant can be patient, which is without any expectation at all. It is simply a thing that has been waiting for summer since before the crayfish currently in the fourth cell were hatched.

The snails around it stop when the lamp flickers. All of them, at once, foot clamped, shell still. The flicker is not electrical. It is mechanical, and it comes from upstream of the dynamo, and we will get to it.

---

The caddis cases are the room's jewellery and also its second archive.

There are three case-building species here and they select differently, and the selection is reliable enough that a case is a signature: one takes cement grit and nothing else, one takes whatever is flat, one takes whatever is bright. The bright one has built with brass swarf, with glass from the burst gauge, with garnet from the abrasive tin that spilled on the bench in some year nobody recorded.

Empty cases don't rot. They go into the silt. There are tens of thousands of them down there, in layers, and the layers are not the same as the silt couplets, because the cases record *taste*. The garnet appears in the record only inside cases, in a band, and above that band it thins out over about nine years as the tin's contents were worked over and carried off — a resource being exhausted, visible only in what an animal chose to pick up. At eighty-one years there is a case with a fragment of charcoal in it. Charcoal from a fire on the far shore, which is also a dark band in the silt, and here is the same event recorded twice: once by physics, once by a larva that found a black grain and thought it would do.

In June the larvae abandon their cases and swim up. There is a brightness at the top of forty-one metres of steel throat and they go toward it and the eels take most of them at the shaft mouth. Most. The ones that don't go are the ones that breed here.

A hundred and twelve generations. The net-makers' mesh has drifted measurably finer over that time, because the food here is fine. And in every generation, the individuals least compelled by the shaft have left the most descendants.

The room selects for staying. It has been at it for a century and it is working.

---

There are eleven dead legs in the hall: conduit stubs capped at one end, the short pipe between the accumulator and the isolation valve, the interior of the switchgear enclosure where the door is gone but the gland plate is not. Total volume about forty litres. The water in them exchanges by diffusion and by nothing else, and it has been in there, in the sense of turnover time, for decades.

It has gone its own way chemically. No oxygen, low pH, its own dissolved everything. And in it: ostracods, a harpacticoid copepod, a flatworm the colour of nothing.

The ostracods are parthenogenetic, which means one founder is a population. They went into those pipes at some point in the first decade and they have not come out and nothing has come in. A hundred and twelve years of complete solitude, in eleven separate volumes, in the dark, at eight degrees. They are measurably different from the animals in the open hall. Not much. Enough.

When the conduit finally perforates they will be released into a room that has been changing without them, and they will either manage or not, and there is no one who will ever know which happened.

---

The mussel beds run down one flank where the silt is thin, and they have a problem, which is that they cannot touch each other.

They broadcast sperm and they brood young and the young — glochidia, microscopic clapping shells — must clamp onto the gill of a fish or die. In a room with six or seven fish in it, holding their distance, this is a bad arrangement. Most of what they release goes into the current and out through the draft tube. Some of it finds a sculpin. A very little of it finds the right sculpin, and rides, and drops off somewhere in the reservoir, and a few of those come back in as fry-sized settlers through the trash rack.

The drum eat the mussels. The *tik* you hear is a pharyngeal tooth going through a shell that took thirty years to make. The drum also carry the mussels' young on their gills. Both things are true at once and neither fish nor mollusc has any opinion about it.

What the mussels leave behind matters more than what they become. Shell hash. Half a metre of it in the lee of pump three, a pavement of nacre, the only new hard substrate being manufactured anywhere in this basin. Everything here needs something to attach to and the machines are finishing. In ten thousand years, when the graphite has slumped and the concrete has spalled to rounded rubble, the thing that will still be hard, in a hundred square kilometres of mud, will be the shells.

The mussels are building the next room out of the corpses of the last one.

---

Under the bench, upside down, there is a male sculpin who has been fanning a patch of eggs for nineteen days.

He chose the underside of the bench top because it is flat and overhung and the flow is slack there, which is what he is built to want. The eggs are amber. The bench is under the second lamp, so for a few hours of the day, when the shaft-light and the lamp agree, they are amber in fact and not only in principle.

No eye has ever received that colour. Not his — he has no cone for it and no interest. Not the snails'. Not, so far, anyone's.

The bench is waterlogged elm, sound-looking, holding its shape the way the stool holds its shape. Aquatic fungi are already through it, a fine white lace, digesting the lignin at a rate set by the temperature, which is to say very slowly and without pause. The nursery and the three-legged stool are the same kind of object: a piece of the old world that is now mostly water and is being kept in order by something small, until it isn't.

The crayfish that will climb the stool is alive now. It is in the fourth cell along the upstream wall, about the length of a thumbnail, and it will do it in three or four years, and the stool will go into a soft brown cloud that will take an hour to settle, and the crayfish will move to the next cell along, because the apron there will need sweeping.

---

The lignum vitae bearing has won, and it is inhabited.

The steel shaft it carried is an orange rumour. The bearing is sharp-edged, oily, dense, sound — and there is a chironomid larva in it, a wood-miner, two millimetres long, tunnelling along the grain with a mouth 0.2 millimetres wide. There are four of them. They have been in there, in the sense of lineage, for a very long time.

The bearing's soundness is a matter of scale. At the scale of a hand it is the one wooden thing that beat the water. At the scale of a larva it is food that happens to be slow.

---

Under the gantry there is a burbot, and it is the largest organ in the room, by which I mean its liver, which is enormous and oily and takes up a third of it.

It has not moved more than three metres because it does not need to. The current is a delivery service. It lives by smell — a chin barbel, nasal barbels, a nose that reads the hall as a map: ammonia and methane from the dead zones, a green oxygen smell from the halos, mucus from the pipe mouths, rubber from the dynamo, the cold leaf-and-iron smell of the incoming river, and nothing at all from the graphite, which is the only thing in the room that has stopped having a chemistry.

In January its species forms a knot. A dozen fish, writhing, in midwinter, under ice, in shallow water, broadcasting into the dark. It is a thing they do together or not at all.

There is one burbot in Valve Hall No. 2. It has been one burbot for a long time. In January it becomes restless and moves the full three metres and comes back.

---

The four eels are not four animals so much as four offices.

Each beat is a circuit: out of the pipe, along the wall base at a fixed height, across the slab downstream of the pump where the scalloped dish is, through the lee where the mussel hash lies, up the flange, along the gantry rail for two metres, down, home. Nineteen minutes, near enough. Seven or eight circuits a night, and the same route in the same order, and the deviations are so small that a person watching would call it a machine.

Where the beats meet, at the base of each pump, two eels arrive within a second or two of each other, most nights, and stop ten centimetres apart, and hold, and both reverse. No contact. No display. In a room where nothing is close enough to touch, that pause is the closest thing to touch there is, and it is a refusal of it, performed on schedule, by animals that have never met before.

Because the offices outlive the officers. Up in the shaft, in the rivet seams, there are always three or four thin brown eels waiting — not growing much, not doing anything, holding station in the cold roar forty metres above the floor. They came down as elvers over the wet face of the dam in the rain, years ago, and found the shaft, and stopped at the smell of a room where eels already are. When a beat opens, its mucus fades over about a week, and the largest of the waiting animals comes down and takes the route without being shown it, because the route is written in the smell of the swept aprons and the worn brick and the faeces at the corners.

The boundaries are older than the eels because the boundaries advertise themselves.

---

The eel in the third pipe silvered eleven years ago.

It happened on schedule: the eyes doubled, the pupil widened, the skin thickened, the belly went from yellow to a bronze-black, the pectorals darkened, the gut shrank to a cord, the gonads swelled. It stopped eating. It has not eaten since. It is not starving; it is finished. It is a swimming envelope of fat and intention running a nineteen-minute circuit eight times a night for a food supply it no longer has any use for.

Its new eyes are tuned to about 480 nanometres, which is the blue of open ocean at depth, which is the light of the Sargasso, which is where it is supposed to go and spawn and die. It is also, almost exactly, the colour that survives forty-one metres of green reservoir water. It has grown the eyes for a sea it will never see and they are precisely right for the shaft.

In October, on the dark of the moon, when the temperature drops half a degree, it goes up.

Forty-one metres of riveted steel, out into the flat grey basin, and then downstream, low over the mud, for nights, following the slope, following the smell of a river that is still there in the water somewhere — and it arrives at the dam. Four kilometres of concrete. Racks it cannot pass. A sluice that is shut. It works the face of it. It goes up the wet spillway and finds the reservoir again on the other side of the drop, or it doesn't. It circles. In November, sometimes not until February, it comes back down the shaft and into the third pipe and resumes the circuit, having eaten nothing for four months.

The burbot knows. For three weeks afterward the third pipe smells of deep reservoir mud and of the dam's downstream face, which is a smell that exists nowhere else in the hall. The burbot has read that smell every winter for decades. It is the only witness in the room, and it lives by nose, and it has no way of saying so.

---

The lamps are being eaten by the things they light.

The dynamo's belt is balata and canvas, eight plies, and it is not leather, which is why it is still here. Isopods graze the film off it. In grazing the film they abrade the outer ply, a few microns a year, and have done since roughly the year the valves were shut. There are three plies left, perhaps four. Under the snowmelt pulse, when the runner speeds and the load jumps, it slips.

That is the flicker. The snails have learned it — not individually, not as a trick, but as a species-wide flinch tuned to a failing belt, because for thirty years the animals that didn't stop got cropped by something that came in on the dark.

When it goes, the green will die in about a week. The pondweed's rhizome will hold for a few years on starch and then go. The diatoms will leave resting stages in the silt under the dead lamps, a seed bank waiting for a light that is not coming back. The snails will switch to film and barely notice. The room will lose its only colour and remain, in every other respect, exactly what it already is: a place that eats what falls.

---

The rarest inhabitant comes every few years and brings the only gas.

You. The bubbles rise and strip a little of the ceiling film on the way up. A fin kick lifts a decade of the ledger into a cloud that takes a day to settle and settles in the wrong order. A hand on the gate stand takes the foundry mark off with it, and the mark is gone, and it was the last one.

The floor has core holes in it. Each is a visit. Each visit removed the pages it came to read.

---

So the roster, at the end of the shift: a film that has eaten the machines and is still eating; millions of worms breathing through their tails; snails keeping fourteen hundred square metres legible; caddis becoming a kind that stays; ostracods becoming a kind in a pipe; mussels manufacturing the substrate their grandchildren will need; a sculpin fanning a colour nobody has seen; a burbot with a liver and a nose and no knot to join; four offices and a queue in the rivets, and one of the four going up the shaft every October toward a sea four thousand kilometres away, and coming back, and resuming.

The gauge reads 4.1 bar. It is correct.

Three millimetres a year. Never missed a shift.
