Begin with the hands, because everything else follows from them.

They are wrapped. Linen next to the skin, wool over that, wound figure-eight through the thumb and around the heel of the palm, tied off at the wrist with a knot everyone learns before they learn their letters. The wraps come off for eating and for sleeping and for nothing else. A Cut hand is a document: you can read a stranger's trade in the thickness of the binding, in where it has been reinforced, in the shine of stone dust worked into the wool. Sawyers bind the thumb and the first two fingers and leave the palm bare for the rope. Bailers bind everything and look clumsy, and are not. Dew children have thin wraps because wringing fleece is delicate work and they do it in the dark.

Greeting is the backs of the wrists pressed together, briefly, both hands held up and open. It is not affection. It is proof that neither of you is holding anything sharp.

They walk with one hand out. Always. Roof, ramp, cellar, the flat white floor of a pan at dawn — the wall-hand goes ahead of the body and touches, and the body follows what the hand has found. You can identify a person from the Cut in a market town a day's walk away by the way they cross an open square: arm slightly raised, fingers spread, feeling for a wall that isn't there, walking a little too close to whoever they are with. They do not like the plain. The larks going up out of the grass make them stop dead, every time, all their lives.

---

They have two names.

The close name is the one everybody uses. It is short, it is soft consonants, it is made to carry no further than a body's length, and it is what is written in the cord-code and what you say into the round doorway of a house before you go down into it.

The other name is the loud name. It has vowels you can push. It is given at birth and it is not used. It is kept, the way a tool is kept oiled in a cloth, and it is spoken exactly once, on the rim, over the thin plain soil, by the crier, who is permitted. Everyone at the graveside hears their own dead shouted and flinches, and the flinch is part of the rite, and the echo comes off the far wall a half-beat later and folds into the middle of the crowd, and this is understood to be the town saying it back.

There is a man on the second course who lost his temper in the year of the bad frost. He shouted two words at a boy who had let a wedge dry. It was thirty-one years ago. He is still called by what he shouted, in the low voice, at arm's length, and he answers to it, because nothing here wears.

At dusk, when the swifts come off the Bone Course bench and start screaming, the children are allowed to shout. It lasts about a quarter of an hour. They stand on the roofs and yell into the noise and the noise covers them, and their mothers let them, and some of the mothers join in. It is called the swift hour and nobody has ever had to explain it to anybody.

---

Height is money, and the way you get money is by going down.

This is the arithmetic the cutting families live inside. The roofs stay where they are. The floor drops a man's height each generation, because every new cellar is cut beneath the last one and every lifted block becomes a wall two arm-spans away, and the people doing the lifting are the people who will live in the new bottom. To earn, you sink. Eighty years of it has produced a town where your altitude is your family's whole financial history, legible at a glance, painted on the wall in red ochre at the level the sun reaches on the shortest day.

Above the line: dry vaults, noon warmth, roofs you can sleep on, lentils in trays, looms in the good light. There is a girl on the upper east side, twelve years old, who has never touched the floor of the Cut. Not once. She has crossed the whole town a hundred times, roof to roof to roof, and the deepest she has ever been is her own doorway. She knows what the Fog Pool looks like from above, which is like looking into a bucket of milk with your cellar at the bottom of it. She is not snobbish about it. She simply has no reason to go down, and the stairs down are cold, and her feet have never been wet.

Below the line: blue damp, the smell of wet wool that never quite dries, the ammoniac warmth of the autumn dung banks, the sound of your neighbours through stone that has finished setting and therefore rings. Everyone down there is a cutter or the child of cutters. In another twenty years they will be three courses under the roofs and their grandchildren will bail water out of the room they are sitting in.

To visit your grandmother you climb. Everyone in the Cut has strong thighs and a complicated relationship with the sky.

---

The rim is where the town keeps its noise.

Kilns there, on the leeward side, smoking. Quicklime slaking in pits with a hiss you can hear from the second course. Flint knapping there, because flint must be struck and striking is forbidden below the second course, so the black knuckles levered out of the Bone Course go up the ramps in baskets to be broken by people who are, most of them, hard of hearing by forty and profoundly deaf by sixty, and who read lips and hands and are the best gossip in the settlement because they watch mouths all day.

The graves are there. The goats are there. The dog is there — old, grey-muzzled, and the only creature in the place with a licence to shout, because when it barks the whole pit stops and looks up, and twice in living memory that has been the difference between a caravan and a raid. The flag posts are there. The crier is there. Rim people marry down constantly and are considered slightly foreign, and they speak in ordinary voices, and their children have to be taught the low voice like a second language, and some of them never quite get it.

Below, in the pit, the work is two people and a copper blade and wet sand.

Saw pairs are the real marriages of the Cut. Whatever the household arrangements, the pair is the pair: two people who have pulled in rhythm across a season, who breathe together, whose slurry ticks in the kerf at a rate an experienced cutter can identify from four courses away the way you'd know a friend's cough. There is a woman on the third course who sawed with her sister for forty-one years. Her sister died in the spring. She has hired a boy, a good boy, willing, strong, and the kerf wanders. She corrects him constantly and gently and it does not help. She says it is like singing with someone who is counting.

---

There is a trade here that consists entirely of listening.

At night the setting stone gives off its pings — thousands of them, all over the bowl, sharp and small and irregular, a valley-sized oven cooling. Most people sleep through it the way you sleep through rain. The ears do not. They lie awake with the cheek on the wall and they take the town's temperature.

A good ear can tell you which vault is drying too fast on its south face and will craze by midsummer; which course is still fat with sap under a crust that has already hardened and is therefore a liar; which of the new work will not make it to frost. They are paid in food and roof-space. They are usually old women who have stopped being able to do anything else, and they are listened to with a seriousness that elsewhere attaches to physicians and priests, and when they say *wrap it tonight* nobody argues about the cost of the fleece.

Because frost in green stone is the end of the world. It chalks. It slumps. It comes apart in the spring like wet bread, and a season's work goes to powder and the family that cut it has to cut it again from the socket of its own failure. In the year of the bad frost eleven households lost their new work in a single night, and there are people alive who were children then and who still, in autumn, cannot sleep for checking.

One more thing about the ears, and it governs the whole interior life of the place: green stone absorbs and set stone rings. A newly cut cell keeps whatever you say in it. An old vault gives it back off the far wall, folded and doubled, to the yard. So privacy here is a thing that decreases with age. Houses become indiscreet as they ripen. When someone has to say the thing that cannot be said — a confession, a diagnosis, a proposal, a betrayal — they borrow a green room, and they go into it, and they pull the door, and the stone drinks it.

The Cut has been building its secrets into its walls for eighty years and the walls have been handing them back ever since.

---

The other residents are few and are all, in some sense, guests.

Forty pairs of swifts in the drill holes of the abandoned bench, screaming at dusk, uncomplained-of since the first year. Springtails in the seep-moss at the Weep, which is the only deep green in a day's walk and which has, by unanimous agreement, the status of a person: you do not pick it, you do not wash in it, you go there to hear water fall and you sit on the basin that was cut to look worn. Midges over the pans. Geckos on the warm south benches, which the children handle and which lose their tails and grow them back, and this is watched with enormous interest by people whose town cannot regrow anything. Mice in the granaries. A toad in the Fog Pool that everyone agrees came down in a wool bale, and which has a name, and which is fed.

The lichen on the oldest face is the size of a thumbnail and appears in three property disputes.

And in the crevices of the upper wall, four fig seedlings and a caper, protected, watched, argued over. There is an old man who came from somewhere with figs. He says the fruit needs a fly, and the fly lives inside the fruit, and there is no fruit here, and therefore no fly, and therefore the four trees will make leaves and wood and nothing else until somebody brings the fly. The children are sent up with feathers to pollinate them by hand, which does not work, and which everyone encourages, because it is the sort of thing you want children to be doing.

He says the valley on the other side of the ridge has figs. He says it as though it were an accusation.

---

On the fourth course there is a woman who cuts wear.

She is the only person in the Cut whose craft is to make a thing look used. The basin under the Weep is hers — hollowed, lipped, the stone thinned where a thousand hands would have thinned it if a thousand hands could thin anything here. She does the hand-grips on the deepest ramps, the shallow dishes at the sides of doorways where shoulders would have rubbed, the dished sill of a well that has never had a rope on it.

She has never seen a worn step. She has seen a spoon.

Her workroom is a cabinet of the outside: a wooden spoon from a valley caravan, a copper coin rubbed featureless, a river pebble, a leather strap, a bone needle-eye, a fragment of a millstone, a child's shoe with the sole gone thin at the ball of the foot. Traders bring her things and she pays badly and they let her, because the alternative is that she comes up and looks at their hands. She sits with the spoon for hours. She turns the pebble in the light. She has opinions about the difference between wear made by water and wear made by wool and wear made by feet, and she has never seen any of them happen, and she is right about most of it, and she does not know which parts.

Some people find her work obscene. More find it necessary. There is a general feeling that a town where nothing wears needs somewhere to keep the idea.

She has a jar set aside, empty, labelled in ochre. It is for the first mud the Cut ever makes.

---

Three courses down, where the round doorways are at knee height in a wall of stored water, there is a room that never set.

It was cut in the first year and flooded in the same week — a mistake, a broken bedding plane, the Weep before anyone knew what the Weep was — and it has been under water ever since, holding its quarry sap, under weight, and it is green. It is the oldest room in the Cut and the only young thing in it. They call it the Unset.

You go down through cold water and a doorway you have to fold yourself to get through, and there is air at the top, and the walls are cheese-rind soft, and they take your hand.

That is what people go for. You press your palm flat and hold it and take it away, and the print stays: every finger, the whorls, the little crease at the base of the thumb. It stays about a day. Then it softens back and is gone, and the wall is blank again, and it will take the next hand as gladly.

Eighty years of hands. Grief hands, mostly. There is nowhere below the rim to put the dead and no soft ground anywhere, and the Cut has exactly one substance that will receive an impression and let it go, and people swim down to it in the dark to put their palms where their mother put hers. It is not a shrine. Nobody has organised it. There is no fee. It is simply known, the way the Weep is known.

There is a keeper. He goes down at the turn of the year to check that it is still soft, and he comes up and does not talk for a while.

The rules are short and every child knows them. One hand. Count two hundred. Do not put your arm in. Do not go when you are tired. There is a story about a man in the second generation who went down drunk and stayed, and the story is not a ghost story, and that is what makes it a good one: the room was not angry, the room was simply still alive, and it closed around him the way it closes around everything, only slower than he expected and faster than he could pull.

They did not drain it to get him out. It would have set.

---

At the eastern end, behind a screen of stacked spoil, there is a man who has spent eleven years lying on his side.

The Adit is a man's width and it is nearly through, and he is the only person in the Cut who works alone, in absolute dark, in a space that is not a cell. He is small and getting smaller; his shoulders have gone narrow the way a sawyer's thumb goes wide. He is also the only person here who shouts. He shouts constantly. He shouts into rock a foot from his face, whole strings of it, songs and obscenities and his wife's loud name, and none of it goes anywhere, and he comes up at the end of a shift and has to stand at the mouth for a minute remembering how to be quiet.

His wife sits at the mouth with the cord. He taps on the wall with a flint and she reads it through the stone with the heel of her wrapped hand, and they have been having the same conversation for eleven years, and it is mostly about food.

When he breaks through, the pans will drain. The water will run. Running water carries grit and grit wears. There will be silt, and a channel, and the channel will cut, and the corners will go, and the chisel ridges on the oldest ramp — every individual stroke, crisp as the day it was made — will finally, slowly, over a lifetime, become a smooth thing that nobody cut.

And the water table will drop. And the Unset will drain.

That is what the old cutters cannot articulate, standing at the screen with their arms folded, uneasy in a way they keep describing as *the mud*. It is not the mud. It is that the last soft place in the world is going to dry out and harden and become permanent, along with everything else, and there will be nowhere left to put a hand and have it taken and given back.

The keeper has thought about this more than anyone. He has decided what he will do. When the water starts to go he will swim down one last time and smooth the walls flat, all of them, every trace, so that it sets blank — and then, at the end, he will press his own palm into the north wall and hold it there and take it away, and go up, and let it harden around the shape of one hand forever. The first thing in the Cut that will never heal.

The woman on the fourth course will get her mud. She will find out whether she was right about the spoon.

And somewhere under the ridge, in the dark, a small man is lying on his side with a flint pick, cutting toward a valley where there are figs, and flies inside the figs, and water that has been running for a very long time, and steps worn down in the middle.
