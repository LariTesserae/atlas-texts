## I. Thirty grams

Start with one, because one is what you can hold.

She weighs what two tablespoons of water weigh. Her heart idles at two hundred and goes to a thousand when she hunts. Her feet are four hooks on a stub of leg, all toes forward, no sole, nothing to stand on: she has never stood, will never stand, cannot stand. Put her on the plain and she is not exiled, she is stuck — a swiftlet on flat ground cannot get airborne, and the grass twelve kilometres out is not a country she has renounced but a surface that would kill her by being level. She clings. She sleeps clinging, or sleeps on the wing at altitude, half her brain at a time. She eats in the air, mates in the air, and drinks by skimming, at speed, in absolute darkness, a bowl of tea-brown water the size of two cupped hands, whose resident frog feels the wind of her and does not look up.

Her clicks are not sonar in the sense you want. They are coarse — wall, not-wall, gap, no-gap. They will not find her door. The clicks find the wall. **The hole is remembered.** She has five hundred metres of geometry in a brain the size of a pea, and she runs it at forty kilometres an hour into black, and she does not slow down, and the not-slowing-down is the whole of her faith.

She lives eight years. Then stop starting here, because she is not the resident. She is a tenant.

## II. The address

The hole is six hundred years old. That is seventy-five tenants, uninterrupted, each relining the cup with fresh saliva each season, each adding a millimetre, each scraping out the mites of the last. The cup is a document with seventy-five signatures in it and no author.

A bird here does not have a name; it has an address, and the address is a drip-line and a height and a hole, and it is spoken in clicks by a chick following its mother out and back, out and back, twice, three times, until the route is in it for life. That is the entire inheritance. There is no territory, no property, no law. There is a wet seam in the dark and a remembered turn.

When a bird dies away from home — over the grass, in a hawk, in a storm — her five hundred metres die with her, and the hole stands empty, and the traffic through that gallery thins, and something is forgotten. Whole corridors go quiet for a decade and are rediscovered by a youngster who took a wrong turn and lived. The map of the largest tower exists nowhere. No bird has it. Nine million birds each hold a fragment the size of a walk to work, and the map is what the fragments do when you put forty million of them in motion at dusk. It is not stored. It is *performed*, nightly, and it has not been performed the same way twice in two hundred thousand years.

The eleven towers have eleven accents. A bird from the southern tower hesitates at a northern junction — a half-second, measurable — and then goes on.

## III. The orphans who measure

The mound-hens are two kilos of dull brown bird with wings that are mostly a nuisance. They walk on the ceiling. They are the only animals here that cross the roof from outside, and a hen crossing moss is a slow earthquake, six chambers at a time, and nobody below reacts, because it happens all day.

They are the only residents who *measure* anything.

The bill goes into the compost to the base of the skull, and the tongue reads the temperature, and the number the tongue wants is thirty-three, and the window is one degree wide. Below it the eggs stall. Above it they cook. The hen adds straw, or scrapes straw off, and goes away, and comes back in an hour, and does it again, for five months. At dusk she walks the crown and pulls the grass plugs from the upper shafts; at dawn she seats them. She is stoking a furnace and working its dampers and she has no idea, in any sense you would accept, what a furnace is.

Her chicks dig themselves out eleven weeks later, fully feathered, competent, and utterly alone. They have never met a parent. They will never meet one. There is no teaching in this species, no childhood, no imitation, no tradition — and yet every one of them, on the first heap it encounters, inserts its bill to the skull and begins to run a thermostat accurate to a degree.

What is inherited here is not knowledge. It is the tongue, and the heap. The heap is the other half of the animal. Take the heap away and the hen is a brown bird standing in the cold with an instrument in its mouth and nothing to read.

There is a mite that lives in the groove of that bill. It goes into the fifty-degree dark with the tongue and comes out again, several times an hour, all season. It is the only creature on the escarpment that has ever been inside the measurement.

## IV. The ones who make stone

The bats are the geology.

Two guilds, and you can read them in the guano-rock with a chisel. The insect bats hunt the plain and come back with beetle chitin in them, and their season is a fine dark line. The fig bats range eleven kilometres to the last fruiting trees on the far side of the wind and come back with seed, and their season is a pale band. Rust seams from a red berry that stopped growing on this side of the escarpment four thousand years ago: a bat lineage that changed its habits, recorded as a colour change, by animals that cannot remember last year.

Every gram of the highland was carried up here in a gut. The mountain is a two-hundred-thousand-year act of importation from the grass, and the grass grows on the dust the mountain blows back down. Neither party is aware of the arrangement.

In the lower galleries they hang in a mass whose air would put you under — oxygen thin, carbon dioxide thick, ammonia at a concentration that would scald your eyes closed. Their blood is not your blood. They have been breathing that for longer than there have been people to be bothered by it. They tolerate it, and their bodies warm the lower city, and the swiftlets above them are living, in part, on bat fever.

They die hanging. They are carried down and laid where the dead are laid, and the silk-ants come, and the heap is bound and daubed, and it becomes a beam. So the bats build the tower twice: once with their guts, once with their femurs. The galleries you would crawl through, if you could crawl, which you can't, are held up by the previous occupants.

In three of the towers there is a thin grey band, about nine centimetres, of nest-scarring: a century when something came on ropes with lamps and cut the cups out. It is the only trace those animals left. They are not residents. They are a stratum.

## V. Eleven peoples

The flying things are one people with eleven accents. The crawling things are eleven peoples, because a tower is an island if you cannot fly, and the plain between them is a sea of level ground.

**The thatch-mouse** weighs twelve grams and cannot dig. There is nothing to dig; everything has already been dug. Its feet have long pads and its tail curls to grip, and it runs the galleries by smell and by sound, and the sound it steers by is tamping. It moves toward work. It has never seen the sky except by accident, and its whiskers span five centimetres, which is the width of a door. When a quarter goes quiet the mice leave it inside a season — before the thatch greys, before the drip-line shifts, before any bird has failed to return. They are the earliest instrument on the mountain and nothing here reads them.

There are eleven kinds. They have diverged tower by tower in the time it takes the birds to not notice.

**The seam-snake** is forty centimetres long and four millimetres thick and lives its entire life in one wet vertical seam. The gallery is too narrow to turn in. It has therefore never turned around: it goes forward and it goes backward, and that is the complete repertoire of its motion. It hunts by laying its jaw against the carton and reading the drum of feet, and it lays its eggs against the warm flank, and it does not know there is an outside. Light is not a thing it avoids. Light is a wall it has never reached.

Its seam drifts north three metres a decade with the rest of the city, and the snake's lineage rides the drift, and no snake in a hundred generations has moved relative to anything it can perceive.

**The course-lizard** lives in the four-centimetre gap between two courses of thatch — but not any gap. It wants stems that have been rained on for two to three years, softened at the butt, carrying the right mites. Fresh thatch is too clean. Old thatch is too grey. Its habitat is therefore not a place. It is an *age*, and the age moves: every four years the course it lives in is pulled out and replaced above it, and the lizard walks sideways into the next bay to find its vintage again. Over a life it may travel three hundred metres laterally and never move four centimetres up. It is the only animal on the escarpment with a taste for a particular year, and it spends its whole existence chasing that year across a wall.

## VI. The demolition, which sings

The flightless cricket eats thatch. Its gut carries a symbiont that can break down silica-loaded grass, which almost nothing else here can, and it works through a stem from the butt outward, and it sings the entire time: six kilohertz, one unbroken sheet, every stem inhabited.

Understand what this means. The four-centimetre rise and the four-centimetre subsidence, the neck-and-neck race that reads from twelve kilometres out as a mature limestone formation — the demolition half of that race is a cricket's mandible. The shape of the highland is a negotiation between a beak and a jaw. Remove the crickets and the towers would not stay the same; they would swell, and split, and become something with no weather of its own.

And the crickets cannot stop talking. Which is why the swiftlets can find the failing courses in absolute darkness: they listen for the loudest thatch and re-daub it. The pest is the surveyor. The mountain's entire inspection system is an insect eating it, announcing, continuously, at the top of its voice, exactly where the damage is.

Nobody arranged this. It has simply been true for so long that everything else has grown around it.

## VII. The ones who actually live here

The birds are a rounding error. Say it plainly: forty million birds is a garnish. The population of this place is arthropod, and its work is digestion, and if you could subtract every vertebrate at once the towers would go on breathing for a season before they noticed.

Turning beetles in the middens, working the faces the hens open. Larvae in sheets so dense the compost moves visibly, like something asleep. Mites on the birds, mites in the nests, mites on the mites; a single swiftlet carries more individuals than the tower carries birds. Springtails in the drip-lines in numbers that make the water look like it is boiling at the edges. A blind pseudoscorpion that lives only in the wax rim of cisterns and has been described from four of the eleven towers and is certainly in all of them.

The silk-ants no longer build. They gave that up. They keep caterpillars in the roof brambles, defend them, milk them, and carry the silk down into the ossuaries to pave their roads, and the paving binds the bone-heaps, and the bone-heaps hold up the galleries. The ants are not making a beam. They are making a road, and the beam is a side effect that has been load-bearing for sixty thousand years. The birds tolerate the ants because the birds want the silk too, and there is a treaty here that no one signed and neither party could describe.

And under all of it, the fungus.

The fifty degrees is not the beetles and not the birds. It is a mycelium threaded through the deep middens, and it is what the hens are actually managing when they think they are managing straw. It cannot cross the plain. There are eleven of them, and they have diverged, and they run at slightly different heats, and that is why the towers are eleven climates and not one, and why the nurseries in the fourth tower move outward a month earlier than the nurseries in the ninth.

The birds feed it. It pays rent in warmth. The hens are its hands and don't know it. It has no mouth and no brain and it is the reason there is a thirty-degree country under a frost, and it is very possibly the oldest continuous individual on the escarpment, and it has never once been seen by anything that lives here.

## VIII. The two-day animal that holds up a tower

The figs in the light wells are structural. Their roots have got into the carton buttresses and become the buttresses. When a fig dies, the quarter fails in a decade and empties in sixty years and lithifies in two hundred, and after that it is bedrock with a shaft through it.

A fig cannot fruit without its wasp.

The wasp is two millimetres long, lives forty-eight hours, has no mouthparts worth the name once it emerges, and must find a receptive fig — a specific fig, in a specific stage — by smell, across dark galleries, from one light well to the next. Its range is about eight hundred metres. The light wells are placed by collapse, and collapse is placed by rain.

So: the structural fate of a hundred-and-forty-metre tower is set by the spacing of its holes in the roof, which is set by storms, measured against the flight range of an insect that lives for two days and never sees the thing it is holding up.

There is a gap on the eastern flank of the sixth tower where the wells are eleven hundred metres apart. There was a fig there. There is a stump, and a dead buttress, and a quarter going grey, and the whole thing was decided by three hundred metres of darkness and a wasp that ran out of afternoon.

The most important animal on the escarpment weighs less than a grain of the stone it stands on.

## IX. Nations of one

Thousands of clay bowls, wax-sealed at the rim, each with its frog. A frog whose entire world is two cupped hands of ammoniacal tea. It cannot leave. There is nowhere to go: the galleries between bowls are dry carton and a hundred metres of vertical nothing.

So the species exists as several thousand isolated populations of one, and each male calls in a bowl that resonates, and the calls have been tuned by the bowl — the same frog in a wide bowl sings a different note than it would in a narrow one, and the bowls are made by birds to no frog's specification, and the frogs have adapted to the pottery.

For forty-three weeks the country is nothing but shouting between animals that will never meet. For nine weeks the storms come and the drip-lines run full and the seams connect, and everything that needs to touch anything descends and touches it. Then the water drops and the doors close and the shouting resumes.

The tadpoles travel on birds' feet and in overflow. Every frog in every bowl got there by accident, and the accidents are the gene flow, and the species is held together by plumage.

The frogs eat the mosquitoes. The mosquitoes eat the birds. The birds drink from the bowls the frogs sit in. There is no clean water and no organism that would recognise it as an improvement; every mouthful here has been inside several things already and is better for it.

Above them, the glowworms: blue-green pinpoints with beaded curtains, fishing. The light is a larva's hunger. The adults that emerge from it have no mouths. They fly four days on what the larva ate, mate in the dark, lay, and are finished. The only illumination native to this country is made by children who will grow up into things that cannot eat.

## X. The threshold

At dusk the bats come out for forty minutes and a hawk works the mouths, and there is a falcon at dawn that works the swiftlet stream, and both of them take from the edges, because the middle of a crowd is the only safe place in a crowd. The dawn rush is a lottery with a hundred and ten decibels of jamming over the top of it, and the birds at the margin die so that the geometry holds.

The entrance tunnels are angled steeply down so that rain runs out and snakes run into a dead end. In the dead ends there is now a spider. It has been there long enough to be endemic, long enough to be blind, long enough that its egg sacs are the reason the dead ends smell different from the rest of the gallery. The defence became a habitat. There is a small snake in two of the towers that has learned to hunt the dead ends, and it is thin enough, and it does not need to turn around either.

Nothing here is a lock that stays a lock.

## XI. What is actually resident

Add it up and stop calling it a colony.

There is a thing here with sensors: hens reading temperature to a degree, mice reading maintenance by ear, crickets announcing structural failure at six kilohertz, wasps measuring the distance between light wells with their lives. There is a thing here with effectors: nine million beaks tamping, ants paving, ants binding bone, beetles turning faces, a mycelium setting the thermostat of a highland. There is a setpoint — thirty degrees, four centimetres a year, one degree of compost — held through frost and drought and ninety-year storms. There is error correction: the towers lean a degree in a millennium and are straightened by asymmetrical repair that no bird intends. There is movement with a direction: three metres a decade north, into the rain-shadow, a decision made continuously by forty million animals none of whom is deciding.

There is memory, and it is in the stone. The mountain remembers the red berry that no longer grows. It remembers the century of the ropes. It remembers, as a slack grey quarter on the southern tower, a storm that happened before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, and it has not repaired it, and that is also a kind of remembering.

None of the sensors knows what the others are for. The hen has never heard of the wasp. The mouse steers by a sound the cricket makes while eating the house. The map is performed and not stored; the thermostat is run by orphans; the beams are ancestors; the load-bearing members are trees kept alive by an insect with two days to live.

It is not a mind. It is not a god, and it is not a superorganism, and calling it any of those flattens it into something with a centre. It has no centre. It has a *setpoint* and forty million tenants who each believe they are doing something small, and the thing that lives on the escarpment is the doing.

The birds are how it thatches. The bats are how it imports. The fungus is how it is warm. The mice are how it knows.

## XII. The fauna of the quiet

And in the grey quarter, where the storm opened the roof ninety years ago and the drip-line moved and the tamping stopped — something lives there too. Not nothing.

Husk-moths, whose larvae case themselves in shed skins and the feathers of birds that stopped coming, working through ninety years of undisturbed nest-lining: an archive being eaten by its own undertakers. Dry-skin beetles. A carton-rotting fungus that gives off no heat at all, the cold cousin of the furnace, moving through the galleries at the temperature of the outside air. Cisterns with cracked wax and a floor of dust and the desiccated shape of a frog that could not leave and did not.

The mice went in the first season. The crickets stayed until the stems went slack and then went too, and their silence is what you hear, which is to say what you hear is your own blood, because there is no longer anything here that needs to announce where the damage is.

This quarter is not a wound. It is the exhaust. It is where the city is being turned back into rock — the cream-and-ochre stone with the chitin lines and the fig-seed bands, the only substrate in the region that could be mistaken for natural. The quiet animals are the ones who make geology out of a place that has stopped being maintained. They are as endemic and as specialised as anything on the mountain and they have never been counted, because counting them requires standing somewhere no one wants to stand.

## XIII. You

You are not a resident and cannot become one. Nothing is sized for you: the doors are five centimetres, the layers are four, the frog's nation is two hands wide, the wasp's whole life is eight hundred metres, and your footfall compacts six chambers, which is six addresses, which is a hundred and eighty years of tenancy.

You are weather. Specifically you are a collapse — the same class of event as the storm on the southern tower, arriving on two legs, in one afternoon instead of one night.

Which is not nothing. A collapse opens a straight run to the sky. A straight run admits a green-white column full of dust and moths and falling seed. Where the column lands there is a garden, and in the garden a fig gets its roots into a buttress, and if — *if* — the next light well is within eight hundred metres, a two-millimetre animal with two days to live will find it by smell across the dark, and the fig will hold, and in sixty years the quarter you crushed will be structural.

That is the only way in. You cannot live here. You can be a hundred-year event that becomes a shaft of light.

And meanwhile, four metres under your boot, in a black that has weight, a thirty-gram bird who has never stood on anything comes home at forty kilometres an hour with five hundred metres of remembered geometry and a mouthful of plain, and does not slow down, and does not slow down, and goes in.
