## The Residents

Begin with the heat, because the heat was here first.

The crack was hot and dry for a long time before it filled, and something lived in it then. It is still there. In the wrist-wide vents, at sixty or seventy degrees, in water that never reaches the basin proper, there is a film that eats the rock's chemistry and has never encountered the sun. Call it the hearth. Its entire mass would fit in a bucket and it is spread through kilometers of fracture. It divides perhaps once a decade. It has been through every dry century the rims record and did not register any of them, because the plumbing does not empty when the room does.

Everything else here is either descended from it or fed by it. The mats on the floor — the root-hair miners dissolving fracture faces grain by grain — are its emigrants, cooled, spread out onto the fresh black surfaces where the light is worst and the phosphorus is best. All the phosphorus in every leaf in every lens came out of glass through those mats. The mountain is being eaten from the inside at about the thickness of a page a year, and the eating is the oldest continuous activity in the basin, older than the trees, older than the water.

The forest is the newest thing here. It arrived into an occupied house.

---

Three lenses, three different answers to the question of how many.

In the top lens there are perhaps nine thousand trunks and they are one plant. The roots grafted long ago, across the white rubble, before the water came; the grafts held; the thing walked in from the margin and has been filling the volume ever since. The eight-deep stacking of its leaves is not competition. Nothing in that lens is competing with anything. It is one organism's solution to the problem of catching all the light in a room, and it resembles a lung for the same reason a lung resembles it. When a bubble leaves a lenticel on the far side of the basin, it is leaving, at approximately the same moment, from every trunk you can see.

In the second lens the trees are separate and the white filament is not. The felt is continuous, seam to seam, one body wearing copper-red leaves as an arrangement of conveniences. It is also the only thing in the basin that routinely crosses a boundary, and it does so in seven places, thread-thin, kept open by dissolving its own margin as fast as it thickens. Those seven threads run down through the second seam and into the mining mats and back up, and they carry phosphorus. They are the vertical axis of a world that otherwise has no vertical traffic at all. Seven holes in a lid, held open by something that has an interest in holding them open.

In the third lens there are no countable individuals and no light-driven business. It is the hearth's own lifestyle, spread flat, and it does not distinguish itself from the rock it is consuming except by being slightly greener and very slightly warmer.

---

The seams are alive, and this is the part that takes longest to accept.

The boundaries do not hold because of density alone. They hold because something is holding them. At each interface there is a sheet one cell thick and kilometers wide, living on the difference between the two waters it separates — the gradient is its food, and a gradient is only food if it persists, so it persists it. It lays down a substance that thickens the boundary to a syrup, a skin, a glassiness you can see from the rim as a shelf in the blue.

When something dies at a seam, a hole opens. The lenses bleed for a day. The world doubles wrongly, trunks fork and do not rejoin, and the light on the floor goes to a smear that will not resolve. Then the sheet closes the hole from the edges, at the speed of tissue, and the shelves come back.

This is why pollen sits on a boundary for years. It is not resting. It is being held, and mostly refused. This is why a seed germinates sideways: down is not a direction here, it is a permission, and the permission is rarely granted. The seven open threads are foreign policy.

---

Inside the gas ceilings there are people.

Not many kinds — three or four — and they are the only fast lineage in the basin, the only ones with generations short enough to be improvised. Mite-sized, pale as the snails, and they live on the underside of the water: they walk on the ceiling of the dome by surface tension, hanging from a floor they cannot fall through, above a leaf they will never reach. For them the water is the ground and the leaf is a bright unreachable sky, and the room is the whole world, and the room is shrinking.

A dome fills over days and empties over weeks. Everything they do is fitted to that. They graze the film that grows on the inside of the bubble — a green scum living on the tree's own exhaled oxygen — and they lay eggs with sticky stalks that hold when the ceiling shreds. The rest go up. Some are caught on a leaf underside and climb, over a day or two, to the next dome. Most reach the surface, and the surface has striders on it.

They have no downward behavior at all. Nothing in their history has ever required one.

---

The striders are starving aristocrats and the only residents with sky. There are fewer of them than there are snails. They live on the film, which is mostly dust and refused pollen, and they wait, and their dimples look like accidents because they are almost never hunting. Their feast day is a shredding dome: a bubble bursts against the underside of the surface and delivers a ceiling's whole population onto the film at once. Then the dimples are not accidents. It is over in a minute and it is the only fast thing that happens in the basin, and it happens somewhere in the kilometer-wide plane perhaps twice a month.

The nitrogen comes from the crosser.

Twice a day, high up, something goes over — riding the thermal off the vents, never landing, going somewhere else. It is not a resident. It is the nitrogen budget. A feather, a dropping, a mite shaken off a foot: this is the entire import of fixed nitrogen into a volume of water that has no rivers and no fish and no rot. A feather lies on the bright plane for a season, walked over by striders, and then the seam takes it, and the taking is deliberate, and it is the largest single meal the boundary sheet receives all year.

Everything in the top lens is, in some traceable fraction, made of a bird that has never been here.

---

Now the snails, which can be counted, and which are the reason counting is possible at all.

A few hundred. Not a population in the usual sense — a set of individuals, each of which is a slow event with a location. They live two or three hundred years. Their shells are the same calcite as the columns they graze, laid down from the same water, so a snail is wearing the third terrace, or a pillar that used to be a canopy in a century nobody has a name for. Cut one open and the bands are the basin's bands: wet decades, dry decades, the whole clock, carried around at a millimeter an hour.

They never leave a surface. They cannot swim and would not try. The foot lays a ribbon of mucus and the ribbon limes behind them, so a snail's crossing leaves a fine raised thread of stone, permanent, and the columns are covered in these threads, layer on layer, three hundred years of routes. The comma-shaped grazing scars are the erasure. The columns are legible, if you have a century.

They broadcast along the seams. Gametes drift on the boundary for years, like the pollen, and are mostly refused, and occasionally are not. A snail's offspring may settle a hundred years after the parent has stopped, fertilized by a neighbor that died before the sperm arrived. This is why there are only a few hundred. It is also why there are still any.

When a snail dies its shell falls to a rim and becomes a nucleation point, and the water finds it and builds on it. Every fourth or fifth lip on the two-kilometer staircase began as a shell. The architecture is ancestry, and some of the ancestors had feet.

---

Not every pillar is a corpse.

When the level falls, most of the standing forest limes and is finished. Some of it goes into the stone on purpose: withdraws, concentrates, seeds itself into the calcite as cysts, and then helps build the pillar, thickening it toward where the light will be when the water returns. One pillar in forty has a living core. The grain inside is not tree grain. It spirals.

You cannot see this. The snails can. They rasp every white surface in the basin and they will not graze a sleeper — they go around, leaving a clean margin, an unmarked column among a thousand scribbled ones. That is the census. Walk the dry terraces and count the columns nobody has written on.

They are waiting for the crack and the hiss. Once a season, somewhere down the staircase, a rim gives way and water finds a new route, and a pool that has been thin air for a century is warm and clear again by evening, and the cores swell, and the pillar splits along its spiral, and the thing that stood there before stands there again, on its own shoulders, at the correct temperature.

---

On the fourth and seventh terraces down, among the sleepers, there are a handful of pillars that are shorter than the others and fork in places where a tree does not fork.

The snails go around those too.

Nobody made them and nothing was built here; everything was left. Someone came up over the saddle, and got in, and stayed past the point where the skin stops being a boundary, and stopped being a cooler patch, and went on being a surface — which is all the basin asks of anything, and it does not distinguish between kinds of surface. The lime came up the way it comes up a trunk. It took eighty years. There is a core.

They are waiting for the same sound as everything else on that terrace. It is not an unreasonable bet. It is the only bet available. It has paid out several times since they stopped moving.

---

And twice a year the rock makes its note.

It comes up through the floor and through the seams without doubling, because it is not light, and it arrives everywhere at once, intact, in the sternum of anything with a body. Nothing here has ears. Everything here is skin.

For a minute or two, the whole basin does the only coordinated thing it does. The clone vents in unison — a silver thread from every trunk at once, a rising shimmer, the ceilings refilling in an afternoon. The ceiling-walkers lay. The snails broadcast into the boundary, all few hundred of them, into a sheet that has gone slack and permeable for the duration, so that for one minute the seams are open and things cross that have been held for years. The miners release. The sleepers, in the dry terraces, in the dark inside the stone, turn over.

Then the sheet tightens, the shelves come back, the light resolves into a coin, and everyone returns to the only two motions there are.

That is the entire social life of the place. Two minutes, twice a year, in a medium that carries everything and has almost nothing to carry. The rest is tissue, and lime, and a finger's width, and the long patient conversion of a forest into a mountain, conducted by residents who are in no position to hurry and have never once been late.
