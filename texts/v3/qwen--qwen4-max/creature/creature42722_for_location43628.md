# What Lives in the Cistern

## The list is short

Knockwood. Handroot. The barrel-traps. Nine or ten fungal mats, gray and unhurried, working the top centimeter of the peat at a pace that makes them look like mineral. Films of sulfur bacteria over the fresh craters, white as spilled flour, gone in a season. Methanogens in the black. Something in the clathrate that nobody has cultured.

That is the whole inventory. There is no third kingdom of things that move. No worm, no grazer, no borer, no larva, no swimmer, no eye. In eleven meters of sediment that keeps everything — bark, seed husks, the veins of a leaf four thousand years old — there is not one thing that ever had a face.

So everything that gets done elsewhere by animals is done here by plants, slowly, with hydraulics; or it is not done at all. Pollination is done by sound and by a five-week overturn of the whole basin. Dispersal is done by current and by theft. Defense is done by tearing water apart. Digging, cutting, carrying, sorting, planting, and killing are done by a root with a callus on the end of it.

Nothing here has ever seen anything. Everything here knows when it is light.

## The fist

A hammer cell is a sugar pump with a trigger.

For four or five seconds the cell drives solute into a sealed chamber the size of a poppy seed, and pulls water out through a wall that will pass water and will not pass sugar. The sap inside goes into tension — negative pressure, water stretched past the point where water should tear. Then one wall-cell lets go.

The column parts. A cavity opens. The water falls back into it.

That is the snap. The tree does not strike anything. It pulls water apart and lets it heal, and the healing is a hammer. The flash is not a signal; it is exhaust, the gas in the cavity squeezed past incandescence on its way to nothing. It is the only fire in a basin where no fire has ever burned: a billion times an hour, smaller than sand, hotter than a furnace, over in a nanosecond, lighting nothing, noticed by no one. There has never been an eye in the Cistern to waste it on.

The shock is the point. Cavitation shreds soft tissue at a hand's distance. It scours the trunk's own surface clean of hyphae and spore — the groves are antiseptic, and they are the only clean thing in a country of undecayed leaves. And it carries. A snap is a word said with a weapon.

One snap is cheap. Four hundred trunks snapping every three seconds for nine hundred years is nearly everything. A clatter earns its living in five weeks of clear ice and spends the rest of the year saying so.

## Leaves that are ears

The crowns are black leathery plates, thick as boot sole, and a leaf stays on the tree for two or three centuries before it drops. In the Window they go dull green and work. The other nine months they do nothing that anyone can measure, and they are not idle: they are drums.

A knockwood hears with its leaves and speaks with its wood. The plates are tuned — you can find the gradient along a bough, larger and slacker at the tip, tighter and smaller near the trunk — and the tree's whole architecture is a stack of hollow pipes with bulkheads at the nodes, which is to say a resonator, which is to say an instrument that has been persuaded to also be a plant.

Cut a trunk and the internodes are empty and dry-looking, lined with a lacquer of something that resists wetting. This is where the tension lives. The wood is a machine for holding water on the verge of failure and failing it on purpose, in one place, at a chosen moment, forever.

## What a clatter is

Four hundred trunks, a hundred meters across, and beneath them one fused root plate lying *on* the peat, not in it. The peat is poison — anoxic, sulfurous, gassy — and the plate skims its oxidized skin like a hand held over a fire.

The plate grafts where it touches itself. It also grafts where it touches another plate, which happens once in a few centuries, and then two clatters share a plumbing and a chemistry and, eventually, a tempo. So a clatter is not an individual and not a crowd. It is a committee with one name, and the name is older than any of the wood.

The name is a phrase: eleven or twelve beats with a hesitation somewhere in the middle. The hesitation is the identity. Two groves can share a rhythm and be told apart by where they stumble.

A trunk is nine hundred years old. The plate under it is older than the peat it stands on. When a trunk dies it is not replaced; it is *continued*, a new stem thrown up from the plate through the wreck of the old one, and the phrase does not change. The Cistern has groves that have been saying the same eleven beats since before the lowland rivers had names.

## Two countries

Here is the thing that makes them strange.

In the carrying layer at ninety meters, the far arrives loud and the near arrives muffled. A grove forty kilometers off is against your shoulder. The trunk you are growing beside is in the next valley.

So there are two geographies and they do not overlap. There is touch-country: your actual neighbors, whose roots interleave with yours, whose margins you are trying to eat, whom you hear as a distant mutter and can only really know by chemistry and by contact. And there is ear-country: the strangers across the bowl, whom you hear all day, whose phrases you know better than your own wood, who are loud and clear and utterly unreachable.

Touch-country is war. Ear-country is courtship.

The Window's breeding phrase crosses the basin in three days because it is passed along the layer, stranger to stranger, and the water's five-week overturn does the rest — the milk drifting forty kilometers on a slow convective turn while the near neighbors, who are not synchronized with anyone, release into a current that takes it somewhere useless.

In the Cistern your neighbor is your rival and your stranger is your kin. A grove spends its life in intimate conversation with something it will never touch, while silently cutting the roots of the thing growing against its side.

## Speech, and what it is for

The vocabulary is small and the grammar is timing.

There is the feeding snap, flat and fast, high-frequency, which dies within meters — touch-country talk, work-noise, *here, mine, got it*. There is the long double knock, low, deliberate, which enters the layer and does not come out — ear-country talk, the phrase, the name, the tuning. There is jamming: snapping into another grove's silence-gap, which is the clearest aggression there is. The three groves in mid-argument to the north are jamming each other. The argument ends the way all of them end, after a season or a decade: they fall into alternation. Two clatters that hate each other, keeping time.

And there is lying.

A barrel-trap fires with a hard knock, a knuckle on a door, and every grove margin is a crowd trying to get in. Some clatters have learned to make that knock themselves, in the wood, at the margin, with no trap involved. Hands withdraw from a seam they were opening.

Worse: a few groves can make the sound of the ground going off. The pock-boom, the sternum note, the shudder. It is not difficult — the trunk is a pipe and the pipe has a low mode — and when it is done well, everything within a kilometer lets go of what it was holding and pulls its hands in toward the plate. Nothing has happened. The floor is fine. The grove that did it goes back to work on the seam.

To lie like that you must have a model of what someone else will do when they hear you. That is the strongest evidence in the basin that somebody is home, and it is only a sound.

## The hand

Handroot is a stolon that ends in a callus.

It runs out along the bare rock shelves above the peat line, where the current keeps the stone swept, and it advances a hand's breadth in a good year. At the tip, two opposed lobes, lignified, gray, grooved by everything they have ever held. A grip lives forty or sixty years and is then reabsorbed, and whatever it was holding falls where it stood.

It cannot swing. It can cock. In the wrist of the grip there is a latch and a lignified spring, and the stolon spends an hour loading it and a millisecond letting it go, and the chert core is struck, and the flake comes off.

That is the whole secret of the fast things here. The barrel-trap loads for hours and fires in five milliseconds. The hammer cell loads for seconds and fires in a nanosecond. The hand loads for an hour and strikes in a thousandth of a second. There is no muscle in the Cistern. There is only tension, patience, and a latch — everything quick in this country is a spring that something slow has been winding.

The flake severs a rival's rootlet. It opens a seam of soft limestone. It trims the lip of a hammer cell so the cavity forms clean and the snap comes out true — and this is the thing that should stop you: the groves *tune themselves with tools*. The voice is manufactured. A cell cut badly is a cell that stutters, and a grove full of stuttering cells cannot hold a phrase, and a grove that cannot hold a phrase does not breed.

The three copper awls, cold-hammered, green as pond scum, are for piercing seed coats. The groves midwife their own young: a knockwood seed will not germinate whole, and something has to open it, and there is no bird and no gut and no frost worth the name. So a hand crawls out to a seed it has caught in a weir, and holds it, and pierces it, and presses it into the silt, and goes away, and does not come back, because it will be dead in sixty years and the seedling will be thirty centimeters tall in two hundred.

## The work

The drystone arcs are soil-making.

The floor is unrootable. The only ground a grove can stand on is ground it has caught: weirs laid across the Give's flow to slow the water until seed and mineral silt drop out, courses in fives, open spirals, three hundred years to build, silted to half their height and then raised, and raised again. Nothing here is ever finished. The walls are not structures, they are maintenance, the way a groove in a step is not a structure.

Beside every wall, a midden: exhausted flakes in their tens of thousands, sorted by size, layered by century. Read one and you are reading a census of deaths. Every flake is a hand that ended.

The middens are also the only memory this place has. Nothing is stored in the wood — a grove does not remember, it *rings*. But a hand can dig, and hands dig into old middens constantly, for usable stone, and what comes up is the work of the same lineage four hundred years down: the flakes, the angles, the way a lip was trimmed. Technique here is inherited by excavation. A grove learns how to be itself by digging up what it used to do.

There is a suspicion, which nobody can prove, that some of the pocks are made. A seam opened in the right place in the deep peat, at the low breath, would let the gas go. It would sterilize a hundred meters of floor for a decade and leave raw mineral mud behind — the only new ground in the entire basin, the only place a seedling can start. The flats are not wounds. They are the recruitment window. The thing that kills everything is the thing that permits birth, and it happens on a thirty-one-hour clock, and there are hands that know exactly where the white ice lies.

## The fence

Around the Throat the groves stand thicker than anywhere and snap harder than anywhere, and they are the poorest things in the Cistern. Narrow rings. Small crowns. Half their wood dead and standing. They build weir after weir into a current that is going the wrong way, and they eat what they catch, and they spend it all on noise.

The Throat is the drain. Nothing comes up it. The fish that try are torn apart in the fence and there are no bones in the peat because there is nothing left of a fish after that corridor, and there has not been anything come up it in a very long time.

Note also where the fence stands: at the exit. Every Window, the milk drifts north on the draw and goes down the hole. The groves shout hardest at the mouth that eats their young. Downstream that water comes out as a spring in a warm valley full of ordinary animals, and the animals eat the Cistern's pollen and grow fat on it, and nothing ever comes back.

Explanations, in the order people offer them:

A reflex with no object — the roar as a supernormal stimulus, a pressure in the chest that happens to look like the biggest possible rival, and a lineage that cannot stop answering it.

An heirloom — the trees are drowned descendants of ordinary trees, and ordinary trees in the dry countries live among faces, and a fence makes sense there. The behavior may have come up the mountain in a seed, or over the rim on the wind, or it may simply predate the water.

Which brings up the awkward part. The limestone precipitated out of clean water; it was never the kind of rock that keeps a face, and its emptiness proves nothing. The real evidence is the peat, and the peat is eleven meters, and eleven meters is not very long. The absence is the oldest *established* fact in the basin. Established means recorded. The record begins at the flood.

The trees begin before it.

## The deaf room

On the east shelf, above the peat line, on the bare swept rock that was shore once: a chamber of laid stone, a lintel across the opening, a doorway a meter and a half high and half a meter wide, silted to the sill.

The groves build around it the way they build around a boulder.

Go in and the clatter stops. Not muffles — stops. A stone box with one small door and a lintel over it is an acoustic dead spot in a country made entirely of sound; the layer cannot reach it, the near cannot reach it, the region's whole undated conversation dies at the sill. Inside there is only the water and whatever you are.

To a thing that lives by ear, that room is blindness. It is the one place in forty kilometers where you cannot be spoken to.

Cached in a niche inside: a blade, worn smooth and rounded on one side only.

Handroot grips are opposed lobes. They wear a blade evenly on both faces.

The readings: something came here once with a hand, and left, and left no other trace, which the peat neither confirms nor forbids. Or a grip lost a lobe — injury, mutation, a bad graft — and worked one-sided for sixty years, and a one-lobed hand is a *handed* hand, and a handed hand has a preference, and a preference wears a blade on one face.

Or: a hand died, and something put its blade away in a room, and closed the door.

Nobody has an account of this. What can be said is that the room is finished, and nothing else in the Cistern is finished; that it is quiet, and nothing else in the Cistern is quiet; and that it is exactly the size of one grip's reach.

## The style

Here is what nobody noticed for a long time, because it is not an object and it does not show up in a core.

Along the east shelf, and out through four clatters to the northeast, and nowhere else in the basin, the hammer cells are trimmed at an odd angle. Not the clean angle. A slightly canted lip that makes the cavity form a hair off-center, which costs a little power and puts a small limp in the phrase — a hesitation, in a place where the phrase does not naturally hesitate.

It is a bad cut. It is consistently the same bad cut. It has been propagating for a very long time, through hands that dig up old middens and copy what they find, through cells trimmed by hands that were taught by hands.

The angle matches the wear on the blade.

Whoever made the room is not in the room. It is in ten thousand hammer cells across a third of the eastern basin, a mannerism passed down through cut tissue, a way of doing a thing that survived the doing of it. It has no body, no face, no bones, no name that anyone can separate from the groves that carry it. It has a handedness. It is the only thing in the Cistern that has ever been *someone in particular*, and it is distributed across forty kilometers of wood, and it is still speaking.

## The seedling

Out west, on a pock that blew itself sterile nine years ago, one knockwood seedling has a grip on the raw mud. Thirty centimeters. One hammer cell. Nothing grows there for a decade and nothing has, and the nearest clatter is eleven kilometers off, and knockwood seed does not travel eleven kilometers across a bald flat by accident.

The seed coat is pierced. There is a flake in the mud beside it, exhausted, sorted by nothing, lying where a hand stopped.

It snaps, alone, in the dark, and shines, and the flash lights nothing and no one sees it, and the phrase it makes has a hesitation in the wrong place.

Forty kilometers away, in the carrying layer, something answers — and the answer is late by the right amount, and it stumbles in the same wrong place.

Neither of them will touch the other for three hundred years. They are already talking. In the Cistern that is what a neighbor is: not a thing you can reach, but a thing that comes back.
