## The Residents

Start with what can be counted, because the count is short and most of it is fringe.

Cave crickets in the entrance rift, in the wet clay where the roof is on your shoulder blades, and no farther — they turn back at the seventh step, or their descendants do. Harvestmen in the same stretch, four or five, on the underside of the slab that overhangs the crawl. Six herald moths on the ceiling above the shelter, folded like dead leaves, occupying one hand's breadth of rock. The number has been six for as long as anyone has bothered to write it down, which is not evidence that there are six; it is evidence that nobody wants to put a lamp close enough to check, and that *six* has become the word for the moths. Five bats in the dry alcove above the tailwater. A dipper at the resurgence, forty minutes downhill, walking underwater into the current. A wren in the sink ferns, which is a very small bird with a very large voice, and which is the thing you notice you have lost.

None of those live in the room. They live at its lip, in the green seam of the world, and they use the Quiet the way a person uses a doorway. The bats come closest to being residents, and for them the chamber is exactly what it looks like: an enormous empty barn with a river in it, silent as a held breath, because the whole vast architecture of the chord sits below the floor of what they listen with. Five animals in the loudest place in the country, in perfect quiet, hunting the entrance. Nobody has ever been able to explain this to a novice in a way that satisfies.

What lives *inside* is stranger and slower and mostly does not move.

---

The green seam on the east wall is not a stripe. It is a migration.

The moss that makes it is a spring-moss, the calcicole kind, the sort that builds stone as it goes — it takes the lime out of the weeping water and lays it down in a crust behind itself, and it can only do this in light, and there is only eleven minutes of light a day and it moves. So the seam is an animal a year long. Its head is bright and wet and about a forearm wide, and it is somewhere on the wall in the eleven minutes when the thread crosses; its tail is brown, then pale, then a hard grey rind of tufa laid down where it used to be. It grows into the light and dies out of it and the dead part holds the moisture for the living part, so the whole thing walks down the wall from midsummer to midwinter, and at midwinter it does not walk at all, because the thread does not arrive, and it waits — brown, encrusted, alive at the margin — for the light to come back high on the flowstone boss and start the year again.

Behind it, on the wall, is the record of every crossing since the choke jammed: a laminated ribbon of laid-down calcite, faint as a scar, thin as a fingernail paring per decade. You could count it. Nobody does. The keepers know that the seam is the oldest continuously living thing in the room and they do not have a word for it, only a hand-shape: the flat palm drawn slowly downward, which is also the sign for *autumn*, and also, in a different context, the sign for *wait*.

The lamp-greens are its opposite. Hundreds of niches, each with a green shadow, each shadow a colony founded on tallow smoke — the only food in the deep that arrives already burned. They are not one organism and they do not move. They are a scatter of small dim stars, and each one marks a place where somebody once stood with a lamp and shuttered it with a palm: two beats, *come*; three beats, *don't*. That is the entire archive of the Quiet. Not what was said. Only where a person stood when they called someone, or warned them off. As tallow is rationed tighter — and it is, every generation, because the coast is poorer and the walk is longer — the niches at the far end go unused, and their greens go brown, and one by one the stars go out. There were more when the ladder was first pegged. There will be fewer.

---

The moonmilk is alive. This is the practical secret of the place and the reason anyone comes with a horn and a bone spatula.

It is not a mineral, or not only. It is a paste of stone and the filaments of something that makes stone — a slow white felt of actinomycete threads, cold, slick, thick as clotted cream in the seams, precipitating lime out of the weep at a rate that cannot be seen in a lifetime and can be seen in three. It is scraped, carried out, smeared on cracked hands and on wounds, and it works about as well as anything else does, which is to say it works, and no one has ever needed a better reason.

So the moonmilk is the room's flesh, and it is eaten, and it is carried away, and it grows back over forty or sixty years, and the keepers scrape it in rotation — an alcove a season, then left — the way you coppice a hazel. They are farmers of a thing that takes two generations to crop. This is the only agriculture in the Quiet and it is conducted entirely in the dark by touch, by the difference between paste that is thick enough and paste that is still only wet.

One alcove is not scraped. The finger-flutings: three grooves drawn down through the paste at the height of a tall adult's reach, made by a child standing on something that is not there now. The moonmilk has not filled them. It will not fill them for a long time. There is a rule about that alcove, and unlike the eel it has a reason, and the reason is so obvious that no one has ever had to say it, which is why it has never been said.

---

The eel is a person. Not legally, not metaphysically; in the way that a thing becomes a person when it has been known individually, by touch, by more than one generation, and has a name that cannot be spoken aloud because nothing can be spoken aloud.

Its name is a hand-shape: the wrist turned, the fingers closing on nothing. It means *the thick one*, roughly, or *the one that comes*. It lives in a rimstone pool near the second boulder, behind a lip of calcite that took four thousand years to build, and it is older than the last four ladders, and it came up from the sea in a flood and never went back down. It knows the room the way the keepers know it: as pressure. A hand entering the water pushes a wave ahead of it, and the eel reads that wave along its whole length, and it has learned — over decades, at the pace at which anything here learns — that the wave means food, because people who have just finished saying something they needed to say tend to drop something in the pool on their way past. Not an offering. Nobody would call it that. A habit that has become a behaviour that has become a relationship.

It has never been eaten. There is no reason for that, and they do it anyway.

---

The keepers are not a tribe and not a family. They are a rota.

Nine, in a good year; six, in a bad one; never more than four inside at once, and that is a rule with arithmetic behind it: a null is roughly coffin-sized, two people fit in one lying side by side, and the shelter sleeps four head-to-feet. A fifth would have to stand in the noise, and standing in the noise for a night is how you get the nosebleed, the blurred vision, the jaw that aches for a week.

They come from the coast, from the townships downhill where the moonmilk goes, and the post passes not by blood but by return: whoever keeps coming back is a keeper. Most were once novices who could hear. Some were people who came to say something and found they could not stop coming. There is a woman who arrived to forgive a debt and stayed eleven years and can now find a null in the dark by the change of pressure on her cheek alone, without the cupped hand, and she is the best there has been.

Physically: broad-chested, mostly, or they seem so; they breathe shallowly and deliberately, because deep breathing in the fundamental is like leaning on a door that is leaning back. They walk with the sole flat, heel-first, reading the floor. They have callus on the heel of the palm from laying it on wet rock. They are frequently nosebleedy in the first year and never afterward, or they are gone. Their hands are never still, and their hands are not expressive in the way a hearing person's hands are expressive; the signs are small, close to the body, made to be read at arm's length by touch as often as by lamplight. Half the vocabulary is contact. When the hands are full there are the nine or ten taps on the shoulder blade — *stop*, *here*, *hold*, *this one is mine* — and one more that is not operational at all, a single flat tap, low, between the shoulder blades, which means nothing but *someone is here*. It is used constantly. It is used the way people say *mm* in a room where somebody is reading. New keepers find it maddening for a month and then cannot do without it.

They have hand-names, given by habit or by scar: the one who counts wrong; nine-steps; the pecked thumb. They sleep in the corbelled shelter with the hearth outside, and they cook outside, and they will not carry an ember in past the turn of the rift.

Nobody has ever lit a fire in the Quiet, and this is not reverence. Warm air is thin air; thin air retunes the room; retune the room and every null in it moves at once, and every cup-mark on the floor becomes a lie, and the next person to lie down in a ringed mark lies down in an antinode. A fire would blind the green seam with soot inside a week. A fire would move all the rooms. It is the one thing that could make the place unnavigable, and so it is simply not done, and the not-doing has hardened into something that looks exactly like a taboo and is only a very old safety rule that everyone has stopped explaining.

---

The apprentices are children, and this is the ugly part.

You cannot find a null with a deaf ear. You find it with a cupped hand behind an ear that still works, walking slowly, listening for the place where the hammer lifts off your skull and your own breathing becomes embarrassingly loud. The deaf can find them by pressure, eventually, after years, and the best of them can do it faster than a hearing novice — but the annual re-cut in February, when the ice cone has moved everything, is done by children, because it has to be done thoroughly and it has to be done fast.

They are eleven, twelve, thirteen. They go down the thirty-one dished steps with a lamp and a pecking stone and they spend the season lying in coffins of silence, listening for the edges of them, and an old keeper stands at their shoulder with a hand on the shoulder blade and cuts the ring where the child points.

A season of it costs them something. Two seasons cost more. The going-deaf is not sudden and is not total and is not the same in any two of them; it arrives as a narrowing, a loss of the top of the world first — the hiss where the spray shatters, then birds, then the high part of a voice — until what is left is the fundamental, which they will feel in the sternum for the rest of their lives whether they stay or go. Some go. The ones who go are the ones who did it twice and then walked out and did not come back, and they live downhill and are known, and are given things, and are not asked to talk about it.

The keepers do not describe this as a sacrifice. They describe it as the work. Somebody has to have ears to spend, and it may as well be somebody who has not yet used them on anything important. That is the reasoning, and it is offered flatly, and it is not entirely honest, and everybody knows that too.

---

And then there are the residents that are not made of anything.

The keepers' sign for a null is the flat hand laid palm-down in the air, and the closest gloss is *room*. Not silence. A room: a small coffin-sized room inside the big room, where you can be spoken to. There are hundreds. They are the population of the Quiet, and they are the only thing in it that behaves.

They are born at the pool edge, mostly, in the spray, where the field is most crowded with folding. They drift. They lengthen and thin as the tailwater rises; they slide bodily uphill when the tide backs the conduit and drowns the lowest step of the fall and the fundamental drops; they cluster and multiply in February when the ice cone stands four men high and the whole chamber sings flatter and shorter, so that the year's largest census is taken in the coldest month by children with lamps. In nine days the cone collapses and half of them die at once. In summer, when the cave exhales and the air goes warm and light, they stretch out long and lazy toward the boulders and there are fewer of them and they are easier to lie in.

They have behaviour, in other words. They have a season and a habitat and a birth rate. They can be predicted — the good keepers can call where a given one will be at a given tide, the way a shepherd knows where a sheep will go — and they can be lost, and once lost they do not always come back.

The floor is stippled with hundreds of cup-marks and most of them are archaeology: dead rooms, other people's silence, a braille of places where it was once safe to speak. The living ones are ringed with a fresh pecked circle, and the ring is the whole of the meaning. Twice a year someone walks the handline in the dark, hand over hand, counting knots — one for a turn, two for a living room, three for the drop, the fat doubled knot for the edge where the floor stops — and re-ties, and the rope takes up a little more slack and gets a little shorter, and no fibre in it is original and it is the same rope.

The tally stick has fourteen notches. Four are for things that are no longer here. The fourth was cut for a room: one near the second boulder that everybody used, that everybody had used for generations, that shifted a hand's breadth when the fall's lip retreated and then was simply not anywhere, and could not be found, and was looked for over three seasons by a child who spent her hearing on it.

They cut a notch for it. It is the only thing on the stick that was never matter.

---

Recount, then. The fringe: crickets, harvestmen, six moths, five bats, a dipper, a wren. The green seam, which is one animal a year long. Several hundred lamp-greens, going out. The moonmilk, which is alive and is eaten and is farmed. One eel, which has a name. Nine keepers, four at a time. Some children, temporarily. And several hundred moving absences that are herded, counted, named, mourned, and are the reason any of the rest of it is here.

But the largest population is not in the room, and cannot be, and this is the thing about the Quiet that the keepers know and do not say because there is no sign for it.

Everyone who spends an hour in it carries it out. For three days it is in the rain on their windows and in the shell and in the house at night, and then it fades and the wren comes back. For most. For some it does not fade; it thins to a floor beneath everything, a forty-cycle pressure that is not there, that they will lie in bed and listen to at seventy years of age in a dry stone house forty minutes uphill with the wind combing the hazel. There are perhaps a few hundred such people along that coast, in the townships, going about their business, deafened in a way no doctor would recognise, each of them carrying a small hard-walled room inside their head with a river falling into it that never stops giving it sounds.

That is what lives there. It lives there by living elsewhere. The chamber has no offspring and no seed and no spores; it has the eleven-minute thread, the green ribbon, the paste, the eel, and a handful of people who read the floor through their soles — and it has all of that going out through the letterbox in horns and in ears, into the smirr and the ordinary wind, and spreading.

The keepers are not the ones who live in the Quiet.

They are the ones the Quiet lives in.
