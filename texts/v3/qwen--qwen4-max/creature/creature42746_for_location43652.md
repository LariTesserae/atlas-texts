# What Lives in the Slake

## The one that is not visiting

Begin under everything, with the nap, because the nap is the only resident in the ordinary sense: it was not delivered here, it does not commute, it does not boom and crash. It is a millimeter of felt and it is continuous across the entire floor, white where the sulfur is, rust where the iron is, slate-grey and pond-smelling where the methane seeps, and if you traced it with a finger you would never find an edge, only a change of appetite.

It is also the only thing in the basin that experiences being buried as weather. Everything else is killed by the stone. The mat is *fed* by it — grows up through the fresh travertine as fast as the travertine arrives, so that a hand's depth of bench is a hundred pages of the same sentence. The mat has no organs and no shape and no front or back. It has extent. It is the largest animal in the Slake, if you are willing to call it an animal, and it is the only thing here that has never had to learn the rule.

At night it shines. Not for us. The gauze come to the glow, the way midges come to any glow, and they settle on the tacky lip beside the seep and are held, and by morning there is a fringe of white grains at the water's edge that was yesterday a swarm, and the mat digests them at its leisure, which is a speed rather than a duration. The mat is the one patient thing in a place that executes patience. It gets away with it because it is a film, and a film cannot stand still, because it has no legs to stand on.

## The tide with backs

The scutch are palm-sized and armored and there is no useful number for them. But it is a mistake to count them, in the way it is a mistake to count the water in a wave. The sheet is a hundred meters across and three animals deep and it flows toward the best-running seep at about the speed of a slow walk, and it does things no scutch can do. It reads the Sour. The leading edge lifts and turns away from a hollow twenty minutes before the gas arrives in it, and no individual in the sheet has sensed anything; the turn propagates backward through touching bodies like a shiver through a horse's flank. The sheet knows where the air is going. The scutch do not.

They rasp nap with a mouth like a file, and the sound of ten million of them filing is the floor of the sound of the Slake, under the hiss, a dry continuous *shush* that you stop hearing on your second day and never stop hearing again.

And when a weir breaches and three hundred meters of Combs drains in four minutes, the sheet does what a tide does when the sea leaves: it goes to the low place. The tubs fill with backs, and then with climbing backs, and by evening there is a crust at the lip, a pavement of dead scutch a hand thick and twenty meters long, and the water seals it into the floor within the week.

Which means the terrace lips are partly made of animals. Which means the blocks are. Saw a loaf out of a fast front and hold it up to the sun and you can see them in it, faint as thumbprints in dough, hundreds of curved backs in cross-section, the ghosts of midge cases sifting between them. The cutters' houses are built of the dead. Their lime is burned from the dead. There is no word in their sixty signs for this that carries any feeling at all.

The crust is also harvest. It is chitin, and it burns, and fuel is the scarcest thing in the basin after wood and copper. Children go out with silk nets and gather the crust into gut bags, and it is dried on the kiln flues, and it fires the kilns that make the lime that softens the hides that you sleep on so that you do not become the floor. The crash is the crop. Nobody down here waits for a good season. They wait for a bad one.

## The chord

The gauze live in the nap as larvae in tapered cases of travertine grit and silk, and the shallows are coarse sand until the sand begins to travel. In the warm hours they come up in columns, forty at once over the Combs, each a rope of insects two meters thick and three hundred tall, leaning, humming.

The chord is an instrument panel and the cutters read it like one. A column's note rises with the richness of the nap under it, so a new seep front announces itself as a sharpening somewhere out in the Combs a day before anyone can see the water change; and a column that drops out of the chord altogether means the Sour has come up through the shallows and taken it, and everyone on that bench looks at their lamp. There are men in this basin who can tell you where the stone will be soft tomorrow by lying on a hide with their eyes shut, listening to forty bowed saws, and they are never wrong by more than a hundred meters.

The columns are also dinner. At the peak of an emergence the fall is thick enough to sweep, and it is swept with silk nets and pressed into cakes on a flat block and dried hard and grey and faintly of ponds. Gauze bread. Grain from the rim is the staple and the luxury; gauze bread is what you actually eat, standing up, walking, because you must keep walking.

Their shed cases drift into every hollow and crack and lee, a gritty white accumulation that is the nearest thing here to soil, and which is not soil, because nothing will grow in it. Seeds come in through the Breach in the storm-wind and germinate on the damp benches and die in a week, white and swollen, and for those weeks the terraces are dotted with small pale ghosts of a country four hundred meters up. The scutch rasp them. Nothing else notices them at all.

## The commuters

The shafters do not live here. This is important and the cutters are precise about it. The shafters *pay* here.

They nest in the wall cavities in colonies of tens of thousands and they go out through the Breach at dawn and spend the day over the green windy ordinary country with the larks, eating insects that ate plants, and they come back at dusk in a river that takes eleven minutes to pass. That river is the basin's clock — the cutters' day is divided by it, *before the river*, *the river*, *after* — and it is also the basin's entire income of nitrogen and phosphorus. Every gram of it falls on the walls below the colonies, and those walls are white-streaked and stinking and are the thickest, fastest, richest stone in the Slake, and the mats there grow like a fever, and the scutch sheets climb toward them and thin out and die on the way, and the gauze columns over the guano benches are the loudest and highest-pitched in the basin, and the cutters work those fronts and are hated for it by everyone downstream.

The shafters have almost no feet. They cannot perch; they cling. A shafter that comes down on the wet floor is a dead shafter, and by the following week it is a small sooty sickle-winged shape in cream rock, and there are tens of thousands of those too.

They are the only things in the Slake that have solved the problem by simply not being here for most of the time, and the cutters regard them with something that is not quite envy and not quite contempt: the sign for shafter is also the sign for a man who trades on the rim and keeps a wife up there.

## The punished

The stilts are waist-high and pale and they pick through the scutch sheets in the shallows, and their whole inheritance is a hunting method: stand still, wait, strike. It worked for a hundred thousand years in a country where standing still cost you nothing.

Here it costs you your ankles in an hour and your knees in a day and your life in a week, and the basin is now full of the results, tens of thousands of stilt-shaped columns mid-preen, mid-strike, mid-nothing, feather-crisp outside and void within.

So the survivors are the ones that fidget. There is a lineage in the northern Combs — the cutters have a sign for it, two fingers waggling — that cannot stand still at all. They shift, they step, they lift each foot in turn and set it down somewhere else, a slow compulsive wading dance that never stops while they feed, and they take fewer scutch per hour than a proper stilt and they are alive. Their chicks that inherit the stillness are columns by the end of the season. It is happening fast enough to watch. A cutter of forty has seen the dance become common. His grandmother's stories had stilts in them standing like posts.

The hooks have the same problem from the other end. A mantis-shaped ambush predator in a rim-lip is a machine for holding one position for a long time, and the Slake bills it accordingly: there is a cast in the eastern Combs of a hook with a column's worth of gauze still legible in its open mouth, which is to say it was successful, and it was successful for slightly too long. The hooks that persist are the patrolling ones, the restless ones, the ones that walk the lips taking gauze by the hundred and never settle, and they are the reason children go out with the copper-headed club.

There are eleven club heads in the basin. Native copper, cold-hammered, drilled, lashed to a shaft of bundled and lacquered silk cord because there is no wood, re-lashed every season. The clubs belong to knots, not to people, and they have two jobs. One is hooks. The other is the neighbors.

## The monthly

The Sump is deep and permanent and unpleasant and it is the only water in the Slake that does not come and go, and out of it, in flood pulses from the aquifer, come the blind eels: arrive, breed in a week, die in a week, and once a month the southern shore is a wrack of them a meter deep and half a kilometer long.

Nobody fishes. Fishing would be a waste of a day. They wait for the water to fish, and then they walk the wrack with barrows, and the eels are split and hung in the kiln flues and rendered, and the oil goes into the lamps whose flame height tells you whether the air will let you live, on wicks of twisted larval silk. The basin's light is made of the basin's dead, and it is the light you check to find out whether you are about to be dead. The stink of a rendering week carries to the rim.

Beyond the wrack, past the last hard knob, the Sump goes down into water that has not met air in a long time, and no stone is being added there, and it is therefore the only place in eleven kilometers where an old surface might be lying exposed. Nobody has gone down. There is no reason to go down. There is a sign that is used only about the Sump, and it is not one of the sixty, and if you ask what it means the answer is a shrug and the sign again, and the fact that a people with sixty words have made a sixty-first for one hole in the ground tells you what they think lives there.

## The neighbors

Now the third population, the one that is neither alive nor mineral, and which is the largest body of individuals in the basin.

The Castings are hollow. Inside every stilt-shaped column is a stilt-shaped absence, and inside that absence, in a bowl full of heavy gas, is Sour. A cast is a bottle of dead air with a bird painted on the outside. They stand in ranks by the tens of thousands and they hold their breath for years, and when the four-o'clock storm slams its rotating slab of wind down the wall, they breathe out, and a knee-height wave of it goes across the Combs and kills whatever is small and standing there.

So the children go out with the clubs and vent them. You crack an old cast at the shin and step back and let it exhale, and you lower a lamp on a silk cord into the break, and if the flame goes out you have done a public service, and if it burns you have found a nest. Because things live in the voids: hooks overwinter in the femurs of stilts, gauze pupate in the throat of a cast scutch, scutch shelter by the hundred in the hollow ribcage of something nobody has named, and the mats line the insides and glow faintly in there, so that on a warm night the Castings are full of small blue-green lit windows, thousands of them, at every height, and the whole field looks like a town.

And in wind, the Castings sing. Every hollow shape with a crack in it has a note. A fresh cast with an open beak is a high thin whistle; as it fills from below and the void shrinks, the note falls; a half-buried grandmother is a low moan you feel in the chest; a sealed one is silent. The field is an organ played by the weather, and it detunes itself year by year, and the cutters navigate by it. *Turn where the two high ones are. Not that one — that one's gone flat.*

There is a sign for a cast that has stopped sounding. It is used about the dead, and it is used about the dead only, and it does not mean *forgotten*. It means *sealed*.

## The forty

Twelve knots, forty people, twenty square kilometers, no town. From the rim they are specks and the smoke of their kilns.

Their names are hand-signs. This is not a cultural flourish; it is acoustics. In a place where you shout or use your hands, a spoken name is useless — you cannot call anyone, ever, from more than ten meters. So a name is a gesture, and to be named you must be *seen*, and a person alone in a hollow is temporarily nameless, and a person up on the rim among the farmers is nameless for the whole visit and comes home hungry for it. The sign for a woman is two fingers drawn down the cheek; the sign for her brother is a fist turned over. Names are not inherited. They are given at about a year, by whoever is watching, and they are usually about the water: *Nine Tubs*, *Long Lip*, *Slack Front*, *Under-Fall*. They are named for places, and the places are gone — swallowed, buried, breached, drained — so a name is the last surviving trace of a terrace that no longer exists, and no one can read it, and they know this, and they go on doing it.

Their speech is small and their tense is strange. Most sentences are about the water and require the water to be present, which means pointing; which means you cannot talk about a place you are not standing in; which means the past is nearly unsayable, and there are no old inscriptions to say it for you, because the Reading Stone is wired smooth every month and everything anyone ever wrote is inside the rock. What they inflect for is *tomorrow*. There is a form of the sign for *soft* that means *will-be-soft*, and it is the most-used word in the language, and the whole of their attention is conjugated into it. They stand in their doorways at evening and read the reorganized flow and say where the stone will be soft, and that is their literature.

The household's most valuable member is a bird. Not a shafter — a shafter cannot be caged, it needs sky. A small dull finch from the rim, bought as a nestling for grain, kept in a cage of white lime-plaster bars sewn with silk, carried on a strap, fed on gauze bread crumbled in the fingers. It is a child's charge. A bird that stops singing means the air is going; a bird on the cage floor means run, now, uphill, and don't stop to be polite. Birds die down here in a year or two of heat and bad air and are constantly re-imported, and when one dies you do not throw it away. You set the cage on a wet lip at the edge of the hollow that killed it, and go back a week later, and there is a small white cage with a small white bird inside it, standing in the shallows, and it means *the air here killed one*. There are hundreds of them along the low routes. They are the only warnings the basin has that are not cairns, and cairns sink.

Everything else follows from the rule, which every child learns before its name: **keep moving, or become the floor.** Boots are greased and flexed hourly. Tools are on cords around your neck. You sleep on a hide on a dried knob and someone moves the hide, which means someone is awake, which means sleep is a rota, which means no adult in the Slake has ever slept a whole night alone and unwatched. Hospitality is not food; hospitality is being kept awake. To be a guest is to be walked gently around a bench in the dark by someone who is tired. Courtship is the same act performed all night with the walking slowed down, and a marriage is a promise to wake the other, and the worst thing one person can say about another — the sign is a flat hand laid palm-down on the air, and it is not used lightly — is *he let me sleep*.
