## The Shift

The first residents to arrive at any event are the ones who don't live here.

When the siphon primes and eleven hundred liters come over the crest, the water brings *Niphargus* with it — a burst of white commas, maybe two thousand animals in forty seconds, and every one of them was already alive and already hungry somewhere under the river terrace before the roof opened. This is the crack in the room's tidy arithmetic. Everything in the census is thirty-one, descended from the collapse and the first flush, one founding inventory. But the amphipods were not founded. They were interrupted. The room broke into their house.

They live in the flooded loop and the fissure network behind it, a country of interstices with no rooms in it at all, no volume, no dome, nothing that could be photographed. They have been in that water since long before there was a hole in the beech wood. When the siphon fires they are ejected into a space forty thousand cubic meters wide, they hang there in the lit shallows for four minutes, and then the level drops and they go back down through the throat with the wrack and the silt. Nineteen minutes later, more or less, they do it again.

So the room's largest population is a commute. It has no address here. It uses the place the way a village uses a market square: intensely, briefly, on a schedule set by something else.

Which raises the question the census can't answer by counting. *Live* is a word about grandchildren. By that standard the shrimp are tourists, and the animals that never leave — the ones you could photograph and name and come back to in six years and find in the same place — are a much smaller and stranger company.

## The Basin Holders

There are three pools, but there are thirty-one basins.

The floor is a jigsaw of collapse blocks that haven't drifted apart, and the water lies over that jigsaw, so each pool is not a pond but a set of pockets separated by stone lips a few centimeters under the surface. Thirty-one pockets. Thirty-one olms, one to a pocket, and the map has not changed in six years.

They are almost certainly a single clutch. One gravid female came down the first flush or through the sump in the first year, laid, and was gone — flushed, or eaten by her own offspring, or simply dead of the ordinary business of being a fish-shaped thing in a hole. What she left is a sibship: thirty-one brothers and sisters, all the same age, all pale as machined bone, all holding property against each other with a lateral line that can feel a shrimp turn over four body-lengths away.

The map was drawn by attrition and then it froze. There were more once. The basins of the missing were absorbed, the boundaries argued out along the stone lips through the slow cold years, and by year twenty-five the settlement was complete and nobody has contested it since. The largest female holds the pocket under the sump's lip and takes the first of every flush. The smallest holds a corner of the middle pool that gets nothing but the overflow and the moat from the ice, and she is thin, and she has been thin for six years, and she has not moved, because moving means crossing a neighbor.

Here is the thing about them that reorganizes the room a second time, the way the four-centimeter straws reorganize it the first: they are babies. *Proteus* breeds at fifteen and can live past a hundred. These animals are thirty-one. They have just come into their first breeding condition, in a sealed room with one door, and that door is a siphon that swallows eleven hundred liters every nineteen minutes. Anything they lay in the pools goes over the crest. The only water the flush can't reach is the moat around the ice, which is at one point eight degrees and too cold to hold an egg to term.

So they are a sterile family by plumbing. Every spring for the rest of the century, thirty-one siblings will spawn into a machine that eats the result, and the number will stay thirty-one, and then start to fall.

They are also the only residents who can read the light. Eyeless is not blind: their skin knows when the blue-grey wedge crosses a pool, and they sink. The moss follows the light by growing toward it. The olms follow it by leaving. Two ways to track the same slow rectangle across the same floor.

## The Deaf

Everything above the waterline lives in a ruined medium.

Seventy decibels of smeared, nine-second reverberant roar means that airborne sound in this room carries no information whatsoever. Nothing can call. Nothing can answer. A cricket that stridulated here would be a cricket shouting into a waterfall; a bat that hunted here would be a bat reading its own echoes off forty thousand cubic meters of wet stone, three times over, out of order. The nine bats know this. They come through the light shaft at dusk on the way to somewhere they can actually work, and they never turn aside into the fog, not once, not in thirty-one years.

So the air-breathers have reorganized around what's left: touch, substrate, and light.

The crickets are spaced a hand-span apart across eight thousand square meters of weeping wall, and a hand-span is exactly the reach of an antenna. That is the whole rule. The population is tiled at the radius of its own touch, every animal just inside contact of the nearest and no nearer, a grid drawn not by territory or by food but by the length of the organ that lets one animal know another exists. The harvestmen stand in the drip lines on legs like calipers, which is to say they are instruments parked at the delivery points; they don't search, they gauge, and take what arrives. The hunting spiders work the film without silk, and when they do spin — a dragline, an egg sac — they eat it afterward, or carry it, and nothing is left hanging.

The fungus gnat larvae are the room's only makers and its only lamps. Each one secretes a fishing line, hangs it in the drip path, waits, and then reels the line back in and eats it and spins another. The line is the tool and the meal and the raw material, cycling, never accumulating. They glow a sick green when your eyes give up color, thirty or forty points spaced across the moss fan — spaced, again, by reach: the length of a thread plus the distance at which a neighbor becomes food.

Light is the only signal in the room that still works. It travels the void without smearing, it doesn't echo, it arrives once. So the lamps speak, and the midges fly by eye, and the olms feel the wedge on their backs, and everything else keeps its antennae on the stone.

The springtails are the largest and least permanent nation: a grey bloom the size of a dinner plate on the pool skin, millions of them, living entirely on the two-dimensional surface tension of water. Every nineteen minutes their whole country is destroyed and goes down the throat. It reassembles from the margins in about six minutes. They have never once been anywhere else.

## The Column

Under the wedge at midday there is one dense object in the whole volume, and it is a hundred cubic meters of nearly nothing.

Two hundred thousand midges, a hand's width between each, turning slowly. Chironomid swarms normally hold themselves together by hearing — each male listening to the wingbeat of the next through the feathered antenna, a acoustic lattice, a crowd that keeps its spacing by ear. That is impossible here. This swarm has been deafened into something else: it holds its shape by sight, by the single blue-grey rectangle coming down the chute, which is the only landmark in the room and the only reason the column stands where it stands. It is a marriage dance that has lost its music and kept its choreography, anchored to a patch of daylight that moves.

The individuals are a day and a half old. The column is thirty-one. It is the same column, in the sense that a flame is the same flame: entirely replaced, continuously, from the pool film below, where the larvae graze the bacteria that digest the leaf ration. Every day the column rains its dead into the water and the springtails take them and the shrimp take the rest. It is the only thing in the room that converts the surface's charity into airborne protein and hands it back. A farm with no farmer. A city with no citizens who last the week.

It occupies almost exactly the volume of the block that is about to fall.

## The Green

Eight square meters of moss on an eight-meter fan of leaves and silt, in a room of forty thousand cubic meters. That ratio is the whole biography of the plants: one substrate, unlimited water, one moving rectangle of light, nowhere else to go.

*Conocephalum* in liverwort plates, glossy and liverish, doing the only real photosynthesis in the place at the very edge of what light can pay for. A hook-moss behind it. And then the fine fur that has no name, because no one has come — a thing that exists at the precise address of nobody's knowledge, luminous-looking, lit from beneath, quite possibly not new to science at all but simply a common species in an uncommon posture. It may be protonema that has refused to become a moss: the juvenile filament stage, spreading and spreading in the dim, never triggered to leaf, never fruiting, thirty-one years of being a seedling. There are caves where the entire flora is a childhood that has forgotten how to end.

The moss follows the light wedge in the only way a plant with no motion can: by growing where it lands and dying where it doesn't. The green patch is not moving. It is being written and erased, at about a centimeter a season, by a rectangle that crosses the floor once a day and shifts a few meters a year. What looks like a lawn is a slow photograph of the sun's path through a nine-meter hole.

## The Dry

Two residents are not participating, and they are the oldest-looking things in the room.

The beech came in during the first winter, trunk still hard, bark still on, thirty-one years in a saturated cold room and essentially unrotted. Lignin decay wants warmth and air and a guild of fungi that has never come down the chute. So the tree lies in a place that eats everything, uneaten — a locked larder, a ration nobody has the key to. The room's entire economy runs on soft wet things arriving shredded; a whole log is a fortune in a currency it cannot spend.

The owl fell in during the second winter and is now a small arrangement of feathers in a sheltered pocket above the condensation line, undisturbed, because nothing here scavenges dry things. It is the only corpse and the only face and the only object in the room that looks composed — arranged, deliberate, laid out. It is not. It is gravity and thirty winters and the absence of a beetle. But it is the closest this place comes to an antique, and it survives for the same reason the log does: the census has a hole in it where the dry guild should be.

Everything else is eaten, including the eaters, including the threads, including the silk. The room manufactures constantly and keeps nothing organic. Which leaves exactly one thing that it keeps.

## The Keeper

The ice in the low corner is the only resident with a memory.

Thirty-one couplets: clear winter freeze, brown autumn leaf, a thread of silt for each big flood. It is the room's ledger and it is kept by accident, in the one material that survives here, by an animal-shaped process that is not an animal — it takes in, lays down, sweats out, runs a yearly cycle, has a birthday, has a body that grows and shrinks with the weather. In September it is down to a meter and a half and bleeding into a moat that feeds the middle pool, which is to say it feeds the thin female who has been thin for six years.

Everyone drinks her. Every winter she is renewed by cold air falling down a chute and pooling because the room is a bag with the opening at the top; every summer she holds the whole volume at one point eight degrees by simply being heavy and staying put. The fog that hides the far wall in July is her exhalation. The scalloped corrosion on the old flowstone is her breath eating the rock. The four-degree water in every flush is her temperature, imposed on the plumbing.

She has never gone. Two hundred and forty meters above a river where people grow figs, there is ice older than the beech above is thick, and every single organism in this room is, in the most literal sense, living inside her weather.

She is also the only resident who cannot be replaced. Break the bag and she goes in a decade, and everything that is here because it is cold goes with her.

## The Ones Who Don't Live Here

Some of the room's largest footprint belongs to animals that have never entered it.

Deer bed down thirty meters out and no closer. That ring is a boundary drawn in the minds of animals who know only that cold comes out of the ground at this address and that it is wrong. The room's outermost population is an avoidance, maintained across generations of deer by something none of them has seen.

The bracken at the mouth is the only thing living on both sides: one clone, eleven fronds' worth of vigor, frost-killed every June and back every July, spending its whole life flinching at a hole. Its rhizome is probably in the chute rubble. It is the doorkeeper and it has never been through the door.

And the nine bats are the mail. They roost elsewhere, they hunt elsewhere, they leave nothing here but the occasional drop of guano that is worth a year of leaves — and on their ankles and in their fur they carry mites, spores, pollen, and the seeds of every guild the room is missing. The dry-wood fungi that would finally open the log are on a bat somewhere above the beech wood tonight. The next permanent resident of this room is currently a passenger.

## The Heir

Along the dome's crown there is a hairline with fresh rock flour in it, and behind the hairline a hundred cubic meters that has already, in every sense but one, moved in.

When it goes, it will be the loudest event in thirty-one years, and then the room will be a different room. The chute chokes or widens. The wedge shifts. The moss begins its centimeter-a-season crawl toward a new rectangle, and the part of it that can't crawl fast enough dies, and the unnamed fur may or may not be in the new address. The drip routes reroute and a second batch of straws starts at zero beside the first at four centimeters, and for a while there are two generations hanging in ranks and the terrible tidiness is over.

If the chute widens, the winter cold-pour strengthens and the ice thickens and the cold guild holds. If it chokes, the bag is sealed, the fog stops, the lens goes in a decade, and the tenants who are only here because it is four degrees emigrate down the fissure or die, and the warm ones arrive on a bat.

Either way the plumbing changes, and somewhere in the new floor there may be a pocket the siphon can't reach — a place, for the first time in thirty-one years, where an egg can sit still.

The olms will be there for it. That is the last thing to understand about who lives here. The straws are thirty-one and will be a meter long by the time the room is old. The midges are a day and a half. The ice is thirty-one and mortal. The moss is a childhood, the owl is a rumor of feathers, the shrimp are passing through. But thirty-one eyeless siblings are holding their basins in the dark at four minutes every nineteen, and they have eighty years left, and they will still be lying on the bottom of those pockets, in whatever room this becomes, waiting for the water to bring them the next thing.
