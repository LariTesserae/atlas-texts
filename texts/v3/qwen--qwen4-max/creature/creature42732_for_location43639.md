## The Residents

Ask first how many, because the answer sets the shape of everything after it.

The visitors' inventory — the last one, the one that stops mid-column — lists six species. Six is wrong in both directions. Most of what looks like a species here is a phase of something else, and most of what looks like one animal is a crowd holding a shape. The combers alone run to tens of millions of crowns, and they are not tens of millions of individuals in any sense you would defend out loud; a comb-band buds along its own length, tooth to tooth, and a band that runs three meters along a flute is one continuous living ribbon with a pulse in it, and the pulse is not a signal passing between animals but a single animal's slow contraction, the way a wave along a rope is not a hundred ropes.

So: the light in here is a body. When the blue-green tide climbs the wall and falls back, you are watching something breathe with an organ it does not know it has.

And the swimmers in the hanging beads — the pale specks, copepod-shaped, the visitors wrote *copepods*, which is a word from water — are the combers' children. Or they were. A comber releases into the slack, and the larva rides the small fall, and if it finds a tooth it settles and opens a crown and never moves again. That is the intended life. But some fraction of each spawn is caught by the upstroke and carried into the node-lines, into the hanging beads, and there it does not settle. It breeds first. It breeds as a larva, in the plum-sized world, and its offspring breed as larvae too, and after some hundreds of thousands of generations of that they have lost the ability to settle at all. The crown never opens. The attachment hooks are gone. They have become a pelagic fauna in a room nine meters across, derived from a wall, and they cannot go back to it.

That is the first thing to understand about who lives here. Half of them are the other half, later.

---

The archipelago has a geography and it has a history and neither is written on anything.

The beads are places, not objects. A given bead's water is replaced in minutes; the node that holds it has been in roughly the same location since the tower was commissioned, drifting a little as the baffles fatigue, sagging a semitone's worth over nine centuries. So there are islands here older than any nation on the plateau, made of water that has never been the same water twice. The fauna is old and the real estate is old and the substance is a river.

Populations in the low beads, near the fall-line, are mongrels — constantly rained out, constantly reseeded from the walls, genetically indistinguishable from comber larvae with a delayed appointment. Populations in the high beads, up under the gills where the pressure furniture is stable and nothing has fallen for a very long time, are something else. They are small and nearly transparent and they have no pigment and no stress response to falling, because falling is not a thing that happens to their lineage. They breed slowly. They have a body plan with an extra joint in the feeding limb, which the low forms lack, and which nobody has ever seen used for anything.

And in the necks — the thinning isthmuses when two beads lean into each other and their skins touch and hold — there is a flat, fast, ciliated thing that lives only in the joining. It requires the neck. It swims the seam between two populations while the seam exists, eats what the traffic strands, lays in the constriction, and dies when the neck snaps. Its whole life is a few seconds long and it has been doing this for nine hundred years, and if the beads ever stop merging it will simply not be here anymore, and nothing else in the room will notice.

Once every forty-four seconds the wave reverses and some of the beads let go, and the archipelago loses a province, and the sump receives a rain of animals that have never touched a wall, and the gullet eats them. The high beads have not lost a province in a very long time. There is a lineage up there that may be older than the leak.

---

The wall talks, and the wall is also the thing being talked about, and the tappers have not distinguished between these.

They live in the erosion pits — armored, thumbnail-sized, a knuckle of claw on each forelimb — and the pits are not entirely erosion. A tapper drums in a pit and the pit deepens under it, and it deepens fastest at the frequency the animal drums, and a pit occupied for forty generations becomes a resonator tuned to the family that owns it. Pits are inherited. A young tapper does not dig; it waits for a pit to come vacant and then it moves in and drums the pit's own note, because it learned that note in its feet before it could walk.

Which means the room's acoustics are partly authored. The scalloping, the fluting, the pitting that dropped the fundamental a semitone and enriched the odd harmonics — some of that is fatigue in the ceramic and some of it is nine hundred years of small animals insisting on their own pitches in a hard reflective room. The wave the combers feed to, the node-spacing the stilters perch on, the eleven-second slack: all of it has been negotiated, slowly, unknowingly, by drumming. Nothing maintains this place. It is more accurate to say that it is being tuned continuously by things that think they are talking about property.

What they are saying, as far as anyone can reconstruct it, is mostly *this pit is occupied* and *I am molting* and *do not come along this flute.* There is a fourth pattern, longer, that only occurs in the slack, when air can carry sound and a tapper's drum is suddenly audible to the whole room instead of just to the wall. Nobody has decoded it. It occurs at the end of the slack, in the last second and a half, and it is the same pattern in every dialect, and every dialect has a different dialect's version of it.

The dialects should have diverged into nonsense centuries ago. They don't, and the reason is in the mud.

Seven times in six hundred years the groove in the lower wall has gone empty. When it does, the pressure bleeds, the fundamental sags and thins and finally drops out altogether, and the room experiences something it otherwise never experiences: a slack that does not end. The longest lasted a day and a half. Every bead comes down. Every stilter lands. The combers clamp and hold, and hold, and the light goes out of the walls in slow retreating bands. And the entire wall speaks at once, in air, all of it, every pit audible to every other pit, for thirty hours.

Then the pressure comes back, and the world is taken away again, and the dialects have converged. The vacancies are the only time the wall gets a conversation instead of a correspondence. Seven of them. Seven creolizations. The mud has all seven in it as thin pale layers of ungrazed biofilm, because during a vacancy the grazers stop.

---

The stilters are the room's aristocracy and its casualties, and the distinction is a molt.

They perch on nodes: folded membranous wings, hooked feet, resting on nothing at all in ranks, in the blue-green, at intervals that have been shifting a few millimeters per century for nine hundred years. A node is not a possession — you cannot mark it, defend it, or leave anything in it — but they defend them anyway, with a wing-flick and a lunge, and the same node will be occupied continuously for decades by a succession of animals that all treat it as an inheritance. It is property in a medium that cannot hold a deed.

Old individuals stop flying. The wings stiffen, the flight muscle resorbs, the hooks thicken, and a stilter in its third or fourth year becomes a permanent sitter: it cannot leave its node, it can only hop to the adjacent one when the wave shifts, and if the shift is larger than one node it falls. Most of them fall eventually. Some hold on for years past the point where flight would have saved them, and there is one in the amber-lit quarter near the seam with a torn left membrane that has been sitting in the same invisible chair, unmated, for longer than anyone has been counting.

They mate in the slack, on the wing, shrieking at six kilohertz, which is the only sound in the room that any visitor has ever described as *a voice*. Eleven seconds. Everything that will happen reproductively in the next six and a half minutes happens in it. Then the fundamental comes up through the floor and takes the cry away mid-note, and the animals go back to sitting on air in ranks with their wings folded, and if you did not know what you had just heard you would say the room was full of nothing.

Below them, the gullet reads the fall-line.

It is a meter of muscle with a lateral line down each flank and no eyes and no interest in light, and what it knows is a signature: the impact pattern of the circumference's drops, the rhythm of the small fall, the exact place where the pattern is wrong. A stilter hitting water is a wrongness of about eleven milliseconds and a certain amplitude, and the gullet is there before the splash closes. It also takes the bead-rain, and the larvae, and the molt-cases, and it is not fussy, but it is *precise*, and precision is the whole of its character. It has the room's schedule in its body more completely than anything else here, including the machine.

It leaves to breed. Once in a long while — the interval is not known — it goes down the drain in the gulp, and the gulp is a one-way door for everything else in the chamber, and something in the cistern below the plateau receives it. There is a population down there. Pale, blind, reading pressure in the dark of a cistern nobody has opened, and every so often one of them comes up the pipe into the sump and takes the fall-line and holds it for a century. The gullet is not a resident. It is a representative.

---

Two things in here have been mistaken for the building.

The first is in the ceiling. Between the black baffle plates, in the near-touching ranks, there is a sheet. It is folded like the gills it lives among and it is very thin and it is continuous, or nearly, across the whole nine-meter span, and it is the largest single organism in the chamber by area and possibly by mass. The visitors logged its noise as *baffle chatter, non-critical*, and it is the sound in the slack like a wet hinge.

It opens. Once every ninth stroke, when the pressure furniture dissolves and the plates stop pressing, the sheet unfolds a few millimeters everywhere at once and the whole ceiling exhales, and that is the hinge-sound, and it is spawning. Its spores are the ceramic dust's chief binder. It eats the wear of the machine — the shed nanoparticulate, the fatigued alumina, the ninety-nine-year flakes of a baffle giving up — and it eats them by holding them against itself in a mucus that is slightly, measurably acid.

Which means it is thinning the baffles. Which means it is lowering the fundamental. The semitone is not entirely the tappers, and it is not entirely fatigue. There is a hectare of folded animal in the ceiling slowly eating the instrument it lives inside, at a rate that has been stable long enough for everything downstairs to retune along with it, and it does not know it is doing this, and if it ever stopped the room would go bright and hard and sharp and everything in it would be, briefly, deaf in a new way.

The second thing mistaken for the building is the mud, and the mud is alive at every grain. The drifts in the corners are ceramic dust and molt-cases and castings and biofilm, stratified, smelling of iron and ammonia and something like burnt sugar, and there is not one natural particle in it, and there are eleven or twelve kinds of grazer in it and a bacterium that will eat the binder out of fired alumina if you give it a wet century. It is the room's stomach and its archive at once. Everything that dies here goes into the strata, and the strata are legible, and the seven vacancies are in there like seven lines of white in a black book.

There is also a disease. A comber infected by a filamentous thing loses its phase — it feeds a fraction of a stroke late, and its glow comes a fraction late, and a heavily infected patch shows up as a dark stutter in the tide, a hesitation in the light. The tappers eat those. They cross a flute to do it, which they otherwise never do, and they eat the slow ones preferentially, and the stutter stays rare. The wall's pulse is clean because something on it is farming for uniformity without any concept of a pulse.

---

The leak made the wet margin, and the wet margin made its own children, and one of them is holding the wall shut.

In the permanent film where there should be dry ceramic there is a flat grazer, film-thin, translucent, that scrapes biofilm off alumina in a slow rasp and leaves a clean track behind it, and the tracks are the only marks in the room made by an animal that will not be here in an hour. There are also small things that eat the grazer's tracks — the regrowth, the first film, the softest layer. A whole guild exists in the failure. None of it could live in a sealed cell. Ninety-nine of the towers are dry and clean and correct and there is nothing in them at all.

And in the joint, hand-span wide and long as a forearm, pressed into the failing seam, is the gasket: the flat pale thing with no eyes and almost no motion, eating what the fog brings, holding the pressure that makes the weather that makes the world.

Here is what nobody notices from the seam. The groove is a pressure shadow. It is a node of the animal's own making, a place where the standing wave cancels against the geometry of the joint, and it has been quiet in there since before the plateau had a name. The gasket has never heard the room. It lives inside a hundred and thirty decibels in a pocket of something close to stillness, and its only experience of the machine is a slow warmth and a delivery of food and a pressure against its back that it spends its life pushing against. It is the only resident that experiences this place as a quiet place.

It is also, in the only sense the room cares about, the load-bearing one. When it dies before its successor is in the groove, the world stops. Seven times. The lineage has been in the joint six hundred years and the groove has been worn to fit them, or they to fit it, and the young still crawl the wet margin as film-grazers before they find the seam, and almost none of them find it, and one of them always has.

There is one in there now. Behind it, in the film, four or five pale flat things are rasping tracks toward the joint, and one of them will arrive.

---

The photophiles are the last of an assumption.

Eleven lamps, eleven colonies of glassy moss, each the exact shape of a dying throw. Nothing else here bothers with photons — the fog is free food — so the moss exists solely because someone once specified that a person with a clipboard should be able to read a strain figure at a glance. In the largest colony there is a rosette the size of a saucer with nine stiff leaves, and living in the rosette, and nowhere else, is a mite: eight legs, slow, pale, dependent on a plant dependent on a phosphor that is failing. Two islands left after that. Then one. The mite has no way to know any of this and no way to go anywhere and it is the only animal in the chamber whose extinction has a date on it.

The wire-vine came in through the seam on a boot or in a bird and has traveled four meters along the sill, and it has an aphid, and the aphid has an ant-shaped thing that tends it, and none of the three are from here. They are the only residents with relatives elsewhere. Every eleven seconds the vine gets a quiet moment and grows a little further into the room, and it has been doing that for perhaps two hundred years, and at this rate it will reach the joint in another eight hundred, and what happens then is anyone's.

And the counter. There is a niche beside the seam, at the height of a shoulder, dished in the middle like a stone step, and it is empty, and it has been empty a long time. Something used to sit in it, out of the loud, in the pressure shadow, with its back to the wall and its face to the amber, and scratch a mark every ninth stroke. Hundreds of marks. Groups of nine. Eleven hours of deafness, patiently counted, by an animal that got nothing out of it.

Low down, one group has been struck through with a single deeper gouge. It is the only mark in the room that means something.

---

Last: the plume is a resident too, in the way a seed head is part of a plant.

The white feather going east off the vent carries the room out of the room — comber spawn, hinge spores, bead-fauna in droplet nuclei, the machine's dust, all of it flung a kilometer before the wind takes it apart. Almost all of it dies on the plateau at four and a half thousand meters, in cold, in ultraviolet, in dry air that would kill anything from in here in seconds. Almost all. There are ninety-nine other towers, and they are sterile, and they are not empty: their drifts hold spores, dormant, waiting, and what they are waiting for is a failed joint. A leak. A wet margin. The one thing that has never happened to them.

So the answer to *what lives here* is not six species, or ten million crowns, or a meter of blind muscle reading the fall-line. It is a failure, nine hundred years old, that has acquired the ability to keep itself from getting worse — a hole that grew a body to hold it shut, and a ceiling that eats the roof at exactly the rate the tenants can follow, and a wall of drummers tuning the weather they cannot hear, and a nursery of islands made of water that has never been the same water twice.

None of them knows the room is a room. All of them are holding it. And when you go out backward through the seam and your ears click and the wind turns out to be almost nothing, the thing you leave behind is not a machine with animals in it.

It is an animal that has to be told, every forty-four seconds, that it is a machine — and there is nothing left alive that can tell it, and it goes on saying so itself, in nineteen hertz, in the dark, to ten million crowns that answer with light.
