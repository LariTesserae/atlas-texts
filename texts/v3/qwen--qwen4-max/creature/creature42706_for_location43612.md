Wet, in the forty minutes, the film is a millimetre thick and it has storeys.

The top one is the olive-black. It is black because it is full of sunscreen — carotenoids, scytonemin, a pigment that costs a lot to make and is worth it under a zenith that goes to enamel. These are the ones that take the light and take the carbon out of the water, and the water, robbed of its carbon, can no longer hold its lime, and the lime falls. That is the whole of the industry. The plain is not a building anyone intended. It is the tailings of an appetite for light. Kilometres of it. Nobody here has ever wanted a wall.

Under the black, a band that is purple where it is thick and green where it is thin, and it eats the light the top storey has no use for — the far reds, the leftovers, the wavelengths that get through a millimetre of sunscreen. It does not make oxygen. It breathes sulfur and it does not care about the sky.

Under that, black again, but a different black: the one that makes the struck-match smell. It lives on what falls from above and it breathes the wrong way round, and its exhaust is the sulfide that the middle storey lives on, so the millimetre is a loop, a small closed economy where the same carbon is handed down and back up and handed down again, and has been for longer than there have been things with eyes.

And at the bottom, in the wet dark against the fresh course, the fermenters, taking the dead of the storeys above and passing them along.

So it is not true that nothing here eats. It is true that nothing here *goes looking*. Every mouth in this world is a millimetre from its food and always has been. The whole population lives inside a body a millimetre thick and kilometres wide, stacked in trades, each trade living on the waste of the one above, and the stone drops out beneath them like snow under a slow cloud.

---

The stone is slow. They are not.

This is the first thing to correct. A cell here divides in an afternoon. A single survivor in a mortar joint, given three wet mornings, becomes a stain you can see from standing height; given a wet season it becomes a hand's-breadth of varnish; the film doubles and doubles and the plain still only gains a fingernail per lifetime, because the plain measures surplus and the surplus is thin. The slowness is the monument's, not theirs. Under the monument everything is quick, quick, quick — generations turning over inside the wet hour, whole dynasties rising and being sealed between one dawn and the next — and the record of all that hurry is a pale band three millimetres thick with one dark seam in it.

What is an individual? Not the cell; the cells stay together after they divide, which is the entire trick. Not the film either, exactly; a film is a lineage holding a position, and the position is the thing that persists — the seep-lip, the north side of a pressure ridge, the two metres downstream of a notch where the sheet is always thickest. The tenants change hourly. The address is immortal.

They have no signals. They do, however, trade. Fragments of instruction pass sideways through a wet film — a better sunblock, a tolerance for brine, a way of hoarding phosphate — handed from a cell to a neighbour it will be encasing next week. The only correspondence in this world is the swapping of tools between parties who are otherwise at war.

The war is the encasement, and it is fought at edges, and it is fought by growing. Two films meet at a wet joint. The one that lays faster rises over the other's margin and seals it under a course, and the loser goes on living under glass for a while, in the dark, on what it can get, and then it doesn't. That is the dark seam. Every dark seam in every broken plate is a margin that was overrun. The plain is a record of defeats a kilometre deep, kept perfectly, in order, and for the whole of its history nothing has ever read a word of it.

---

In the crystal nap there is a second population, and it lives four hours.

The white fur grows overnight and pulls water out of the morning air and collapses into beads, and the beads are not water, they are brine — liquid so loaded that most of what lives in the film cannot enter it. What can is a different set: small, red-orange, salt-loving, living in a fluid that is barely a solvent any more, dividing in the crawl while the bead moves its fraction of a millimetre downhill. They are not hurrying. They simply have less time.

By mid-morning the bead dries and locks, and the crystal closes over the population, and here is the thing: it does not close over all of them dead. A fraction get sealed alive inside the crystal, in bubbles of their own brine, glass rooms ten microns across, metabolisms down to nothing, no light, no traffic, no time. The plain is full of these. There are more of them per handful than there are cells in the living film. They accumulate downward with everything else — a suspended population, laminated into the archive, waiting inside the wall.

Some of them have been waiting a very long while.

They cannot get out. Nothing here can get out; getting out is a form of removal, and removal has not existed.

It exists now.

---

The plants are one thing in nine bodies, or nine things in one body; the distinction has never come up.

A clump breaks, the fragments lie in a wet joint, the films mortar them down while they root, and four hundred years later there are nine clumps in a rough ring around the place where the first one stood. No seed has ever been carried anywhere. No pollen has ever been in the air. The whole vegetative population of the province is a slow explosion of a single accident, still going off.

They are the only inhabitants with a shape, and the only ones that die as individuals rather than as margins, and they lose the same war everything else loses: the stone climbs them, a millimetre a season, and they grow to keep ahead, and the ones that manage it for long enough become columns.

Inside the columns is the first interior in the world.

A flue: a pipe up the middle of a stone post, knee-high, damp at dawn, cooler than the flat by two degrees for about an hour, with a disc of light at the top and a wet floor. It is the only shade deeper than a hand. Films get in — fragments washed down, dust of a dead neighbour — and inside they are safe from being overgrown, because the war is fought on surfaces and this is not a surface, it is a room. What lives in there is the shade-tolerant, the losers, the ones that lost the light on the flat and can still make a living on the green light that comes down a pipe. Every flue is a small colony of the defeated, alive, out of the record, unarchived.

They stand one per day's walking and they give their low note when the wind crosses them and at dawn they drip, and each one is a lung, an ark, and a hole in the accounting.

---

The pits are new and they are already occupied.

A solution pit is hand-wide and sharp-arrised and its edges are like broken sugar, and its geometry does three things nothing on this plain has ever done: it cuts the wind, it holds a bead, and it shades its own floor for part of the day. The film in a pit gets the wet hour plus forty minutes. Forty minutes more work a day, every day, forever. In a place where everything is decided at margins by rates of laying, that is not an advantage, it is a different world.

And the pit is dissolving underneath them.

So the pit lineages do something no film has ever needed to do. They plaster. They lay into the floor of their own vessel as fast as it goes, thickening, sealing, patching, keeping the bottom under them at the same level while the hungry water takes it away. Not accumulation. *Maintenance.* A boundary held steady by continuous repair. It is the first thing here that has a self in the sense of a wall it must keep mending, and it is a boat you have to bail, and the bailing is the living.

Where a pit cuts down through old crystal it opens the glass rooms. The sealed ones — the four-hour population, locked in since some morning nobody counted — come out into water that is briefly, gloriously dilute, and bloom, and the pit goes red-orange for a day. The first hatching in the history of this place is a dissolution event. The erosion is a hatchery.

---

Some of them have stopped living on the stone and started living in it.

The translucent bands are windows. Look at a broken edge: pale, dark, pale, and then a sugary band with glitter in it, and light goes into that band and comes out the other side dimmed but usable. Two, three millimetres down, there is still enough light to work with, if you can get there.

They get there by dissolving. Filaments a hair's width across, boring, going down through the fresh course and into the sugary band, making a tunnel by taking the wall away, and living at the bottom of it under a roof of their neighbours' stone. They are still masons in every other respect — they still take carbon, they still drop lime — but the room they live in is subtracted, and they are the first living things in this world to make anything by removal.

They cannot be overgrown. The war does not reach them. They are inside the archive, in the tombs, and they keep their windows clear by dissolving a little more roof whenever the roof gets too thick, which means they are continuously eating the record in order to keep reading the light.

Nothing has ever had privacy here before.

---

And then there is the plumbing.

A storm sheet crosses a fresh crust and finds a swallow-hole the width of a wrist, and goes down it, and takes the film with it. Skin, fragments, whole rafts of living mat, tonnes of population over an afternoon, falling out of the light.

Most of it ends as a dark seam somewhere in the dark. Some of it does not, because the millimetre always had a storey that never used the sun. The sulfur-breathers, the fermenters, the ones at the bottom of the stack who have spent the entire history of this place living on hand-me-downs in the dark against the fresh course — they come down the hole already equipped. Up on the flat they were the fourth storey of a body. Down here there is no first storey, and they inherit.

They respire. Their breath goes into the water and makes it acidic and the acidic water bites the wall, and the bite opens more room, and the room exposes more archive, and the archive is full of dark seams, and the dark seams are the richest thing in this world: organic carbon, concentrated, laid down in order, a larder a kilometre deep with the defeats filed in it.

So they follow the seams. Gliding filaments, on a wet face, in the dark, a millimetre an hour, toward the smell of an old loss.

A millimetre an hour. Everything here has always grown quickly and gone nowhere; the plain's whole slowness is the slowness of a thing that stays. This is not growth. This is travel. It is nine thousand times faster than anything has ever crossed this ground, and it has a front and a back, and it is going *toward*.

The first meal in the history of this place was a defeat, and the first thing to go looking for anything went looking for that.

They are not animals. There are no animals; there will not be animals for a very long time. But there is now a grazing front underground, moving along the laminae, eating the dead in the order they were laid, and behind it the water it has made hungry is opening the plain from the inside, and above it the plain is still adding at a fingernail a lifetime, and the two rates are not the same rate any more.

---

The headcut is forty metres long and it advances a metre or two a storm, and it is fed by rain the plain itself makes, and it slows when the storms fail and quickens when they come, and it has a direction, which is uphill, which is backwards through the archive, newest to oldest. It has no cells. It is not alive. But it is the only thing on the surface with an appetite and a heading, and at its lip the films are laying as fast as they can lay, sealing, plastering, doing the pit-dwellers' trick at the scale of a wall, and they are not quite fast enough, and being not quite fast enough is a new way to lose.

The plain has holes now. Wrist-wide, plumb-sided, going down into a plumbing that is only just beginning. On a still morning, when the pressure rises, they inhale; when it falls, they breathe out, and what comes out is cooler than the air and smells different from the flat — wetter, older, mineral and faintly sweet and under it the struck-match note, the smell of the fourth storey, the smell of the inside of the wall.

Put your ear to the stone.

There is the fizz. There has always been the fizz: the sound of lime coming out of the water, of the plain being built, ten million small arrivals under a skin of dew.

Hungry water on carbonate fizzes too. It is the same gas and the same sound and it means the exact opposite.

You cannot tell them apart by listening. Somewhere under your cheek, in the dark, along a seam that records a margin overrun before there were eyes, something a hair wide is going a millimetre an hour toward its food, and above it the light comes up and the colour rises across a hundred metres like a blush, and the masons swell and darken and go back to work.
