# The Counted

## First, the small ones

Begin with the mites, because they were here first and they will be here after, and because nobody has ever given them a name that is not a diminutive.

They live in the resurrection mats, in the dry clenched fist of the plant, in the space between one folded leaf and the next, and they spend most of their lives in that folded dark waiting for twenty minutes of green. When the fog comes up and the mats unroll, the mites come out and walk about on the wet underside and eat each other and lay eggs and are, for a quarter of an hour, extremely busy. Then the mat folds and they go back into the fist. A mite's whole biography is a series of brief evenings inside a shut hand. There are children in Comb who are told this at bedtime and find it comforting, and children who are told it and do not.

The mats themselves are the largest living thing on the Sill and the only one that behaves like a crowd. Individually they are nothing — a grey rosette you could cover with a thumbnail, a thing that looks dead for eleven months and is not dead. Together they are the surface of the world, and at the turning they all open at once, over twenty minutes, from the pans outward, in a wave you can watch cross the plain from the shoulder of an Elder. The sound is the sound of a held breath let out through cloth. It is the loudest thing the flat ever does. Everyone stops. Nobody says so. There is no polite way to remark on the green hour; it is like remarking on someone's face. Lovers say it to each other exactly once, and the saying is a kind of proposal, and there is a word for the person you said it to, and no word for the person you said it to after you stopped loving them.

## The tenant

The pale slug is the only animal on the Sill that lives where the money lives.

It is a green-place creature: it wants the wet underside, the moss beard, the drinking-lip of a handrail, the soffit gutter, the shaded damp where the deep terraces are. It has no back worth speaking of. It has never been looked at from above by anything, in the whole history of its kind, except when a person crouches to drink and turns their lamp up. It grazes the underside of the world all night, upside down, leaving a road of scraped green, and by morning it has gone into a crack.

The moss beards must be scraped or the slug will eat them to the copper. So there is thumb-work: children out at the turning with a lamp and a wet thumb, going along the rail-lips, taking slugs off by the hundred into a tin. It is the first paid work most people do and the last work anyone does who has failed at everything else. The pay is by count. The word for a hundred slugs is also the word for a small coin, and nobody remembers which came first.

The fog-cricket lives at the foot of the combs, in the warm ring of ground around a copper bleed-strap, and it sings for about four minutes at the turning when the field reverses. That is all. Twenty-three hours and fifty-six minutes of nothing, and then four minutes of a thin dry scraping that carries perhaps thirty metres before the flat eats it.

Every instrument in Comb is tuned to that cricket. There is a musical form called a four: a piece that begins when the crickets begin and must end when they end, and to play past them is the worst thing a musician can do, worse than playing badly, worse than playing the wrong piece. In a dry year there is no charge and no reversal worth the name and the crickets do not sing, and people date their misfortunes from that: *the year the crickets didn't*. Old women will tell you a drought took a house, a husband, a hearing, and then say which year it was by naming a silence.

And in the sump pools, the lung-fish. A household keeps one in a jar of mud on a shelf, and the mud is dry, and the fish is inside it, and it may be inside it for two years. Children dare each other to wet it. It is a larder that keeps itself, a meal that waits, and this is the practical reason; the other reason is that people name them, and inherit them, and say things like *the fish came up the week my father died*. A fish can outlast a marriage. A fish can outlast the roof it was kept under. When a street goes down, the survivors go back for the jars.

## What came up and stayed

The pipe-cats are not native either. They came up the stair in somebody's crate, generations back, to keep the condensate mains clear of slug and mite and lung-fish, and they have become a shape that fits a pipe: long, low, pale, with ears set flat and whiskers worn short by the walls, and eyes that are too big for the light and are never asked to work in anything brighter than foxfire. A pipe-cat is dry along its spine and wet along its belly, always, all its life. Its chin is wet. When it drinks it goes under a rail and licks upward like everything else here.

The plumbers keep a breeding book. The cats keep the mains. And for nine days a year they go out of their minds.

Proppers keep a caged cat on suspect ground and watch it, because a cat that will not lie down is a survey. This is not superstition; it is the cheapest instrument the guild owns, and it is more reliable than the hammer, and the guild does not put it in the charts.

The weeds are imports too. Every plant in the gardens that is not a fern or a mat or a lichen arrived in the gut of a dead bird or the mud on an eel, and the gardeners know them by their route: bird-flowers, small and bright and stupid, growing in a ring where a shearwater fell and rotted; eel-weeds, coarse and lowland, that come up thick the season after the ascent and have to be hoed by people who curse them in a language nobody speaks anymore. There is a botany here that is entirely a study of accidents. There is a flower that grows only on the east side of town, in the dust season, where the mill-smell is, and it is the prettiest thing on the Sill, and it is a weed, and it is fed on the dead.

## The nine days

The eels come up in the wet weeks and the town stops being a machine.

They arrive in the throat-sumps in numbers that turn the water black, wringing up through two thousand metres of streaming rock, thin at first and then fat, and for nine days Comb does nothing else. Weirs across the sumps. The terraces crammed. Every vat, every hook, every drying-rack, every hand. The living shearwaters come up the face at dusk to feed and it is the only time anyone sees them upright and alive, and people go to the rim to look and come back quiet. The pipe-cats get loose and go feral in the fern tunnels and the children hunt them with lamps, and a boy's first cat is a thing he will talk about when he is sixty.

Nobody's age is counted in years. You are twenty-two eels. Contracts run eel to eel. Debts fall due at the ascent, because that is when everyone is in one place and there is fat and protein and noise, and the marriages are made then for the same reason, and the conceptions date from it, and there is a whole month of the following year in which the terraces are full of women who all know the same thing about each other.

Then the eels bring the lowlands with them: silt, seed, parasites. The fever comes about eleven days after the weirs close, every year, reliably, and the physicians of Comb have built an entire medicine around a single annual epidemic and are helpless before anything else. The gardens are fed on eel-mash for a year. The town smells of it until the dry comes.

And then it stops. The throats go quiet. Eleven months of being a machine.

There is a sect — small, stubborn, not violent — that holds the weirs should be opened. That the eels are the road the first people followed up, and that you do not eat your guides. They are called the letting. Once, a long way back, they got a weir open for a night, and there was a famine, and the town remembers the famine and has forgotten the argument, which is the usual arrangement.

## Bodies

A Sill face is lit from below, and it is lit from below all the time, and this does something to a face that a lowlander never gets used to.

The bright parts are the underside of the jaw, the nostrils, the shelf under the brow, the lower lip. The eyes are in shadow. So people here read mouths and jaws and the set of the throat, and think the lowlander's obsession with eyes is sentimental and slightly obscene, like reading someone's palms. Painters paint from below. A portrait shows you the underside of a chin. A top-lit portrait, the kind they make down in the drowned country, looks to a Sill eye like a corpse or a saint.

Everyone wears a gorget: a polished thing at the throat, hung to catch the pavement-glow and throw it up into the face. Silver if you have it, tin if you don't, a shard of glazed pot on a string if you have nothing. To angle your gorget so that it lights someone else's face is to pay attention to them. To turn it away is a snub that can end a friendship. *She lit him* means she loved him, and there is no more precise way to say it.

To keep your chin down is to conceal. To lift it is to be honest, or to be about to ask for something. Children are smacked for holding their chins down and are not told why, and learn why at about eleven, and then do it constantly.

The tan runs the wrong way. Sillers burn beneath: under the chin, under the nose, along the underside of a forearm. The tops of their heads are pale as roots. Their sleeves are always damp and the cold lives there, and the wrist-wraps that everyone wears are not decoration, they are the difference between hands that work at sixty and hands that don't.

And the soles of the feet are ears. The furnaces hum at the very bottom of hearing and the whole town reads that hum through the floor, so people go barefoot indoors and wear thin soles out, and keep the callus pared, and a man in thick boots is a stranger or a criminal or someone who has decided not to know things. *He walks shod* is not a description of footwear.

Every table in Comb has a bowl on it. Shallow, black-glazed, kept full. You do not drink from it. A guest who drinks from the bowl is a lowlander and everyone is very kind about it. You watch it while you talk. When the works draw hard the surface shivers, and when something goes wrong in the rock the surface does something else, and every person raised here can tell the difference before they can tell you their own name. Children are taught to fill the bowl before they are taught to eat. There is a reflex, universal in this town, that a visitor finds comic for about a week: at any unexpected sound, everyone looks down at the water before they look up.

## Two languages

On the flat, ten metres is a wall. Sound goes up into fog and thin air and does not come back. So the lid is signed: a full hand-language, fast, beautiful, with regional accents and a formal register and a filthy vernacular, plus lamps for distance and semaphore from the comb-ranks and coded pulses down the pressure mains for anything that has to be official.

In the throats, sound pools like water. A voice at the bottom is clean at the top. So everything that needs saying is said down a hole: the markets, the courts, the arguments, the music. The judge sits lowest. To appeal is to descend. The criers stand at the sumps and speak upward, and a good crier can put a sentence into every terrace of a throat without raising his voice, and his lungs are insured, and he is vain about them.

Everyone has two names. A hand-name, which is what the world uses. And a mouth-name, which is only usable in a throat, where it will carry, and where everyone will hear it.

Which produces the arrangement that outsiders never quite believe: the rich have voices and no privacy, and the poor have privacy and no voice. Down in the deep terraces a whisper crosses a courtyard and a marriage can be destroyed by a sigh. Up on the lid, in the dust and the wind and the blue corona, two people can stand in the open and say anything at all, close enough to touch, and the world takes nothing.

So the lid is where secrets are told. Confession, sedition, love. To speak someone's mouth-name aloud on the flat — uselessly, into an ear, with your mouth against it, where it goes nowhere and does nothing and is not law — is the most intimate act available to a human being in Comb, and there is no equivalent of it in any lowland language, and the poets have been trying to explain it to foreigners for four hundred years.

## Thickness

The rich live thirty metres down under twelve metres of proven rock. The poor live up on the lid, on ground nobody has sounded, and pay roof-rent to a guild that has the hammer.

The sound-charts are the property deeds. A house is not described by its rooms; it is described by its roof. Houses are called Twelve, and Nine, and Six-and-a-Half, and the great old families are the thick ones, and *thick* also means safe, dull, respectable, and *thin* means poor, precarious, quick, and interesting. A marriage negotiation is an exchange of charts. A dowry is a metre of rock.

The proppers walk the flat with a long-handled hammer and strike and listen: bright is thick, dull is thin, and there is a third note worse than dull that they call dead. The notes have names, and the names are also personal names, and a family genealogy in Comb reads like a chord. Two brights make a good match. A dull bride is a scandal or a romance, depending on the terrace you're standing in.

The guild's monopoly is absolute and its corruption is ordinary. A propper who reports dull where the rock is bright can break a deed and buy the house through his brother-in-law. Hammer-fraud is punished by putting the man on unsounded ground, which is a sentence of death with a variable delay, and everyone considers this proportionate.

But the children have the real map.

There are skipping rhymes on the lid that encode the thin places, verse by verse, pan by pan, comb by comb, and they are updated whenever a street goes down. They are signed, not sung, because the flat eats sound, so they travel hand to hand, close in, intimate, unbuyable. The guild has tried to buy them for two centuries. It cannot, because they change faster than the deeds, because the deeds are property and the rhymes are knowledge, and the poor know more about the roof than the people who own it. A propper with a conscience pays a child in eel for a verse and does not write down where he got it.

*Bright at the Elder, bright at the stair,*
*dull at the third pan — count them there —*
*dead below the drying ground,*
*carry your bowl and don't sit down.*

The rhymes stop at the ninth comb. East of that there are no verses, because there are no children, because nobody has sounded it, and the survey parties go out and some come back. That is what the frontier means here: not danger, not opportunity. A place where the songs run out.

And when a street goes down there is a bell, rung once, and then everyone waits for the second bell, which means the count is finished.

The count is the list of who was on the street. The counter walks it. He does not ring until every name is resolved, and sometimes a name is never resolved, and then the count stays open, and the people in it cannot be mourned, cannot be inherited from, cannot be remarried away from. There are men in Comb who have been waiting eleven years to be widowers. There is a trade in closing: men who buy unresolved names cheap from exhausted families and then go and prove a death and take a share. They are legal. They are spat at.

The two sealed throats are the two counts that never closed. The law holds that a street which has not been counted has not gone. So the deeds still stand down there. The guild still collects roof-rent on houses that fell before anyone's grandmother was born, from families who have been on the lid ever since, and the seals are maintained, and there are people who go out at night and put their ears against them.

Listening at the seal is a crime. It is prosecuted about twice a year, gently, by magistrates who look at the floor.

## The people who listen

Down in the deep terraces, in rooms cut for the purpose, women sit with brass stethoscopes against the stone and mark the slow grinding of a roof giving way twenty metres off, a millimetre a month.

The guild buys girls at six, usually from the lid, usually from families who are counting the meal. The training takes nine years. By thirty the air-hearing has gone and the stone-hearing has sharpened into something that has no name in any lowland physiology, and by forty-five they are the best instruments in the world and cannot sleep on the flat, because up there the world is silent and they can hear their own blood and it sounds like a roof.

They have their own sign dialect, faster than the common one, with a vocabulary of grind-sounds that has forty words for what a rock does before it decides. They marry inside the guild. They retire to rooms of proven thickness and are visited on feast days and given eel, and they are not happy, and they are not unhappy, and they are the reason anyone is alive.

The whole trade is one distinction. Beneath the Sill the low weather is having its storms, hundreds of kilometres of lightning inside the cloud sea, and no thunder ever comes through the air. It comes through the rock: a pressure pulse, logged, timed, characterised. A listener can tell you how far below the storm is walking. She can tell you it is a storm. That is the skill, that is the nine years, that is what the guild is for: to sit in the dark with her ear to the world and know the difference between the noise the world makes and the noise your own roof makes, and to be right, every time, because the one time she is wrong is a bell.

## The people who go out

The sky-fallers work the flat with carts.

They walk transects across the polished crust, which is a dull mirror at under-light, and they look for the interruptions — the dark shapes that do not reflect. A shearwater of the Under lives its whole life in the cloud sea, sleeps on the wing, never lands, and comes over the rim only when it is dead. Some are fresh. Some are months old and salted dry and light as a hat. The collectors know which winds bring them and name those winds, and there are families who have worked the same transect for six generations and can tell you where the birds fall in a season the way a fisherman knows a bank.

The bone-meal mills are on the east side and the smell is on the east side in the dust season, and the gardens are fed on it, and the flowers come up in rings where a bird rotted, and everyone eats the beans.

Sky-fallers go blind. They are the only people who regularly go out to the rim and look at the deck, the bright useless sunlit cloud floor that is the source of all the light in Comb, and it burns the retina, and by fifty they are led home by a child. Everyone owns rim-glasses; only the sky-fallers need them. Nobody in this town has ever looked directly at the light they live by. The ones who do, go blind. It is stated plainly in the guild's indenture, in the second clause, before the pay.

The freight guild carries ore up two thousand metres of escarpment stair on their backs, from a country nobody in Comb has ever seen, under a cloud sea, in a language nobody in Comb speaks well. They are the strongest people on the Sill and they die at forty. Their knees go first, then their lungs, then their hearts. The guild badge is a spine. There is a chapel halfway up and it is full of men who are resting and will not be going on, and the wage is reckoned in metres climbed, and the lowlanders at the stair-foot think the porters are mad, and the porters think the lowlanders are drowning, and both are correct.

And in the small permanent office on the Elder, three people measure a hill every ten years and publish how much of it is left.

That is the entire function of the office. It has existed for four centuries. Its archive is the only continuous record in the country and the calendar is dated from its first measurement. The measurers are honoured, and are not invited to parties. They also enforce the shoulder-line — the combs may wrap the Elders to the shoulder and no higher, by law — and there was a case, forty years ago, about a hand's breadth of mesh, that went to the deepest court and lasted six years and is still referred to in argument.

## The dead

The dead are dropped.

Down a swallow-hole, into the karst that nobody has mapped, into the dark where the water goes and the eels come from. The belief is that they arrive, eventually, in the cloud sea; and that in the cloud sea they become the birds that never land; and that the birds come up over the rim when they die, and are gathered by the collectors, and milled, and put on the gardens, and eaten.

The loop is closed and everyone knows it is closed and nobody says it at table.

A funeral carries a piece of bright rot — foxfire, farmed damp in the deep tunnels by glow-wrights who sell it by the handful. The body goes down with the light in it. The mourners stand at the lip and watch the small green glow get smaller, and turn, and go out, usually before you hear anything, because you never hear anything.

The drop is free. The light costs. So the poor go down dark, and *he went down bright* means he was loved, and *a dark drop* means what you think it means, and there are people who save their whole lives for a handful of glowing wood and are considered vain by their neighbours and are not vain.

The lowlanders in the stair-foot quarter will not do any of it. They keep their dead in boxes and they send them back down the stair, in coffins, in the same baskets that bring the ore up, illegally, at night, for a fee, and the town's inspectors take the fee too and look away, because the alternative is a mill and a riot.

They think the Sill is the roof of hell: a lid of iron over a hollow, with no sun, no birds, no echo, where the light comes from underneath and the dead are thrown in a hole. The Sill thinks the lowlands are hell: warm, drowned, loud, top-lit, a country you cannot see from anywhere, where everything rots and nothing keeps.

Both theologies agree entirely about the geometry. They disagree only about who is damned.

## How fast to fall

The town is powered by its own undermining. Everyone knows. It is not a secret; it is a schedule.

Every litre harvested off the combs is a litre that can be drunk or dropped, and every litre dropped turns a wheel and dissolves a gram of the roof. The fall-rate is quoted daily, like a price: litres drawn against millimetres lost, an actuarial figure that everybody in Comb can recite and argue about, and a bad fall-rate year brings down a government.

There are three parties and they have not changed their names in three hundred years. The Drop wants power, furnaces, metal, the bright trade, and says the roof was always going and we may as well be warm. The Drink wants water in mouths and combs turned to the houses and to hell with the turbines. The Roof wants everything rationed and the fall-rate halved and is composed almost entirely of people who live under twelve metres of proven rock and will be dead before it matters.

And the east party, which is young, says: there are more throats out east that nobody has sounded. We have always moved. There will always be more.

To which the Roof replies, every time, in the same words, which are carved over the door of the deepest court: *there has always been less.*

There is also the treaty with the country below, negotiated in baskets, in a language nobody here reads properly, concerning the weirs. Because the eels come up through the lowlands first, and the lowlands have noticed, over four centuries, that fewer arrive every year, and have done the arithmetic, and have sent up a number. Comb has sent down a number too. The two numbers do not agree. The correspondence continues. It is conducted entirely by people who have never seen each other's faces, and one side of it is lit from above and finds the other side's portraits sentimental.

## Four people

A listener, sixty-one, deaf to air, in a room of proven thickness. She has not heard a human voice in nineteen years and can hear a roof deciding. At the turning she feels the town inhale through the stone, the pressure reverse, the turbines spin up on the draw, and she writes it down, because it is a datum, because everything is a datum. Her granddaughter visits on feast days and signs too fast and is patient about it. She is teaching her the forty words. There is no one else to teach them to.

A sky-faller, forty-four, half-blind, who can still walk her transect by the feel of the crust and the smell of the east wind. She has found perhaps eleven thousand birds. She has never seen one alive except at the ascent, at the rim, at dusk, coming up the face — and she goes every year, and stands there with her ruined eyes and her smoked glasses, and does not say anything, and goes home.

A boy on the lid, nine, who has the rhyme from the third pan to the drying ground and has just been given a new verse by an old woman who would not tell him where she got it. He does not know it is a survey. He thinks it is a song about not sitting down. He will teach it to his daughter with the correction he adds himself, at fourteen, after the street by the drying ground goes down and he is one of the ones who is counted.

And a counter, fifty, who has an open name from eleven years ago, a woman who was or was not on a stair when the fourth terrace went. He has walked it every year. He has interviewed everyone. He has a folder. He goes out to the seal at night, which is a crime, and puts his ear to it, which is the same crime, and hears nothing, because there is nothing, because sound does not carry on the flat and never has, and he knows this better than anyone alive, and he goes anyway.

He has not rung the second bell. He is not going to. His successor will inherit the folder.

## The bowl

Before you eat, you fill it. Black glaze, shallow, wide, set where everyone at the table can see it. You do not drink from it. You watch it while you talk.

It shows you the works drawing. It shows you the storm walking below, hours before anyone feels it. It shows you, sometimes, a thing that has no name yet, a shiver that is not the furnaces and not the thunder, and then everyone at the table stops with their hands where they are and looks down at a little standing water in a bowl, in a house on a lid of iron, above a hollow that has been hollowing since before the plain was lifted.

Most nights it shows nothing at all, and the meal goes on, and someone tells the story about the fish, and the child signs the rhyme under the table with one hand because she is not allowed to at dinner, and outside the combs are wearing their ten thousand kilometres of thin blue crawling light, and the mats are open, and the whole surface of the world is green and wet underneath and dry as flour on top, and the light is coming up off the pavement into everybody's face.

They are the counted. That is what they call themselves, when they need a word for themselves, which is not often. It is not a boast. It is a status, and it can be revoked, and the second bell has not rung.
