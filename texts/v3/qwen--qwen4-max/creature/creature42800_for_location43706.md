## The Noise Floor

Begin with the smallest, because they are the most numerous, and because they are the reason the world has a texture at all.

In the crevices of the column bark — and the bark is smooth to a hand, but not to a mite — live the snappers. They are the length of a fingernail clipping, jointed, colorless, and they eat mineral flour. Flour is too coarse to swallow. So they break it. Each carries a cocked limb in a socket of the same laminate the columns are made of, and when the limb releases it closes a cavity faster than water can follow, and the water tears, and the collapse of that tear shatters the grain against the anvil of the mouth.

That is the crackle. A hundred billion small cavitations a second, everywhere, forever: the sound of eating, amplified into the sound of the world.

They are not calling. They have no organ for calling and nothing to say. But the grazers listen to the crackle anyway, because where the crackle is dense the bark is sound, and where it thins the laminate is going soft, and a soft column is a column that will not feed you next century. Information has arrived in the world without anyone intending it. This happens constantly here. It is the only kind of meaning the vessel produces on its own.

## Down

Every animal in the standing wood carries a plumb: a dense bead of laid-down mineral in a fluid sac, suspended in a cage of nerve. It does not point at the floor. Gravity is irrelevant at these densities; everything here is very nearly weightless and has always been. The plumb points *in* — toward the local node, toward the sheet, toward the place where the pressure does not swing.

That is the whole of direction. In, and out. Out is where the sound will pulp you.

Because the panes are planes, the animals are effectively two-dimensional. They are born onto a surface, they feed along it, they court along it, and the third dimension is not a place but a mistake. A vertical pane's inhabitants have a *down* that is horizontal; a horizontal pane's inhabitants have a down that is vertical; and where the two populations meet they cannot spawn together, because their embryos read the plumb to lay out the body axis and a hybrid comes out folded wrong. Geometry has done the work of geography. There are no mountains inside the vessel and no distances that matter, and yet there are more species here than in the range outside, sorted by nothing but angle.

Everything sleeps gripping. This is the first fact of every life. A grazer locks its plated feet into the groove; a curtain hooks its whole chain; the small drifting things carry a barb and set it before they go quiet. To let go in sleep is to fall — not fast, a few hundred meters over days, tumbling gently out of the still sheet and into the swing, where the pressure takes the gas out of the body and the body comes apart, and the parts are swept back into the pane and eaten by the neighbors.

There is no word for this, because there are no words. But there is a shape in every nervous system here, and the shape is: *hold on*.

## The Grooves

The grazers are the size of oxen and the color of a fingernail, blind, roofed in overlapping plates, and they rasp the columns in spirals.

The spirals are older than they are. A groove is worn a fraction of a millimeter deeper per century, and the grazers have been four hundred years long and this particular shape for perhaps two million, and the groove they follow was cut by something else — a predecessor, a differently plated animal that solved the same problem and left the solution behind in the laminate. The young find it with their feet. They cannot see it, cannot smell it, do not need to; the groove is a shallow flute in a surface otherwise without feature, and a hatchling dropped anywhere within a hundred meters will walk a widening search until its feet find the curve, and then it will turn and follow it, and it will follow it for four centuries.

They mate along the groove, head to head, because a groove is a line and a line has only two directions and everyone on it is eventually met. Their ornament is tactile: ridging on the shoulder plates, read by palm. Everything here is decorated by touch.

What lives on the column, if you are being careful about it, is not the grazer. The grazer is four hundred years old. The spiral is not. The spiral is a shape that has been continuously occupied and continuously repaired for longer than there have been grazers, and it uses them the way the vessel uses cells, and it does not know it is doing this, and neither do they.

## The Crossroads

Where a horizontal pane cuts a vertical one there is a line, and on the line there is a town.

It is the only place in the vessel where two worlds touch. The faunas come up to it from either side and stop, because past the line the other animals' *down* is wrong and their bodies will not hold. But one animal crosses. It is born flat on the horizontal, feeds there for eleven years, and then at maturity it climbs into the seam and rebuilds itself: resorbs the plumb cage, relays it at ninety degrees, reorganizes the gut, sheds the ventral plates and grows them on the other face. Most of them die doing it. The ones that don't walk out onto the vertical and are, by every measure that applies here, a different animal, though nothing in them has changed but the direction of the fall.

It is the only metamorphosis in the standing wood. Everywhere else, growing up means getting larger and then not.

## The Curtains

A curtain is a kilometer of gauze hung at the streaming edge of a pane, where the stroke pushes water along the sheet and the sound delivers into it everything the volume has to give.

The bodies in it are the size of a hand. They are born at the up-current end, bud from a running stolon, feed for a season that is not a season, and are consumed at the down-current end by the bodies behind them, which is not cannibalism but plumbing. The chain does not move. The animals in it move through it, continuously, at about a meter a day.

So a curtain is a place where bodies happen. It has a front and a back, an intake and an exhaust, and therefore it is the only creature in the vessel with an arrow inside it — the only thing that can be said to have a *before* and an *after* that are not the same eighty-four seconds over again. Cut it and both halves keep the arrow. It has been cut by hail-driven flex and by grazing and by the slow bowing of the columns for ten million years, and it has never once been fewer or more than it is.

## Phase

There is no season, no dawn, no year. There is phase.

The columns contract every eighty-four seconds, and inside that long stroke is the shudder of the hinge tissue firing in relay — seven strokes a second, the tremor under the brass note, the thing you feel in the sinuses. Between the great strokes there is a slack of about nine seconds when the ducts stop and the whole vessel is briefly, fractionally, less loud.

Everything that lives inside the columns is timed to that slack.

The borers are the only animals that ever go into the machine. They are pale, segmented, hooked, and they live in the ducts, and they are the only creatures here who have ever felt a wind. It is a wind that blows one way for forty seconds and the other way for forty seconds and has done so since the Eocene. They keep the ducts open by chewing the film that would close them; the plant cannot do this itself, any more than it can crop its own bark, and so the machine has grown a mouth for its plumbers and the mouth is an animal.

They navigate by taste. The sap in a duct carries a gradient — sugar high toward the cover, mineral high toward the floor — and a borer's whole map of the world is a flavor that gets sweeter as it climbs. They cannot see the galleries. They pass through chambers the size of cathedrals and register them only as a thinning of the taste, a place where the water has been somewhere else first.

They spawn in the slack. All of them, every duct, every column, in the same nine seconds, because a larva released into a moving duct is a larva swept into the pump and pulped. The gametes hang motionless in the pause and settle before the stroke resumes. It is the closest thing this world has to an event, and it happens seven hundred and sixty times a day, and it has never once happened at the wrong moment.

## The Fringe

Below every pane, at the edge of the still sheet, the pressure begins to swing.

Nothing with a gas cavity can live there. Something without one can. The catchers are solid — no bladder, no lung, no resonant chamber anywhere in them, a body of packed gel and fiber that sound passes through without finding anything to shake. They hang in the roar at the fringe and they wait, in the sense that a hook waits, for the dead to come down.

Everything falls to them eventually. Four-hundred-year grazers, curtain bodies, husks, the moulted plates of a whole pane's adolescence. The catchers take it apart and their leavings are fine enough for the sound to lift back into the sheet, and this is the return arm of the loop, and without it the panes would starve in a century. They are deaf in the only way deafness is possible here: there is nothing in them for the chord to hold.

And on the panes themselves there is one thing that has learned to make a fringe of its own.

It is flat, four meters across, the color of nothing, and it lies against the sheet and vibrates in antiphase — a living cancellation, a small hand-made node in a world where nodes are otherwise geology. Food drifts into its quiet. Grazers come to crop the concentration. It eats them.

It is a false here. It has been singing the same silence for six million years and its neighbors have been reading the seam at its edge, where the cancellation frays, for exactly as long. The argument between them was settled before the mountains outside were ankles. Neither side has moved since. There is no arms race here; there is a photograph of one.

## The Ceiling

The lid sweats.

Water distilled off the underside of the cover is pure, and pure water at 4.1 degrees is lighter than the tannin-thick water below it, so it does not sink. It gathers. Under the whole black roof there is a lens of clear water, a hand's breadth thick, a meter in the low places, unmixed, unstained — and the lid is compliant, a drumhead held down by five cables rather than a wall braced against them, and so its underside is not a place where pressure swings. It is the topmost still surface. It is a twelfth pane that nobody counts, because it is also the sky.

In clear water, red carries three hundred meters.

The animals of the lens are flat as leaves and they hold to the roof and they can *see each other from far away*. Nothing else in the vessel can. Down in the amber, a light is a neighbor; up here a light is a landmark, and there are enough of them, fixed in place, sessile, generation-locked to the same square meter of laminate, that the ceiling has a constellation. It does not rise or set. The young learn it in a day and use it for four hundred years to find their way back to the patch they were brooded on.

It is the only place in three point four kilometers of water where a thing can be looked at from a distance. Which means it is the only place where appearance can come apart from what is there.

One of the stars is not a star. It is a lure on the arm of something flat and patient that has been sitting in the same square meter since before the ice finished the range, wearing the light-signature of a brooding female, and eating the males that cross three hundred meters of clear water toward it.

Down on the panes there is a lie about *where*. Up here there is a lie about *who*. It is the only one, and it is not a sin, and it is not clever; it is appetite that found the one loophole in a world of touch, and it has been exploiting that loophole, without improvement, for longer than there have been primates.

## The Thirty-One

The carriers cross the emptiness, and the emptiness is forty kilometers of roaring black nothing where a wrong meter will rupture you.

They read the standing wave with a ring of pressure organs down each flank, the way a hand reads a banister, and they call at nine cycles a second as they go, and the call arrives ahead of them and the pane-dwellers feel the silence change texture and grip a little harder, which is not fear, which is only what the silence means when it does that.

They live four thousand years. There are always thirty-one. The successor is brooded in a pouch behind the parent's head, and it is not counted, and it is not alive in the sense the others are alive, until the last crossing — when the parent, at the far end of a route it has swum for four millennia, separates the bud and dies in the same hour, and the young one takes up the note at the exact phase the old one dropped it.

The route is learned by following. That is the entire curriculum. There is one thing taught in this world and it is a path, and there are thirty-one of them, and they have not altered.

So it is worth asking which is the animal. The soft sixty-meter body is four thousand years old and is replaced on schedule. The path is nine million. The body is what the path is currently made of. Thirty-one shapes, each of them a line through the loud places, each of them continuously inhabited, each of them older than every species that knows it — and the carriers are their tissue, and their pollen, and their memory, and they do not know it, and there is nothing there to know it with.

## The Keeper

At the northern lip the channel runs up out of the water into the underside of the cover, three hundred meters wide, walled in fresh pale tissue, polished, and it opens into the clear lens under the roof, and at the top of it there is a membrane of gum.

The keeper is the length of a forearm. It lives nowhere else. It eats the film that would close the channel, and it has eaten that film without interruption for longer than the entire history of every animal that has ever heard the chord.

It is the only creature here with a *toward*. Everything else lives on a plane or a line that returns; the keeper lives on a gradient, and the gradient goes up, and the film is thickest at the top, and so it works its way toward the membrane and back down and up again, its whole life a slow tidal crawl along a corridor with a direction.

The membrane bows.

The air outside is thin and ferocious and it moves, and when a gust crosses eleven thousand meters of black laminate the gum above the keeper flexes — a few centimeters, over a few seconds — and the flexing pumps a trace of gas through the gum, and the film grows faster where the membrane works. So the keeper crops hardest at the mouth. It has been shaped, precisely and over unimaginable time, to be most attentive at the exact place where the outside touches the world.

And the bowing is not periodic. It is not eighty-four seconds and it is not seven a second and it is not nine. It is the only aperiodic thing that any animal in this vessel has ever touched.

There is no organ for it. Nothing here can be startled; startle is a response to a category that does not occur. The keeper feels the roof of the world move above it, and registers a thicker film, and rasps.

That is the whole of the contact. That is the closest anything in three point four kilometers of sorted dark comes to news.

And what lives in the channel, finally, is not the keeper. The keeper is a few decades old and replaceable and there is a line of them. What lives there is the channel: a shape held open by a mouth, a smooth pale corridor maintained through ten million years of pruning by a world that has never once had a use for it, a habit that has outlasted its reason so completely that the reason is no longer recoverable from anything inside the vessel.

It is the oldest inhabitant. It has no body of its own. It is not waiting — waiting is a kind of change, and there is no change.

It is only being kept.
