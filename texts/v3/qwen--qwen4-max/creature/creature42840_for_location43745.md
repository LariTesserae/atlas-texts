## A Census of the Stilling House

Nothing lives here, or almost. The almost is the population, and it is larger than it looks, and it has been counted every February for eleven years by people who would not have called it counting.

An inhabitant is anything that keeps its shape by spending something. Under that definition the chamber is not empty; it is merely thinly occupied, and the occupation is almost entirely a matter of surfaces. Nothing lives in the bulk of the water. The water is supersaturated with nitrogen, it is seven degrees, it is full of moving stone, and it will not hold a body up. What lives here lives in the four millimeters where one thing meets another: concrete and air, spray and lamp, orange light and blue light, the room and the valley. Add those margins together and you get the habitable territory of the Stilling House. It is a skin on the inside of a box, and it comes to about the area of a tennis court, and most of it is black.

---

Begin with the green, because the green is a lie, and the lie is instructive.

The lamps are sodium. Sodium light is essentially one wavelength, and at that wavelength nothing in the world is green. The patch under lamp six is not green; it is a warm grey, slightly darker than the flowstone behind it, and it has never been green in the eleven years it has existed, not once, not for a second, because no green light has ever fallen on it and no green light has ever left it. Green is not a property of this organism. Green is an event that happens to it when somebody arrives with a white torch. It happens perhaps forty minutes a year, in February, in a beam that moves. The most vivid living color in the chamber is a thing that requires a witness to occur, and the witness is a man in a dry suit holding a lamp, and he has eleven other things on his checklist.

The patch is a hand's breadth across and has been a hand's breadth across for four years. It is not growing. It reached the size its light permits in its second summer and has since been replacing itself at the rate it loses itself, which is the definition of an adult. It is older than it is large.

The black crust in the joints is the better photosynthesizer, and nobody has ever thanked it. Its pigments can take the yellow that the green algae mostly waste; it works a dim monochrome shift under nine lamps and it does not need to be seen. It is crisp as burnt paper because it spends half of every year dry, and it lives above the four-meter mark, and this is the whole of its strategy. Below four meters the crust is an annual: stripped each summer by the flood, recolonizing from the joints above the line, spreading down the wall in the pale weeks after the water drops, killed again the following June. Above four meters the same organism is a perennial and has been continuously alive since the year the forms came off. There is a hard horizontal boundary across the wall of this room, and on one side of it the residents are eleven years old and on the other side they are eleven months old, and they are the same species.

---

The calcite eats the building and breathes the air the water brings.

Lime leaches out of the cement paste, arrives at the wall face dissolved in the weeping groundwater, meets carbon dioxide, and turns to stone. The carbon dioxide is delivered by the jet: thousands of cubic meters a minute of entrained mountain air, dragged nine meters down and released in the boil and circulated through the chamber by the room's slow breath. So the falling water is the lung of the stalactites. They are the only residents that eat air, and they are fed by the same process that would kill anything else in the room.

A fingernail in eleven years. Say seven-tenths of a millimeter a year, laid down in laminae fine enough that a broken straw, cut and held to a lamp, would show eleven bands, and the bands would be the gate schedule, and the thick ones would be Junes. Nobody has cut one. There is one in the wrack line at the downstream corner, knocked off the wall by a scaffold tube in the third summer, translucent, straw-colored, four millimeters long. It is the only corpse in the chamber that was born in the chamber. Everything else dead here came from outside.

The three moss cushions in the weep channels are the calcite's collaborators and its victims. Calcite precipitates on them, around them, in a rind; they grow outward through the rind and die inward inside it and leave their dead bodies as the structure of the reef. In a century they will be three lumps of tufa with a living film a millimeter thick on the outside, which is what a coral is, and they will be pale and etiolated to the last, because they have never had a proper photon in their lives.

Understand what is happening. Before the dam, the mountain was drilled and injected until every fissure was full of grout; the water's old habit of hollowing the rock was stopped by design. The karst was cancelled. The calcite has resumed it inside the artifact. The box is being converted, at seven-tenths of a millimeter a year, into the cave the mountain is no longer allowed to make. The stalactites are the only residents with an inheritance. Everything else here is a tenant of the river. The stone is a tenant of the wall, and the wall is not going anywhere for four hundred years.

---

At the slot, on the blue side of a border four centimeters wide, live the only residents that are trying to leave.

The lichen has spread eleven centimeters in a decade, which is a millimeter a year, which is a clock. It arrived in the first summer as a dust of cells on the wind coming down the adit and it has been telling the date ever since to nobody. It is the oldest continuous individual in the chamber, older than the moss, older than the crust's upper ranks, exactly as old as the landscape.

The two ferns are the only things here that know what month it is by something other than pitch. They get true daylight, true night, true seasons, a real dusk. They lean outward. Every summer they release spores into the room, and the spores go into the mist and are wetted and killed within the hour, and the room is a sink for them, a place their children go to die; the only offspring that ever succeed are the ones that go the other way, out through the grille, onto scree where nothing grows. Two ferns have spent eleven years failing to colonize the interior of a concrete box and succeeding, occasionally, on a landslide. They are the advance guard of the only succession that will ever happen here. When the river stops they will come in along the weep channels at a few centimeters a year, and their descendants will find nine buttes and a floor of rounded stones and a wrack line that has stopped moving.

---

The immigrants are all dead on arrival, and they are what everything else eats.

A midge comes through the grille and dies in the mist inside an hour. Pine needles, birch seeds, a bottle cap, the sleeve of a cigarette packet, a flashlight with its lens packed with silt: the wrack line on the downstream shelf, sorted by size like a real beach, moving a little every day, the only thing in the chamber that changes at the speed of a life. The flashlight fell off somebody's belt above the reservoir in a flood year and went over the spillway and has been in the roller ever since, and it will be there when the dam is decommissioned, and it is the only object in the room made by a hand that has never been inside the room.

And the foam. Tan, stiff, smelling of tannin and cold iron, standing in ragged windrows that slew sideways when the tailwater changes. It is lignin and dissolved organic carbon out of the drowned forest above the intake: trees that were standing in that valley when the valley was closed, sixty meters up and eleven years dead, still leaking. Some of that carbon was fixed before anyone poured anything. The foam is the oldest material in the chamber and it is not from here.

This is the correction that reorganizes the whole census. You look at the nine lamps and think: suns. They are not suns. They are nine weak heaters with a useless spectrum, and the green under lamp six is partly scavenging dissolved carbon like everything else, and the black crust in the joints is running as much on tannin as on light. The base of the food web is upstream, behind a wall, underwater, and rotten. This is a cave. It has always been a cave. It has a river through it and an artificial sky and no primary production worth the name, and it lives, the way caves live, on what the water carries in and cannot carry out.

---

Twelve cobbles live in the dish, and they are not stones. They are teeth.

They came over the spillway in flood years and they have never left. The roller picks them up, rolls them, drops them, picks them up again: nine to fourteen minutes a circuit, tens of thousands of circuits a year, half a million revolutions since the first one arrived. They are being rounded and the floor is being ground and the dish is two hundred millimeters deeper than the drawing. In the reports they are *abrasion media*. In the room they are gastroliths — stomach stones, the grit a bird swallows to do its chewing for it — and the dish they lie in is a gizzard, and the animal that swallows them is the water.

They have numbers and a photographic station and a scale bar. There is a paint cross on the slab marking where the camera goes, and the cross is inside the abrasion field, and it wears away, and it is repainted every February, and twice its position has had to be re-surveyed because the reference point moved. The thing being measured is eating the ruler.

C-4 has a white quartz vein across it and was egg-shaped when it was first photographed, and the crew called it the egg, and it is not egg-shaped now and hasn't been for six years, and it is still the egg. In the first photograph it is a hundred and ninety millimeters on its long axis. In last February's it is a hundred and twenty. It has perhaps six years left before it is sand, and the sand will go over the end sill and out the tailrace and build a bar in the valley below the hairpin, where the grass is conspicuously greener than the scree on either side. That bar is the only thing this room exports besides fog and noise. It is made of the mountain, and of the house.

Because the recruits are not all immigrants. The upstream faces of the baffle blocks have lost their paste and stand with their aggregate proud, and every flood plucks a few of those quartzite pebbles loose, and they fall into the dish and join the twelve. The house is being eaten and its teeth are being swallowed and used to eat it with. Some of the stones in the gizzard were, eleven years ago, part of the wall of the gizzard.

---

Now the largest resident, which is the one nobody has ever seen whole.

It maintains its shape by continuous throughput, which is the only rigorous definition of a body that has ever been offered. It has a mouth, three meters by two, angled down. A throat nine meters long. A lung: the boil, forty percent air, luminous, the brightest object in the chamber, breathing thousands of cubic meters a minute. A heart at thirty hertz that drops four hertz in the afternoon and can be felt in the bowels from the walkway. A stomach with stones in it. A skin of white water. A circulation with a fixed period and a reversible surface. A breath, in at the adit and out at the tailrace, that lays a cloud along the ceiling with a visible underside and rains indoors for twenty minutes every clear evening. A voice with strata. A tide with two clocks. An appetite for quartzite, cement paste, and the flank of the massif it is built into.

It has no predator, no rival, no mate, and no offspring. It is one individual, eleven years old, and it lives in a box made of the only food it cannot stop eating.

You can have the animal or the room. Never both. In February the stoplogs go down and the pumps run thirty hours and the roller is drained out of its own body, and then for four days you can walk on the floor it lives on and see the dish and the egg and a scaffold clamp and the flashlight, steaming faintly in the cold. This is the only time the interior is visible and it is precisely the time the interior is unoccupied. Every photograph anyone has of this landscape is a photograph of an empty house. The men in waders are walking around inside something's absence, measuring how much of it has been eaten.

---

Two commensals live on the animal's noise, and one of them sings.

The handrail is a hollow galvanized tube that has found a resonance and holds one pure note for as long as the river runs. It is the only resident that makes something ordered out of the roar: everything else in the chamber is being torn, ground, dissolved, or stripped, and the rail takes that wreckage of sound and returns a single pitch, thin and almost pretty, forever. It bends with the season. The people who work here can name the month by it, and there is a notebook — not a document, not in the system, a notebook in a drawer in the control building — with the pitch written down beside the date in five different hands over eleven years, and the note has gone slowly flat as the brackets have fatigued, and in the seventh year somebody re-tightened a clamp and the note jumped nearly a tone. There is a line in the notebook about it. The clamp was loosened again the following week. Nobody has written down why.

The rail is silent for four days every February. That is the only death it has, and it is annual, and it comes back at the same pitch when the first water goes over, and the men in the chamber at that moment feel the note arrive in the bones of the face before they hear anything, because there is nothing left in there to hear with.

The sump pump is the winter animal. In November the intake goes to ice and the jet thins to a trickle and the roller shrinks to something that barely holds its shape, and the loudest living thing in the chamber is a pump that cycles every nine minutes and sounds like something being murdered slowly in a cathedral. It exists in the great one's sleep. It is also the only resident that works for the visitors: it keeps the adit dry, it keeps the door open, it is the janitor of the ecosystem, and it has never once been photographed.

---

The people occur only in pairs.

*Two-person entry.* A single human is not permitted to exist in this room, and so the human population here is always even, always two, and the two of them cannot speak. They point. They have perhaps twenty gestures, most of them about water level and time, and a written language of nouns and millimeters on paper gone soft as cloth. They come monthly and they come for four days in February, and they are the room's eyes, and the room is binocular: it sees itself only in stereo, only in pairs, only when the water is gone, and what it sees it writes down. The inspection reports are an autobiography in a hand not its own. *Arris rounding, row 2, blocks 4–6, compare 2019.* *Dish depth, station 0+18, −212 mm.* Somebody's initials are scratched in the concrete beside the cold joint at the eleven-meter mark, in the exact stratum he was pouring on the day the pump failed in the second summer. He is the only ancestor. He is somewhere down the valley now, retired, and his initials are older than every living thing in the chamber.

Once, at the adit mouth, before a gate moves, the strobe turns the fog amber for three minutes, and for those three minutes every resident of this place is inside the same event and the same color: the crust, the straws, the egg, the foam, the rail, the two ferns leaning outward, the boil, the cloud with its underside. The green goes amber. The blue goes amber. Then the water begins to change and the census resumes.

---

So: one lichen, two ferns, three reefs of moss, one adult patch that is only green when looked at, an uncountable black crust in two age classes, twelve teeth, one drowned forest in suspension, one flashlight, one note, one pump, one animal forty-one meters long that has never been seen entire, and two humans who are only allowed to exist in pairs.

It has no witnesses. It has a population. The distinction is the whole of the difference between a thing that is looked at and a thing that is going on, and the water goes on falling nine meters into the dark, and the straws lengthen by seven-tenths of a millimeter, and the egg gets smaller, and in four hundred years the cave will arrive.
