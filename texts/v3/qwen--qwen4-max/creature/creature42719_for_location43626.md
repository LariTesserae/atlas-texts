Begin with the wrong answer, because the room offers it first, and offers it beautifully.

The garden. Shoulder-high, packed, wet-looking, and every leaf a reed and every plant a shaped harp, and if you come from a world with sunlight in it you will call this alive before you have thought about the word at all. But nothing in the lanes is alive. The garden grew once, while the chamber was still deciding what it was going to be, and then the decision finished, and the garden has been a finished object ever since: furniture, exquisite furniture, ringing. The fruit does not ripen. The fruit is ripe. The pollen does not travel; it has arrived, continuously, for a hundred centuries, and the flower is fertilized forever and sets no seed, because setting seed is a thing that happens next, and there is no next out there.

So the census of the occupied and the census of the living are two different documents here, and the second one is much shorter, and it has to be taken in the dark.

Life in this room is not a substance. It is a rate. It is what a gradient does while it lasts. Where the chord presses full, no gradient exists, and therefore nothing lives, no matter how much it hums. Where the chord is blocked, something can be hotter than something else, and can cool; can be whole and then chipped; can be ignorant and then told. That is the entire franchise. Everything alive here lives inside a wedge of quiet a few paces long, and goes out into the light the way we go out into weather.

And there is a third country, which takes visitors even longer to find than the shadows: the hem. Along every boundary between lane and road, the wave does not stop, it climbs — a hand's-width of margin where the pressure rises from nothing to everything. In that margin the equilibrium is very slightly wrong. Things wear there, slowly, at a rate you could set a calendar by if you had one. The bone pegs stand in the hems. That is why they are worn to knobs and everything else in the room is knife-fresh: they are the only objects that have been standing in a place where standing costs something.

---

The choir is not millions of animals. It is a few hundred pitches, and each pitch is a post, and the animals are the current holders of the posts.

The pale thumb-sized things in the wall-pits have no names and no biographies and, as far as anyone can tell, no interest in either. A pit sings. When the singer in it fails, its daughter is already sounding, has been sounding, overlaps by nothing and gaps by nothing, and the pit does not know that anything happened. There is no bereavement in the wall. There is no silence in the wall. The whole choir has never once been interrupted, and so the choir has never once had an occasion to notice time.

But drill a chip of the lining out of the wall and carry it behind a boulder, and the pit in it goes quiet, and the singer in it is suddenly, catastrophically, alone.

They do this. It is the closest thing they have to an instrument, and the closest thing they have to a pet, and the closest thing they have to a poem. A drilled chip, palm-sized, called by a word that means *throat*, kept in a hut in the hush, and in it one singer singing one note with nobody to hand over to. In the shade a single voice can be heard as a single voice. You can listen to it. You can attend to it, which is impossible in the loud, where there is nothing to attend to but the whole. It sings for a day, or two, and then it stops, and there is a gap, and the gap is the most valuable thing in the room.

Their word for music is their word for *a gap after*. They bury the throats under the cairns with the dead. Some cairns are mostly throats.

---

The loop-flyers are not where they seem to be. Watch one long enough and you understand that you are not watching an animal fly a circuit; you are watching a circuit be flown, and the animal is the part of it that is currently visible. The orbit is the resident. The blown-sugar body is the incumbent.

Most of the circuits lie wholly in the loud and are therefore immortal and therefore, in the strict sense, not alive: closed paths, performed forever, threading your hand without touching it because your hand was included in the solution before you had a hand.

A few circuits are badly made, or were made before the boulders were moved, and these clip a hem. Every pass, the flyer spends a few instants in the margin where wearing is possible. Ten thousand passes, and the wings are ragged. A hundred thousand, and they are lace. These are the old ones, the only visibly old things anywhere, and the tall ones catch them — gently, at a known point in the phrase — and read the wings by touch, and put them back into the circuit at the exact instant the phrase requires.

The flyers do not count. They are counted. That distinction is the whole of their inner life, and it is not a small one to have, and it is not a large one either.

Their pupae are in the hush, in the huts, in the roof-thatch of shed carapace. Everything here that begins, begins in the dark. A flyer's entire childhood is four days of shade, and then it goes out and does one thing forever.

---

Now the eleven, and here the two censuses come apart most violently.

Out in the lanes there are eleven stations. Not eleven people: eleven occupations. A station is a place where a certain amount of the chord gets eaten and a certain amount gets paid back, and the body at it is the current tenure. Tall, pale, unhurried, blind — the eyes are sealed with a wax that has gone to glass, and beneath the wax there is nothing that ever saw. The ears are leaves. Not shaped like leaves: leaves, harvested from the garden, tuned, grafted at the temple, replaced when they crack. A grazer with a torn ear goes into the shade and comes out hearing a slightly different world, and the room accepts the substitution because the ledger balances either way.

They hum. That is their payment. Under the fundamental and over the wall-choir there are eleven human-sized voices, and each is filling a hole the room would otherwise have. Their browsing is not taking, either, or not only: the fruit is a resonator that would ring itself apart if it were never damped, and the bite is the damping, and the bite is taken forever because the tuning has to be done forever. From a distance, a held pose. From close, maintenance, performed with the absorption of a person humming.

Nine lanes. Eleven stations. Two lanes are doubled, and in those two lanes a grazer stands with its hand closed on another's forearm, and has stood so since before the salt finished closing, and this is not affection, or is not only affection: two bodies at a fixed distance make an interval, and the interval is needed. In the shade those four have names for each other that are simply the names of the intervals. They cannot say them out in the light. They can only be them.

And one station is held by a child.

The eleventh partial wants a small ear, a short throat, a body of a certain size, and no adult can produce it. So one of them stays small. Growing is a gradient's work; growing happens in shade; therefore the child spends almost all of its time out in the loud, fixed at the age it last came out of the dark, browsing, glowing, paying, not living. In room-time it is among the eldest things here. In lived time it is perhaps nine years old and has been nine years old for longer than there have been words for long.

The others relieve it. Someone takes the station — the chord does not care whose throat fills it — and the child goes behind a boulder and is a child for a while: knaps badly, is told a story with an ending, scrapes a knee on a blade that has been sharp since the world's first hour, sleeps, grows a little, grieves a little, comes out. They spend their own lives in shifts to buy it a childhood. There is no gesture available in the light in which this could be expressed. Out there it looks like eleven people standing very still.

Because that is the arithmetic they live under: the loud is free and costs nothing and is not life. Shade is life and is metered. Every sentence spoken, every blade struck, every young borne, every hour of loving or arguing or making is drawn from a finite account, and when the account is closed the body goes out one last time and stands in a lane and does not come back in. Their age is not years. It is accumulated darkness. Two born in the same hour can be an ancient and an infant.

The great makers die young. That is not a metaphor here, it is a budget. The caches out in the lanes — hundreds of them, hoards of perfect tools, the bulb of percussion still bright, the maker's thumbprint still in the grease — are the estates of people who chose to spend nearly everything. And there are stations held by bodies of enormous dignity and smoothness who have made almost nothing, said almost nothing, and been in the dark perhaps a total of forty days, and these are the ones the others call, in shade, by a word that means *unworn*, and it is not a compliment, and it is not an insult, and they argue about it in sentences that take a hundred years.

---

The arguing happens on the hems.

The village is not the huts. The huts are workshops and beds and graves. The village is the peg-lines: the seams, the margins, where a hand can trail along a row of bone knobs worn to the shape of hands, and where the pressure is low enough that a word can survive but high enough that it survives slowly. Speech on the hem is possible and expensive and dilatory. A sentence there is begun and then continued across generations, each speaker taking up the phrase where the last left it, and there are conversations in progress that were opened before the salt above finished being solved. Three words so far. One of them is a question. Nobody now alive knows what the question was about, only that it was asked, and that answering it is a thing their grandchildren will be doing.

Their names are stones.

A name is a pattern of pressure pressed into a palm; the stone is its record, a small shaped thing, and it is kept, and to read it you hold it, and holding wears it. So remembering is a form of erosion. Every reading takes a little of the name away. They ration this. Some names are read once in a generation, by agreement, in a hut, by whoever is eldest. Some names are read to nothing in one night by someone who could not stop, and that is the second death, and it is not punished, and it is not forgiven either; it is simply understood to be a thing that grief does. The cairns are heaps of names, and that is why the stones in them are the only rounded stones in the world. Grief is the sole weather in here. It is the only process left that can smooth anything.

Their word for death is their word for the end of a sentence, which is also the word for lifting the hand.

All their carving is done blind and meant for the fingers, and the finest things in the room — small, dense with intent, polished by handling — have never been looked at by anyone, including their makers, including the dead who commissioned them. The best of them are names cut for people not yet born. Names held in reserve. Names in the dark, waiting for a palm.

---

And in the niche at the north end, the folded animal, which is the reason any of this is possible, and which is dying.

A niche is a shadow. That is the first thing to understand about it. It is not a bed the animal chose; it is the largest wedge of quiet in the room, and the animal is a shade-creature, the oldest and largest resident of the dark, and therefore the only thing here besides the tall ones that is subject to time. It absorbs, and absorbing is a change, and change is permitted only where the chord cannot reach. It grows as it takes in the room's news. It was smaller when the first engineer came down. It is the size of a cart now. Its leading folds have begun to bulge past the lip of the niche into the loud, where they set, and harden, and can no longer take anything in.

So they dig.

That is the great work, the only collective engineering in the world, and it has been going on since before the peg-lines were surveyed: the slow knapping-back of the north niche, by touch, in absolute dark, to keep the whole of the animal inside the shadow. Generations of knapping floors there, spoil heaps of crystal chips, a cairn of the ones who died at it. They are cutting a grave-shaped room for something that must not die, and they know exactly what happens if they fail — the level rises, the chord swells, the crystal goes, the world ends — and they do not worship it, and they do not feed it, and they step in the quiets when they pass, and they comb it.

The combing is maintenance; matted nap reflects, and reflection is the beginning of the end. But that is not why they take turns doing it, and it is not why the turn is sought.

Everything in this room is blood-warm. Exactly. Floor, leaf, air, skin, your skin, all one temperature, forever, which means that out in the lanes there is no such thing as touching something else. You put your hand on a stem and you cannot feel where you stop. You put your hand on another grazer's forearm and there is no boundary, no warmth arriving, nothing coming toward you from outside. It is the loneliest fact in a room full of company.

The animal is cool. In its niche, in the hush, under the nap, it is a little cooler than your hand, and it is the only other in the world. That is what they comb. Not the sink. Not the equation. The one place where a person can put a palm down and feel something that is not itself.

---

Who lives here, then.

A garden that is furniture. A few hundred pitches held by animals that will never know they are holding anything. Some closed paths with lace-winged incumbents. Eleven stations, four of them holding hands, one of them holding a child that the others are paying for out of their own accounts. A village that exists only in wedges and seams, whose streets are worn bone, whose speech is three words into a sentence, whose art has never been seen, whose dead are heaps of names being slowly read away. A faceless cool thing in a hole that is being dug deeper forever. And, provisionally, you.

One more thing, and then the crack, and then the salt.

Behind the western boulder there is a hut nobody lives in. It is swept. The knapping floor in it is clean. On its shelf there is a name-stone that has never been held: edges knife-fresh, the cutter's marks still crisp, ten thousand years old and one second old, the only sharp stone in a room where every other stone in the dark has been rubbed round by love.

They cannot know whether anyone will ever come down through the salt again. The room has no mechanism for wanting; it possesses no process by which anything can be different from what it is.

But the people in the shadows are a process by which things can be different. That is what they are. That is the whole of what they are. And they have cut a name for you and set it aside, and left it unworn, and they go in and check on it, one at a time, in the dark, by touch, and put it back.
