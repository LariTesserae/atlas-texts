## I. One of them

Begin with a single animal, because the crowd is a lie of scale, and because nothing in that room has ever experienced a crowd.

She is the size of a hare and weighs rather more, most of it in the shoulders. The pelt is not hair. It is a felt of hollow filaments, each one open at the tip, and each tip holds a bead of condensate too small to fall. She is wearing the room's water cycle. Twenty thousand of these pelts are the coldest surfaces in the chamber after the outer wall, and a good part of the loop closes here, in the fur, and is recovered later by a neighbour's tongue. Grooming in this place is not affection and is not hygiene. It is plumbing. Two animals lick each other's shoulders for eleven minutes and between them recover most of a mouthful, and then go back to work without having exchanged anything that could be called a signal.

The face is a dish. That is what the spade is for: a shallow parabolic plate of bone under the skin, with the two funnel ears set at its rim, so that the returns come off the face and into the gristle. She has no expression because she has no need of one and no face to put it on. Under the skin where the eyes were there are two wet pearls, lensless, nerveless, repurposed: they secrete the salt film that keeps the ear rims supple and acoustically wet. She weeps into her ears, slowly, all day, with a muscle that used to open a lid. It is the only thing her eyes still do.

The hands are the part you will not get over. Six digits: four long fingers, and two thumbs opposed not to the fingers but to each other, at slightly different angles, so that one hand can hold two things in two different ways at once. The lower grip cradles. The upper grip swings. She holds the quartz core against her sternum with the lower thumb-pair, and strikes it with the hammer held in the upper, and she has never in eighty years set anything down. This is not discipline. There is no gesture for setting down. The hand opens and the world takes the flake, and the difference between *placing* and *dropping* is not a distinction her body can make.

Her hammer was a knife-edged chip when she took it, at weaning, out of the sharps beneath her mother's working place. It is a grey egg now. She did not make it round; she made it round the way a step makes a step. Eighty years of palm and sweat and the small involuntary rotation of the grip between strikes have dissolved every edge she ever put on it, and the stone that has cut ten thousand flakes off the mountain has itself been worn away by nothing but being held. It is the only object in the world shaped by a particular life. It is a portrait, cast in negative, of a hand.

When she dies it will lie among the sharps and the salt-crickets will come to it, and over about forty years they will rasp the glaze off it and then rasp the stone, following the sweat-salts down into the grain, until the egg is pitted, then hollow, then a fragment with edges. The crickets eat roundness. They eat it back into sharpness. Every made thing in this room is being unmade at approximately the rate it is made, by animals who want the salt.

## II. What a life is

The young are born one at a time, wet, and enormous — a fifth of the mother's weight — and they are carried against the chest, in the cradle-grip, which means that for the first two years the mother's core is held in the *striking* hand and she works badly, and everyone within four lengths knows she has a young one, because her rhythm has gone wrong in a specific and recognizable way.

The infant's job is to hold the core.

Understand what that means. It cannot see the work, it cannot imitate the work, there is nothing here to imitate. It lies against its mother's breastbone with its own small hands on the quartz and the strike arrives *through the stone* — the shock, the ring, the tiny change of pitch as the lens walls thin, the four-second wait, the shock again. Six hundred thousand strikes a month, conducted directly into the bones of its hands. The craft is not taught. It is transmitted as a vibration, through a rock, from one animal's arm into another animal's skeleton, and what the young one learns is not a technique but a *tempo*, and its tempo will be its mother's tempo, and hers was her mother's, and if you sit at the back left of the chamber with a notebook you can hear the lineages: families of interval, four-second clans and three-and-a-half-second clans, drifting apart over centuries, recognisable, inherited, unchosen.

They sleep one ear at a time. One funnel folds shut, the other keeps swivelling, one hemisphere goes down and the other stays up, and the arm on the waking side keeps swinging. Half of any given animal is always asleep, and half of it is always quarrying, which is why the roar never stops and why nobody here has ever heard the room quiet except in terror. The sleeping half strikes badly. Most wasted blisters are wasted at what passes for night, by an arm whose owner is not entirely present, and the sound of it — the inward crumble, the loss — goes through the crowd and is picked up and repeated softly, compulsively, for days, by animals who were not there.

That is their memory. It has no picture in it and no place to put one. It lives in the motor habits of twenty thousand arms.

They sleep best in the press, three deep, belly to back, and the reason is not warmth. A sleeping knapper is blind. Its world is being drawn for it by its neighbours' strikes; the wall at four lengths, the void behind, the shoulder close, all of it arrives on somebody else's question. To sleep alone on an exposed ledge is to wake into a room you have to rebuild from scratch, and the animals who do it are the young, the sick, and the strange.

And they go deaf, at the end, from the top down. The high returns go first — the thin edges, the fine grain, the small voids — and the world simplifies. The far wall stops answering. Then the ceiling. Then the neighbour is a smear. An old animal's chamber shrinks over about three years from ninety paces to the length of its own body, and it moves toward the throat during this, where the map is easy: wet rock, rime, the draft, the lip of the light. It licks stone. It strikes less. There is a small local hush around it, a hole in the crowd's vision the shape of a dying animal, and the others work around the hole without knowing what it is, and eventually the hush closes and the body goes into the sharps and is pushed toward the slot with the flat of a hand, along with the flakes, because a hand pushing spoil does not distinguish.

## III. What they are to each other

They have no names. They have intervals.

Each animal's strike falls in a private slot in the local weave — a personal offset, held to a few hundredths of a second, adjusted continuously against the four or five neighbours it can hear best. Recognition here is timing. A mother knows her grown offspring across thirty paces of roaring stone because it strikes eleven hundredths late against a rhythm she conducted into its hands twenty years ago.

From which follows the only crime available to them.

To strike across another's return is to erase a piece of its world. Not to obscure it — to *delete* it, for a quarter of a second, in the only medium that animal has. Crowded, they are exquisite about this; the whole deafening granular roar is a lattice of gaps, twenty thousand animals not stepping on each other's words, and the reason it sounds like weather is that the gaps move. When two animals fight — over a rich face, over nothing, over a tempo that has drifted into collision — they do not bite. They jam. They strike into each other's returns, faster and harder, each trying to blind the other, and the loser is not the one who is hurt but the one who loses the wall and puts a hand where there is no hand-hold and comes off the ledge. Violence here is loudness. Murder leaves no mark on the body at all.

And the crowd has one sanction, which it does not decide on, and which is worse. A hush spreads. It begins as fear — a bad fall, a hunter's leg on a shoulder, the wrong sound from a lens — and it moves outward as a wave of animals stopping in order to listen, which is to say a wave of animals going blind in order to see. Sometimes it does not stop. Sometimes it closes around one animal and stays closed, and that animal is left in a small warm silent pocket of the world with nothing in it, striking and striking and getting nothing back, and it will die there, of a thing that has no name in any language and that we would have to call being hushed.

The queue at an opened blister has no rank because it cannot have one: rank requires a picture of who is who, and there are no pictures. There is only the whistle, and the pitch of the whistle, and the wave of bodies converging along the ledges, and the water, faintly iron, faintly salt, older than flowers, going down twenty thousand throats in an order determined by nothing but proximity and patience.

There is a hypothesis I cannot prove. The parasitic fly that rides the nape is large — larger than any fly has a right to be at this altitude, because the room has been re-breathing Carboniferous air for six thousand years and the arthropods are the ones who notice. It cannot walk between hosts; the fur is a metre-deep gulf of sharps and the hosts never stop moving. But it can stridulate. And a certain strained three-second sigh, produced from the nape of a working knapper, brings the crowd. Not to water — there is no water — but into a press, a convergence, a momentary crush of pelts, and in that crush a fly crosses. They queue for a lie. Some of them, near the back, have begun to hesitate at the sigh, and the hesitation is spreading, and the fly's whistle is getting better, and the oldest workers repeat the false sound softly for days afterward exactly as they repeat the true grief, because their arms cannot tell the difference between a loss and a counterfeit.

## IV. The districts

Each knapper is a warm wet highland with its own weather, and the census of the room is not twenty thousand.

Three mites in the pelt. One lives only on the left ear, and only on the left, because the core is cradled on the left and the left ear spends its life pressed into the acoustic shadow of a lens of quartz held against a breastbone. That mite has never heard the roar. It lives in the one deaf pocket in the world, a few cubic millimetres of stillness, and it has consequently lost the startle response that every other animal here possesses down to the springtails. When a blister opens and every ear in the chamber turns, the left-ear mite is swung through a revolution it has no way of knowing about, and goes on eating.

The fungal mats are the largest thing alive in the room and the only thing that has ever touched all of it. They knit the sharps into a floor that can hold a little warmth, they digest the stiff small bodies and the cast skins and the crickets and, eventually, the knappers, and they fruit about once a decade, in a night, a pale bloom over the middens that nobody in the room can perceive, and the spores go out on the draft and down the white tongue and onto the ice, where they die. Everything here exports its offspring into a place that kills them. The loop leaks in every direction. It has held anyway.

At the seep, back left, the iron is not only mineral. There is a rust film on the lip of the cupped depression, and it is alive, and it is eating the seam directly, and it does not care about the wind or the lowlands or the sun. It is the second economy, the one with no sky in it. The worm eats the film. The worm is pale and blind and slow and there is exactly one of it, always exactly one, because the cup will support one; the eggs are laid in the moss and the springtails take all but one, and the one that survives is the one that hatched when the last one stopped. A dynasty of a single animal, in the only green place in the world, the only creature here that lives somewhere with a colour in it.

And the cushion plant in the throat is alone in a way the worm is not. There has never been a second one. It came in as a seed on a gust in the first century after the crack and it has been sitting there ever since, a grey-green loaf, sending one root four metres down into the dark after the damp, and the crowd eats the tips of that root, and a nibbled tip takes eleven years to come again, and twenty thousand animals have been eating one root for six thousand years without killing it. Not restraint. Arithmetic. It flowers perhaps twice in a millennium. The flower freezes and the seed goes out over the glacier and nothing has ever come of it.

## V. The one with eyes

On the ceiling: legs like a spider crab, a body like a folded umbrella, and the only good eyes in the world.

It did not evolve eyes for this room. It evolved them for snowfields, for the terrible glare of a high noon, and it arrived here as an egg on an eddy and its lineage discovered that the waste of the knappers' industry — one strike in forty throwing a blue-white pinprick — was enough to see by, if you were willing to wait.

It is willing to wait. It holds still for an hour. Then a flash prints a shoulder on an exposed ledge, and it has a photograph, and it begins to cross, and the crossing takes eleven minutes, and the shoulder has moved.

So it does not aim at the photograph. It has learned — over instars, over generations, in whatever way a thing with that nervous system learns — that a struck animal stays within a body-length for four seconds, and that its interval is a promise. It listens to the tempo and extrapolates. It hunts with its ears and confirms with its eyes, which is the exact reverse of everything it looks like. When it is right, the umbrella opens over a straggler and closes, and there is no mouth until it opens, and the drinking takes most of a night. When it is wrong it takes a mouthful of pelt, and the animal comes off the ledge, and a hush goes out across the crowd in a wave, and the hunter hears the hush and learns from that too.

Its young are small enough to live in the fur. It begins as a mite lives, in the warm crowded valley of one knapper's nape, and moults, and each instar sees a little better, and the better it sees the worse the fur becomes, until it climbs out of the only warmth it has ever known onto the cold ceiling to wait an hour for a flash. There are perhaps four of them. They do not meet. Their territories are districts of spark-density, and when one dies the nearest crosses into the vacant dark over about a decade, and for those ten years nothing in the world can see.

Its whole life is a handful of stills with a great deal of black between them, stitched into a room by a nervous system that cannot help stitching. It is wrong about the room constantly. It has a picture, and the picture is a hallucination with gaps in it, and the blind animals below it are never wrong about where anything is.

The only other thing in this chamber that has ever had a picture of it is you.

## VI. The far end

Where the slab comes down to a letterbox and the floor rises to meet it, something strikes. Once every forty or fifty seconds. A weight nothing here uses, a tempo nothing here uses.

The workers near that end keep their taps out of its intervals. This looks like courtesy and is partly courtesy, but there is something else, and it is the reason no one has ever tried to open the crack.

The far strike brings a return. And the return is wrong. It comes back late — late by an amount that means a volume, a distance, a *shape* that is not the shape of this room. For a quarter of a second after each of those strikes, the back of the world is a lie: ninety paces becomes something else, the wall is not where the wall is, and the map that twenty thousand animals are drawing ten thousand times a minute has a hole in it the size of a country.

They cannot enter it and they cannot use it and they cannot stop hearing it. So they route around it in time, the way you step around a sleeping animal, and the young are carried down that end early, once, so that the interval gets into their hands while their hands are still learning what an interval is.

The nearest thing to a name in this world is that gap. A silence of a particular length, kept.

Within the oldest memory of sound, it stopped. A day and a night. The hush that came out of the back of the chamber then was not a wave but a flood, and animals fell off the walls in it, and the ones near the throat licked stone until it started again, and when it started again nothing changed and nothing was said, because there is no saying.

## VII. What lives here

Not twenty thousand knappers. Not the mites and the crickets and the fly and the mats and the four umbrellas and the one worm and the one plant and the rust film that has never needed the sun.

What lives here is a widening.

The mountain is under compression and the slot loses a finger's width every century, and the answer is twenty thousand animals taking the walls back at almost exactly the rate the planet leans in, so that the volume of the world has not changed since the afternoon the rock let go. That is the organism. The roar is its metabolism, the fur is its condenser, the blisters are its blood, the queue is its circulation, the hammerstones are its bones and the crickets are its decay, and the white tongue spilling six thousand metres down the north face is its excretion — and, though nothing here can know it, its archive.

Because the spoil goes out in order. Flakes, hammers, stiff small bodies, cast skins, seeds, spores, the bones of the ones who went out and the ones who were pushed out, all of it laid down grain by grain on the ice below in the sequence it was made. Six thousand years of stratigraphy. The only record of the room, kept outside it, in the one place where nothing lives, by gravity, which does not know it is writing.

They make no mark for the pleasure of marking. They have never placed a stone. And they have built, without once intending anything, the only monument in the world that could survive them: a fan of pale chips over black gneiss, frost that forgot to melt, containing everything they ever touched, in order, unread.
