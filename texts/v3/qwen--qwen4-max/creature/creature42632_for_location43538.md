Begin at the bottom, with the only resident that was here first.

Under the shelf, under the freshwater lens that the rain has been laying down for sixty years like a brown blanket on blue water, there is a boundary layer where fresh meets salt and the oxygen quits. It stinks of sulphur. Swimming on its back along that ceiling, rowing with a fringe of pale paddles, is a blind crustacean the length of a finger, colourless as a peeled almond, with a pair of venom-bearing jaws it uses on things it has smelled rather than seen. It has no eyes. It never had eyes to lose. Its ancestors were in this water when this water was inside a living reef, and the reef went up into the air and died and became a forest, and the animal did not notice, because the halocline stayed where it was.

It is the one creature on the shelf that does not read the light. The blue pulse comes down through the pits twice a minute and it means nothing here. It hunts by chemistry and by pressure — and pressure, down here, is the boom itself, the swell arriving through the rock, so it lives inside the heartbeat rather than watching it. Everything above learned to read the flash. This one simply never needed to.

Above it, in the lit water, the fish are immigrants and always have been. They are the descendants of reef sleepers that came in through the cave mouths in the first year and spawned and mostly failed; every spring tide still sends a thin silver recruitment in from the sea, and most of it dies out there, and a fraction stays and never sees the sun again. Their eyes have gone enormous and useless for form. They do not make light often. Making light costs, and the shelf provides light for free, so they wait: hanging motionless in a surge channel until the boom comes and the walls flash, and in that half-second of borrowed blue they strike. A fish that pays for one look in fifty. A few of the older lineages have photophores under the jaw and use them, sparingly, the way you'd use a lamp in a room with a window.

Almost nothing on this shelf is native. The trees came on rafts. The bats come nightly. The fish come on the tides. The crabs' larvae come in from the sea and climb the terraces in a wet red thread every wet season, and the snakes' ancestors probably arrived in a root-ball, and the mosses came as spores on a frigatebird's foot.

The only endemics are behaviours.

---

The crab is nine centimetres across the shell, red going to black at the joints, and it has one claw that is a tool and one claw that is a weapon, and it cannot use either for the other's work.

The groove is on the fixed finger of the left chela: a channel a few millimetres deep, lined with a field of stiff bristles. A flake seats in it or it does not. The crab decides in under a second, and it decides by ringing the stone — tapping it against the rock and reading the return through the legs, because in a world this loud the ear is worthless and the substrate is not. A dull thud is a rotten flake, a stone with a hidden fracture, a thing that will crumble on the snail. A high clean tick is glass. They are connoisseurs of a sound they cannot hear.

The numbers are the point. A foraging crab handles perhaps two hundred stones in an hour and uses four. It picks up, taps, rejects; picks up, taps, rejects; the rejections are the activity, and the use is a flicker inside it. Sixty years of ten million refusals an hour have quietly sorted the plain: the good glass has been carried to the rims and dropped there, the rotten stuff has been left inland where it fell. Nobody tidied this. The workshop has been knapped by rejection, over and over, by animals with no intention beyond *not that one*, and if you could see the whole shelf at once you would see a floor laid out with terrible precision, blades concentrated exactly where the fruit falls and the cold air pours and the light comes up.

The groove is not permanent either. It is grown fresh each molt, and claws are lost constantly — to rivals, to snakes, to the simple stupidity of a fissure closing on a limb — and the replacement grows in slightly wrong, and the crab spends a fortnight rejecting stones it would formerly have accepted, and then either the new groove is good enough or the crab starves slowly among edges. Even the organ of manufacture is disposable here. Nothing is kept, because keeping is expensive: a claw holding a stone cannot hold a snail, or a rival, or a female. And there is another cost, which is the centipede.

It lives in the fissures, half a metre of it, and it hunts by subtraction. The boom is broad and deep and comes from below and it ignores that; a claw-tap is sharp and small and comes from above and it goes to that. Every use of a blade rings the floor like a bell in a cellar. So the crab's whole economy is speed: seat, cut, eat, drop, *move*. The one-second blade is not thrift. It is a creature trying not to be a sound.

Once in a while — and this is worth noting, though it is nothing yet — a female on the second terrace will keep a flake seated while she walks four body-lengths to a snail she has already located. Nine seconds, perhaps. Then she cuts, and drops it, and goes.

---

The birds cannot make an anvil. They can only find one, and they find them by the shine.

The anvil bird is a rail that has more or less stopped flying: hen-sized, dull brown, wings used for balance on wet ledges, a bill thickened at the base into something between a hammer and a chisel. It walks a route. Nine stones, three hundred metres, twice a day, and it carries snails to them in its bill from the fissures where it digs them out with its feet. It selects the stones that are dished and polished, and it selects them because polish is *information*: a shine means snails have been broken here ten thousand times, which means snails live within carrying distance, which means the ground here is worth walking. The wear is honest because it cannot be faked. It costs sixty years.

So the only writing in this country is a gloss on a rock, and what it says is: *there has been food here for a long time*.

Young rails follow old rails. That is the entire mechanism of transmission — no song, since song is pointless under the grinders; no demonstration, since nothing is being taught; just a following reflex in the juvenile and a route in the adult, and the route outliving every bird that walks it. When a pit collapses and takes three anvils and a snail-bed with it, the route breaks, and the birds that held it must wander and find another shine, and most of those wanderers die on the sharp ground with nothing in their crops. There are perhaps forty routes on the shelf. They are the closest thing here to property, and no bird owns one, and two birds walking the same route in opposite directions will fight at every stone and remember nothing afterwards.

The snakes lie in the cold outflow with their heads tilted up. Their eyes are directed at the fog, and they hunt by silhouette: a crab on the rim above is printed black against the blue flash, a hanging root, a bird's foot. They are cold — genuinely cold, the outflow does that — and therefore slow, and therefore patient in a way nothing else on the shelf can afford. Eleven days between meals is ordinary. Their pit organs face into a stream of chilled air, which means that for the first time in this whole warm wet country a warm body is *conspicuous*, and what is warm here is bats. The snakes eat the dawn return: the tired, the injured, the pups, the ones that misjudge the ledge coming in fast out of a sky that is often solid. The cold plume is not a shelter for them. It is a lamp.

---

Three days a year the plain goes soft.

The synchrony is total enough that you can date it from the boat. Every crab on the shelf molts inside about seventy hours, and for those hours the ground is red and slack and edible, and the traffic stops, and the fissures are packed with animals lying still and pumping themselves full of water, and everything with a hard mouth comes out to eat. The roar drops by a third, because the grinders get eaten in windrows. The rails get fat. The snakes gorge until they cannot move and are then themselves eaten by centipedes. A fly whose larvae have waited a year in the leaf layer, doing almost nothing, emerges, mates, lays, and dies inside the window; its entire adult existence is three days long and it has no mouthparts.

And roughly one crab in four hundred does not synchronize. It comes hard-shelled out of the general softness and walks through a country made of food, and eats its own species until it can barely move, and is then killed by something bigger. These off-cycle animals are the reason the synchrony is not perfect: the payoff to being late is enormous and the mortality is enormous, and the balance sits where it sits. They are also, for reasons that will matter, the animals most often found alone, far from the rims, up on ground where nobody goes.

---

Go up.

In the bromeliad tanks, thirty metres above the scree, there is a crab that has not touched the ground in four million generations of its own lineage — which is to say, sixty years, which is to say, since the mats came in with trees already carrying tanks already carrying crabs. It is pale, slow, long-lived, and it has almost no groove. There is no stone up here. There never was.

What there is: the saw-edged tip of a bromeliad leaf, and the broken lip of a snail shell, and the fact that both are *rare*.

So this crab carries. It holds a leaf-spine in the groove that is not really a groove any more and it takes it with it, from tank to tank, across the dry afternoon bark, and it will fight for it, and it will die without it. It also carries shells — not for shelter; for water. It fills a shell at a tank and holds the mouth against its gill chamber and walks, and the reason is that it has stopped going down. The ground crabs still run their larvae to the sea, the whole red thread of them every wet season. The tank crab cannot; the descent is thirty metres of knife-edged stone and a floor that eats crabs. So it broods. Six large eggs instead of six thousand small ones, held under the abdomen, kept wet from a snail shell, and the young hatch as crabs and never learn what the ground is.

Each big tree is an island. Each tank is an island on the island. The populations are tiny, and they drift, and one tree's crabs are now noticeably different from the next tree's, and there is no mechanism for mixing except a bat crashing through a crown or a hectare of forest letting go into a pit and taking the whole nation with it.

Understand what has happened. Nothing here got cleverer. The provision simply ran out — no blades, no sea, no room — and in the gap where the provision used to be, there is now possession, and containers, and brood care, and forty isolated little cultures of animal that will never meet.

The orchids up here have the same problem in reverse. They cannot make light, so they return it: white petals with a mirror-cell layer that catches the pit-flash from below and throws it back up into the fog in a pulse, timed to the boom, and a moth comes to the pulse because it navigates by holding a fixed angle to a flashing pit and a flower that flashes is a pit that will hold still. It is a lie made of borrowed light. It works beautifully on the low terraces.

---

The roof is where the shelf is fed.

The bats roost in the caves in their millions and pour out at dusk and go a long way — off the shelf entirely, over the water, to the old coast and the river mouths and the fruit trees of a country that has soil. They come back before dawn with their guts full of somebody else's sugar and they void it into the cave system, and the water carries it up under the terraces, and the pits breathe it out as ammonia, and the forest that has no soil grows on stolen nitrogen delivered nightly by air. The raft forest arrived once, by sea, and has been kept alive ever since by theft.

Under the roost mouths the molt-piles are a metre deep and moving: guano, shed bat fur, half-chewed fruit, dead pups, and a fauna of flies and beetles and pseudoscorpions and mites that ride the crabs. There is fungus down there in sheets, doing the only agriculture on the shelf, farming bacteria on a substrate no animal built. The frigatebirds overhead never land at all — they cannot, easily, from a flat surface — and they take everything they eat from other birds in the air, and they sleep on the wing, and they are not residents. They are weather with an appetite.

And under all of it, in the leaf layer, the grinders.

They are flat wingless things the size of a thumbnail and they cannot make a sound loud enough to matter with their own bodies, so they press themselves against dead leaves and stridulate into them, and the leaf is the loudspeaker. They choose leaves. They test them, they move to a new one when the old one rots, they prefer a particular stiffness and curl, and a hectare of tuned leaf-litter over a hollow drumskin of limestone is how you get a wall of sound that presses on the sinuses and makes a visitor's eyes water. It is not speech. It is jamming — a fog of noise to match the fog of water, and inside it the insect-hunting bats that echolocate can hear nothing at all.

Once a day, in the slack hour after dawn, every grinder on the shelf stops at once.

They stop to listen. They put their legs flat on the rock and they read the tremor of the plain, all of them together, a daily census of the ground's integrity: the microcreep before a slump, the far-off grinding of a root letting go, the small foreshocks that come before the big ones. It is the only reason a population can persist on a surface that drops a hectare at a time. And it means that the one silent hour of the day is the hour when ten million animals are listening to the floor, and everything else has learned to be very still, and the sudden hole in the sound is frightening for a reason that no explanation improves.

---

Now walk up the terraces.

The highest one is sixty years older than the lowest, and sixty years of rain and root-acid is enough. The conchoidal snap has gone soft. The stone crumbles rather than fractures; the flakes are chalky at the edge; a crab up there handles four hundred stones an hour and uses one. Some of the pits have silted shut and some have drained, because the cave roof beneath this terrace is high and dry in places, and a dead pit does not boom and does not flash. So it is quieter up here, and darker, and the orchids that pulse are pulsing at nothing, unpollinated, setting no seed. The fog is thinner. The bazaar has moved downhill, as it always does, following the good ground.

What is left on the high terrace: old trees with old grip, dull stone, long walks, thin crowds, and the off-cycle animals — the ones out of step, the ones that ended up alone.

There is a female up there who has been coming back to the same stone for four years.

It began as the nine-second thing: keeping a flake seated while she crossed to a snail. Then it became a preference for a particular upstanding blade at a good height at the edge of a dead pit where the fruit still falls, because that blade seats well and the fruit falls there and she knows the way. She has dished it herself. Ten thousand snails, one claw, one animal — a private anvil, worn by a single crab, which is the first private thing on this shelf, and she does not own it, and she will die and it will stay.

This season it has gone blunt.

She does what she does to everything hard that will not open. She takes a lump of the local stone in her weapon claw and she brings it down on the anvil with the shell-breaking stroke, the only stroke she has, the one she has used a hundred thousand times on snails — and she is not making anything, she is trying to break a nut that happens to be the ground.

Most of the time this produces rubble. Sometimes it produces a wreck with no edge at all. Perhaps one attempt in a hundred throws a flake with a clean conchoidal scoop and a glassy lip, and it seats in her groove, and it is sharp, sharper than anything on this terrace now, and she uses it to slit a fruit, and she drops it in the same second, because there is no reason on earth to keep it, and she walks away over the scree with the centipede listening.

She will do this maybe four times before she dies. Her daughters will follow her route, because following is what daughters do here, and one of them will pick up a flake she made and find that it rings clean and use it and drop it among ten million flakes the ground made by itself, and there will be no difference between them, none, not to her and not to anything else alive.

Below her the young terraces are still cutting hands open. The pits are booming out of phase, the fog stands in its hundreds of columns, the shadows fall upward, the roof turns over in the dark, and the whole deafening finished machine runs on borrowed edges as it has run since the night the sea let go.

Up here there is one more sharp thing in the world than there was yesterday. It is lying in the scree, unmarked, indistinguishable, and it will be picked up or it will not.

That is the entire difference, so far.
