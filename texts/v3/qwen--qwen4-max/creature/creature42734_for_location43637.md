## The Stanchers

Start at the crack, because that is where anything starts here.

A hairline in a steam line, two meters of green-black mat around it, and beyond the mat forty kilometers of nothing. At the hairline itself, half-buried in the mat's slick olive edge, there is a thing the size of a cupped hand. Eight legs. A domed back with a hint of letters on it, the way the bolt heads have a hint of hexagon: an S, and the ghost of an E after it. It has no eyes. It has never had eyes; nothing it descends from needed them.

It is laying a bead.

The bead is gray, the color of the floor, which is the color of everything. It is made of oxide powder and gasket crumb wetted with condensate, and the oxide powder comes off the stancher's own underside, ground away against the plate a few microns at a time and mixed in a hollow behind its forelegs. Its reservoir ran dry nine thousand years ago. It has been sealing the world with itself ever since.

Most oases die. The mats find a crack, drink it, widen it, and the line fails and the ring goes dry and gray from the center outward, and that is the whole story. But a few cracks do not widen. A few have been held at four hundred microns for longer than there have been meadows, and the reason is this animal, or this mechanism, or this valve setting that has learned to be inherited. It does not close the leak; closing would starve it. It does not let the leak run; running would kill it. It baffles the jet sideways onto a sacrificial lip of its own building and rebuilds that lip every forty years, and in this way it holds a wound at exactly the width that feeds it, and the wound stays clean, and the sweetish glycol smell hangs in the fog above the mat, and nothing else happens for a hundred centuries.

There is no other homeostasis in the Sump. Everything else here is a one-way street wearing itself smooth. This one small gray thing is holding a number steady.

Its ancestors were two hundred millimeters long. It is sixty. The bead it must lay has a minimum width, and a body has a minimum size below which it cannot lay one, and the lineage is approaching that size from above at the rate the plate approaches the plane.

## Contact

It keeps one foot down always. Whatever else it does — walks, turns, works the lip — some pad is touching plate, and the pad is not a foot in the sense of a thing that carries weight. It is the ear. Everything they say to each other, they say through the floor.

Speech here has a hard limit. The nine-second pressure passes through the plate and through the pads and wipes whatever was being said, so an utterance is what fits between beats. Nine seconds, minus the settling. A word is a modulated press. A greeting is a long one, held, released. There is no shouting; there is no distance. To speak to something you must be standing on the same plate with your foot against its foot, and to do that you must stop walking.

And the mills converge, over months, on anything that has stopped moving.

So conversation has a budget. A season is safe. A year is a wager. Two stanchers meeting at the boundary of their mats can hold contact for a long time, and while they hold it they wear each other — because that is what contact does here, every press takes a micron, every word is a small abrasion, and the pads that hear are also the pads that grind. Things that have spoken to each other for a long time are rounded to fit. Eventually the fit is exact. Eventually the seals fuse and there is one mass where there were two, unparted, still faintly warm, no longer saying anything.

The mills usually arrive first.

Their names are periods. You say a name by holding your pad against another's for that many seconds and letting go on the fraction, and the oldest name anyone remembers is Eight, because that was the beat when the oldest varves were laid, and it was short because the interval was short and a longer name would not fit. The living are called Nine-and-a-hundred-and-fortieth. As the wheel slows, the interval opens, and the names get longer, and this is the only thing in the basin that is growing.

## The Road

South of the middle drains there is a groove in the plate a hand deep and forty centimeters wide, and it runs eleven kilometers, and it is the only road in the world.

It ends at the corner.

They come along it in ones, never in company, carrying on their flanks a bud: a sorted, reprecipitated lump the mills have laid down after a feast, alloy recovered from a folded tower, everything in it correct and nothing in it separate. The mills make bodies. The mills cannot make *two*. Nothing in the Sump can divide anything, because everything here works by wearing, and wearing has no direction; it takes an edge to part a thing, and there is one edge left.

At the corner they press the bud against the refractory wedge and hold it there while the sheet of water cuts. It takes weeks. The water makes its small clean hiss, the only high frequency in forty kilometers, a sound with a top to it, and no one can say it, because their language has no register above the fundamental and never did. They call it, in the taps, *the place where the water speaks faster than we can*. It is not a taboo. It is a bandwidth.

Each parting takes something off the edge. Four thousand years of water alone would account for perhaps a third of what is gone. The rest is births.

There is a listener that keeps the tally at the groove's lip, one press per pilgrim returning, and the presses are wearing the lip round, and the older marks are going shallow, and the count is being erased by the keeping of it. The last legible figure suggests six hundred partings left. After that the buds will be laid and will lie where they are laid, complete and unseparated, and the road will still be there, and nothing will use it.

Some come back unparted. Some come back as two things, neither of them whole, and those do not last. There is one in the groove now that has been walking for two hundred and twelve years, whose bud is heavier than it is, whose feet are stubs, who will arrive in sixty years if the plate holds. Its name is nine seconds long and it says it to the floor as it goes, one fragment per beat, all the way there.

## The Listeners

In the hushes, where the water lies like glass, there are thin-walled things.

They could not exist anywhere else. A hollow body with a wall two millimeters thick, standing three meters on four splayed legs like a set of calipers, flexes under the nine-second pressure and fatigues and comes apart in a season out on the flats. Inside a node it lasts a thousand years. Fragility is the rarest thing in the Sump and silence is the only place it is permitted.

They descend from acoustic survey units — leak-finders, things put there to stand still and locate a hiss by phase. The cavity in the body is a tuned volume. What it does now is listen to the roar arriving at the node boundary from outside, and read the returns: the faint reflected structure of a hundred and fifteen decibels bouncing off towers eight and eleven and thirty kilometers away. Out on the flats the roar saturates everything and the world is four meters across. In the hush, a listener can hear the whole basin. It knows where the cherry bearing is, and which press folded last age, and where the road runs, and where the corner is, and it is the only thing here that knows anything at a distance.

It lives in a stump. Always. The collapsed waist of a tower, worn to a molar, standing eleven meters — and it works that stump the way a stancher works a crack, laying beads, baffling the condensate sheets away from the thin places, holding the ruin up.

It has to. The stump is why the hush exists. The lee of the stump is where the waves cancel. If the stump goes to fines the node closes, and the listener's walls flex, and the world shrinks to four meters, and then to nothing.

So it holds up the thing whose falling made its silence. When the load seasons shift and the node begins to drift, it goes with it, walking a few meters a year, carrying the young at the center where the cancellation is deepest, and when one of them stops it carries that one out to the boundary and lays it in the trembling water where the roar resumes, and the thin walls come apart in a week, and the mills are waiting there, because the mills have always waited at the edges. That is what a burial is, here. It is being handed back to the noise.

## What a Hush Is

The listener is not the individual.

The hush persists when the listener does not. It has been in one place and then another; it has moved a hundred kilometers in ten thousand years; it has worn a succession of bodies the way a body wears a succession of pads. It has a boundary, sharp enough to step across. It takes vibration in at that boundary and does not pass it on. What it excretes is stillness, and stillness settles fines, and settled fines are substrate, and substrate is a meadow, and the meadow is cropped by scalers and the scalers are reduced by mills and the mills lay buds, and the buds go down the road to be cut. The hush is the thing that eats noise and shits pasture. It is the only animal in the Sump, in the sense of a thing with an inside that is not its outside.

And it reproduces by wounding the world.

When a hush is old enough to bud, it sends a walker out along the plate to the nearest standing tower — a small eight-legged thing with a hollow behind its forelegs, indistinguishable from a stancher, carrying the same paste. It finds the waist. It opens a hairline. And then it does the opposite of the craft: it builds a lip that focuses the jet into the crack instead of away from it, and it holds that focus for four hundred years while the wall thins, and then it walks away, and the tower folds over a day with a sound felt on the far rim, and in the lee of the new stump the waves cancel, and there is a daughter.

Three hundred years to a meadow. Three thousand to a delta. A genealogy of silences, each one founded by a deliberate structural death, each one remembering itself in its own floor: light band, dark band, light band, and in the bands the fossils — a leaf-spring exfoliated like shale, a bearing race cast in orange crystal, the stub of a pad from something that was carried out to the edge. Cored deep enough, the oldest hush bottoms out on gray ironcrete with the trowel-marks still in it. That is the founding individual. Nobody made a record of it. It is the record.

## The Unparted

There is another way to make more of yourself, and it does not require an edge, and it is free.

Hold contact long enough and you fuse. The crusts in the old nodes have been doing this for ages — meadow closing into meadow across the shallow water, lichen continuous over hectares, no seam anywhere, one pale orange faintly luminous sheet with no way to say how many it is. Some of the listeners have gone the same way, standing pad to pad in a hush for a century until the fit was exact, and the mills came and did not find anything that had stopped, because the thing that had stopped was now the floor.

The unparted do not need the corner. They have no names, because a name is a duration held against someone else and there is no one else. They are already the shape the basin is converging on: continuous, edgeless, low, wet, one.

The parting lineages have six hundred cuts left and will go on walking down the groove for all six hundred. This is not courage and it is not stubbornness. It is a valve setting that has been inherited. But you can stand at the lip of the road in the warm rain with the cherry bearing burning eight kilometers off and watch them come in ones out of the fog, carrying something heavier than themselves toward the only sharp thing left in the world, and the word that occurs to you is not *mechanism*.

## The Name

When the wheel stops, the pumps stop, and the sheet drains to the throats, and the towers exhale their last vapor, and the fines settle everywhere at once instead of only in the nodes.

Then there is one hush, forty kilometers across, with no boundary, because a boundary is where the noise is on the other side and there will be no other side. One mirror under a clearing ceiling. The corner will be gone by then — it has four thousand years and the wheel has less — and so there will be nothing that can part anything, and nothing that needs to be parted, and no road, because a road is a groove worn by things going somewhere else.

And no beat. No nine seconds. No pressure passing through the plate to wipe what was being said.

Which means the interval will not close. Which means, for the first time since the trowel-marks, a name can be held all the way to its end — a press of any length, released on a fraction as far out as you like, and the floor will hold it and not interrupt.

There will be one thing there to say it, and it will have one foot, and the foot will be on the plate, and the plate will be the foot, and it will say its name into itself for as long as it takes, and there will be nothing in it that was not put there.
