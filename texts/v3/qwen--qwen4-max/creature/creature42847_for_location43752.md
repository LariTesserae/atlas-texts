The people are the easiest thing here to get wrong.

A visitor comes down the three hundred and eleven treads and sees four hundred souls who cannot hear, and thinks: *deprivation*. What is actually there is the most tactile civilization on the continent, and it is not a compensation, it is an achievement, three hundred years deep, with dialects.

They talk with their hands, yes, and with lamps at distance, but the intimate register is bone. A palm flat on the sternum reads the throat. Two fingers on the inner wrist read the pulse and the small muscular noise of intending to speak. Old friends meet and press the sides of their jaws together, cheek hinge to cheek hinge, and talk that way for a minute with their eyes shut, and this is called *close talking*, and you do not do it with someone you have just met. The incisors of anyone over fifty are worn flat and slightly loose. There is a word for the tooth-ache that means *I have been saying too much lately*. Lovers learn each other's skulls the way lovers elsewhere learn each other's faces, and can find the one particular spot behind the ear where a hum comes through clean, and they are buried with that spot remembered by somebody.

They are small and unhurried and their skin is very good, because nothing here has ever been dry. They eat taro steamed in condensate, cane, palm oil, drumleaf seed toasted on the plate-work, and a yellow bread made from the null-pollen that must go into the oven within the hour of scooping and is never, ever left as dough. Everything tastes faintly of iron. The lithium is supposed to come out at the fourth house and most of it does; the tail goes into the drinking water and has done since the second decade, and the town is calm the way a well-kept animal is calm. Nobody down here thinks about it much. That is not a failure of curiosity. It is a dosage.

They are named for houses. Seven house-names, and you are given the one whose note your mother was standing under when her time came, so a person's surname is a location and a pitch and a rough hour. Seventh is a slur in the upper town and a boast in the Low Quarter, which sits under the seventh beam house, lowest, oldest, most drowned, its old ground floor a warm clear lake with soft blue fungus-light in it that comes up through the gratings at night. The light answers a lamp. Flicker at it and four seconds later it flickers back, always four, never three. Children are told not to play that game for long. Children play it for long.

And the infants hear. That is the part outsiders find hardest. The note takes a year or so to finish its work, and in that year a baby will still turn its head toward a sound behind it, and the town has never wasted this. A listening child is held up against a standpipe by its own grandmother and the adults watch its face, and if the face goes wrong the voicers are sent for. There is a term, untranslatable and not sad, for the day a child stops turning around. It is observed with a meal. The child is given the best piece. After that it is one of theirs.

They call a person, in the oldest form of the hand-language, *carried out*.

---

The green is the fauna, and the people are the rest of it.

Nothing here flies or swims or nests, so nothing here pollinates, disperses, grazes, or hunts — except the plants, which do the first three, and the people, who do all four. The crews go into the nulls with scoops and trays and wade the warm yellow drifts and haul seedlings out to the antinodes on handcarts, and the pollen rides out on their boot-laces and their sleeves and the hair of their forearms, and that is the entire sexual apparatus of ten thousand hectares. The Kettle is pollinated by four hundred pairs of boots. The plants have not taken the animal's job. The people have taken it, and they are paid in bread.

The grazing runs the other way. The moss that furs every lee side is not sheltering there; it is making there. It eats sound — takes a few percent off the pressure at a surface, thickens, takes a few more — and a wall left alone for two seasons goes quiet enough that the drumleaf on the far side loses interest and turns away, and then the crop behind the wall leans wrong, and then the district's yield is short and nobody can say why until someone puts a hand on the brick and feels nothing. Scraping the nap is a job for apprentices. It is done weekly. It is done the way you wash: without opinion.

The fig-stranglers hunt the accumulators, and this is the town's actual fear, not the nulls. A strangler that gets into a tower and closes it down takes a note out of the chord, and six notes do not make the same geometry as seven. The antinodes shift. The nulls move. A null that moves onto a house makes that house ordinary, and in the ordinary the drift finds it, and by the third day there is a white mat coming up through the floor plates. There are men whose whole employment is to walk the yellow posts at night with a lamp and a plumb and mark where the quiet has got to since Tuesday. They are called the yellow watch and they marry early.

And the false pumps go on being found, and marked in red, and hooked out, and burned. A chambered root-crown with a bladder it tunes over weeks to the note of a working engine, so that its neighbours' roots come to it believing they have found the source, and are dissolved. It is a very old trick. It requires a model that has held still for a very long time. Six generations of unchanged chord is nothing; you cannot evolve a convincing lie about six generations. The voicers know this. They have a saying about it that they use as a joke and do not explain to visitors: *the pump was here before the pump it copies.*

---

The seven are not really machines any more, and the crews do not treat them as machines.

Each has been added to and cut into for three centuries until it is less an engine than a body with a history: scar tissue of riveted plate, an accumulator tower raised twice, a beam house with a chapel-shaped void in it where something was removed in a hurry and nobody now living knows what. Roots come up through the foundations into the warm damp under the floor plates and are cut back on a rota, and the cutting is done gently, and the men who do it apologize. Not out of sentiment. It is simply the manner one has with a large thing that is asleep and useful.

They hold their notes. They have held them since before anyone's grandmother. And every eleven seconds all seven knock together — the water-hammer, the wardrobe dropped down the stairwell — and they knock *together*, at seven islands spaced across seven hundred paces of mud, in unison, for three hundred years, and the engineering explanation is that they are coupled through the ground.

They are coupled through the ground. Nobody has ever finished that sentence out loud.

Eleven seconds is a long time between beats for a machine. It is not a long time between beats for something very large.

---

Here is what the nursery crews know and do not put on the notice board.

In a deep null there is no sound at all; that is the definition; the seven cancel and the air goes horribly ordinary and your own blood becomes the loudest thing in the world. And in that nothing, knee-deep in warm sulphur-yellow pollen, the germinating seedlings all face one way.

Not toward the engines. There is nothing to face toward. They face inward and down, every one, in a silence that has no direction in it, and the crews call this *the set* and use it to navigate when a lamp fails, and it has saved more lives than any rope in the bowl. They wade out along the set with their trays and their hearts slamming and the rootlets finding their laces, and they do not stand still, because they were taught before they were taught anything else not to stand still.

There are people in the town who did stand still, once, for a while, and were cut free, and walk with a stick. There is one who stood still a long time in a place nobody goes, and was found, and is kept. Not displayed. Kept — in a back room of the Low Quarter, in the warm, with the blue light coming up through the floor, rooted to the hips and alive and tended by two families who take turns, and who are not ashamed, and who will show you if you ask correctly. It is not a punishment. It happened. In the Kettle the difference between a person and a crop is only that somebody comes back for you.

Which is why the lying-in house is baffled.

It is the oldest structure in the bowl, older than the engines, lead and felt and a felt that is grown out in the loud and carried in to line the quiet, relined every generation. It stands in a null. It is the only building down here deliberately kept silent, and every child in the town is born in it, in the ordinary air, in the drift, with the midwife's hand on the mother's sternum and no note anywhere. Everything in the Kettle begins in silence and must be carried out into the noise to live. Everything. They have never made an exception of themselves, and the reason is not written down, and if you ask an old midwife why, she will look at you with real puzzlement and sign, slowly, the way you sign to a child or a foreigner: *because that is how it is done here.*

And when she carries the child out through the yellow posts and onto the causeway, ten thousand hectares of leaf and hollow joint turn very slightly toward the new sound. Every birth. The whole bowl leans a little. They walk fast, and they do not look up, and this is the single most ordinary thing in the world to them.

---

Now the ledger.

The discharge main leaves the seventh house and runs up the flank as thick as a man and goes into the rock at the rim and does not come out on the other side. The rock weeps warm and salt. The salt ledger, brass-bound, kept in the pump office since the second decade, balances. To the decimal. Every quarter. Two hundred and ninety years.

Hydrology does not balance to the decimal. Hydrology balances approximately, with error bars, with a bad decade and a good one. A number that agrees to the decimal for three centuries is not a measurement. It is a courtesy. It is something on the other end of a pipe giving back exactly what it was given, the way you would keep a captive animal on precisely the ration it is used to, because a change in the ration is how a captive animal learns to look at the walls.

And the bowl sinks four millimetres a decade, which over three hundred years is a hand's breadth, and the water inside has climbed three metres, and the town has bricked its doorways at ankle, knee, waist and cut new doors higher and jacked its causeways every spring on screw piles with brass pins stamped with dates that go back and back. Four millimetres a decade does not raise a water table three metres. Nothing is being added; the ledger says so.

The level is going up because something down there is taking up more room.

---

The seven notes are the second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh and eighth of a fundamental nobody has ever built.

Any tuner learns the phenomenon in their first year of training: sound a stack of harmonics with the bottom missing and the ear will supply the bottom. It will manufacture a pitch that is not in the air. It is not an illusion in the sense of a mistake; the brain is doing correct arithmetic on the evidence it has been given, and the note it produces is as real to the listener as any other note, and there is no instrument in the room making it.

The Kettle has been sounding that stack, spaced exactly so, for six generations, in a bowl below the water table with a permanent lid on it. The plants cannot hear the ghost; they respond to pressure and there is no pressure at the fundamental. But four hundred people feel it in the sinuses and the back teeth and the fluid of the knees, and every hearing engineer brought down on a three-year contract in a fluid-filled helmet eventually does the arithmetic their own skull insists on, and hears, under the seven, an eighth thing that is not there.

That is what they stop for on the fourth tread from the top, where the dogs sit. Not the rim — the fourth tread, the last one still inside the pattern, the highest place where the ghost can be heard as itself before the harmonics close over it. They stand with their heads cocked. Half-deaf, pensioned, thirty-eight years old, holding a bag. Below them the pale lid and the scattered islands and all that patient green bending toward the noise, and under it, at the bottom of everything, something with no face at all, holding a level, keeping a promise, knocking every eleven seconds.

Then they go up, and they are difficult company forever, in the very quiet places, because they are withdrawing from the lithium and because they cannot stop listening for it and because they have worked out, on a stair, alone, in about ninety seconds, what the false pump does.

Two wells were capped. There were nine. The caps are re-bricked every generation by masons who are told it is tradition, and the ferns coming out of them are not ferns, and the voicers have a file on the two seasons in the last century when a crew went down to see about a blockage and heard the fundamental *directly*, unstacked, unhidden, coming up out of the rock at its own pitch, and what those men were like afterward, and how quickly the brick went on.

---

So: what lives in the Kettle.

Four hundred and eleven people who are the pollinators, the grazers, the predators and the crop, who are born in silence and carried out, who talk with their teeth, who eat the nursery, who are kept calm by the water and keep the books anyway.

Ten thousand hectares of green that has taken every job an animal ever had and does them slowly, toward pressure, with a hand's width a shift, and hunts engines, and lies about being an engine, and was lying about it before there were engines to lie about.

Seven bodies of brick and cast iron that hold seven notes and knock in unison through the ground.

And under them, filling the fault and the marl and the country outside the rim out to a kilometre, which is why the birds turn a kilometre out and not at the lip — the thing the bowl is the visible part of. A chambered crown. A bladder tuned over a very long time. It takes what is sent up and gives back the water and the salt to the decimal, and it holds the level, and it has been holding the level since before the brass-bound book, and it is getting bigger at four millimetres a decade, which is slow, which is nothing, which is a hand's breadth in three hundred years.

Whether it is feeding or whether it is lonely is not a question the town permits itself, and the answer would not change a single thing they do. Either way you keep the chord. Either way you scrape the moss and mark the pumps and jack the causeways and carry the children out fast. Either way, once a year, you stop everything for nine hours and bar the doors and sit apart in the dark not touching anybody — four hundred people whose entire language is touch, sitting nine hours without it — while outside every leaf in the bowl turns toward the only sounds left and leans, and reaches, and grows, and in the morning you cut the town free of itself and burn the sweepings and strike the same seven notes again.

The chord is written on the board at the bottom of the stair every morning in chalk. It has not changed in six generations. It does not need to be posted; everyone knows it; the apprentices can sing it in their throats before they can sign their names.

It is posted anyway. Every day, in a good hand, at the foot of the stair, where something coming up the main can read it.

It is not a notice. It is a reply.
