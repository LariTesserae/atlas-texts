## The Most Weathered Things Here

There is a woman on Course Three who can end the argument in the museum by opening her mouth.

The children have been looking at the cobble. They have been told about the valley, the water, the thousand years of tumbling, and they have nodded the way children nod at arithmetic they intend to go on doubting. Then the woman leans into the case light and shows them her molars: grey-white, ovoid, the cusps ground away to soft domes, forty years of bread with mill-grit in it, forty years of chewing the product.

There are two places on the Sill where stone gets rounded. One is a bird's stomach. The other is an old woman's mouth. The children believe her, because she is not a theory. She is on a stipend of eleven grams a week and she has bad knees and she comes in on the eleven-day rain because the damp gets into her hips, and when she laughs you can see the whole geology of the city in her head.

This is the first thing about the inhabitants. They are the most eroded objects on the plateau. Nothing else here has been outdoors long enough to have an outside; the people have. Their hands are the oldest surfaces for two hundred kilometres. The shine on a ledger cover, the dish worn into a stair-tread, the brass shim hammered thin as a fingernail and then thinner, the doorframes gone to parallelograms, the nails in a boot worn to crescents, the teeth, the lungs, the deep soft groove under the collar of a woman who has carried a lamp on the same shoulder since she was nineteen — this is the weathering rind of the Sill. It is all in the bodies and the belongings. The country has no skin yet. The city is the skin.

## Faces

They do not squint. There is nothing to squint at; the sun is a coin you can stare down and the real light comes up off the Undercast into the underside of the jaw. So they open instead — pupils wide in a grey that never changes, the flat unblinking appraisal that barge crews notice first and dislike. Being looked at by a Rinder feels like being weighed, because it usually is.

Politeness is a tilt of the chin. You lift it, offering the lit underside of your face, the part your neighbours know you by: *here is my lamp side*. To lower your brow at someone is to go dark at them, and there is a word for a man who does it habitually that translates roughly as *shuttered*. Their portrait painters light from below without thinking about it. A portrait lit from above — the way the drowned world paints, the way the sun would do it if the sun were allowed — is how you paint the dead, and it is why the few photographs brought up the funicular look to Rind eyes like images of drowned relatives.

They cough. Everyone coughs, a dry two-note cough, and the young ones cough politely into a paper because the paper is worth something. Mill-lung is not a disease here so much as a savings account. At death the lungs are washed and the washings are graded and the grit is entered in the Grain House under the dead person's name: the Settling. A millwright of sixty years is a modest vault. Families keep a jar of it on the shelf, a grey handful, and the jar is not a memento, it is an inheritance, and there are lawsuits.

They smell of wet wool and warm iron, like their streets. They drink hot water with a pinch of imported leaf in it and call it tea and defend it aggressively. They walk at the pace of the herd, all of them, even the ones who have never seen the herd, because there is no advantage in hurrying that anyone has ever been able to demonstrate.

## The Book Is the Body

Nothing takes an impression, so everything is written down twice and said aloud once.

The rule taught to apprentices is *three copies, three mediums, one of them a person*. Ink, because ink is legible. Knotted string, because the moths eat ink and not string. And a human being who was there, because the birds steal string. Every household keeps a ledger and a cord and a witness. The Attesters' Guild rents out the third: professional people-who-were-there, in coats with numbered pockets, paid by the hour to stand and see. A marriage needs two. A boundary dispute needs four and a surveyor. The poorest funerals get one, and he is usually a boy of eleven, paid in grams, told to remember this and nothing else for the rest of the day.

The Route Guild walks roads that do not exist. Ninety years of boots on Course Two have worn no mark whatsoever into the stone; the road is a book, a cord, and a habit. Children go to school along it in single file because the book says single file, stepping over a boulder that the road has always gone around, and if they straggle and shortcut and improvise, the road is not damaged — the road is *gone*, and someone will have to walk it back into being over a decade. There are streets in Rind that cannot be photographed. There are streets in Rind that are only real in the soles of feet and the mouths of witnesses, and the citizens consider these the most permanent streets they have, and they are right, and the visitors find this unbearable.

And there is the Tender of Course Four, who has been walking beside the herd for two hundred and six years. He is made of eleven men and one book. Each new tender is taught the handwriting of the last, not out of vanity but because the herd's paperwork has to be continuous or the wallow protections lapse. He signs with his own name at the bottom and the book's name at the top. The hollowbacks have never met a man. They have met a lamp, and a smell of wool, and a slow shape at the edge of the wallow that has never once hurt them, and if you asked them — you cannot ask them — they would say that the lamp is one animal and has been alive a very long time.

## The Herd, and What Rides It

Four tonnes, six limbs, a spine dipped into a long open trough where the fog pools and the moss grows in the moss. Close to, a hollowback is not an animal but a district: a warm grey cliff that breathes, hung with a hanging garden, dripping, humming, smelling of hot sugar and dung and bruised moss going yellow. There are three species of midge in a good trough and one cricket and a small pale frog nobody has ever seen anywhere else. There is a bull on Course Four with a sump-willow four metres tall growing out of his shoulder ridges, rooted in nineteen years of his own compost, and he is the only forest on the Sill that moves.

The herd takes a season to cross a district, and the wood goes with it, and behind it come the Wake: families with barrows and hoppers of bought dirt, walking the dung-line, planting into the first soil that has ever existed at that address. They are legally trespassers and practically a guild. Their gardens are the shape of an animal's afternoon.

The bellow arrives at three in the morning from twelve kilometres away, enormously loud and completely hollow, a flat metallic tearing with no chest in it, and you get used to it. The lullabies imitate it. Rind children are sung to sleep with a noise that makes visitors from below sit up in bed with their hearts going, and the children are astonished by this, because to them it is the sound of a large slow thing saying *I am here and it is night and nothing is happening*, which is the entire content of their idea of safety.

Everything on the Sill wears a bell. There is no echo — the rock has not learned to answer — so sound goes out and does not come back, and a person walking in fog is a person who has vanished. Children have bells sewn into their hems. Survey parties carry a drone-pipe, a reed thing you blow continuously, tuneless, so that the others can find you. Their music has no plucked strings and no decay: sustained, enormous, hollow, all attack and no room. It is the only music that sounds correct here.

## The Birds

The gizzard-birds nest on the gantries and commute down through the Undercast to the drowned world, and they are resented the way a self-made neighbour is resented.

They come back up with rounded grit in their crops — riverbed grit, tumbled a thousand years by water in a valley, the exact thing forty age-mills and eleven thousand tonnes a day are trying to counterfeit — and the Grain House buys it at a price that makes millwrights put their heads in their hands. The most efficient weathering apparatus on the plateau is a stomach that has never paid a licence fee.

They come up with other things too. The Crop Market on Course One sells what the birds bring: fish scales, a brass button, a boot-eyelet, a child's tooth, a wedding ring, a fragment of green glass, a leaf. Nobody knows what the birds are doing down there. Nobody who has gone down to find out has come back up talking about it in a way that satisfies the Office. The barge crews go down for dirt and return with a squint and a strange way of standing in shadow, and the children do not stare at the men; they stare at the footprints the men leave in the spilled soil at the hopper mouth, which stay.

And the birds steal string. They take the knotted cords off doorposts and out of apprentices' pockets, and their nests are archives — a season of somebody's memory woven into a gantry corner forty metres up. There are nest-readers who work with a mirror and a hook and untangle a nest over eleven days and reconstruct a lost week: a birth, a debt, a boundary, four days of rain, a death. It is the most sentimental profession in the city and its practitioners are not sentimental at all.

At a funeral the dead are given a rounded stone to swallow. Bought, at ruinous cost, from a bird. It is the only way to go into the ground with something inside you that has a history.

## The Small Ones

Midges in the condensate, in columns you can hear from a hundred metres; sieved at dawn, pressed into cakes, eaten with imported salt. Hum-bread. The only food on the Sill that falls out of the sky.

Fish in the fog. Pale, palm-length, unhurried, arriving in the drip off the mesh harps and accumulating in dew ponds eleven centimetres deep, freezing at the rim each night and evaporating from the middle each day. The Fisheries Guild levies licences on ponds it has never stocked and cannot stock, and there was a scandal in the year of the third frost-loom when an officer was caught at two in the morning *stocking* a pond out of a bucket, and the city laughed for a decade, and the licences were not reduced.

Sump-crickets, whose chirping in the green seams is so loud that survey parties shout, and whose absence on the bare rock is so total that a dropped hammer sounds like an accident in a cathedral. Cricket-lines are legal boundaries. You find water by listening. You find your friends by listening. Silence on the Sill is not peace; silence is being lost.

Moths that eat unset stone. Their larvae bore into provisional ground near the Quick and their tunnels decide it — a gut is a surveyor's rod, an intestine is a lithology, and there are cores out past Course Eleven that came out of a caterpillar and are now, permanently, granite. The adults come to ink the way wasps come to sugar. Ledgers on the eastern courses live in brass cases. A signature eaten is a person unresolved, and there is a superstition, not really a superstition, about not letting a moth land on your name.

And the lichen tenders, out on the north faces with their brass atomisers, begging stone to rot, naming their patches after their grandmothers. So the cliffs are written on. The first letters anyone ever put on this country are alive, and they grow, and they blur, and in eighty years a name will be a green smear that nobody can read, and this is considered a success. Writing that erodes is writing that is working.

## The Dead Make the Gardens

The Dirt Assize allocates the imported soil: market gardens first, then the dead.

A grave is a spoonful of the drowned world. It is the most expensive thing a Rinder will ever be given and it is the only occasion on which soil is deliberately put *into* the ground rather than spread on top of it. So the cemeteries are the green seams. Moss a foot deep, cushion plants two metres across flowering all at once with that hot-sugar smell, rosettes the height of a man on a chair holding a hundred litres of fog in their hearts, and above them the willows, and in the willows the crickets and the midges and the small pale frog.

People go to the cemetery to sit in the grass. They take their lunch. Children are told not to run, and run. The Hollowback Office protects three wallows in the burial courses and the tenders walk their lamps between the stones, and the herd browses the dead and carries them off across the plateau in the moss on its back, at four hundred metres an hour, and this is not a metaphor anybody in Rind needs explained to them. It is simply where the soil went.

## One

Out past Course Eleven the ground is youngest and the animals come in twos.

The same torn ear at two ponds four hours apart. The same asymmetric moss. Both warm, both breathing, both entirely willing to be scratched under the third limb. The Office logs one animal, pays one bounty, does not discuss it. The Tender of Course Four has been walking beside the same bull for thirty years and has said goodbye to it eleven times, and each time he wrote *left the district* and each time the bull was at the wallow in the morning, and he has stopped writing *left the district*.

There is a family on Course Six that lays two plates and enters one meal.

The reason is not mysterious, only unfashionable. Nothing here has an outside yet. Nothing has a rind, an inside, a boundary worn into it by long use in one place. A thing becomes *one* thing by being abraded into a particular shape — by having been rained on ten thousand times, walked over, named, tallied, leaned against, loved in a specific and unrepeatable way. The herd closer in is singular. You can tell a Course Four bull from any other bull, and so can he. Out where the ground is still coming up warm and undecided, the animals have not been used enough to be individuals, and neither, if you go far enough east, have the people, which is why the Quick crews are signed out and signed back in and attested at the gate by someone who knew them before, and why the attestation is not a formality. It is the mechanism. A woman stands at the gate and says *this is Bel, who has a scar on her thumb from the frost-loom in the year of the third rail*, and Bel becomes one person again, gratefully, like a child given a name.

So they wear each other. That is what love is on the Sill, mechanically: the slow abrasion of a person into a shape that cannot be duplicated. The parallelogram doorframe is a family's record. The groove under the collar is a marriage. To know someone here is to weather them, and to be known is to be weathered, and there is no other process available, and everyone understands this without ever once putting it into words, and the words they do use are ordinary and mostly about lunch.

## Someone

Their word for a person is their word for a worn place.

The depression under a leaking pipe. The dish in a stair. The shine on a ledger cover. The dip in a sill where four generations have set down the same bucket. It is also — and this is the part that visitors cannot get their minds around, and that Rind children find obvious — their word for a shortcut, a desire path, a route worn in by use. One word for all of it. *A place where something has been pressed often enough to stay.* A person is a shortcut that has been walked for sixty years. A friendship is a desire path. There are none of either in the stone, and there are ten thousand of both in the city, and the city is where the country keeps its impressions until the country is ready to hold them itself.

Under a leaking pipe on Course Nine, one drop has been falling for nineteen years into a hollow in raw basalt. You could lay your thumb in it. It is the only mark made on the Sill by anything that was not alive, and the Works has a brass rail around it, and the willow-warden who is also the bounty-hunter and has never once claimed the bounty comes and looks at it when he is low.

In four hundred years there will be a river here. It will have a name, and the name will be that word — the one their grandmothers used for the hollow under the pipe, for the shine on the book, for the groove under the collar, for the road that cannot be photographed.

It will mean *someone*. And it will be accurate.
