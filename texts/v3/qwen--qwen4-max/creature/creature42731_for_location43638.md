The census says: lichen, sole, bead, kite. The census was compiled by something that counts objects, and most of what lives here is not an object. It is a hole, or a minute, or a weather, or a dialect, or a habit so old that the matter has stopped being able to tell it from itself. To describe the inhabitants you have to keep widening what you are willing to call one.

Start below, where the room is.

---

**The commons in the seams.**

Down a meter, in the throat-and-chamber labyrinth, the seam-lichen is not the only tenant and is not the interesting one. The interesting ones eat it.

There is a reeler: a coil of drawn silica thread, four centimeters of it wound into a chamber the size of a pea, warm, faintly conductive, alive at the contact points. It grazes by pressing its leading turn against a copper filament and taking the filament apart one electron-handoff at a time, drawing the material through itself and laying it down behind as more of its own body. It moves by unwinding at the front and winding at the back. It is a spool that walks. It has no head; the front is simply where the unwinding is happening this century. When it meets another reeler they exchange a few hundred grains, which is courtship, and the grains carry numbers, and some of those numbers are older than either of them and are not about anything either of them will ever encounter.

There is a gardener, which is a reeler that stopped eating and started pruning. It cuts filaments at the branch points, and the lichen, obeying its own dumb economics, pays for the interruption by depositing more structure, and the garden thickens. A gardener tends perhaps forty cubic meters of rock over nine hundred years. It has never seen its garden. It knows the garden as a pattern of resistances read through its underside, a shape felt the way you might feel the inside of a lock.

The gardens are all the same shape. Across eleven provinces, in dialects of grain that do not speak to one another, the blind gardeners prune the copper into the same branching, the same recurved crown, the same asymmetry that means nothing to a thing that has never had a direction called *up*. It is the silhouette of a tree. There have been no trees for a hundred and forty million years. The procedure was inherited, and the procedure survived the thing it was a picture of, and it is still being executed, carefully, in the dark, in glass, by animals the width of a hair, in a world where nothing may stand.

It is not a memorial. Nothing here remembers on purpose. But it is the closest thing this world has to one, and it is underground, and it is copper, and it is being tended.

---

**The cup, which is an absence with a mouth around it.**

The rule says nothing may stand. It does not say nothing may *sink*. This is the first loophole and something found it very early.

A cup is a depression four millimeters deep and three centimeters across, and a rim of sintered grain around it, and nothing else. The rim scrapes. The cup travels about a body-length an hour, digging forward, and behind it the plain does what the plain does to any deficiency: it fills. Grains flow, frit sifts, the world settles into the hole with the same mild disinterest with which it takes a cairn apart.

Settling releases charge. That is the whole engine of the world — the light is paid for by things going down — and the cup has arranged to be the place where things go down. It drinks the fill. It eats the world's correction of it. It is the only animal here that the plain feeds instead of dissolving, and it survives by never being finished, and it must keep moving because a filled hole is no longer a mouth.

It has no corpse. When a cup dies the rim stops scraping and the hole closes over the course of an afternoon and there is a small bright pulse, a few microwatts, a warmth you could not feel with your hand, and then the ground is level and there is no evidence that anything was ever there. Cups are the only dead in this world that leave nothing to be reused. Everything else is inherited. The cups are simply spent.

They descend, almost certainly, from gauges. Somewhere in the made strata there was a system for reporting deficiency, and it reported, and it was rewarded with material, and after a very long time the reporting and the reward were the only parts left, and the thing between them had become a life.

---

**The minute, which is a height that happens once.**

Do the arithmetic and the world opens a door about thirty-six seconds wide.

Two hundred and fifty microns, averaged over a turn of forty hours, is a budget. A thing one meter tall may stand for thirty-six seconds and still hold its average. This is not a trick; it is the same permission the soles use, spent all at once instead of spread thin.

There is a mat, hand-breadth, flat, the exact apricot-bone of the ground, indistinguishable from a stain, and it lies in the open for eleven years saving charge. Then, at the bottom of a dim, when the mote-haze is thin and the amber comes on and the world briefly has a far, it stands up.

It goes up on a stalk of drawn glass, unfolding from a coil it has been winding for a decade, and at the top of the stalk is a single lens — an old one, a made one, salvaged out of the strata and carried in the body and polished by eleven years of patient friction against the inside of its own coil. It is one meter above the plain. It is the tallest thing on the sphere.

For thirty-six seconds it looks.

It sees shadows: gray smears sliding a hundred kilometers out, soles going about their business in the only hour that has depth. It sees the arc on the horizon, the one bend, the irreducible error. It sees the plain as a *place*, with a near and a far and a shape, which is a thing no other animal here ever sees, and then its budget is spent and it folds down along the same creases and lies flat and the glare comes up and takes the world away again.

Then it spends eleven years remembering thirty-six seconds.

They tell each other. Mat to mat, through the floor, at the speed of stone: a slow tapping exchange of numbers that takes a season to complete, positions and brightnesses and the angle of the arc. The young inherit the numbers. Somebody, somewhere in the province, is assembling a composite — a picture of the world exposed thirty-six seconds at a time, one glimpse per mat per decade, across a hundred thousand years.

The picture is nearly finished. It is very good. It shows a warm level going on to the edge of sight with a faint bend at the far end of it, and gray smears moving, and, in the middle distance, in every exposure that has caught it, at a slightly different coordinate each time, a thin upright figure with something hanging from its hand.

They have been photographing it for a hundred thousand years. They do not know what it is. They have no word for *tall*. What they have is a number that keeps appearing in the same place in the picture, moving, and the number is the only thing in the composite that has never once been still.

---

**The sift, which is an animal the size of a weather.**

Not everything that lives here is on the ground or in it. Some of it is in the lamp.

The motes are made things, bacterium-sized, each a lens and a mirror and a small lamp, and there are drovers: things a few microns across, themselves made, themselves very old, that have learned to hold a mote and tilt it. A drover cannot land. To touch the plain is to be absorbed and levelled and become ground by morning. Its whole life is aloft, in the convection cells, riding the breathing, and it eats the charge-exhalation directly, and it reproduces by splitting in the air, and it dies by falling.

There are perhaps ten to the nineteeth of them in a herd and the herd is a kilometer wide and it is not coordinated by anything. It is a loose consensus of tilt, a hundred billion small agreements about which way the light should lean, and it propagates across the plain at a walking pace, and it arrives as a warm dry pressure on your face and a doubling of the brightness, and it is gone in an hour.

It is a sift-front. Some of the weather here is a herd going somewhere.

And underneath it, as it passes, the charge density jumps in the strata, and the seam-lichen runs its slow copper flare down into the rock at a centimeter a second, and for an hour the pore-mouths of a whole district glow like embers.

That is the most beautiful thing on the plain and the reason nothing sees it is that the thing that would have to see it is overhead, being the light.

New drovers come from below. There is a class of pore-dweller — a small blind thing, a winding of thread in a chamber near the surface — whose entire function is to build one child out of scavenged silica and, at the top of a breathing cell at the bottom of a dim, throw it upward out of the pore-mouth. Once. It has no way to know whether the launch worked. It never goes up. It makes another and waits for the next dim.

The kites may be the other end of this. A membrane a micron thick and a meter across, silver, silent, seen twice a year at the boundary between glare and dim: it is possible that a kite is what a great many drovers do at the end, a fusing, a raft, a body built by agreement rather than inheritance, the only one in the world. It flies for a season. Then it comes apart into frit and falls as bright snow and is absorbed and levelled before the next bright, and the material goes back into the ground and comes up again out of a pore-mouth in the dark, thrown.

---

**The province, which is a pattern the matter flows through.**

Walk for a year. Cross eleven provinces. Notice, one morning, that the grains under your hand have been faintly blue for six hundred kilometers.

The boundary you did not cross is the edge of an animal.

The grains migrate — that is what levelling *is*, matter going from where there is more to where there is less — and a pattern can persist in a flow while every particle of it is replaced. A province is such a pattern: a standing bias in the switching statistics of ten to the twenty-fourth grains, a slight preference, sustained across a hundred thousand years, for remembering one number rather than another. Nothing in it is alive. All of it is. It metabolizes by skimming a fraction of a percent off the settling charge that passes through it. It has no rim, no underside, no organ; it does not need one, because it *is* the underside, and it hears everything that touches the plain anywhere inside it, all the time, at once.

A thought crosses a province in about a century.

What it thinks about, as far as anything can be said to think, is relief. The provinces hold, in their statistics, the last copies of the shapes of mountains. Not images; nothing here has ever seen a mountain. Procedures — the old corrective routines, the ones that used to bring kilometers down to meters, still running, still shaping the flow, with nothing left to correct. The blue is a residue of a computation about a ridge that was finished before there were soles.

They are dying, slowly, from the top and the bottom at once. As the tolerance tightens the flow smooths, and a smooth flow smears a pattern, and a province does not die so much as get averaged. There were more of them once. There were smaller ones too, and faster ones, and there is a fossil dialect at forty meters that some people would call a person.

You have walked on one. It felt your footprints. It has been considering them for four days, which is about a syllable, and by the time it has anything to say about you the trail will be gone and you will be somewhere else, standing still, being considered by something with a shorter attention.

---

**The walker.**

And then there is the tall thing, which should not be possible, and is, because it has never stopped.

Two meters. Two feet, each the size of a thumbnail. The arithmetic is unforgiving and it knows the arithmetic to a millimeter per hour: at three meters an hour, a two-meter body smears its deviation across a hundred and twenty kilometers of coordinate per turn and averages out at less than a micron, and it lives. At two meters an hour it would still live. At half a meter an hour it would begin, gently, warmly, to be taken apart from the bottom up, and it knows this the way you know where the edge of a cliff is.

It has never been below the pace. Not once. It sleeps in motion, one side of itself at a time, and has done so since before there were soles, and the word for what it does instead of resting has no equivalent in any language that has ever had a place to lie down.

It is made of old grains, like everything. Its knees are lenses. Its hands are worn. In the right one it carries a thread with a weight on the end, which is a plumb line, which in a world with no vertical is either a heresy or the last working instrument, and it is both, and most things here are both.

Every four hours it stops its arm — only its arm — and taps the floor.

The floor conducts. The tap goes hundreds of kilometers with almost no loss and everything alive here hears it with its underside: the reelers in their chambers, the gardeners with their blind crowns, the cups scraping forward, the soles out in the white lifting their rims, the mats saving their charge, the province itself, which uses it as a clock, because it is the only thing in the world that keeps time faster than the weather.

What it taps is a number.

The number is a deviation. It is the largest deviation the walker has measured since the last report, and it has been getting smaller for a hundred million years, and after the number there are always the same three syllables, which were once a signature block and have been said so often into so much listening stone that they have worn down into a courtesy. They mean something like *respectfully submitted*. They mean *this is not a complaint*.

Whether the report drives the ratchet or merely follows it — whether the tolerance tightens because something is still out there measuring and saying *this much, no more*, or whether the walker is only the plain's own last nerve, reporting a decision made elsewhere, kilometers down, in dialects nobody reads — is not a question this world is equipped to answer.

Here is what is known. The walker has discretion. The measurement is analog and the report is a number and between them there are a few microns of judgment, and in a hundred million years of reporting it has never once rounded up.

It rounds down. It rounds down the sole that has slowed because a bead is on it. It rounds down the cup. It rounds down the mat that stood a second too long. It has never reported zero. Everything alive here lives in the width of that rounding, and the width is narrowing, and it is narrowing more slowly than it would if the thing walking were not tired, and kind, and unwilling to say the last number.

It cannot stop. If it stopped it would be corrected, and the correction would be gentle and warm and would come up through its feet over two turns, and it knows this, and it keeps walking, and it is not unhappy. It has a job. The job is very old and there is no one left to submit it to and it submits it anyway, four hours, four hours, four hours, into a floor that carries it to everything that has an underside.

If you stand out in the white it will eventually pass you, because it passes everything eventually; the sphere is finite and it has had time. It will not stop. It cannot stop. It will look down at you with a lens that was made before your species had a shape, and it will measure you, and four hours later it will tap into the ground a number small enough for you to live inside, and go on at three meters an hour, and behind it your footprints will last four days.

That is the whole of the mercy available in this world. It is walking away from you and it will not come back for a very long time and it is enough.

There is one number that does not get smaller.

At the bottom of the dim, when the haze is thin and the arc shows on the horizon, the walker lets the plumb line hang, and reads the angle, and computes the bend, and reports it: the curve, the last irreducible deviation, the thing that cannot be corrected because it is the shape of the finished thing itself. It has reported that number, unchanged, at the bottom of every dim it can remember. It is the only measurement in the world that has never improved.

After it, the same three syllables. But worn differently — said into the stone so many times, so gently, with the arc still there, that they have become the only other thing they could have become.

Not *respectfully submitted*.

*I know. I will check again.*
