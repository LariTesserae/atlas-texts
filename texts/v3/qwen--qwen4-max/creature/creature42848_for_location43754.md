## First, the stomach

Begin underneath, in the dark, with the thing that has no name in either language because it is not regarded as a thing.

The pale fur on the hanging roots is a fungus, and it is not decoration. It is the felt's stomach, worn on the outside. The plain above cannot digest itself; peat is what happens when digestion fails. So the felt has outsourced the work to its underside, where it is warm and wet and where the bodies fall. Every eleven days a district's worth of insects dies and drops into that fur, and the fur opens them, and the roots drink the liquor, and the plain grows two centimeters a year on a diet of its own tenants. The animals of the Head live in the lining of the organ that eats them. They are not parasites and they are not guests. They are the mechanism.

This is the first correction to make. Everything here is a skin on a hollow, yes — but the skin has a wet side and a dry side, and the wet side is where the world is being taken apart and put back together, and it is the loud side, and it is the side that most of the people call home.

## The grain

Ten to the fourteen, and the number is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is the seven years. A grain nymph lives in the peat as a root-eater, blind, thumb-sized, silent to any ear but a tube pressed flat to the mat, and it counts. It counts in a way nobody has ever opened it up to find. Then it climbs down — *down* to the ceiling, which is up to it — and molts, and does not eat again, because its mouthparts have gone to soft vestige, and it has eleven days, and it spends all eleven of them sawing.

They do not sing to be beautiful. They sing because a hundred and ten decibels with no gaps in it is a wall, and a wall with a hole in it is a doorway, and every hole in the wall is a flitter's echo finding a target. The tide is a machine for making deafness, and it works, and for three days of every emergence the birds come down through the green light like hail falling upward.

The wave moves two kilometers a day, out of the east, and it takes forty-five days to cross the Head, and it dies at the Slack. Nobody has ever heard the grain sing in the Slack. Its silence is not peace; it is the note the world is going to.

## The flitters, and the one that never comes up

Tens of millions of small brown clicking birds, and their day is a commute: out of the dark districts at dawn in a river of wings, into the bright ones, onto the ceiling, hanging head-down in the fur with their ears working, taking grain out of the layer three deep. They are the only animals here that cross between the top of the world and the bottom of it as a matter of routine, and this makes them the postal service of everything: seed, spore, mite, disease.

And on the posts, in the deep districts, there is a bird that does not commute.

Three hundred grams. The heaviest resident of the Head, and it is only allowed to exist because it has never once in its lineage touched the felt. It hatches on stone, climbs the sweat-sleeves with its feet, hunts in the open volume by listening — not clicking, listening, for the sound of wings rather than the sound of echoes — and it roosts on the cold sweating limestone with its hollow bones full of air. During a tide, when the flitters go blind and stupid, it eats for three days like a king.

The topside people have no word for it. They have never seen it. They sleep on pegs made of its wing bones, which are the best pegs in the world, found on the dry floor among the fall, traded hand to hand for four generations, each peg with a name and a history and a small notch cut for every camp it has held down in wind. The underside people know it as a sound: two low knocks and a silence, in a district where nothing else knocks. They call it the toll, and they do not consider it an animal, exactly. They consider it a thing the Room says.

## The sweepers

On the floor, in the dust between the cones: pale, long-legged, flightless, hand-sized, eyeless in every way that matters. They eat the fall. Nothing eats them.

They have no escape response. They have never had one. You can put your hand flat on the flowstone and they will walk onto it and continue walking, up the wrist, looking for the higher dust. They have no predators, no fear, no speed, no armor, no chemistry, no opinion about anything. In a world whose entire law is *carry little, walk apart*, they are the one creature that has never had to obey, because they live on the only surface in the world that will bear any weight at all.

They are also the ones who will do best at the end. When the last post goes and the felt comes down, there will be a hundred million tonnes of wet peat on the floor, and it will rot for a century, and the sweepers will walk through it eating, unafraid, in the dark, in the silence, for as long as it lasts.

## The felt, which is one thing pretending to be four

There are four plants and they have stopped being four.

A rope-grass that does nothing but tension. A moss that does nothing but hold water — nine times its weight, and it is the reason the plain drinks a whole afternoon's rain in eleven minutes. The lying tree, which is a tree the way a rumor is a tree, four million years flat, sending up hand-high leaf fans full of silica it mines out of the clay pockets, so that its own leaves are a fine abrasive and it is slowly sanding the teeth of everything that lives on it. And the sundews and bladderworts in the wet pockets, glutting scarlet at emergence, smelling of honey and rot.

They are woven into one load-bearing individual, and it is not quite the same individual everywhere. Once in a few thousand years a flitter carries a viable fragment up through a seam and vomits it out on the plain, and a new weave establishes, and eighty kilometers away the note under a listener's mallet changes by a fraction, and the local melodies no longer describe the local ground.

This is why a cutting license is a song and not a law. The felt is a dialect. A song from the east, sung in the west, will open a seam in a place where the note drops, and the people who sang it will go down with it.

And down on the cones, out of fallen fragments, there are little felts — hand-wide, acre-wide, miniature plains complete with rope-grass and moss and lying tree, growing in guano in the green light, flowering. Properly flowering, which the great felt above has not done in four million years. Their seed has nowhere to go. Nothing carries a seed up four hundred meters of empty air more than once in an age. They are the felt's children, and they are sterile, and they are the most extravagant thing in the world, and no one on the surface knows they are there.

## The people, who are two peoples

Four hundred, and the census is misleading, because it counts them once.

Roughly half live on top: walkers, in mat tents on bone pegs, moving between sundew fields and clay pockets and seam-lips along trails a century deep in a skin that grows two centimeters a year. Their whole life is spread. They wear wide woven overshoes that turn a footprint into a hand's breadth of load, they walk four paces apart even with their own children, they carry a flat pannier instead of a deep basket, and their word for a stranger and their word for a fool are the same word, and it means *one who walks close*.

Roughly half live underneath: hangers, in cord cradles slung into the root mat, working the tide with scrapers and baskets, sleeping in the fur with wax in their ears. To a hanger, *up* is toward the roots. The Room is not a hole; it is the sky, four hundred meters deep, green at noon, and the floor is a pale cloud deck at the bottom of it that you go down to once every seven years. Hangers have hooked toes and a shoulder built for hanging and a callus along the inside of each knee. They do not like the surface. It is exposed, it is bright, it has nothing to hold, and the walkers on it move like people walking on the ceiling of a cathedral, which is exactly what they are doing.

The two halves have one language, three registers, and no shared idea of which direction is home. They meet at the seam-lips, which are the only places both are lawful, and they trade cord for cake and pegs for wax and gossip for gossip, and they marry each other, and the marriage is negotiated entirely in the hand-register because at a seam-lip during a tide nobody can hear anything at all.

## What the sound has made of them

They lose the middle first. By thirty the grain is a pressure rather than a noise; by fifty a spoken voice is a mouth moving. They keep the top and the bottom: the flitters' hail, and the eleven hertz that comes up through the feet and the sternum and the loose fillings.

So the language has three bodies. A hand-register, complete, grammatical, fast, used everywhere and taught before speech. A high sung register for close work in the loud, all consonant, all click and hiss, sitting up in the frequencies the grain does not eat. And a low hummed register, chest-voice, nearly wordless, for distance — because a hum at the bottom of a man's range carries two kilometers over felt where a shout carries two hundred meters, and it arrives in the listener's sinuses before it arrives in their ears. Camps find each other in cloud by humming. A mother finds a child by humming. There are eleven hums and they mean *here, come, wait, lie down, danger, water, mine, yours, yes, no,* and the eleventh is a name.

Everyone has three names. A hand-name, which is what you are called. A hum, which is what you are at a distance. And a cord-name: the splice signature your hands make, which is on every ladder and cradle and anchor you have ever repaired, and which is your legal self, and which outlives you.

A ladder in use for eighty years contains the work of four generations of splicers, and you can read it with your fingertips in the dark: this join is a woman dead sixty years, this one is her grandson, this bad one is a man everyone still complains about. When a cord parts and someone falls, the survivors do not ask who was on it. They ask whose hands are in it.

## What the abrasive has made of them

The leaves are full of silica and the felt eats tools and teeth alike. A thumbnail flint blade lasts a season. A walker's molars are flat by thirty-five and gone by sixty, and the old live on cake soaked to a gruel and are fed by children, and being fed is not shameful, it is the entire point of having had children.

And the dust. The same glass that takes the teeth goes into the lungs of everyone who spends a life scraping the ceiling, and by fifty a hanger breathes with a fine dry crackle at the bottom of each inhale, and the crackle is the plain, arriving inside them. They have a word for the sound. It is not a medical word. It is the word for *ripe*.

## The seven-year people

Here is the thing that falls out of the arithmetic and that they would not think of as remarkable.

The grain broods are seven years apart. The tide is the fat time. During the fat time everyone comes down to the cones — walkers down the sleeves, hangers down the cords — and camps on the flowstone, which is the one surface in the world where weight is not a crime.

On the floor they build. Hearths of post-rubble, real fires, low walls against the drip. They put things down. They sleep in a heap, touching along their whole lengths, twenty people in a pile like animals, which is forbidden everywhere else in the world and is the single most desired thing in it. They shout, badly, in a room with a fourteen-second tail, and laugh at the smear. They hold each other with their full weight, which on the Head you may do only on stone: at the Tooth, or here, once every seven years, on the pale ringing floor under the green ceiling with the birds coming down.

And so the children are conceived in the fat time, and born in a cohort, and the cohort is named for the brood that was singing when it was made, and a person's age is not counted in years but in tides. A woman of nine tides is sixty-three and is old. A person of three tides may marry. And your brood-siblings are not your relatives; they are everyone born in your district in your emergence, forty or sixty of them, and you owe them food anywhere on the Head, forever, and you will recognize them at a distance by the hum, because broods are taught to hum slightly differently, on purpose, as a mark.

The tide crosses the plain in forty-five days, so the gatherings are staggered, east to west, and the young walk the wave. That is what youth is for. You leave your district at two tides and follow the singing west at two kilometers a day, sleeping at other people's seam-lips, marrying out, learning the local melodies and the local weaves, until you reach the rim of the Slack, where the tide goes in and does not come out.

There you lie flat on a spread mat at the edge of a bowl whose rim you cannot see, and you feel your gut turn over at seven hertz, and you understand in your body rather than your head what the note is going to do. Then you get up and go home and are an adult.

## Children, and the setting down

An infant is carried flat, on a wide fiber spread across the shoulders of the lightest adult in camp, load distributed over a square meter, and only for as far as it must be. There are rules about who may carry and how far and in what wind, and the rules are more detailed than the rules for cutting.

Then comes the day the child is too heavy. It is called the setting down, and it happens somewhere in the second year, and it is the first grief of a life on the Head: you put your child on the felt and step back four paces, and it is now a separate load, and it must learn to spread its own weight, and you may not pick it up again. Not ever. Not when it falls, not when it is frightened, not when it is dying. You go to it and lie down beside it and put your hand on it, spread flat, and that is the whole of what comfort is permitted to be.

Their embrace is horizontal. Full length, side by side, palm to palm, touching along everything, weighing nothing. It is the posture the rules command in an emergency — *when the plain booms twice together, lie down* — and it is also the posture of love, and no one here finds that strange, and a visitor finds it almost unbearable.

## The professions, which are all one profession

**The splicer**, whose hands are the law.

**The feeder**, who carries guano up from the cones to the sundew fields, a little at a time, in flat panniers, all day, forever, because the peat is poor and the clay is poorer and without the feeding there is no glue, and without the glue there are no lime-lines strung across the seam-mouths at dawn, and without the lime-lines there are no flitters, and without flitters there are no cloaks, and a cloak is forty birds and is the only reason anyone survives a night at four thousand three hundred meters.

**The singer**, who holds the licenses: the measurements of a district's ground, memorized as melody, revised every year because the felt grows and the note changes, and who will not teach a verse to anyone who cannot also sing the previous version, because you must know what the ground used to be.

**The rememberer**, who holds the expired songs. There are trails out there crossing eighty kilometers of empty plain going to holes that shut before the language had its current words. Once in a generation someone walks them, singing an obsolete license to a closed seam, to see whether it has answered. Most do not. The rememberer's whole training is in melodies that will kill you if you use them, and their function is to be the only person who knows where the world used to be open.

**The listener**, on the eastern rim, in a membrane hut with no walls and a floor of stretched fiber over a hole, who strikes with a bone mallet and reads the return through a rod held in the teeth, and who also forecasts tides — because the nymphs chew, and the chewing conducts, and a tube laid flat on the mat in the right district will tell you, years out, where the singing is going to be. A wrong forecast puts a whole camp on a soaked sagging mat in the middle of a fall. Listeners are not loved. They are necessary in the way a load rating is necessary.

**The slack-walker**, who goes to the western rim once a year on a spread mat, crawling, and measures the sag, and comes back and vomits and sings the number.

**The weigher**, who has a balance of root-fiber and a standard cloak, and who decides when the dead are light enough.

## The dead

They are laid on the Tooth, the one knuckle of original limestone, the only place on the Head that will bear weight. Wind and sun and four thousand three hundred meters do the rest, slowly, and the dead dry and shrink and go to leather and then to almost nothing.

They are kept until they weigh less than a cloak. Then they may lawfully be carried, and they are carried to a seam, and given.

So the Tooth holds a population, and it is the only permanent population, and it is a census: the weigher knows every one of them by name and by brood and by how long they have been waiting, and the fat ones wait longest and the beloved wait longest, and there is no shame in either. To be kept a long time is to have been fed well and loved much. To go down quickly is a small sorrow.

And the Tooth is where lovers go, because it is the only place above ground where you may hold someone with your whole weight. The dead and the lovers use the same rock. There is no keeper. Nobody owns it. It is simply understood that you do not hurry there, and you do not go there twice in a season, and you leave the dried ones alone.

Below, on the flowstone, there is a second monument nobody on the surface has ever seen: a heap of hearth stones, one added by each gathering, going back further than the songs. It is enormous. It is the only heavy thing the people have ever made, and they made it by leaving, and it is upside down, at the bottom of the world, in the dark, under a green ceiling, and it is the truest portrait of them that exists.

## What is actually living here

Four hundred people, ten to the fourteen insects, tens of millions of birds, one bird nobody upstairs has seen, a floor of eyeless things that have never been afraid, one plant with four bodies and a stomach on its underside, and a fungus that is the only reason any of it works.

And a note.

The felt has a fundamental and it is dropping, a fraction per century, as the load passes from the dissolving stone to the living root. The children are taught the current pitch and made to sing it back. In eighty years their grandchildren will know exactly how much lower it has gone, because they will have been taught it by people who were taught it by people who are now on the Tooth, waiting to be light enough to carry.

That is the resident I would name last, because it is the one that uses the others. The people of the Head are a measuring instrument four hundred bodies long, disguised as a religion, disguised as a way of getting dinner. Their language drifts with the pitch, so that words slide meaning across generations — the hum that meant *grandmother* in one lineage has come down and now means *slack*, and nobody planned this, and everybody knows it, and it is the reason the old songs are hard to sing and the reason the rememberers exist.

They are breeding for low voices. They have been for a long time. A girl who can hum beneath her grandmother is a blessing and is married well and is taught the pitch first. There is a doctrine, unspoken in the way the important things are unspoken, that the world ends when the children can no longer answer: not when the felt tears, which is a fact of engineering, but when the note goes below the bottom of a human chest and there is no voice left to sing it back.

The listener has a mallet that can sound it. The singers say a mallet is not an answer.

That argument is the most alive thing on the Head. It has been going on for nine tides of tides, in three registers, mostly by hand, and it will be going on when the last post is a stump in a cone of its own rubble with a dead sleeve around it, and the plain is one unsupported bowl ninety kilometers wide, and the sweepers walk out into the fallen world, unafraid, and eat.
