## The greys

They cannot land. That is the first thing, and it explains the rest. A grey's feet are four small hooks on a pad of skin, made for clinging to a vertical surface and for nothing else — not for a branch, not for a wire, not for the grass. Out in the ordinary world it can only fly and feed and fly. It cannot set down anywhere. There is no branch it could rest on, no ledge it would recognise. The whole surface of the earth is, to a grey, a place it passes through.

So the pot is not property. The pot is the only condition under which a grey can stop moving. Twenty thousand of them, each the weight of a wet leaf, each with a heart going like a moth in a lamp, and every one of them owned, in the only sense the word has down here, by a clay throat the length of a forearm with a lip worn round.

They find it by sound. The click a grey throws is not one click but a small dry chord, and the pot answers it — the gullet has a note, the way a bottle has a note, and the bird knows its own hole by the shape of what comes back. This is why the keepers never scour a pot. Not out of tenderness. A scoured pot rings a half-tone bright and its owner will hang outside it for a day and a night with its wings going and will not go in, and then it will be unhoused, and unhoused greys die on the floor where the cats find them, and the ledger has a column for that and the column is read aloud at the turn of the year.

When a pot cracks — and pots crack, four or five a season — the potters up top take a wax squeeze of the old interior before it is lifted out, and throw the new one to the squeeze: to the worn shape, to the exact sanded throat that four centuries of turning around has made. They are the only potters in the world who throw to a gauge of damage. A new pot is a forgery of an old one. It has to pass. Usually it does.

The pots hold two birds each, mostly, which is how eleven thousand holes make twenty thousand tenants, and the pairing is not romantic; a pot is inherited by whichever daughter stays, and the sons go on the vacancy list, and the vacancy list is what the ledger actually is. Not a census of birds. A register of holes and who is owed them.

Where the greys go all day, nobody down here knows. Eleven minutes of rope going out, eleven coming back, and between them a hole in the family's knowledge the size of the sky. They have sent people up to follow. Those people watched birds go over a hill and came back and said *over the hill*, and were written down as having said it. The surface people call them cote-birds and assume they sleep in the air. Two populations, four hundred years apart in the same ground, each certain the other lives in the weather.

## The leathers

The bats take the crown courses, where the dome curves in and the pots are short — a hand's depth, no more, and tilted least. They go in tail-first and hang head-down inside the sleeve with their faces in the dark of it, folded like letters put back in envelopes. They smell of wet dog and old fat. Their droppings are dry and go into a separate line of the accounts and are sold to a different buyer.

They are also the barometer. A leather knows a pressure change an hour before the shutter-woman's knees do, and when the crown courses start shifting and rustling and dropping one at a time to circle low, the shutter comes down early and the lamps are lit and nobody argues about it. The family has a verb that means *to check the bats before believing the sky*.

At night the rush-lamps burn in the galleries — a hundred flames in fat, never a hundred and ten. The light draws moths down the throat. The bats eat the moths. Too many lamps and the moths outrun the bats and lay in the chaff, and a moth year is a thing the keepers speak of the way surface people speak of a war. So the light is not decoration and not comfort. It is a fishing lure with a legal limit, and the limit is enforced by a woman who counts wicks.

The changeover at dusk and dawn — bats out, greys in, the knot in the throat — is not silence. Everyone assumes it must be, and it is the opposite: for about four seconds the throat is the loudest volume of air in the world, both species interrogating at once, a solid brick of ticking, a shout of mutual attention so dense it has texture. Then it unravels and the room is merely deafening again. Nobody has ever found a body on the floor after it. Nobody expects to.

## The keepers

Fourteen this year. Nine sleeping-throats in the second gallery, thrown by the same potters, mouth-outward like all the rest, big enough to take a person feet-first. The lips are worn. Not by tongues. By forearms, four hundred years of them, hauling in.

They lie in the wall with their heads out and their eyes on the room, and they wake at the boom, and they cannot sleep anywhere else. A room with corners is, to a keeper, a room with something wrong in it. The word they have for the ordinary eight-hour unconsciousness of surface people translates roughly as *the long drowning*, and it is not contemptuous. It is the way you would speak of a relative who sleeps through fire alarms.

Their hearing has gone at the top. Not deafness — a shaping. They live in the low band, the drone band, the counting-chant, the drip-scale, the windlass, the gecko, the belly-rasp; above that register the world has been sanded off them, and a lark is nothing, a whistle is nothing, and the surface at noon strikes them as an unnerving hush. They think it is quiet up there. The surface thinks the Cote is a riot. Both are simply hearing their own animal.

Their skin is the colour of the sump fish, and for the same reason, and they know it, and find the joke acceptable.

They speak into an ear or they use their hands: sixty-odd signs, most of them concerning water, four concerning brick, one that means *don't* and is made by simply lowering the whole body two inches. Children learn the numbers before they learn words, because the numbers are chanted all day in a band the birds don't use, and a child on a hip is inside that chant before it is inside language. Some of them count in their sleep. Their parents listen to it and are reassured, the way you'd listen to a heart.

Nobody is called by their name. They are called Shutter, Ladle, Spatula, Windlass, Rake, Trim, and the office passes on when the hands do. A given name is said out loud once, when it is given, and then once a year, and the second time is in the grief minute, and it does not matter whether you are alive.

## The offices

Two rat-snakes work the galleries and are not killed. One is Aunt. The other is Sister. They have never been seen in the same gallery, not once, not by anyone, and the keepers have a rule about that: if the two are ever seen together, something has moved in the fill and the harvest stops. They are weighed twice a year, gently, in a sack on the windlass scale, and the weights go in the book, and the line of weights is four hundred years long and does not vary much, which is the sort of thing that makes a family religious without their ever noticing they have become so.

Aunt has a pot. It was thrown for her, oversize, at the landing, and its lip is worn to a shine by bellies. She is entered as a tenant. She is also entered as staff, in the column that means *works here*, and the double entry is not a joke; it is the same double entry the family uses for itself.

Five cats, dust-coloured, half deaf, hunting by whisker and by the tremor of a ladder stile. They are named for the drip-note that was sounding when they were born — five notes, five names, five cats, and the names come round again. If a sixth is born under an occupied note it goes up. That is the punishment, and it is the only one: to be sent to the surface, into the weather, into the wide. It is used on cats and it has been used three times on people. One of them comes back at midday once a year and stands at the head of the throat during the minute with the shutter closed below and no way of hearing it, and then goes away again.

The fish in the sump are the colour of skimmed milk and have never been touched by a human hand. They are counted only when the twice-yearly rain brings them up, and the count is a check on the water, not on the fish. The worms came down in a basket before the current language existed and are now pale, slow, and unable to eat anything except this chaff; offer them a leaf off the grass and they will starve on it. They are called the stock. They are moved by the double-handful every ninth day. They are, as far as anyone can tell, found nowhere else in the world, and neither, in any useful sense, are the cats, or the crickets, or the family.

## The small

The crickets have moved their song. Four hundred years ago they sang where crickets sing; now they sing in a narrow gap between the greys' clicking and the bats' listening, because the ones that sang elsewhere were eaten, and the floor fires away all night in a frequency that exists only in this room. It is not a species. It is a dialect that has become an organ.

The orb-weavers string the niche-mouths in a lattice you must sweep with a reed at face height, along the walking line only. The rest is left, because the webs are the survey. A web hangs at an angle set by a draft, and a draft is a joint, and a joint that has opened is a course that will need lifting. There is a child whose whole work is to walk the far galleries and look at spiders and say nothing unless something has changed. She is nine. She is better at it than her uncle.

The moss necklace is a clock: grey to a shocking wet green in the time it takes to eat a meal, and the family's word for morning is that colour-word. Four maidenhair ferns in the lip of the throat, one of them older than the records. Algae staining the whitewash where the noon falls, scraped and re-limed on the rotation.

And the vine, which came down uninvited eighty years ago and is cut back every spring to exactly two arms. Its roots are up in the grass. It is the only tenant with a foot in both worlds, claimed by the surface people as a weed and by the Cote as a fixture, and it is in the ledger under a heading that translates, imperfectly but not wrongly, as *liked*. Everything else in the book is under a heading that means *used*. The whole surplus of the place, all its unnecessary affection, is entered in one column and measured in arms, and there are two, and there will not be three.

## The roll

Once a year at midday the shutter comes down and the room goes quiet, and the birds do not panic, because the drone goes on. It has to; the drone is what tells eleven thousand heads that this is not an emergency. So the keepers stand in the dark they have made and mutter the low chant, and for one minute the chant is not numbers.

It is the list. Everyone who has ever been of the Cote, dead and living, in order, in the counting band, indistinguishable to any visitor from the sound of arithmetic. It begins with the seventh cote and the people it came down on, and it has never been closed, and it now takes rather more than a minute, and the minute has quietly become four minutes over four hundred years, and no one has voted on this.

In it you can hear the dome settling one grain at a time. You can hear the drip-pots finding their notes. You can hear your own blood going round, which is the same sound the fish make in the sump when they turn.

Then the rope, the false dawn, the cold falling, the sweat course beading in a hundred heartbeats, the moss going green, twenty thousand tongues out sideways on the brick, and the hiss coming up like a fire catching.

## What is actually living here

It is tempting to say the birds are the tenants and the family the landlords, and the ledger does not think so. The ledger has no column for ownership. It has *occupied*, *vacant*, *works here*, *owed*, *liked*. Every brick has been replaced twice and the hollow in the threshold is four fingers deep and older than all of them, because each new brick is laid to the worn line. The same is true of the pots, and the snakes, and the cats, and the offices, and the names, and the hands.

Nothing in the Low Cote is the right size. Everything in it has been *made* the right size, by being used, by being sanded — tongues on clay, forearms on lips, bellies on brick, four centuries of bats selecting the pitch of a cricket, four centuries of a room selecting the hearing of a family until the larks fell out of it. Nothing here is native. The greys came from somewhere, the worms came in a basket, the vine came down, the people came down. Everything here is now endemic. That is what the place does. It does not house its tenants. It manufactures them, slowly, out of whatever arrives, and it has been at it long enough that the products no longer fit anywhere else.

At the landing, beside the windlass, at the height of a passing hand, there is one pot that no bird has ever nested in. It is polished inside to a glaze no tongue could make. Everyone touches it going by — not for luck, they'd say, just because it's there and your hand is at that height. It is entered in the book as occupied. The occupant is given as *whoever is up*: the trim boy on the catwalk with his eyes shut against the glare, the daughter sent to market, the sixth cat, the one who comes back once a year and stands in the weather with his ear to a hole.

A pot kept for the one who is not in it. When they come down, they put their hand in it, and the wear gets a little deeper, and the ledger balances, and the made world under the grass goes on being a living thing, which it was never designed to be.
