Begin below the floor, with the ones who have never had a sky.

Two kilometres down, in the wet fracture walls where the stone is still giving up its heat, there are cells living on the difference between what the rock offers and what the water takes away. Hydrogen, sulphide, carbon dioxide, a few electrons' worth of margin. They do not divide often. Some of the lineages down there are thought to turn over on a scale of centuries, which is not a lifespan so much as a rate of maintenance. They are not rare; they are slow. Their total mass, spread through that volume, would surprise you.

The important consequence is this: the water that arrives at the floor arrives already inhabited. Every one of those tens of millions of pores is inoculating the surface, continuously, from a place with no weather. The orange mats in the outflow channels are not the beginning of anything. They are the visible scum on a biosphere whose bulk is underground and whose surface expression is a stain a few millimetres thick. When you look at the pans you are looking at the exhaust of something that has been eating the mountain since before there was a graben to eat it into.

At the other end of the pipe, the sky is doing its own chemistry, and the two economies meet in the peat.

The wires live at that meeting. Sulphide from below, oxygen from above, and between them a centimetre or four of sediment that ought to be a dead zone where nothing can happen until diffusion gets around to it. The filaments abolish the wait. They stand upright in the mud, thousands per square centimetre, each one a living cable holding a reducing bottom against an oxidising top, and they run the reaction across a distance that chemistry alone could not bridge in a season.

This is the border of the country, and it is a customs post, and the currency is electrons.

Everything else at the interface is brokering. There are mats that have become obligate partners with the wires — the mat organisms sit at the top and take the oxygen side of the deal, the wires bring up the reducing side, and neither can be cultured alone. There are grazers that crop these mats preferentially, and the reason is argued about: the mats may be better food where the current runs, or merely less poisoned, sulphide stripped out of their surface layer by the traffic. The snails that do this cropping have a shell you would call ordinary until you notice that they are always, everywhere on the flat, arranged in lines. Not random. Lines, running the same direction across a hundred metres of mud, shifting a little each season with the redox front. They are grazing the field.

Now the animals that live in the skin, and the fact that thirty centimetres is a mountain range.

A black slug, thumb-long, spends the day at eight centimetres down where it is thirty-one and wet, and comes up at dusk to four centimetres where it is twenty-six and there is fungal film on the moss tips. At two in the morning the cold pours off the rim and the top of the skin goes to frost, and the slug is already at fifteen. It has never travelled more than half a metre horizontally in its life. Vertically it has migrated the equivalent of the tropics to the arctic, twice a day, for eleven weeks.

There is a wingless cricket that does the same thing on a tighter schedule, and a fly larva that does it in reverse, descending at dawn to avoid the day's peak and rising at night to pupate in the cool. Everything small here is an altitudinal migrant. The whole fauna has a tide, and the tide is drawn by cold: at dusk the country's animals sink into the floor, and after sunrise they come back up, and for four silver hours the surface belongs to nothing that cannot afford to be there.

Frost is the predator. It takes the ones that mistime. On a bad night in a dry year the top of the skin freezes down to eleven centimetres and there is a quiet massacre of things that were three centimetres too shallow, and in the morning the rails walk the pans and eat the casualties, and the population that comes back up the next evening is the population that reads the gradient correctly.

The exceptions are instructive. The kiln bird stands out in the silver hours with frost on her back, because she is carrying her own furnace and paying for it, and she can. The beetles are inside their rented rooms at twenty-two degrees with the door shut. Everything else is underground.

---

Sound does not carry in the air here. It carries beautifully in the stone.

Sinter is a dense, well-cemented, continuous solid, and it runs unbroken under the whole basin, and there is a cricket that has stopped bothering with the air. The male puts his abdomen against a sinter boss and drums, and the drum goes out through the rock at three kilometres a second and comes up through the feet of every female on the terrace. In the air, at two metres, you hear a faint dry clicking like a fingernail on a cup. Through a boot sole it is a knock at the door.

So the country is not silent. It is silent in one medium and loud in another, and the inhabitants have simply moved their conversation into the medium that works. There is a rail that stamps a warning into the sinter and gets an answer from birds it cannot see. The frogs in the cold-mouth pools call with their throats pressed against the bottom, and the call arrives in the next pool through the rock before it arrives through the water, and the females read the difference and know how far away he is.
