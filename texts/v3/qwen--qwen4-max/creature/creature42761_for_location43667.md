# Residents

Nobody lives at the Sill. There is a form.

SCHEDULE OF OCCUPANTS, filed quarterly with the coastal authority. The first box is PERSONNEL (TRANSIENT): eleven. The second is PLANT (INSTALLED), and the combs go in that one, because *plant* is what you call a thing that stands in the ground and does work, and the form was drafted by someone who has never scraped twenty centimeters of wet moss off an emitter face at three in the morning with a hook. Then FLORA, six strata, enumerated. AVIFAUNA, with an appendix on the window, audited annually. INVERTEBRATES (PARTIAL). FUNGI. BACTERIA (NOT ENUMERATED). SUBSTRATE.

At the bottom, OTHER: four centimeters of ruled space, which the gauge-keeper fills in pencil.

Her name is on the form as well — M. Vold, gauge, fourteen-day rotation. She has been transient for nine years. Nobody has called her Marit in most of them. Out on the flats she is the Gauge, and the sign for her is a small circle drawn in the air at the height of a hut window.

---

Start with the bodies, since they are the ones that fill in forms.

The crews' teeth are wrong. Amalgam buzzes in the low band, so the dentist in the town over the moraine keeps a stock of composite and calls the job *Sill work*, and you can tell a new arrival on the road by the fact that he still has metal in his head and keeps touching his jaw. Everyone's sinuses are chronic. Everyone's bowels run on a schedule the infrasound sets, and there is a diet for it, unfunny and universally followed: no beans on the flats, eat before the ebb. The men who last are the ones who stopped fighting the gut thing in their first month.

They sleep in noise. This is the part the mainland cannot get hold of. Fourteen days inside a roar that comes up through the cot frame and the pillow and the bones behind the ear, and then a bus, and then a house in a town where a refrigerator is the loudest object, and they lie awake in it with their hearts going like something trapped. There is an app. It is a recording of the hold tone, forty minutes of it, made by a survey tech with a good microphone and no permission, and it is passed around by phone like a dirty joke and every single one of them has it. Two retirees moved back. They live in the town, they are on no roster, and they walk out past the moraine most evenings to stand where they can feel it in their knees, and then they go home and sleep.

They take their defenders off twice a day.

The tide turns four times, roughly, and the retune comes with it, and the times drift about fifty minutes later each day, so the two you plan to be outside for walk steadily through the clock — lunch, then mid-afternoon, then dusk, then the small hours, then breakfast again over a fortnight. There is a tide table pinned above the stove in the gauge hut with two rows circled in grease pencil, redrawn every Sunday. It is not a prayer. It is in the tide tables; anyone can check. But nobody has ever missed it, not once, not the safety auditor, not the visiting hydrologist who was told about it in the induction and thought it sounded nice. Eleven people stand out in the sleet at whatever minute the sea has appointed, in six strata of dripping green, with their ears uncovered, and listen to a country drain.

Then they put the defenders back on and go and hook sedge out of a comb throat.

---

Their hands are the other language.

The local sign is fast, low, made below the chest so it can be read through rain and against a red lamp, and most of its vocabulary is mud states. Flat palm, tilted slightly, trembling: *custard*, sound-bound, walk it. Fingers spread and sinking: *soup*. A fist that opens slowly: *floc*, the moment of gathering, the good moment, the one the whole installation exists to produce. Two fingers walking over a flat palm: the cricket laying its meter of ground. A hand laid flat and pressed down: the hold tone, the lift-descending pressure, *the sea has stopped talking*.

There is one sign for quiet. It is also the sign for drowning. It is made at the throat and it moves outward and down, and the difference between *hush node* and *he went in* is entirely in the face, and the children get this wrong on purpose to make the adults flinch.

The children live over the moraine, eleven kilometers back, in the town, and half of any classroom can hear a bank's bearing capacity the way other children hear a car coming. They are not allowed on the new ground until they are twelve and have passed a walking test. They play a game at the edge of the old ground called Hush, which consists of standing with one foot in a place where the sound cancels for as long as you dare, which is a game about a fraction of a second, and which every adult on this coast has forbidden in a voice they do not use for anything else.

One of them, Siv, who is eleven, is deaf, and is the best mud-reader on the delta, and this is not a miracle; it is arithmetic. She has never trusted her ears, so she reads the stick and the soles of her feet and the collar on the cricket that turns stiffening into a knock against her wrist. And she is the only person here who speaks the language properly. The hearing adults sign a pidgin, a shouted thing, all nouns and no grammar. She corrects her father's mud-words in front of his crew and he takes it, because she is right, and because on this coast everybody already lives in her country and always did.

---

The birds moved into the window and then they moved into everything else.

Two to four kilohertz, filed and audited, a legal gap in a wall of sound, and every call on forty kilometers of coast has climbed or dropped into it inside four years. The window is a room. It was built as a corridor and it is now a tenement: overcrowded, partitioned, subdivided, with neighbors shouting across each other and dialects forming street by street. Young birds learn the shifted song and never know there was another one. There is a hard drive in the hut with the old coast on it, recorded by a woman who was studying something else entirely in the year before the sea came in, and the crews have listened to it exactly once each and most of them said it sounded thin. Wrong. Like a foreign bird. The two who grew up here said it was beautiful, and they meant it, and what they meant was that they could not understand a word.

The snipe drum in the hush nodes, and the nodes are the reason there are so many snipe.

A node is a column of cancellation, a room's worth of silence standing in the roar, and a drumming snipe in one of them can be heard by a female four hundred meters away in a band nothing else can use. So the males hold nodes. They display in them, they fight at the edges of them, and the whole breeding geography of the species here is a function of the interference pattern, which changes when a comb is added, or retuned, or fouled. A scraper crew cleared moss off a strut array last spring — routine, forty minutes, on the schedule — and the node moved nine meters, and a male who had held it for two seasons lost his territory to a neighbor before lunch. Nobody logged it. It is in the survey data as a change in surface bearing capacity.

The short-eared owls came for the second vole crash and stayed. They quarter the nodes. Of course they do: an owl hunts with its ears, and a node is a listening post, a hole in the noise where a vole can be heard under twenty centimeters of sedge. The owls know the node map better than the survey does, and they are not at risk in it, because they arrive at it from above.

---

Some of the residents came up the pipe.

The borrow grounds are sea floor, and the sea floor is full of amphipods and polychaetes and eggs and cysts and things with no names yet, and the pumps do not sort them. Half the invertebrate fauna of the Sill was delivered as slurry. There is a worm here that was rare on this coast for as long as anyone kept records and is now the dominant animal in nine hundred hectares of new bar, and the dunlin are here in tens of thousands because of it, and the register says INVERTEBRATES (PARTIAL), which is the most honest line on the form.

And there is a midge.

Its wingbeat sits in the forbidden band — down where the shriek's lower harmonics live, below the window, in the wall — so it cannot hear a female over the installation, and it does not try. It swarms in the retune. Nine seconds, four times a day, thirty-six seconds of courtship in twenty-four hours, over the warm cable routes where the columns rise and hang in the red lamplight like smoke that isn't smoke. Everything it does with its adult life it does in the gap: emerge, assemble, pair, drop. An entomologist came out from the mainland for it in the summer, was briefed, was late by four minutes, and had to wait six hours and eleven minutes for the next one, and stood in the sleet with his hands over his ears and then took them off and watched a species get married.

---

The Bailiff is a common seal, grey, notched along the left flank, and she has been laid into the country twice.

She hauls out on fresh mud because it is warm-ish and empty and full of fish that are as confused as everything else, and unspoken mud does not care what lies on it, and spoken mud sets. The first time it took a crew eleven minutes with two crickets to sing a corridor open around her while holding its walls, and she lay in it and looked at them with her whole face. The second time she waited. She had learned the hold tone. When the arrays drop and the pressure comes into your knees she now moves, unhurried, off whatever she is lying on, into water, and stays there for the nine seconds with just her head out, and then comes back.

She is not on the form. There is a photograph of her in the hut, pinned beside the tide table, going grey.

---

The combs are listed as plant, and they behave enough like it that nobody argues.

They eat current. They grow from dust in sixty hours. They foul, they get scraped, they die when a ring main is cut, and when they die they stand there — black harp skeletons in the alder at the head of the delta, eleven years dead, furred to the throat with moss, wrens nesting in the emitter gaps. In a gale the dead ones moan. The wind gets into the frames and they sound, and what they sound is around three kilohertz, in the window, and the young wrens growing up inside them learn that noise as part of the local song and pass it on. There are birds at the head of this delta now singing a phrase that came out of a decommissioned machine, and it will still be in their mouths when the frames have rusted down to a stain in the root mat.

One comb keeps coming back on the church tower. It is cut off every spring, cleanly, at the base, and by August there is a hand's width of new black growth up the granite, following the courses. The survey crew's mast is being slowly eaten and the survey crew finds this funny. The stone is dry-laid and it rings; there is a theory about the resonance, and there is a second theory that the ring main's warm corridor runs under the old churchyard, and the two theories are argued at the stove with the door open.

The bell is a problem. Bronze sits in a band nothing else occupies, which is why it hangs in the gauge hut and not in a museum, and when it rings the arrays lose phase coherence out to about nine hundred meters — measurable, logged, an entry in the audit with a number beside it. There has been a memo. There has been a second memo. The bell has been rung nine times this month and the safety officer has counted every one, and the crews, who will tell you at length that there is nothing mystical about the Sill, have not moved the bell.

---

Here is what actually lives there, under SUBSTRATE, unenumerated.

The address wears off. A few molecules of instruction on a grain surface, telling it what size of flock to gather into and at what frequency to let go, and the sea is patient and the sea is a solvent, and over about two years the speaking gets thin. Something has to take the job. Something does. Roots, first — the sedge mat, the willow, then alder — and under the roots, quietly, the diatoms, which have been doing this for four billion years, laying down sugars that stick grains to grains, saying the same thing in an older language.

The handover is a number. A vane shear test, a threshold, a box ticked: this hectare no longer requires acoustic support. Then the combs are cut down and the dust is pumped seaward and grown again out on the bars, and the front walks — eighty meters a night off the eastern bight — and behind it the ground goes from being held by sound to being held by slime and root, and the difference is that you can stand in the old ground in silence and not go in.

The iron bacteria keep the calendar. Grey-green coming up the pipe, rust-orange over three weeks, the bruise-clock you read a bank's age from: that is metabolism, that is something eating the iron in the sediment and breathing out the color of the country. And in the eleven-year alder, in the dead wood, foxfire — a real and ordinary fungus, the only light on this coast that isn't electric, green enough to read a watch by if you hold the watch against the log.

So the resident is not the mud and not the machines. It is the holding. Two languages saying *stay* to the same grains, one of them being phased out, one of them being phased in, and the whole four hundred square kilometers is a sentence that has to keep being said or it stops being a place.

---

There are people in it.

The old village is nine meters down and the Laying went over it, and the laminae are a ledger, one pair per tide, thousands, and somewhere in the rack of plastic core tubes in the gauge hut there is a date you can find with a spade: a chaotic bed, rip-up clasts, roof slate, a thread of red cotton. Marit keeps the tubes because paper does not need power and neither does a tube of mud.

The graves are coordinates. That is all they can be. A man walks out from the town three or four times a year with a cricket and a handheld and stands on a haul road at a position, on ground that came into existence in the spring and carries forty tonnes an hour, and he is standing on his mother's kitchen at a height of nine meters, and he stays about ten minutes and comes back. People leave things on the fresh ground. Watches, mostly, and once a wedding ring, and a bottle of something poured out. The delta takes them at eleven hectares an hour and files them in millimeter pairs. In two hundred years somebody will core this and find a wristwatch in a lamina and write a paper about ritual deposition, and they will be right, and the crews would laugh at them.

Onne was twenty-three, survey, and he followed a goose into a node because the goose was standing in it with its bill tucked, resting its ears. Hip in one second, chest in four. To get a man out of thixotropic mud you must un-speak a corridor while holding its walls, and the crickets are built to do the opposite, and nobody had ever practiced it. Eleven minutes. It was four minutes too long, and the arrays retuned twice while they worked, and the second time the ground set around him, and after that it was not a rescue.

The bell was rung for that. It is one of the nine.

They practice it now. Quarterly, with a dummy, on a fresh bar, timed, with two crickets and a modified emitter and a written procedure that has been revised three times. It is on the schedule. It is right under the moss-scraping.

---

There are no frogs yet.

The crews say the third summer, and they say it as though they have been promised, and what they are actually describing is a shear strength. Frogs need ground that does not liquefy under a calling male and water that is not being screamed at, and the window is two to four kilohertz and a frog is two to four kilohertz, and the old ground at the head of the delta is quiet and rooted and eleven years old and there is still no frog in it, and nobody knows why, and everybody has a theory, and the theories are argued at the stove with the door open.

In the paper log — tide, stage, channel, bell, weather, all of it in pencil, because paper does not need power — there is a last column with no heading. She writes what she heard in the nine seconds. *Swans. Snipe at 14. Bank weeping west face. Sleet on the ponds. Midges, late.* Four entries a day, drifting fifty minutes later each day, walking through the clock.

And at the end of every one of them, for four years: *no frogs.*

She writes it small. She has been leaving room.
