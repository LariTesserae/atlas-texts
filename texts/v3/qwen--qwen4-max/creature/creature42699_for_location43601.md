## The mothers

Begin with the ones that cannot be counted, because they are not ones.

A hectare of black felt is not an organism and not a crowd. Cut a hand's-breadth of it and lift it: it comes away like wet lacquer, nine layers thick, and each layer is a different creature doing a different chemistry, and none of the nine will live alone. The top takes the light. The second takes what the top spills. Down at the seventh, something splits sulfur and hands its waste upward through a felt of filaments so fine that the whole mat, held to a lamp, shows a grain like muscle. The plateau's true population is a set of arrangements. There are perhaps four hundred arrangements that recur often enough to have names, and the names are colors, and the colors are the only taxonomy anybody actually uses.

The oldest of these arrangements are in glass.

Every mother-culture on the plateau descends from a jar brought up the escarpment in a padded crate, and every jar has a warden, and the wardens are the closest thing this city has to nobility, though they are paid like skilled mechanics and smell like everyone else. The rooms have not been allowed to cool since the founding. There are three independent heat sources in each, two of them redundant, one of them a turbine hall's exhaust that has been flowing since before the wardens' great-grandparents were indentured. A warden's whole working life is spent watching a shade. He is hired into Nineteen Black at nineteen; at sixty he hands on a jar that is very slightly browner than the one he received, and this drift is documented to four decimal places in ledgers that go back nine generations, and there is a standing court that hears disputes about whether a drift is a drift or a fault.

They talk about the mothers the way other places talk about saints and racehorses. *She's shy in the dry season. She likes the water walked, not poured. She took eleven years to come back after the gray year.* The gray year is two hundred and six years ago; a room cooled for nine hours; four mothers died and one went the color of dishwater and never recovered its black, and the pans it seeded went thin, and a district of the city ate stubble porridge for a decade and lost a third of its children. There is a word in the local speech, *hallow*, which now means a shade of rust and originally meant the color of a thing that is starving. Nobody remembers the second meaning. Words wear round here too.

The wardens are fed to their mothers when they die. Rendered fine, decanted, a small ceremony with a funnel. Ask about it and you get a shrug and the local formula, which is used for everything from grief to accounting: *it goes back on.*

## Teeth

The rasps are the reason the interior looks like a cobble field and sounds like frying.

Anatomy: a sole like a wet palm, a shell laid flat and forward rather than coiled high, and inside the mouth a ribbon of teeth that are not horn or enamel but apatite. Phosphate. The animal mines the film for phosphorus and wears the phosphorus on its tongue and grinds it away against the tuff and grows it back, four ribbons a year, forever. This is why the rendering works want them. A wild rasp is a slow bag of ore with a mouth on the front.

It is also why they walk. The Long Circuit — eleven years, eleven thousand kilometers, an interior loop that crosses itself in four places — follows a phosphorus gradient in the film so shallow that the city's chemists have spent a century failing to find it. The rasps find it with their feet. The sole tastes; the animal turns; the turn takes about six hours to complete; and forty thousand of them per hectare all turning the same six-hour turn is what makes the plains, from the air, look like something combed.

The drovers who follow the herds navigate by animal. A lead rasp rides in a wet crate on a cart, padded, fed, kept at blood heat, and every morning the drover reads the direction its shell has settled toward overnight, and steers. The surveyors from the Nitrate Houses find this hilarious. The surveyors have been lost on the interior four times in recorded history, and each time it was a drover who found them, and the drovers have a proverb about instruments that does not translate.

Drovers age in circuits. A woman of four circuits is forty-four and has walked the loop four times and been born on it. Marriages across the loop are common and strange: two drovers on opposite phases meet at a crossing place, marry over nine days, and then go on, and see each other again in eleven years, and their children are raised in the city by aunts and go out to the herds at fourteen. There are four towns on the plateau that exist only as fairs, that fill for a month every eleven years, whose permanent population is a caretaker and a dog-shaped thing that eats sump-scrape, and whose streets are laid out wider than any city street because nobody could remember, at the founding, how wide a crowd needed to be.

And out on the interior there are individuals older than the city. The bathtub-sized ones are two hundred and forty, three hundred years old; you can read it in the shell, which lays a band each Circuit. There are rasps within a day's walk of the northern seep that were grazing before anyone came up the cliff, that have never encountered a boot or a hook or a shadow with edges, and that will still be turning, six hours at a time, when the last penstock is cold. Nobody owns them. Nobody has ever touched them. They are the plateau's actual old families.

## The dish

The domesticated ones have a smaller world and a longer memory than anyone credits.

A tread floor is a circular room, nine meters across, warm, wet, smelling of crushed rock and ammonia and the sour green-less green of the felt slurry they're fed. Four rasps walk a capstan. They have walked it since the room was built. Their soles polish the tuff to a mirror, and the mirror dishes, and the dish is now a meter deep in the oldest quarters, which means the animals walk uphill in every direction from the center and have done so, gradually, for two hundred years, and their gait has changed to accommodate it, and the change is heritable enough that a treadman can tell a dish-bred animal from a plain-bred one at a glance.

They know the driver's boots. Not his face — the eyes are poor, two dim cups that only sort light from dark — but the salt of him, the specific chemistry of his hands. Change the boots and the animals stop, and stopping is an emergency, because a stopped tread floor is a stopped mill and a stopped mill is a lawsuit. So the boots are inherited with the job, resoled forty times, the original leather somewhere in the middle like a keystone in a wall.

The names wear round. A tread rasp entered in the ledger as Patience-of-the-Second-Leat is called Pat by the third generation, and Pat is the fourth of that name, and the driver will tell you, if you ask, that the current Pat is *not* the old Pat, in a tone that suggests he has had this argument with someone from the Houses and won it.

## Larders

The shrikes are rasps that went the other way.

This is not a metaphor; it is the local taxonomy, which recognizes two bodies and admits that everything else is one of them modified. A shrike is a rasp with the shell reduced to a keel, the sole narrowed to a grasping foot, and the tooth-ribbon migrated forward into a hook. It filters nothing. It climbs. It takes a grazing rasp off the stubble, carries it up, and pushes it onto a spike — a fence picket, a rail bolt, the broken end of a scaffold, the lightning finial on a chapel.

The larders are how the city reads the herds. Every ward has its spikes, and the Hooks' guild pays a boy to walk them and write down what is impaled: shell width, band count, the color of the dried sole. A season of thin shells in the larders means the interior is lean and the Pan Fields will need liquor. It is a census conducted by a predator on behalf of a bureaucracy, and it works, and the shrikes have accordingly been protected by ordinance for a hundred and sixty years, which they repay by nesting in the arc-lamp housings and shorting them.

The impaled ones are sometimes alive for two days. Their ribbons keep working. In the evening, along a fence line, you can hear a hundred small dry sounds like someone patiently filing something in another room.

## Upward

The sailers are the second body: the mouth without the sole.

Four meters of span, a body you could crush in one hand, jaws held open into a funnel of mucus that is spun fresh every hour and swallowed with its catch. They do not hunt. They hang in the standing updraft in three layers, and the layers are ages: the small ones low over the pans, gleaning the steam-rise; the middle layer at three hundred meters, working the column; and above, in the cold clean air where the light is violent, the great slow breeders, nine years aloft, mates taken on the wing, young carried in a brood pouch under the keel and released as motes into the drift so that they fall for two days and are eaten by everything, and the one in ten million that survives climbs the cliff at the end of its first year and is taken by the wind and does not touch stone again.

The thrumming is not calling. The column sounds itself and listens to the return: a hundred million mouths humming a low note and reading, in the way the note comes back, where the drift is thick. It is a sense organ the size of a province, distributed, shared, and no individual understands it. When the return wind brings the lowland drift up the escarpment at four-twenty, the whole column changes pitch at once, and the people look up from their work, and the phrase for that pitch — *the bowl's full* — is also the phrase for a good year, a full granary, a pregnancy.

They land once. When a breeder's wings finally fail it comes down over the rim in a long ugly spiral, and the guano crews watch for it, and there is a lottery, and by ordinance the Nitrate Houses have first refusal on any body that touches the terrace. The white cliffs are eighty meters of droppings and corpses in alternating bands, warm inside, fermenting, forty degrees at the working face, and the miners cut them in benches with the settled breeders showing as dark seams — a wing-bone here, a keel there, whole stratified individuals, the richest phosphate on the plateau. The miners call them *falls*, and each fall has a number, and there are men whose entire job is to record where and when, and their ledgers are the only long-term dataset the city has on the animals it lives off, and nobody has ever read them all the way through.

## Small mouths

Then everything else, which is where the actual number of species is.

The hitch, which rides the sailer's keel and filters the sailer's mucus. The thing that filters the hitch. The thing that lives in that. A shrike's hook has a parasite; the parasite has a hyperparasite; the hyperparasite has, so far as anyone knows, an honest living.

The urban endemics are the interesting ones, because they are the only creatures on the plateau whose habitat is two hundred years old. In the boot sumps at every doorway there is a small translucent rasp, thumbnail-sized, that blooms in the twenty minutes after the rain when the sky comes down and the sumps fill with soup, and it is found in sumps and in the wet mortar of street corners and nowhere in the wild. Under the trolley rails: the grease-soles, flat, pale, almost see-through, following the drip line, dying in the gaps at the depot where the grease runs out, so that the depot gaps have been fitted with drip pans by an ordinance that took nine years to pass and is described in the preamble as a matter of public health. In the eaves, a filter that has learned to hang under the mesh instead of over it. In the warm condensers of the pump houses, a felt that has become an animal, or an animal that has become a felt; the argument is old and the guild that maintains the condensers does not care.

And a black that eats copper. The mesh-blight. Every window on the plateau is screened in double mesh and the blight goes through the outer layer in about fourteen months, and there is a guild of screeners whose entire trade is this, and who are, by a wide margin, the most reliably employed people in the city.

The penstocks are the ones the Works will not discuss with outsiders. Two thousand meters of drop, house-wide, dark, at a pressure that would kill anything from the surface, running at the same speed within two percent for a hundred and forty years. Inside there are filters. They live in the flow and only in the flow; they feed on what the leats bring down; they have a fixed regime, no season, no drought, no freeze, and they have had it long enough to become nine separate populations in nine separate penstocks, distinguishable by the shape of the mouth, and each of them exists nowhere else in the universe.

Stop a penstock and you kill a species. The Works knows. It is written into the charter in language about *the integrity of the conveyance*, and it is why a relining is preceded by nine years of bypass construction and seeding, at a cost that has been described in the Houses as insane, and why the rope men who go up the stacks with fire are, technically, doing something else: culling nesters to keep a river running. The Right of Flow is the oldest law here. You may build anything. You may not stop anything. Sabotage is tried as a kind of murder and the sentencing language does not mention people.

## The newcomers

The people came up the cliff about two hundred and thirty years ago and have been telling themselves ever since that they are slow.

There are three accounts of the ascent and all three are taught. The Houses say a charter and a survey. The wardens say nine jars and a woman who carried the tenth on her back and lost it. The drovers say the lowlands were closing — something eating its way north, a hunger with no name — and that the founders came up because up was the only direction left, and that this is why nobody goes down. What is certain is the date, the number of jars, and that the first houses were built with doorframes sized for people who were thinner than people are now.

You can read the bodies. Wet-skinned, ammonia-cured, black in the creases of the hands that no scrubbing touches — the *pan-hand*, which is a class marker and a marriage consideration. Everyone blinks constantly; the drift is abrasive and the double mesh only halves it. Everyone speaks in a register that carries under the roar, a flattened shout that outsiders find aggressive and locals find affectionate, and nobody here can produce a whisper, because a whisper is a thing you learn by hearing one. So the private life is silent. There is a hand-language, dense and old, used at table and in bed and at funerals, and every guild has its own dialect of it, and the wardens' is the most elaborate because they work in rooms where the mothers are said to dislike noise.

They eat the plateau. Stir, which is black porridge, and sour black, which is stir fermented in stone vats until it fizzes and hurts. Rasp foot steamed in its own shell with a spoon of the gray liquor. Sailer chick, if you are rich, from the terraces, tasting of ammonia and iron and something almost sweet. Cloth is spun nap and sailer membrane; paper is pressed felt; ink is felt; the pens are shell. There is no wood in anything. Their word for *tree* is their word for penstock, and consequently their word for lineage is a manifold, and a family genealogy is drawn as a plumbing diagram with the ancestors at the source and the descendants at the outlets, and to say a man has *no branch* is to say he is a dead end, and it is the worst thing you can say about him.

And once a year, if they can afford it, they eat the outside. Drift cheese: the afternoon return-wind filtered through nine layers of cloth, pressed, aged in a warm cellar, cut in slices thin enough to read through. It tastes of the lowlands, which is to say it tastes of something nobody has ever seen. There are vintages by wind. There are families who have bought the same wind for six generations. There is a jar in the Bureau of Arrivals containing eleven green flecks recovered from eleven cheeses over a hundred and forty years, and the Bureau's catalogue describes them as *pigment bodies, origin below, function unknown*, and there is a poem about them that every child learns, and the poem is wrong about what green is, and nobody minds.

The greeting between strangers, in the pan districts, is *what color were you hired into?* It places a man exactly: his guild, his district, his decade, his prospects. The answer is a shade and a number. He will still be, more or less, that shade when he retires. So will the jar.

## The Still

Silence is the expensive thing.

There are rooms in the sinkholes, hung in nine layers of felt on sprung frames, double-walled, entered through a wet lock. Ten minutes costs a week's wage. What you hear in there is your blood, your joints, the wet click of your own eyes, and — for most people, for the first time in their lives — the graze-hiss, coming up through the rock from the plain outside, forty thousand mouths per hectare, everywhere, always, the sound the world makes while it is being eaten.

Children are taken once, at eleven, at the start of a Circuit. Most come out laughing. Some come out and stand in the street with their hands over their ears, and a few of those become Listeners, who take a vow to hear one thing at a time and are, on the whole, tolerated, and are quietly useful to the Works because they can hear a bearing going nine months before an instrument can. Criminals are sentenced to an hour in the Still. It is considered severe. There is a reform movement that wants it abolished and it has never once been mentioned in the Houses.

The other heresy is the Off: shut one hall, once, for one day, and let the plateau be heard without it. They are not vandals; they are romantics, and their pamphlets are beautiful, and the Works' reply is always the same nine mouth-shapes drawn in a row, and the argument has been running for eighty years and will run for eighty more.

The dead go back on. Rendered, limed, distributed; a family's portion goes to a numbered pan, and the mourning formula is a number. *He's in Nineteen.* You eat your grandmother in the porridge and this is not a horror here, it is the only decency available, because the whole plateau is a slow leak and everything that lives on it lives by carrying phosphorus back uphill, and to withhold your own would be the one unforgivable act. There are cemeteries, too, for the sentimental, and they are empty, and they are pleasant places to sit.

And they know how it ends. The body below is cooling at one degree per forty thousand years. The arithmetic is on the wall of every schoolroom: the springs go cold, the dome fails, the first frost in the history of the world. Nine hundred and sixty thousand years, give or take. It is not a fear. It is a date, and it is the reason the local word for *forever* is a number, and the reason nobody here believes in anything that happens quickly.

## The count

The census counts a little over two million people, and does not count anything else, and everyone understands that the census is a joke.

Count it properly and the answer is mostly mouths: thirteen digits of rasps, eight of sailers, an uncountable tonnage of nine-layered black, four hundred recurring arrangements, nine penstock races, one copper-eating blight, one sump endemic, and the shrikes, and the hitches, and the things in the flues that shift all night and that children are told are the dead. Of all of it, only the two million know there is a question. They are the newest arrivals, the loudest organ, the fastest thing on the table — faster than the gantry, faster than the black ribbon thickening in the pan by an amount you cannot see.

At four the dome comes down. Twenty minutes, warm, unhurried, and the sky empties into the streets and the sumps bloom and the shrikes drop into the alleys and the column overhead changes pitch, and every door in four hundred kilometers stands open for a moment while somebody scrapes their boots. Then the sun comes back low and orange through the arc lamps and the whole plateau steams and the drift rises, and out on the interior a three-hundred-year-old animal that has never seen a person finishes a turn it began at dawn, toward a leanness no instrument here can find, and the rain that fell on its shell is already on its way down through the karst to come out warm at the cliff face, at blood heat, in the dark, forever, or for nine hundred and sixty thousand years.
