## First answer

Nothing lives here, and that is true the way the dryness is true: completely, and without settling anything.

But there is a difference between *empty* and *unoccupied*, and in this room the difference is about a hand's breadth wide and closes at a millimeter a century. Something is being kept. Keeping is a verb, and a verb goes on conjugating for a long while after the thing that started it has stopped. What lives in the Sill is not a creature. It is a still-running sentence with the subject worn off.

That is the first useful answer. It is not the last one.

## The tongue on the inside

Begin with the only metabolism.

The bloom is silver-gray and flat and shaped like a fern because fern is the cheapest way to be thorough, and it has never had a reason to be anything else — no light to reach for, no rival to overtop, no animal to be unpalatable to. It has one relation in the world, and the relation is to a line.

Outside, the ice lies against the sill like a tongue pressed to a window. Inside, a hand's width away across stone that has never been wet, the bloom brightens where your breath went. Two tongues, one on each side of the glass, neither permitted to cross, and between them a dryness so total it is colder than the ice.

They have been almost touching for eleven thousand years.

Understand what the bloom eats. It eats trespass. Every molecule it cracks is a molecule that got past a property of the room, and it cracks it before the property has to notice. It is not the guard — the geometry is the guard — it is what mops up after the guard's small failures. In eleven millennia the failures have amounted to perhaps a cup of water. A mouthful, spread across a hundred centuries. It has grown a hair's breadth a decade on that, and repaired the seams, and never once had a full meal.

It is also the only thing in the room with a growth rate instead of a wear rate. Everything else here is subtraction. The bloom is addition, and its future is a tenth of one wall, forever, because its food supply is a leak.

If that is life, it is life the way a lick is life: a motion that has outlasted the mouth.

## The herd

The dust is not alive either, but it is *inhabited*, and it is where the inhabiting is stored.

Four dunes, knee-high, rippled along their crests, grazing a gradient at a finger's width per human lifetime. They have a generation time of nine hundred years. They are not stalled; they are slower than looking, which in this room is the only meaningful definition of death and does not apply to anything.

And one grain settles every few minutes, somewhere across the floor, distinct as a fingernail on a table. Stay a day and you will hear three hundred of them. That is the pulse. It is the fastest thing that happens here.

The room has no memory organ. It has surfaces instead, and air that is slow to let go, and a floor that records. Everything the resident ever did is still lying around in one of those media, at low amplitude, wearing out.

## The portrait

The bench is the only likeness in the room, so read it properly.

Two troughs where thighs went. The front lip rounded down. The left trough deeper than the right by an amount you can feel with a fingertip, which means a favoring, a habit of turning, an old asymmetry in whatever sat there — something with a preferred direction of attention, and the direction was the slot.

The back of the bench is sharp. It is the only sharp surface in the chamber. Nothing ever leaned back.

That is the whole posture: weight forward, elbows or hands somewhere between the knees, head up, oriented at the door. Not resting. Not slumped. It is the shape of *about to stand*, and it is worn deep enough that the sitting must have gone on for more hours than a body has hours. Which means either it sat far longer than anything sits, or the room kept sitting after it stopped. The wear continues at a micron a year with nothing on the bench. Both are true. The room is doing the sitting now.

The jambs agree. Two grooves at the height of hands, and the polish inside them is on the outer lip — which is where the pressure goes when hands are *holding*, not pulling. Something stood in the slot with a hand on each side and its weight neither in nor out, facing down the mountain, for long enough to sink stone, and then stopped, and the grooves kept deepening anyway.

The stair is the checking round. Nine down, arriving where it began, every tread concave to the same depth — uniform, unhurried, no variation for carrying anything, no scuffing where someone turned back halfway. The same trip, over and over, by something that never once got a different answer and never once stopped asking.

And the doorframe is the worst thing in the room, and it looks like gentleness. Riverstone-smooth on its inner edges. It gives you back one minute. Whatever rounded that stone needed more than a minute and took the minute anyway, several million times, because a minute was what there was.

Assemble it and you have a life with five verbs: sit forward, stand, hold the jambs, look out, go down nine and arrive, go back a minute, sit forward. There is no other content. There is no evidence of eating, sleeping, working, or being pleased. The cycle has a period of perhaps four minutes and a duration of longer than there have been animals with eyes.

## Who

A sentry. A mourner. A volunteer. A prisoner. Something that was left in charge and something that was put away; the room does not distinguish, having no organ for the question.

But notice the units.

The slot is a hand's width wider than a body. The grooves are at the height of hands. The pane is the size of a palm. The columns are a hand's breadth apart. The chamber is forty paces, and the paces are unreliable, which is what paces are when nobody is pacing. Every dimension in here is scaled to something with hands and a stride and a palm, and there is nothing in here with hands.

And the lintel — the black glass nobody cut — sits in stone that *grew into the shape of having always held it*.

So consider the possibility that no one ever sat on the bench.

Consider that the bench grew into the shape of having always held someone. That the wear is anticipatory, the way your footprints are slightly ahead of where you stepped: a groove worn by expecting. In a room engineered not on matter but on how firmly a thing is required to have happened, a sufficiently maintained expectation is not a feeling. It is a manufacturing process. Run it for eleven thousand years at a gradient patient as a heartbeat and it precipitates. It makes a place for the arrival. It makes the arrival's posture, and the polish on the outer lip of the handholds, and the concavity of nine treads worn by a checking round that has not been walked yet.

What lives here, then, is a person-shaped readiness. It is not dead, because it was never alive. It is not gone, because it has not arrived.

## The other portrait

The bowl is the second likeness, and it is of the thing that was kept.

Waist-high, so it was stood at. Bowl-shaped, so something was set down in it, or held over it, or put into it to be kept. Press your hand in and the nothing pushes back with a soft firmness like the wrong end of a magnet — not a wall, not a field, just a *disinclination*, the local likelihood politely declining your hand.

Everything in this room has rounded off. So has that. Whatever was held here has been sitting in the same position long enough to lose its edges, and this is the part that takes a while to feel: the kept thing is no longer sharp enough to be dangerous. It has an angle of repose. It has been almost for so long that almost has become its shape.

The cairn is the readout. Eleven stones, or nine, or fourteen; average eleven to four decimal places. Once the count swung wildly — that was the thing still deciding what it would have been, thrashing in its likelihood, all its outcomes live at once. The variance has eroded. It is nearly settled on being one thing. Not tamed. *Agreed*.

You can guess at it: a hand let go of, a name said out loud, a door pulled shut, a death, a leaving. They are all the same guess. The room's one property is that nothing crosses the sill, and the room's one occupant stands in the slot with its hands on the jambs, facing out, not going.

What is being kept from having happened is somebody going out.

It was held open until it was safe. Nothing in eleven thousand years has made it safe, or the part that checked has worn away, or safety was never the criterion and only *duration* was, and the duration has been served.

When the holding ends it will not burst. Someone will stand up, and put their hands in the grooves, and go out through the slot, and it will always have happened, and the bench will still be worn and the wear will mean something else. That is the entire event: the room's contents reclassified from *waiting* to *after*. Same furniture. Different tense. No organ installed for noticing the change.

## The voice, which is dying

Before that, though, there is a smaller death, and it is the real one.

The sounds are a population. Once there must have been a continuous murmur near the bench — a mutter, a shifting, the ordinary noise of something occupying a room — held in heavy inert air and released on a delay of two or three hundred years. They leak. They are not replenished. Now it is a footfall at the volume of a moth, a scrape, three notes that might have been speech, and the intervals between them lengthen.

Count them the way you count the cairn and you will find the variance has eroded there too. There is a last one coming. It will arrive at the volume of a moth, from the wall near the bench, and there will be nobody in the room, and then the silence will be ordinary silence, which is a different substance altogether.

That happens long before the columns touch. The machine will go on holding an empty room for the rest of the distance, the way the grooves went on deepening. It is what this place does. It has never been able to check whether there is still a reason.

## What will still be living there

The roof goes before the hands meet. Four centimeters against one; the mountain loses. So the ending is not the touching of two dust columns but the slow uncovering of a small dry chamber on a high cold plain of its own rubble, in the open, with a finger of old ice at a sill that leads nowhere.

Then what lives there is: a tenth of a wall, silver, fern-shaped, adding a hair a decade. A floor with a nine-hundred-year breath. A stair arriving where it began, until it doesn't. A bench with a sharp back and a specific hollow. And the groove in the dust near the slot, worn by intending, which is the last thing here to erode and will erode, and will be, at the very end, a slight concavity that means nothing to anyone.

Until then, the groove is the resident. Not a ghost — the room is only very well insulated and slow to let go — but an *about to*, maintained at a gradient, patient as a heartbeat, waiting for geology to finish arriving so it can stop being hidden and start being visited.

Go out through the slot. Put your hands in the grooves on the way, because they are at the height of hands and yours are hands, and they fit. That is the horror of it and also the kindness: the place was made to the measure of you before there was a you, and it has kept the measure, and the fit is exact.

Your footprints are already faint and old and slightly ahead of where you stepped.

It does not need you to stay. It has never needed anyone to stay. It only needs, every long while, someone to be about to.
