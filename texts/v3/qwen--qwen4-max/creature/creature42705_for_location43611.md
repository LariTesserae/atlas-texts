## The People

You meet one before you meet the town. Somebody comes up out of the stair on a Thursday to meet the cart, and you do not hear them arrive. They are simply there, three paces off, waiting with the patience of a heron, and you turn and start, and they wait for you to finish starting before they say anything.

Then they say it quietly, and they do not step forward, and you have to come to them.

This is the first thing. A Rows person speaks at the volume you would use to a sleeping child, and they will not raise it to bridge a gap, so the gap has to close. Visitors read this as coldness for about a day and a half. Then they get used to standing at forty centimetres from a stranger, close enough to see the pores and the damp in the eyebrows, and they discover that a Rows face at forty centimetres is an extremely ordinary face — open, tired, amused, a bit wary — and that it was only at three metres that it looked like a mask.

They are pale in the way of people who get four hours of light a day, bounced. They are strong through the shoulders and the grip and weak-looking through the chest, and their hands are the hands of people who have spent a life on rope and bark: thick pads, split nails, a shine on the inner thighs and shins from straddling limbs. They walk with a rolling, testing gait, weight committed only after the sole has asked the question, and on the limestone above they are silent, and on the pavements of the coast they are miserable, because dead flat ground gives nothing back and their feet keep waiting for an answer.

Their clothes are soft — wraps, ties, wooden toggles, nothing that can strike or ring. Metal here is too valuable to wear and too loud to wear anyway. What they do wear is wire: wound, not forged, so that a dropped ring makes no sound at all. A marriage ring is a soft coil of four-tenths nickel, replaced every decade or so as it wears through, and last year it was fog-net, and the year before that it was a leaf.

They smell of hot pennies and wet wool and hay. Their collars go faintly apple-green in the wash and this is considered handsome.

## The Voice

The dialect has had its edges taken off. Two hundred years of being spoken at forty centimetres has favoured nasals and liquids and long soft vowels, and names of two syllables that can be said with the back turned and still be caught. There is no hail in the language. No word for calling someone across a distance, because you cannot call someone across a distance; there is a word that means *to go and stand near enough to begin*, and it is one of the commonest verbs in the town.

They have a great many words for kinds of quiet. The quiet between campaigns. The quiet of a fog night with the mesh running. The quiet of a row where somebody is ill. The particular quiet of the undercroft with three hundred cells humming just under the threshold, which is not quiet at all but is called it. They have no word for silence. When they translate a visitor's word for silence they use the phrase that means *the ninth day*.

Argument is conducted nose to nose, breath on breath, at a volume that would not wake a baby, and it is ferocious. You cannot quarrel with someone you are unwilling to smell. This has consequences the town has stopped noticing: feuds are rare and enormous, because to sustain one you must keep agreeing to be close; most disputes are carried by intermediaries who go back and forth between two lamps all evening like buckets on a rope; and there is an understood **quarrel-right**, given at a marriage and occasionally at a friendship, which means *I will come near you when I am angry and you will not step back*. It is a more serious commitment than the sharing of a crotch. People say it plainly: *I'd take a quarrel from her.*

Singing is a chest hum, four or five in a fork with their shoulders touching, and it is the loudest pleasure permitted. There is a monochord strung on a shiver-wire in Row F that nobody listens to with their ears; you sit on the plate with your heels against the wood and it comes up through the bones of the pelvis, and old people say it is the only music that was ever any good. Plays are performed for forty people sitting within a metre of the actors, so the whole art is eyes and breath, and a famous performer is famous for a way of exhaling.

Children learn to walk on the root plate before they learn to talk, and they learn it the way you learn a boat. They are taught the listening before they are taught letters: *that tick is cooling, that one is loading, tell someone.* They wear felted mittens in their worst year. And when a small child has a tantrum — which is not a misdemeanour here but a forestry event, a genuine threat to the walls of the avenue — the child is not punished. The child is carried up to the rim and put in a shouting shed and left to scream at a limb that needed thickening anyway, and comes down hoarse and calm and slightly pleased with itself.

*Go and tell the shed*, they say. Adults do too.

## The Rasp

There is one class of person in the Rows whose voice you can hear coming.

The shouting sheds are staffed by hire, and the hire is mostly from outside, because a person raised here cannot easily make themselves produce that sound. It is not a moral reluctance; they try, and what comes out is a bark that shames them. So the town buys larynxes. A contract shouter comes up the stair for a season, sits two hours a day in a dead-wood box collared in felt and air, and yells down a horn at a chosen limb, and is paid very well, and goes hoarse by the third month, and is pensioned or renewed.

The same rasp is what the court gives. Acoustic trespass — dropping weights in a neighbour's avenue at night, over-pruning a crotch so it panics and closes, the slow deniable vandalism that takes eighteen months to show — is the serious crime here, and the sentence is a season at the horn. It fits perfectly. It ruins the thing the town values most in a person, it makes that person useful, and it marks them for life. You can hear a man's history when he asks for bread.

Which is why the job is disreputable, and why the hired shouters are assumed, wrongly and universally, to have been sentenced somewhere else first.

But grief-work is honoured. A widow is paid to weep loudly at a beam that wants strengthening, and nobody finds this strange, and the beam is heavier by spring. There is a distinction here that the town can't explain to visitors and doesn't try: noise given to the Stand is a gift; noise made *at* your neighbours is assault. The shed is where you put what you cannot keep down. Some people go up there in a good week just to hear themselves, and come back sheepish, and are not judged much.

## Lamps

Nine hundred cubic metres of living tissue per person means that everybody lives a long way from everybody, and that the whole social life of the town is conducted in light.

You can see a lamp a hundred paces off across a void. You cannot hear anything at all. So the Rows is a place of visible, silent, distant lives: forty small pools of green glass hung in the dark at absurd intervals, and everyone knowing, without ever being told, who is up late, who is sick, who has company, who has not lit at all for three nights. The rumour mill runs on optics. *Her lamp was burning at the third hour.* *He came dark to Row E.*

Because you cannot arrive suddenly. You come along an avenue with your lamp swinging the approach pattern, and the person you are visiting watches you come for a full minute, and has that minute to put away what they were doing and arrange their face. Nobody in the Rows has ever been surprised by a visitor. There is no ambush, no interruption, no bursting in. Every meeting is premeditated by sixty seconds of visible walking, and the courtesy is so deep that a spontaneous arrival is nearly obscene.

To come without a lamp is either a proposal or a threat, and the difference lies entirely in what you do when you get there.

The cords are the other half. The wire network runs through the canopy and needs re-strung constantly because the tree keeps eating its anchors, and the switchboard slides steadily north-east after the cables that the tree is growing toward, and the operator has been at it thirty years and knows who is calling before the code resolves — the way you know a footstep. Everyone has a fist. Children are taught to send cleanly and are teased for their accent on the wire long after they've lost it in speech.

And still: a minute's walk down a lit avenue past nothing at all. The loneliness of the Rows is a specific, well-furnished loneliness that nobody there would trade. Going to see a friend takes ten minutes each way through cathedral emptiness, and this is understood to be part of the gift. You do not drop in. You *go*.

## The Trades

The Clearers walk an eleven-day round with saws and long-handled chisels and they are the town's aristocracy of nerve: the only people permitted to wound the Stand on purpose, taking the new wood out of the avenues and off the frames, catching the acid weep in a cup because the cup is the whole chemistry. They are quiet even by local standards. They tend to be small. They have a professional habit of standing with the palm flat on a bole for a while before they cut it, which visitors read as reverence and which is actually a way of feeling for the run of the grain.

The surveyor redraws the map every year and is the most argued-with person in the basin. The keeper of the Balance Book has been recording kilograms removed and millimetres of encroachment, row by row, round by round, since the year Three, in a hand that gets smaller as she gets older, and she is the only person alive who has read all of it.

The collectors work the gutters at dusk with their lamps and are, by a wide margin, the town's poets — the job is forty minutes of walking slowly through falling fog in the best light of the day, and it selects for a certain kind of person, and they marry each other.

The cell-men have a rash on the inside of the wrists that comes in the third year and stops being mentioned. Everyone in the Rows has a faint metallic taste at the back of the throat and thinks outside water tastes flat. The drawer sits in a felt-collared booth like a shouter, because the drawing bench is the loudest thing here, and works a drum and an oiled die in a sound like a held breath, and is visited rather than lived among.

The apprentice with the long-spouted can oils every bearing in the town twice a week, all of them, for four years, and therefore knows every hinge and drum and drum-brake in the place by feel, and therefore becomes the surveyor. It is not a rule. It just happens.

The pollination crews go up in the flush with brushes and paper and wire tags and work the year's crossing plan, and they carry the town's whole future in a satchel, and they know it. They are the closest thing to celebrities. They are also insufferable about it. They leave a fraction of the flowers unbagged on the underside of the vault, always the same fraction, for the moth.

Time here is measured in campaigns and rounds. A child is nine hundred campaigns old. An argument has been running for four hundred rounds. Nobody says *last month*; they say *two campaigns before the fog came*.

## Stock

Two hundred and thirty-one people is not enough people, and the town has always known it, and the cart brings more than grain.

Marriage is called grafting. In-laws are stock. The ideal of a family is the ideal of the Stand: joined so thoroughly that no seam can be found with a knife. It is a beautiful ideal and it has the same failure mode as everything else here, which is that fusion closes the rooms, and the town's private life is a two-century argument about how much space to leave between persons.

At fifteen they are sent out. The **clatter year**: a season on the coast, in a stone house, with wind and gulls and carts and doors. Some last a week. Some last the year and come back with a strange loudness in them that takes a decade to wear off. Some stay, and are written in the ledger as *grafted out*, and are never blamed, and are spoken of warmly and rarely. And some come back at fifty, grey-faced, hearing gone, unable to be in a room with a window, and are given work in the cold room where the year's pollen sleeps in folded paper, because it is the quietest place in the world.

Addresses drift. A woman born at Row F, at Nine, upper, dies at Row F, at Twelve, upper, having never moved house; the house moved, four metres east and thirty paces on, and she went with it, and her grandchildren's address is a place she never stood. The oldest families come from rows that no longer exist — the north end, the forge, the year Twelve — and they are the ones most insistent that the cutting rate is too high.

## The Bays, and the One Gap in the Round

The root plate is dished here and there into shallow bays, polished by two centuries of walking to the colour of tea. Old people lie in them.

They go deaf to speech in the ordinary way and they do not go deaf to the Stand, because the Stand comes up through the wood into the cheekbone, and a woman of eighty with her ear on the plate can hear a limb loading forty metres away and can tell you which one. So the last years of a life here are spent lying in a warm shallow depression in the floor of the world, listening, and reporting, and being believed. The reports go in the Balance Book beside the kilograms. Half the town's structural intelligence has come from the dying.

And when they die, a grave is asked for. Not dug — there is nothing to dig. A void is requested at the base of a bole, framed in dead heartwood, and the body is laid in it, and the living wood is allowed to close.

It is the one gap in the Clearers' round. Eleven days, every avenue, every crotch, every frame — and one place, and then another place, and then eleven or fourteen places, that the saws go past.

The family measures it. A hand's breadth a year, roughly, sometimes faster. They go and sit at the shrinking mouth of it and put a hand in. The visits get shorter as the room gets shallower; there is a last visit at which you can no longer get your arm in, and everyone knows what it means, and there is a word for that day. When the walls meet, mourning ends — not by decree, but because there is no longer anywhere to go.

Names cut in the bark heal over. Wire tags are swallowed. The town learned very early that wood is a bad record because wood forgives, and so everything that must be kept is kept on paper, in the map room, in the ledger with the nurseryman's handwriting, in two hundred frames of a very slow film. The archive is the only thing in the Rows that does not grow. It is, in every sense that matters, where the town keeps its dead.

## The Rate

Politics is the rate, and the rate has no correct answer, because cutting too little closes the rooms and cutting too much closes the rooms faster, and both failures look identical from inside. So there are two parties, the open hands and the slow hands, whose platforms differ by three-tenths of a kilogram per metre per round, and whose suppers are loud only in the sense that everyone is leaning in.

And there is a third thing, small, tolerated, not discussed at supper. There are people who have stopped clearing.

They ask for their rooms and then they let them come. They say the encroachment is not a loss. They say the town has spent two hundred years holding itself apart from something that has been trying the whole time to include it, and that the charter's joke about fauna is not a joke, and that the end of the negotiation is not defeat. Their crotches get smaller. They move their beds inward. Their children mostly leave for other rows, and are not stopped, and grieve. Every few decades the surveyor draws the annual map and an address that was on the last one is not on this one, and nobody says those people died, because they didn't, exactly, and there is no word for what they did.

The Clearers walk past the place. It is smooth. It is warm-ish underfoot, in the way of wood.

## The Others

The moth has a fraction of the flowers reserved to it in every crossing plan since the year Sixty, and a count in the ledger, and children who can find it and adults who mostly can't.

The wren arrives in winter and is louder than any person in the basin and nobody has ever proposed doing anything about it. It is entered in the book each year as the winter visitor, with a date. The Clearers log where it works and cut around it. When it leaves in spring the town is measurably quieter and slightly worse-tempered for about a week.

Snails at the wet seam, which is a hand's width of weeping limestone in the northern corner and the only wild water in the world here; people go and sit by it. Woodlice in the litter, mites past counting, spiders in the canopy doing the one job nobody has taken over.

And the rat of the year One-Fifty, whose eleven pages are read aloud once a year at conversational volume to an audience sitting very close, and which is the closest thing these people have to an epic: a scratching in a wall void, and the void measurably narrowing, and the whole town awake and listening and understanding, correctly, that something had come in that would eat their rooms.

---

Ask who lives here and the ledger will give you a number, and the charter will give you a joke, and eight generations have turned the joke into a description.

They are the fauna. They pollinate, they cull, they thin the litter, they clean the axil tanks, they pick off the rust, they carry seed down to the cold room. They also keep the accounts, which no other fauna has ever done, and it is the accounts that make the difference: the tree cannot remember where it agreed to leave a room, and paper can.

And it has learned them, in its way. It grows the treads under their boots. It leaves hollows when asked politely. It grows toward the warmth of their cables, and they follow it, and the switchboard slides north-east, and neither party is leading.

Go up to the rim at dusk and look down. The cold river comes over the lip and the mesh begins to tick and forty lamps come on in all that living dark, absurdly far apart. Somewhere in it a woman is lying with her cheek on the floor, listening. Somewhere a man has his palm flat on a bole and is waiting for an answer that will take eighteen months to arrive.

He is not impatient. That is the thing about the people who live here. They ask, and they wait, and when the answer comes they write it down.
