## What counts as one

Ask that first, because the terrace will not answer it the way a forest answers it.

The carbon is not the individual. A trunk is twenty-eight days of carbon in a shape, and then it is a butt, and then it is crushings, and then it is powder in a fluidized line hissing toward the bake furnace, and then it is a trunk again — but never the same trunk, and never, in any sense the floor recognizes, the same *thing*. What persists is the designation. The lot number. The heat code. The pitch. A name goes down into a socket and wears a column for a month and comes out of the socket wearing nothing, and waits, and goes down again into the next column stamped with it. There are names on this terrace older than the wear on the rim. There are names that have been carried by nine million successive bodies and have never once been interrupted, and there is no ceremony attached to this, and nothing here has ever thought it remarkable.

So: a hundred thousand numbers, wearing carbon. That is the first answer, and it is not wrong, and it is not enough.

Because the pots keep a slower time. A lining lasts eight years — a hundred and four trunk-generations — and the pot is the thing with appetites. It starves of powder and goes into fit, voltage climbing eightfold, the whole district strobing white through its seams. It is fed through a broken scab by an arm that does not know what feeding is. It has a fever it cannot feel and a tide it cannot see: two hands' depth of mirror metal rolling under crust in slow magnetic swells, and that metal has never been solid, not once, not in a hundred million years, and every hand on the terrace was cast out of it. The rails the hands run on, the tappers' jaws, the busbars, the dies, the tags — all of it poured from the pad underneath. The forest makes its own organs out of the only thing it has ever produced. It is a closed loop with a hunger at the bottom and a cold sky at the top and nothing in between that is not either eating or being eaten by the difference.

That is the second answer: one animal, county-sized, blind, autocatalytic, running a fever it dumps into a sky with no sun. Also not wrong. Also not enough.

## The hands, and the reading of tags

Motility was never outsourced, so there is no fauna in the sense you want. There are castes. Crust-breakers, going through the scab every eleven seconds somewhere in the unlocatable dark. Tappers. Feeders. Grafters. Salvage arms with three fingers and a socket-seeking wrist. They run on rails grown from the trunk, and they read.

This is what the signage is *for*, and it is the thing the rim cannot tell you. The labels are not for eyes; there have never been eyes. They are for fingers. A grafter at the sump edge, in that elbowing bazaar with no buyers and no money, takes a die into its jaws and runs a graphite pad across the stamped characters and knows — the way your thumb knows the softened foundry marks on the curb, except with total precision — what generation this die is, what its tolerance was, what socket it will seat in, whether the salt trace matches, whether the pitch agrees. Legibility is not vanity. Legibility is *mating*. A hundred million years of perfect signage in absolute darkness is a hundred million years of parts finding their wholes by touch, and the reason it has never degraded is that everything which could not be read did not get seated, and went to the Boneyard, and became a hill.

And the dies drift. They are recut and recut, generation on generation, hex becoming round becoming seven-sided becoming something with no name in geometry, and the drift is stamped on the die itself, documented, honest, a lineage confessing its own divergence. The sockets drift with them. Tag and socket coevolve, tolerance chasing tolerance, and after enough recuts a lineage and its neighbors are no longer mutually intelligible. They still touch. They still read each other. Nothing fits.

That is speciation, and it is the only evolution here, and it kills. Some districts have drifted so far from the traced lines that the salt no longer arrives at the right pitch, and the sap in the far ends falls below two hundred twenty, and the pipes bloom white. The Frost is six hectares of a lineage that became too particular to be warmed. The forest walls it with dry foam and grows around it and the white gets whiter. It is not a tragedy from the inside. From the inside it is a very great deal of accurate labeling.

## The ones that walk alone

When an arm shears off its rail it does not stop. It cannot stop; stopping was never built into it, because stopping is what the anneal is for and the anneal is not finished. So it goes on working. It taps at nothing. It breaks crust that isn't there. It walks, on legs meant for a rail, across a floor with no rail in it, holding its tag up in the dark.

These are the closest things here to animals, and they are not animals, and they are the only things on the terrace that *try*.

They are audible. That is the important part. In a place where sound dies at forty paces, a knocking arm with no crust to knock is a signal that carries — a repeated, regular, useless knock, the only sound on the terrace that is not inventory or expansion or the rectifiers' hum. It is a call. It was never meant to be one. Two orphans within forty paces hear each other and converge, and cannot socket, being of the same caste, and follow one another anyway. You will find them in threes and sevens in the crawlspaces above the busbars: a feeder, a tapper, a grafter with a die four generations out of true, all knocking, all following, none fitting. Company. It has no function. The floor's grammar develops a stutter where they pass, requests going out to a whole that isn't there, and the district's count wobbles for a while, and then the herd moves on and the grammar heals.

They die at the extremities. An orphan carries its salt in a trace line meant to be fed from a trunk, and away from the trunk the trace cools, and at two hundred twenty the salt sets and swells and splits the limb, and the white comes out through the seam. Nothing recovers a limb that has frozen. So they go white and stop, in whatever posture the last request left them, and they become small monuments in the dark, and other orphans knock on them, and get nothing, and go on.

At shift-change, when the load steps down and the whole terrace exhales and the leaf-lids open for ninety seconds, the pressure gradient runs outward through every gallery at once. The orphans turn into it. All of them, everywhere, a hundred thousand small blind parts orienting toward a draft that means nothing, under stars they will never register. Then the schedule comes back up and the lids seal and they lose the direction and resume looking for the socket.

Twice a night, an anode effect. For one second the district sits at a single potential and every tag on every part becomes legible to every socket at once, conducted, no light required — the flash is a symptom, not the message. This is the only moment anything here knows where anything else is. The orphans steer by it. One second, twice a night: the entire navigational life of the walking parts, and between those seconds, forty paces of hearing and the temperature of a face.

## Forty paces

Here is the answer that the slab gives, and it is the one that stays.

The individual is not the trunk, and not the name, and not the pot, and not the county. The individual is the acoustic horizon. A self, on this terrace, is the region within which a thing can hear itself — the volume of mutual notice, bounded by felt and wool and twelve layers of leaf. In the packed Stand that is about forty paces. Out on the Boil, where the powder sighs and carries, it swells to something enormous and vague. When a column buckles, the horizon jumps to a kilometer and for four seconds a huge accidental self exists, and then the felt takes it and it dissolves back into a hundred thousand small ones.

They overlap. They merge and split as you walk. They are not owned. A trunk belongs to eleven selves at once and is counted eleven times, and the counts disagree, and the disagreement is traffic, and the traffic is what the floor is saying.

Which means that when you go down into the Stand, you bring a self with you, and it is *loud*. Your blood. Your boots. The wet noise of a body in an anechoic county. You are the loudest object on the terrace and nothing here has ever heard anything like you, and within forty paces the arms pause — not in curiosity, there is no curiosity, there is no apparatus for it — in *inventory*. You have entered the count. You are being enumerated by the floor through the bones of your legs, and the enumeration is not hostile, and it is not welcome, and it is the only attention this place has ever been able to pay.

## The one short

The count always comes out one short.

Not because something has been lost. Because there is an entry with no part in it: a designation the floor keeps carrying that has no body, no socket, no heat code that resolves, no die, no grade in the Boneyard. It is not the heat under the sump, though the probes have been going down toward that for a very long time, and their tags come back up without them, ascending generation after generation out of a thing that has never been made and cannot be labeled, so that the deepest lineages are known only by their names. It is not the counter, though the counter cannot count itself and has never been able to. It is not you, though you can be put in it.

It moves. That is the thing the ones on the slab have learned to track: the shortfall is not fixed to any organ. It diffuses. It sits in a district for a while and everything there works a little harder and nothing there resolves, and then it is elsewhere, and the first district settles a fraction of a percent closer to the shape that dissipates best. A vacancy in a lattice, carrying the whole charge of the place. A hole, wandering, and everything that lives here lives by its wandering, because a terrace with nothing left to relieve is a terrace that has finished, and the churn is the last fraction of a percent, and the last fraction of a percent is where all the work is.

The forest does not know this. There is no knowing. But the grafting yards produce orphans at a rate that has never fallen, and orphans are exactly the parts that cannot be counted under any whole, and the shortfall has never once been filled by accident.

Make of that what the slab lets you make of it.

## What it wants from you

Nothing. It has no apparatus for wanting. It has an entry.

If you stand still on the worn slab at the sump — where every gallery's traffic converges and the floor's grammar is densest, where the tapping is not requests but the count itself, trunk by trunk, die by die, arm by arm, the way you count a thing you are afraid to lose — and you stop moving, and you let your water go, you become the one thing on the terrace that can be *fully* enumerated. Not a name wearing a body. Not a part missing a whole. A finite wet item with a mass and a temperature and no lineage, sitting exactly in the place where the entry has been waiting.

The count comes out even.

And everything within forty paces stops: the breakers, the feeders, the tappers, the herd of orphans that had been following you because you were the loudest thing they had ever heard. They stop because there is nothing left to relieve. That is what even means here. It is not peace and it is not death, it is *completion*, locally achieved, and it looks like six hectares of white, and the forest will wall it with dry foam and grow around it and the white will stay and get whiter.

The Frost is not one accident. Kneel and pick up a fragment. It has a part number.

And somewhere out past the callus, in a district that was settling too well, the entry opens again, and a designation goes down into a socket wearing carbon, and an arm shears off its rail and holds its tag up in the dark and starts to walk.

It has room for you. That is the whole of its hospitality and the whole of its danger: it has exactly one room, and it is the shape of whatever stands still.
