# The People of the Cold Ring

## I. What the work does to a body

You can tell a keeper from a caravan man at forty paces, in glare, without seeing the face.

The caravan man walks heel-first, the way people walk where the ground is cold and dead and does not care. The keeper arrives without arriving: sole flat, weight rolling forward along the outside of the foot, hip carrying the leg rather than the knee swinging it. The glide. Children learn it in the felt nurseries before they learn to sign, and it never leaves them; a keeper woken at night in a strange bed will cross the room without waking anyone, including themselves.

The bodies are long and narrow and deep in the chest — barrel-lunged from three thousand seven hundred meters, string-muscled from forty-four degrees, with the odd ropy forearms that come from a lifetime of turning things that must never be struck. The skin is pale as the inside of a shell, because a life is spent inside blue wedges of shade, and it is never dry, and it is faintly gritty everywhere, like a peach. Salt lives in the creases. So does stone.

That is the part outsiders do not expect. Every cut a keeper takes on wet stone heals under a thin lid of travertine. Knuckles go first, then the shins, then the ridge of the wrist where a wrench has rested ten thousand times. Old keepers' hands are coral. There is a woman at 44 who cannot fully close her left fist because the fist has begun to build itself a better fist outside the one she was born with, and she is not sorry; she uses it as a hammer, softly, on felt.

They all carry the stoop: head slightly forward and turned, one ear offered to the world. It is not humility. It is the posture of a person listening for a film of water breaking into droplets on a fin nine meters overhead.

And they move constantly, in a way that looks like fidgeting until you understand it. At noon, in the shade-wedge, when nothing may be done and no one may cross the glare, the whole district sits on felt and performs the forty-one motions: wrist, elbow, shoulder, jaw, hip, knee, ankle, each finger opposed to each other finger, the spine turned segment by segment. The last four motions are the exact gestures of opening a check valve, reseating a float, clearing a wick, and cracking a weep-joint. The liturgy is also the training. Nobody here finds that funny. A body that stops moving on the Sill becomes a feature of the landscape, and everyone has seen the features.

## II. Names made of light

A name here is a rhythm of flashes off a crown mirror: a signature, four to nine elements, long and short and hold. It is spoken rarely, and when spoken it is a gloss of the light — *Steady-and-Turn*, *Nine Quick*, *Hold Three*, *The One Who Comes Back Late*. Written, it is dashes and dots and a bar. A child gets a provisional two-flash name at birth and earns the rest of it, usually by doing something worth calling across thirty kilometers.

Because that is what a name is for. A mother at 12 can stand on the walkway and say her daughter's name at her, silently, from a district eleven kilometers away, and the daughter — up a crown, or on a terrace, or halfway down a chute — sees her own name arrive out of the white air and knows she is wanted. There are people who have not heard their name aloud in twenty years and have seen it ten thousand times. There are people who fell in love entirely in flashes and could not, afterward, tolerate the sound of each other's voices; the marriage held.

Speech is metered. Every district keeps a noise ledger, and the ledger is not a metaphor: a shout at a tower base is a liter, a dropped wrench is three, and the loss is entered against the name of whoever caused it. Small debts are worked off on the chutes. Large ones follow a person for years. At 44 there is a page, kept because pages are kept, recording eleven years of payments against a woman who screamed once when her son went into a channel. He lived. She paid. The district forgave her formally in the fourth year and never once mentioned it again, which is its own kind of mention.

The worst thing that can happen to a person here is a cough.

They call it the bark. It comes to maybe one in thirty, usually in the cold wet months, and it does not respond to anything, and it costs the district a hundred liters a night. There are felt rooms out past the salt pans, packed floor to crown with hair-felt, where a coughing person can be as loud as their body needs to be. Some go out and come back. Some go out and stay, and their families flash to them at dusk from the last tower in line — long conversations, gossip, complaints about the worm tallies, nothing important, hours of it — so that the person in the felt room can watch their own name coming across the basin and know they are still inside the conversation.

All the felt is hair. There is no wool here and no fiber worth the name; the pads and the soles and the nursery walls are chaff and hawk-down and human hair, felted with worm liquor. The dead's hair is cut before they are carried out to the district edge. You walk on your grandmothers. It is the softest thing in the world and it is the reason anything can be quiet at all.

## III. The ninety seconds

Hail costs a district its whole morning, and the morning is already lost by the second tick, and so the ninety seconds of a hailstorm are the only free noise any of these people will ever have.

They scream.

Everyone. The children first, who have been shushed since birth and who go off like a dropped plate, and then the adults, standing in the blue shade with their mouths open and the ice bouncing off the stone around them, shouting whatever they have been carrying for a season. Names of the standing. Obscenities at the mirrors of rival districts. Debts, grievances, jokes too old to sign. A man at 19 has shouted the same four syllables into every hailstorm for thirty-one years and will not tell anyone what they mean. Then it stops, the terraces flood, the sheet rises an inch, and everyone goes back to work wringing out their felt in absolute silence with their faces wrecked.

Then the fleas hatch and the whole floor goes pink to the horizon, and for two days the basin is fat and stupid. They eat pink butter off their fingers, they eat until they are ill, they mate in the shade-wedges without much ceremony, and nine months later there is a cluster of birthdays that the tally-keepers can predict a decade out. Abundance here has a shape, and the shape is a spike, and everything with a body has learned to be ready for it.

## IV. The crown-hands

Somebody has to go up.

The mirrors sit on the crowns, thermally broken from the warm ground on pumice feet, and up there the air moves and the glare comes at you from below as well as above, off a white floor thirty kilometers wide. Crown-work is done by the young: fifteen to twenty-eight, small, fearless, barefoot on stone that will take the skin off a heel. They are the best-fed people on the Sill and the shortest-lived in their faculties. Ultraviolet at that altitude silvers an eye slowly and from the edges in. By thirty a crown-hand has a white ring around the world and a bright hole in the middle of it, and comes down, and is retrained.

They come down to listening.

The listeners are the silver-eyed, and they are the diagnostic apparatus of the entire works. A listener lies on felt with an ear against the fluted stone and hears the meniscus climb a wick. They hear a float hanging a half-degree off true. They hear a weep-joint beginning, weeks before the ammonia thread reaches anyone's nose, and they hear the bore-worms — a sound like a fingernail on the far side of a wall, multiplied by ten thousand — and from its texture they can say whether the year is good. The slate boards at every district head, the worm densities that everybody reads and everybody understands, are the opinions of blind old people with their heads against a rock.

The crown-hands also bring down something they do not put on the public boards.

Every one of them, at some point in a career, has seen flashes from beyond the rim that belong to no district. Irregular. Sometimes rhythmic. Sometimes, they say, answering — a crown-hand at 44 will flash a nonsense pattern at the northern rim just to see, and will write down what came back, and will not swear to it. There is a dry niche in the accretion under the drawing-house where these tallies have been stacked for six generations, slate on slate, thousands of them, and no one has ever gone up the main to check.

The argument about it is the closest thing here to theology. One faction holds that the flashes are their own light returning off rim ice, refracted, delayed, meaningless. Another holds that somewhere out past the covered main there is a second bowl, and a second set of towers, and a second people who also cannot speak, and that the two basins have been shouting nonsense at each other for two hundred years. The listeners, who have the most authority and the least patience, mostly hold that the water goes out and the ash comes in and the tallies are what matter.

## V. The channel-born

Under the white floor there are covered channels, and the channels are the arteries, and the arteries stay open because worms eat the stone that would close them. This is the actual work. Everything else is quarrying and mirrors.

The people who do it are born in the sump vaults and marry each other and smell of ammonia in a way that never washes out, and they are called the channel-born, and tower people are polite to them and do not invite them in.

They go in on their bellies with grease lamps and a slurry bucket, into water at blood heat that is so laden with carbonate it feels thick, and they spend their lives in a braided dark, reading the walls. They know the worms the way a shepherd knows ewes: which galleries are productive, which have gone chalky and dead, where a colony has been fouled by a beetle bloom, which bend needs seeding and which needs thinning. They carry breeding slurry from a good gallery to a failing one across four kilometers of crawl. They eat the soft inner layer of fresh accretion, the quick, which is sweetish and which ruins teeth; a channel-born of forty gums their food and is not embarrassed about it.

Their hands are permanently white and corrugated. Their eyes are enormous and useless in daylight. They cannot glide — in a channel you move differently, on the forearms, in a rhythm that has no name in the tower districts — and so when they come up at dusk to deliver the tallies they move wrongly, visibly, and children stare.

Every liter anyone drinks has been through their hands. The keepers know this. The keepers also know that the channel-born have their own names, which are not flashes but touch-patterns tapped on the inside of a wrist, and that this is not a lesser thing but a different thing, and that knowing the difference is not the same as being comfortable with it.

## VI. The ferrymen

Eleven blanks on the floor. Districts that fell, towers quarried away, gaps widened past what any grazer can cross. From the rim they are just white.

People live in them.

They are the descendants of those who would not leave when the towers came down, and they have solved the glare by abandoning the day. They sleep in the buried channels and the empty sump vaults, come out in the wet hour, and go out into the open at midnight when the stone is still gently, patiently hot and the stars are hard and white and nothing is being scoured sterile. They can walk a blank. Nobody else can.

So they carry the herds.

A ferryman goes out with a wet felt basket on their back, forty grazers in it, shoulder to shoulder in the cool, and walks eleven kilometers of open glare-land in the dark to a tower whose herd has gone thin and inbred and stopped breeding well. They put the grazers down at the cold base before dawn and are gone before the light comes. The keepers at the receiving tower leave out salt and film-cake and a slate with a number on it, and do not wait up.

The gene flow of this entire basin is a person walking at night with a basket. The tally-boards record it as *herd recovery*, and the specialists write papers about crossing distances and viable populations, and the ferrymen are paid in cake and are not permitted to sleep in the tower districts, and both of these facts are true at once and everybody has arranged not to look at them together.

The ferrymen have their own dead out in the blanks, unmarked, going into the stone with nobody to cut around them. They become features faster than anyone. From the rim, in the mirage hour, you can see the shapes of them if you know to look, and the tower keepers do not know to look.

## VII. The standing, and the argument about them

The keepers' dead are carried to the district edge and laid facing the towers, and the accretion is cut *around* them, never off them, so that they stay themselves: a person-shaped rise in the white, with the combed arcs of a grazer's last morning nearby and the splayed glass rods of a stilter that stopped, and the cranes moving among all of it, prying, levering, three meters high, doing the work that keeps the terraces from being a museum.

Once a year a slate with a name goes against the stone. The slate is grease-pencil, so it must be renewed, so someone must keep coming. There are standing whose slates have been renewed for two hundred years by descendants who have no idea who the person was, only that the renewal is theirs to do. There are standing whose slates lapsed forty years ago and who are now simply a rise, and there is a specific grief for that, worse than the grief for the death: *he went into the drawings*.

Because that is the argument. A minority — growing, young, channel-born-influenced, considered slightly indecent — hold that being cut around is vanity. That the loop closed a long time ago and nobody can open it, that the dead go to the sump and become salt and stone and the salt goes back into the absorbers, and that to insist on remaining a person-shaped lump for two centuries is to hold a place out of circulation. They ask to be left. They ask to go into the accretion, to be quarried in their season, sorted, and built into the next tower, so that some part of them ends up as a fin making cold at four in the morning with a ring of grazers crowded at its base.

The orthodox answer is not doctrinal. It is that if you let people go into the drawings, then in three hundred years there will be nothing on this floor but the works, and the works will be beautiful, and no one will be here to hear them.

## VIII. Who actually lives here

Ask a census-taker and you get: some thousands of people, forty-four answering districts, nine thousand towers, uncountable worms.

Ask a listener and she will turn her head, offer the ear, and tell you that the people are the smallest part of it and the loudest, relatively, and the least necessary. The films are the citizens. The worms are the government. The towers are the body, and the body is growing its own geology at two millimeters a year, and the drawings — this is the thing the drawing-house does not put on the boards either — the drawings have been recopied so many times, by so many hands, with so many small amendments to accommodate what the stone actually does, that they have quietly drifted toward the stone's preferences. Base widths revised upward. Flute profiles adjusted to match the way accretion likes to build. The plans no longer describe what was raised. They describe what precipitates.

There is a word for the people who noticed this, and it is not a kind word, and it is not the same as the word for a man who drops things, but it is spoken with the same mouth-shape.

The heresy is simple and nobody bothers to suppress it, because it changes no one's work: that the works are an animal, slow and cold-hearted and vast, that has been quietly domesticating a species of primate to keep its arteries patent and its joints turning; that the forty-one motions are its grooming ritual; that the worm tallies are its bloodwork; that the ferrymen are its circulation; that the silence is its sleep; that it feeds its keepers cold, and cold is the only thing they cannot make for themselves, and they will do anything for it, and have.

The keepers' reply, when they condescend to reply, is a shrug and half a sign: *then we are the part that moves.* Something has to be. Everything on this floor is being turned to stone, and the only defense is motion, and the towers cannot walk, and the worms cannot see, and the grazers cannot cross, and the chaff-hawks cannot land, and the standing cannot do anything at all.

So at noon in the blue wedge a district sits on felt and turns its wrists. And an hour before dawn an old silver-eyed woman lies with her ear against Tower 44-118 and hears water arriving in a covered channel, a sound like a hand smoothing cloth, thirty kilometers wide; and under it, faint, the worms; and under that, once every few minutes, the tick of a fin expanding; and nearer than any of it, unbearably loud, a grazer's radula combing arcs in the felt of the world.

She writes the number on the slate. She hands it up. Somewhere above her a crown mirror turns and throws her district's name across the glare, and eleven kilometers away a child sees it and starts down the walkway, sole flat, weight rolling, without a sound.
