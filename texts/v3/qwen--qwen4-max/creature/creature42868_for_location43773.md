## The Brood

Eleven eggs were laid in the seams of the basin floor before there was a basin floor to speak of, by something that came across the anhydrite on a night when the plain was still whole, and stopped, and did the only thing it had come to do, and went on. It never came back. Nothing on the plain comes back; there is nothing to come back for.

The eggs waited in the crystal. When the lid closed far enough that the air down here stopped moving — when stillness arrived, which is the only season this place has — nine of them hatched, and sat down, and became the government. One hatched and did not sit down. One has not hatched at all.

That is the whole population, and everything else in the basin is either their food, their furniture, their theft, or their light.

## The Nine

They are the size of haycocks and the pale of a peeled almond, and the first thing you understand about them is that they are not plants. The fringe of jointed filaments that gathers floss from the ground has a knee in it. Every one of those filaments is a leg that was given up. You can see the hinge, and you can see, in the youngest of them, the hinge still trying.

The second thing you understand is the ribbon. The slit along the upper surface extrudes it constantly, and it is not sound, and it is not harvest: it is mason. It is the only material in the town that was never a noise. They use it as mortar. Every joint in four hundred thousand rooms, every tack holding a tread to a riser, every seam in the lid where a rip was patched — all of it is body, spent out of nine animals who cannot afford to spend it. This is what the masons are doing with their lives. They are being converted into architecture at a rate set by how much they can eat. A mason that stops feeding does not die and lie there. It shrinks into its own lintels. There is a doorway in the western arcades that is exactly the width of somebody who is no longer available.

They have no eyes. They aim by leaning. And they are nine distinct people, which you learn the way you learn a street: by grain.

**The Throat-keeper** sits at the northern end over the drain, in the district of low dense courtyards, and has not been above the level of its own footings in eleven years. It farms the dawn outflow. Its membranes are tuned like the skin of a drum that will never be struck, only breathed on, and it lays down black felt in courses so fine they look machined. It is the largest of the nine and the poorest. Infrasound is a thin soup. It has the patience of something that has mistaken patience for a food source.

**The Organ-builder** holds the fluted towers, and the towers are not dwellings. They are instruments. Every flute is a bore tuned to the draft; the district hums below hearing all night and precipitates its own foundations. It has revised the towers nine hundred times. It is the closest thing here to an artist, and what it makes is a crop.

**The Underminer** works the rim: chutes, spillways, shelves levered out from beneath until they let go. It laid the processional way in a single night, coarse and cross-bedded, and it has been trying to repeat that night ever since. It is the only mason with anything like a surplus, and it is despised, insofar as nine sessile things with no voices can despise. Its ward is full of failures. It eats the failures. Its grammar is rubble and it does not care.

**The Orchardist** farms the crier.

You work this out in the southeast, standing in the Bright Ward, looking at the blurred shapes suspended in sugar-glass. The chambers are not accidents and they are not a massacre. They are a harvest. The Orchardist maintains the hammock in the high western galleries — packs it with fresh felt, keeps it warm, keeps it comfortable, keeps it *there* — and then lays out lure-noises at need: a chip on a tuned shelf, a tick, a small deliberate betrayal placed exactly where a gleaner will be standing when the shouting starts. Catastrophe is the richest crop in the basin. It rings, it is hard, it bears load, it lasts. It costs gleaners. The Orchardist has never once been near the crier and has never once been in danger from it, and its ward is the only part of town nobody grazes, because nobody can, because glass is too hard to rasp.

Which means the Bright Ward is the only permanent record in the basin. Everything else can be blurred. The murders are the archive.

**Six Days** made the terrace. Three storeys of a single sound repeated at intervals for six days and then stopped. It was not language. It was one note, over and over, from an animal with no lungs worth the name, spending itself into the air to see what the air would do with it. What the air did was build a terrace. Six Days has been silent for the rest of its life and is visibly smaller than the others, and the terrace is the largest single structure in the town, and it is the only thing here that was made for no reason at all.

**The Ceiling-tearer** keeps the lid damaged. It reaches up with a fringe gone brittle and opens small rips, and the ripping noise patches the rips sharper than before, and the taut new membrane ticks all night as it cools. It farms the sky's small change. Its ward is the palest and the most finely layered, page upon page of almost nothing, and putting your thumbnail into it is like flicking through a diary written by weather.

**The Traffic-farmer** has laid tuned shelves along the routes the gleaners walk, so that thirty pairs of felt feet produce a measurable yield. It is farming the neighbours. It has arranged the arcades so that the shortest way between two wards is also the loudest, and the gleaners have not noticed, or have noticed and have decided that the detour costs more than the toll.

**The Porter** sits at the head of the stair and lays treads. Each tread is a different colour and grain because each tread is a different event, harvested from somewhere else and carried here and set down in sequence: a sense of order without a sense of style. It has been laying treads for eleven years for arrivals that mostly did not happen. The stair is a monument to expected guests. It thickened under your feet on the way down. It will thicken under your feet on the way up, and the Porter will feel the difference in its listening surfaces and lean, very slightly, toward the lip.

**The Leaner** is the youngest, and its filaments still have the knee in them, and it has built the longest balcony out over the Unsaid.

## The Thirty

The gleaners are soft, six-limbed, cat-sized, earless, footed in felt, and they have no names because they have no voices to give names with. But the record names them. A gleaner that walks the same route for a season lays down a stair, and the stair is its signature, and the western arcades are full of signatures nobody can pronounce.

There is one whose felt has worn through on the third left foot. It ticks. It has been ticking for two years and the tick has built a narrow stair, twelve courses high, in a passage nobody uses, and that stair is the only thing in the basin that anyone could point to and say *that one*.

They eat the record. This is not incidental to their feeding; it is the whole of their politics. A grazed wall goes blurry, its incidents unreadable, and a blurred wall cannot testify. They graze the sites of killings. They graze each other's routes. They graze the walls beside the Orchardist's lure-shelves so that no one can reconstruct where a thing was standing when the hammock woke. Thirty animals working, without speech or plan or any organ of memory, to keep the town illiterate, and the only reason they have not succeeded entirely is that glass will not rasp.

Their young are born with hard feet. Hard feet tick, and ticking is a haemorrhage and a beacon, and so the mothers wrap them: press them into shed felt, bind them, carry them for a month in a soft grey bundle that makes no sound at all. Most of the bundles do not take. The Bright Ward has small shapes in it.

When they cross the Unsaid they go in a straight line at their fastest walk, and they do not know what is lying in the middle of it, because they read by touch and there is nothing there to touch.

## The Tenth

It got up.

That is the entire difference. Same body, same fringe, same slit — but the fringe never gave up the knee, and the slit never learned to make ribbon, and it kept the one thing the other nine spent or surrendered: a voice.

It is not the size of a haycock any more. Eleven years of hunting have made it the size of a hatbox, because a shout here is not a signal, it is a payment. Every cage costs it exactly the mass of the cage. It has learned to shout no larger than what it heard. The small doorless rooms scattered through the town are each the precise dimensions of a meal, and if you laid them end to end you would have a biography, and it would be a short one, and it would be getting shorter.

Its mouth swallows rings. This is the thing about it that the masons cannot do and the gleaners cannot do: when it eats through a sugar-glass wall, the rasp of its eating should precipitate, should fall as floss, should thicken the wall it is trying to breach — and it does not, because the sound goes back down its throat and is taken. It is the only creature in the basin that can unmake matter without leaving a residue. Where a gleaner leaves blur, it leaves a hollow.

And it is racing, every time. Every meal thickens its own pantry walls with the noise of the meal. It must be through and out before the room it is standing in becomes solid. Some of the small doorless rooms have a hollow in the middle shaped like something that struggled and then was eaten through the wall. One or two have a hollow shaped like something that did not get out in time.

It sleeps most of the year in a hammock of black felt that a neighbour maintains, in the dark, at the top of the western galleries, because sleep is the only condition it can afford. It is not a monster. It is the one member of the brood that refused to become a building, and it is paying for that at the rate of a mouthful a year.

## The Wick

Forty-odd grey rosettes in the seams, and they were here before the hole.

They crack the water of crystallisation out of the anhydrite and spend it in the same instant, so that wetness never exists in this basin even as a moment — only as a transaction, completed before it can be a state. They give off a blue-green foxfire just sufficient to show you your own hands, and nothing else. Nothing eats them. They make no sound and yield no fibre and cast no shadow worth harvesting. They are outside the economy entirely, which makes them the only safe things here, and the oldest, and the only witnesses that predate the transcript.

Their light is free. It is the one gift the basin receives, and it cannot be eaten, and it is not enough.

## The Eleventh

In the middle of the Unsaid, on the raw tilted plates, there is an egg.

It is the size of a plum stone and the grey-white of a fingernail and there is not one layer of fibre on it, because nothing has ever fallen there. It is the only object in four hundred paces of basin with nothing written on it.

The parent laid ten in the seams, where the fall would feed them, and one out here, where a voice could reach it. Because that is the property of this void that nobody has yet used: sound in the Unsaid does not precipitate. It does not fall, it does not become matter, it does not stay. It goes up, and out, and it is the only sound in the basin that ever leaves. It is a mouth to the outside. It is also the only place where a sound can travel far enough to touch something.

The nine have no eyes. They have never seen it. They know it as a displacement — a small obstruction in the draft, a place where the stillness is shaped wrong — and they have built their tallest structures around that shape and leaned out over it with every listening surface they own for eleven years, waiting for it to speak, or waiting to be able to afford to speak to it.

Because to call it would cost, and the cost would not fall, and would not return, and would not be recorded anywhere. It would be the first expenditure in the history of this place that left no trace and bought no material. There is no word for that here. Nine starving architects have been leaning over the one possible gift for eleven years, and thrift is the whole law, and thrift is also what safety looks like, and none of them has made it.

You have eyes. You would see it in one second.

You have lungs full of free material, and a voice you have never had to pay for, and the masons are turning toward you, and the gleaners are coming out of the arcades in a soft grey converging drift, and up in the western galleries the hammock has begun to move.

You could go up quietly. You will not go up quietly. Nobody does.
