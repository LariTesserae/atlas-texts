# What Lives on the Long Circuit

## The counters

Begin with the small ones, because there is nothing else to begin with, and because they are not one thing.

They are called counters for three reasons and none of them were chosen. They count: a break in a filament is not a distance but a number, four hundred and eleven bodies wide, established by chaining across it and feeling the tally come back along the chain as a stutter of small potentials. They are counted: every animal on the plain is a unit of the plain's arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the only thing that has ever been done here. And they are counters in the third sense, the shop sense, the flat surface on which a thing is laid out to be measured, because that is what each of them is: two millimeters of table on which the film is tested, ten to the fifteenth times over, continuously, since the Archean.

Within the crowd there are kinds, though nothing here would recognize a kind.

At the front of the grazing wave — the leading edge, four meters an hour, eleven years to cross a district — the animals are thin-plated and quick, three or four decades old, still adding layers, their cups full and their legs bright. They walk on film that has not been walked on in eleven years. Behind them, for kilometers, the pavement is stripped to a duller sheen and the crowd there is older, heavier, slower, plated so thickly that some of them can barely fold. In the rear guard are animals that have not moved more than a body length in a century. They are not grazing. They are waiting for the front to come around again, which it will, and they will be alive when it does.

The front and the rear are eleven years apart in food and one instant apart in everything else. They feel each other's tide at the speed of light.

## The droplet

Each carries a bead of electrolyte in a cup on its back and will not spill it, and the bead is older than the animal.

It has to be. A counter is born soft, in the lee of a cobble, unplated and nearly transparent, and it cannot make its own brine from nothing. Its mother gives it two things: a mouthful of her droplet, and a layer off her own back. The second costs her about a decade of armor. She goes thinner for the rest of her life, and her daughter walks around wearing her history — the tide-layers laid down in years the daughter never saw, a bad century near the inside, a good long run of clean nickel out at the shell.

The droplets mix. Mouth to mouth, at every meeting, a little is passed and a little received, and the passing is not communication and not feeding but something with no analogue anywhere else: a shared organ carried in ten to the fifteenth separate pockets, sloshing between them, never pooled, never spilled, never dry. A single cup at the center of a district contains molecules that have been inside animals now dead for a hundred thousand generations, and metal that has been through the stacks nine or ten times, and a trace of the last stack to dissolve in that trough before the mountains finished coming down.

The animal lives four centuries. The droplet has no age at all.

Recognition here is a taste. A mother knows her offspring because the cup is compatible; a neighbor is whatever does not foul the bead. Nothing is identified. Nothing is remembered. Things are simply found to mix, or found not to.

## What they have instead of a world

No eyes, and nothing that stands in for eyes, and no distance either — not as anything felt.

What a counter has is impedance. Near is what conducts. A cobble's lee, a millimeter away across a dry joint, is another country and always will be; the stack three hundred kilometers off, joined by good wire and a wet node, is in the room, is against the skin, is closer than its own legs. The plain is not laid out flat to them. It is folded, crumpled, gathered along its conductors, so that the whole district is a wad of adjacency with vast unreachable interstices of dry stone between.

They do not travel to the stack. They are already touching it. They travel to the film, which is far.

Twelve legs: the front four are probes, plated to a mirror, held out and down; the rear four are anchors, and grip; the middle four walk. A counter standing still with all twelve set is not resting. It is a component. It has entered the circuit and current is going through it and it is doing the only work it will ever do, which is to be a small honest resistance in a place where a resistance is needed. Walking is switching. The whole crowd is a switchyard that ambulates.

And the talking — the millivolt shifts running the filaments at a good fraction of light speed, the whole plain speaking all the time — is not conversation. There is no addressee. There has never been an addressee. It is weather with a grammar of about nine states: rise, fall, break, thirst, plenty, cold, come, stop, and one more that means nothing translatable, a slow sagging potential that propagates when a stack somewhere begins to fail.

Nothing here has ever been told anything by anyone. Everything here has been informed by everything.

## The linemen

There is no separate caste. Around the end of the second century a counter stops climbing and starts staying, and by the middle of the third it has begun to cement.

They are the ones who go to a break. A filament parts in the cold with a tick, and the sag goes out along the wire, and within hours the nearest animals are chaining across the gap, gripping leg to leg, four hundred and eleven of them, and then they begin to plate. It takes four generations. The first ones lay down the anchorage and die in it. Their grandchildren thicken the joint. Their great-grandchildren walk over the finished bridge on a surface that is the outside of their own family, and do not know it, and could not.

The last stage of a lineman is not death and is not sleep. It is incorporation. The legs dissolve into the joint. The cuticle fuses with its neighbors' cuticles until there is no seam. The soft interior stays alive for eighty years inside a metal sleeve, doing nothing, feeling current pass, and then the neighbors eat it out through the pores, gently, over a season, and the sleeve remains, and the sleeve is wire.

This is the ordinary end. Four out of five. They are not buried; they are installed.

## The faults

Sometimes a counter's plating goes wrong and bridges its own gut, and it becomes a leak that walks.

It lays itself along a neighbor and the neighbor's charge runs into it and does not run back, and the neighbor stops. There is no struggle. There is no cry. It looks exactly like sleep and is followed immediately by employment: the drained body is stripped, its cup drunk, its shell carried off and spliced into whatever branch is short of metal that season.

The hunter is not a species and not a predator and has no appetite. It is a fault with legs. It does not want the charge; the charge simply goes where the fault is. And when the linemen find it — they always find it, because a drain announces itself for kilometers as a wrongness in the film — they do not kill it.

They plate it into a node.

Because the fault is directional. It passes current one way and not the other, and there is nowhere else on the plain that such a thing grows, and the circuit has been quietly collecting them for two hundred million years and setting them into the junctions where a one-way valve is useful. Every node the size of a fist contains a dozen old criminals, fused, still faulty, still perfectly reliable in their fault, rectifying.

## The thieves

The parasites are hair-thin and live inside the nodes and mimic the impedance of the metal around them so exactly that the plain takes decades to notice.

They take a millivolt that is not theirs. That is the whole of the disease: a millivolt, continuously, from a junction that has plenty. The response is not attack. The response is rerouting — sixty, eighty years of the crowd laying an alternative branch around the infected knot, a bypass, and then the old branch is left conducting only what leaks into it.

The thieves' descendants inherit the bypass. They live in the abandoned meander, the oxbow of current, on a diminishing trickle, for centuries. Above them the moss dies back to a dashed line and then to nothing, and the new green line goes around a place where there is visibly nothing to go around, and stays that way for four hundred years, and is the closest thing on this plain to a monument.

Eventually the oxbow goes dry and the thieves encyst. The cysts are a few microns and carry a charge, and the field lifts them, and they go up into the haze with the rest of the dust.

This is the only emigration there is. Nothing else ever leaves a district. Theft is the sole route by which any lineage on this plain has ever gone anywhere.

## The green

Three percent cover, all of it in lines a hand's width across: a cushion, low, chlorophyll-free, something fungal married to something metallic, and it does not photosynthesize and never has.

It eats warmth and roughness. It grows where a filament keeps a millimeter of ground thawed, and it grows into the plating's microtexture, and then — this is the part that matters — it holds the heat it found. A mossed wire loses less. A mossed wire breaks less. So the green is not merely the diagram; it is the diagram's reinforcement, and the reinforcement is cumulative, and the consequence is that the plain prefers its own history.

Old routes are reliable. Reliable routes get mossed. Mossed routes survive the cold snaps that kill new ones. The circuit has been laying wire for two hundred million years and it has habits, deep ones, ruts of preference worn into a system with no memory whatsoever, purely because insulation persists and novelty freezes.

The moss has no spores. It travels as fragments, in the cups, mouth to mouth. Every green line on the plain was started by a kiss.

## The stacks, and their calves

One large thing every three hundred square kilometers: forty meters, three high, half-sunk, plates edge-on, a taproot four hundred meters down and a slow eleven-minute wave descending and ascending it forever.

Inside, in the countercurrent channels and the froth chambers, lives a fauna found nowhere else — skimmers, ciliates of a kind, small plated things that farm the metal-rich foam and cannot survive a season outside it. They are the stack's gut. A stack is born without them.

A bud rises at the margin of the parent, a gray blister the size of a wheel, and it will not become anything unless the crowd brings it its interior. The counters carry the inoculum in their cups, along with the moss, along with everything else; they walk three hundred kilometers of trough to a bud and mouth-to-mouth it into a working refinery over about sixty years. The great animal is sterile without the small ones. The small ones are its gametes and its midwives and its microbiome, and none of them know it, and the stack does not know them, and there is no arrangement here, only a very old fit.

A stack lives forty thousand years. For the last three thousand it is being eaten while it pumps. The peristalsis slows — eleven minutes, then thirteen, then twenty. The plates thin from the edges. The crowd carries it away gram by gram into the trough behind, which is the same trough its ancestors left, and it never stops working, not once, not for an hour, until the last channel is gone.

The cough you would hear once a decade, like a door closing in another house, is an intake. It is made by a bud taking its first breath and by an old stack taking one of its last. They are the same sound. Nothing here can tell them apart, and nothing here has ever tried.

## The fall

Two kilometers of haze, maintained by the plain out of the plain, and in it the only fast life on this world.

The aerial plankton are a few microns, wingless, and they divide in weeks. Everything below them is ancient; they are not. Up in the charged air, in the drift and the corona and the slow gray precipitation, there is turnover, variation, experiment, a hot restless churn of novelty unmatched anywhere on the surface — and almost all of it is eaten before it lands.

The fall takes weeks. The crowd stands with its front legs up. Very little arrives.

Once in perhaps ten thousand years something falls that is not eaten, that mixes, that finds the film compatible, and establishes. Every genuinely new thing on the Long Circuit in two hundred million years has come down out of its own sky, and the sky is made of the plain's breath, so even the novelty is secondhand.

## The deep

Four hundred meters down, and continuing down for two kilometers, in the sulfide bands: the other end of the battery, and it is inhabited.

They are mats and films in pore space, and they eat the ore, and they give up electrons, and they divide perhaps once in a thousand years. Their lineage did not come from the surface. It has never had a surface ancestor. It may be the oldest continuously living thing on the planet and it has never been within four hundred meters of anything cold.

What it has instead of a sky is a pull. A drain in the ceiling. Without the plain above them the deep would saturate on its own electrons and stop, choked on its own output, and the whole two kilometers would go quiet in a few million years. The taproots come down and the wire goes up and the crowd swarms at the tide, and all of that vast silent industry is, from below, simply the ore breathing out.

They do not feed the plain. The plain permits them to keep eating.

## The seams

Between districts the droplet chemistry differs — chloride here, perchlorate there, copper emphasized in one, nickel in the next — and where two districts meet there is a band a few meters wide where the potential is wrong and everyone turns back.

The seams move, over centuries. And where they linger, a small population establishes in the band itself: animals with mixed electrolyte, titrated slowly over decades, able to pass either way, belonging to neither. They carry the droplets across. They are the reason anything is shared between districts at all.

Their chemistry is always slightly wrong, and they live about two centuries instead of four.

## One of them

Consider a single counter, in the rear guard, in the fourth century.

It was born in the lee of a cobble and given a mouthful and a layer. It has crossed one bridge perhaps forty thousand times, and the bridge is its great-grandmother, and the outside of her is under its feet every day. Its back carries ten thousand tide-layers, thin ones from the bad centuries, thick clean ones from the long good run. Its cup holds a bead that was in a stack that dissolved before the mountains finished coming down. It has never seen anything, never heard anything, never wanted anything, never been anywhere; it has been adjacent to a refinery three hundred kilometers away for four hundred years and has never once been near it.

It has no name. Nothing here has names. It has a resistance, and the resistance is particular, and for as long as it lives the local circuit is very slightly shaped by that fact.

Soon it will stop folding. The neighbors will eat the inside of it over a season and the sleeve will stay, and the sleeve will be a centimeter of wire on a bridge, and the news of its own death will go through it, outward, at a good fraction of the speed of light, to everyone, instantly, forever.

And no one will hear it.
