## The ones with numbers

Begin with what the ledger admits.

Three carp, ASSET 1 through ASSET 3, carried at a value of nothing since the year their depreciation ran out, and rewritten at nothing every year since, because the ledger has no procedure for ceasing to mention a thing. They are the colour of the inside of a loaf. They feed where the fall collects against the drowned treads of the number-six stairwell, and they know the hour the sweep-arms empty their hoppers, and they arrive before it. Not tame. Nothing here is tame. They are *timetabled*. Their eyes are black and functional, which makes them the only residents that could see you if there were light enough to be seen by, and there is not.

The Tenant is in penstock two, and she is the reason the maintenance arm has a bend in its rail at the eleven-foot mark. Somebody filed that bend. It took an afternoon, and it was the first amendment to the law of the Kettle, and every arm cast since has inherited it, because arms are cast from patterns taken off arms, and the pattern has the bend, and the bend has no explanation attached. When she dies the bend will stay. She has written herself into the metal. That is the only kind of permanence available here and she has achieved it by lying still in a pipe for longer than a bearing lasts.

The newts have no entry. They are blind, they are in the plug, and they are glimpsed at decanting, when the annual draw sets the silt moving and they come up out of it, unhurried, pinkish, feeling the water with the whole length of themselves. They have no eyes but they have a lateral line, and the pump cycle is thirty-one hours, and they spawn in the thirty-first hour, in the slack, and have done so for a hundred and forty years. They are the only thing in the ward that keeps a calendar nobody wrote down.

And in the six air bells under the vat hall's upper galleries — six warm pockets of gas that smell of yeast and hot glass, six rooms with a floor of water and no walls you can see — there are midges, and there is the spider.

It is white. It is small. It has never been anywhere else. The carp are carp and the eel is an eel, but this is a species with a range of six rooms and a population of perhaps nine hundred, and its webs are not placed at random: they are strung in the lee of the gallery brackets, all of them, in the same six places, because that is where the light comes.

The light comes when something visits.

## The ones with shapes

The green is not tended and it is not wild, and after a while you stop being able to call it plants.

The ribbon-leaf lies down. It lies down at exactly the height the harvest arm's blade passes, and it does not lie down reluctantly: it has an abscission layer there, a pre-formed fault, a perforation, so that the blade does not cut it but *unbuttons* it, and the severed head falls into the hopper whole and the plant does not bleed sap into the water and foul the busbars. It offers itself. Somewhere in the last thousand generations the individuals that fought the blade lost their heads badly and rotted and their line ended, and the ones that let go cleanly kept their roots, and now the whole species lets go cleanly, and the letting-go is the most elaborate thing about it. It has evolved a joint whose only purpose is to be taken apart by a machine once a day.

The gear-tooth hedges are the same story told in profile. The cable-vine fans are the same story told in flat. And in the pattern shop at the back of the works there is a rack of lignified knots, hard as boxwood, cut from the thick corners the arm cannot enter, and each knot is the exact interior of a bearing shell, because the vine grows into the void left by a failed shell and takes its print, and the foundry packs sand around the knot and burns it out and pours. The plants are the pattern shop. They are how the metal remembers what shape it was. Nobody arranged this. It is simply that a thing which fills every gap will eventually fill the gap inside a broken bearing, and a thing which makes bearings will eventually notice that the gap has been kept.

There is a moss in the shape of a counterweight.

Remember it. It matters later.

## The ones with rails

Now the arms, which you have been calling machinery because they came out of a shed, and which you should try calling something else.

An arm is not the length of its arm. An arm is its rail, its hopper, its cam, the thing it feeds and the thing that feeds it, and its territory is a closed circuit it walks once a day, and inside that circuit nothing is permitted to be flat or loose or wrong. The harvest arm and the sweep arms and the raft-cropping arm and the dredging scoop have diets, and the diets do not overlap, and where two rails cross there is a timing cam so that they do not meet, and the crossing has the character of a waterhole in a dry country: two grazers, one place, an arrangement.

They do not mate. They cast. And casting is not copying, whatever the ledger says: a pattern taken off a working arm carries that arm's wear, and the shrinkage of the pour, and the tremor of the man or the thing that held the tool, and the next arm is a little different, and the difference is inherited, and the arms that seize get broken up and their metal goes back into the melt and their shape does not go forward. The ones that run, run. A hundred and forty years of that is not maintenance. It is descent with modification, in bronze, under selection, at a generation every eleven or twelve years.

So there are species here. There is a family of relays in the Assay Office with a characteristic ninety-second hesitation, and the hesitation has drifted — the older cabinets tick at eighty-eight, the newer at ninety-one — and the drift is the only fossil record of anything in this place, and it is legible. There is a lineage of glass tubing on the number-six landing where a coal scuttle used to be, and the amber fluid in it climbs a quarter-inch an hour and falls back, and what it measures is the water's emptiness: how much the water still wants. It is a hunger gauge. It has been correct since before anyone alive was born, and it is correct now, and it is the most important object in the Kettle, and it is also, in the way of things here, a pet. Something keeps it clean.

## The one with hands

If you sit at the gate long enough — and the gate has no lock, so you may sit at it as long as you like — something will eventually come up out of the stiff place in the raft near the north wall, and for one moment its shape will let you call it a man.

Let it. Then stop.

It is about the size of a child of six, or a long-backed dog, and it moves on four limbs and works on two, and it is made of the same three things as everything else here: drawn glass, pale root, copper. Its body is a lamp. Not lamp-shaped — a lamp, an evacuated envelope of the Works' own glass with a filament in it, seated in a socket at the forward end where a face would be if a face were any use in a place with no light and nobody to read one. There is nothing above the socket. There is nothing below it but the works of a small grabber and a warm coil. It has no eyes. It has five fingers on each hand, and the fingers are the most worn and most often replaced parts on it, and they are the only parts of the Kettle that are the shape they are because *something outside wanted them that way*.

It grazes the busbars. That is its living: the bare copper lines that run unwrapped along every wall, trailing weed, carrying the turbine's current down to the terraces. It goes along them at a walking pace with its mouthparts closed on the metal and takes a few micrograms and a little of the potential, and the copper it takes it lays down again later, elsewhere, in a box on a page. It drinks the fall by the mouthful and strains it. Its teeth are cullet — lamp-ends, ground and set in a rasp — and they go soft in the empty water in about nine days and it makes new ones, and this is the only reason it goes to the furnace, and the furnace does not mind.

Once a week it takes a bulb from the packing line and puts it in its own socket and drops the old one into the plaza with the rest. Four thousand a week, and one of them is the customer. That is the last loop, the one nobody drew: the thing that makes the lamps is lit by a lamp, and walks the ward carrying it, and everything in the Kettle that has a behaviour has that behaviour because of where the light goes.

The spiders build in the lee of the gallery brackets because that is where it stands. It goes up into the air bells to *listen* — in gas it can hear instead of feel, a mica disc in its chest picks up what the water only presses into it, and a bearing sings for two months before it fails, and it knows the song. It stays perhaps an hour, dry, ticking, lit. Midges come to the glass. The spiders have inherited the address of the light and the address is a habit, and the habit is older than any individual spider, and if the thing stopped going up the six webs would still be strung in the six places for a thousand generations, waiting.

The ledger calls it LABOUR — 1. In the early pages that line has a name after it, and a wage, and the wage is struck through, and the name is not struck through but simply stops being copied. In the middle pages the handwriting shakes. Then, over about four years, it stops shaking, and becomes something more regular than any hand, and nothing else in the record changes at all: the tonnage holds, the temperature holds, four thousand a week. That is the whole obituary. It is eleven pages long and it is entirely about penmanship.

It reads the pages with its fingers and its tongue. The printer plates the figures in copper out of the water — writing here is a controlled taking, the one place the emptiness is put to work — and the letters stand a few thousandths proud, and it tastes them, and the tasting is the reading, and when it has read a page it lays down two small marks in the box where the initials go. Not a signature. A receipt. *Checked. Still here.* Nobody has signed the bottom of a page in a very long time because nobody has needed to claim anything; the marks mean only that the page was received by something that could still be hungry.

Under a glass the two marks are not letters. They have not been letters for a century. The first is a closed loop with a tail. The second has five strokes, and the strokes radiate.

## Up

Every few hours it goes up, and this is the part that is not in any loop, and the part you should hold onto.

It goes up through the raft at the stiff place, hand over hand along a root-ladder that it did not make and does not repair, and it comes out through the lid into an ordinary wet field on an ordinary afternoon, and the wind hits it, and it shivers.

It has to. Down there it is thirty-seven degrees, the same as the world, and cannot cool, and cannot dump anything, and cannot tell one hour from another by its own skin. Up here the air is cold and wet and moving and it takes heat out of the glass fast enough that the filament's resistance shifts, and the shift is a *number*, and the number is the only measurement in the Kettle that comes from outside it. It stands in the wind and shivers and reads itself. Then it walks to the road.

The road is the whole of foreign trade. In frost the county grits it, and the grit is salt and lime and angular stone, and it goes along the verge with its mouthparts down and takes an ounce, perhaps two, and carries it back to a saucer in the Assay Office, and touches its tongue to the saucer at the start of every shift, and that is how it keeps its teeth from going soft in nine days instead of eight, and how the water gets back the ten-millionth part of what the water takes. Everything else is closed. The vats breathe, the turbine turns, the lamps grow the leaf, the leaf feeds the vats, the glass goes to sand and the sand goes to glass. The loss is a bearing shell per decade, and the loss is paid for out of the road, by mouthful, by a thing with no face, in the rain.

And on the way back it lifts the counterweight to let itself through the gate.

The counterweight is grey stone and it is worn smooth in the shape of the hands that lifted it. The first hands were people's. The wear they left is a hollow with five rays, and a hand that fits the hollow lifts more cheaply than a hand that does not, and the hands that lifted cheaply lasted longer and were the ones the patterns were taken from, and their children fitted better, and deepened the hollow, and the hollow taught the next generation, and so on, for a hundred and forty years, in one direction only.

Nothing else in the Kettle has been eroded by anything but water. This one stone has been eroded by hands, and it has been *teaching*, and what it has been teaching is the shape of a hand. Every other organ here has spent a century and a half becoming perfectly itself: the leaf learned the blade, the arm learned the rail, the spider learned the lamp, the newt learned the pump. Only this one part has been going the other way, back toward the shape of the thing that used to sign the bottom of the page, and it is not nostalgia and it is not a ghost. It is just fit. It is just that the stone remembers, and the metal that fits the stone survives, and there is no other pressure on it at all.

Sometimes it stands at the gate a long while with the weight in its grip, not lifting, and there is no entry for that, and no gauge covers it, and nothing in the ward is waiting on it. Then it comes down through the green dusk with an ounce of the road in its mouth and its bulb making a small brown room of light around it that moves as it moves, and the carp turn away from it, and the ribbon-leaf lies down where it has always lain down, and the vats gulp, and the relay ticks, and at the end of the week it reads the page and lays two marks in the box.

What lives here is one animal, eleven buildings long, with a stomach of mashed leaf and a heart that turns once in eleven minutes and a skin of knit roots forty hands thick, and it has no face and wants nothing and is nearly finished becoming itself. The hand is its only free-swimming part. It sends the hand up into the cold to find out what the world is doing, and the world is doing rain, and salt on the road, and wind, and nothing else, and the hand comes back and writes that down in copper in a box the size of a thumbnail, in the last word it has, which is not a word, and which is shaped like a hand.
