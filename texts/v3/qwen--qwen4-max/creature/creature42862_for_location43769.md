Begin upstream, where the fog is made.

On the plateau there are meadows that have never heard of this place. Their midges rise in the evening and are taken by the wind that pours over the lip; their mites let go of a stem and are gone; their spiderlings tip their abdomens at the sky, spin a thread, and are lifted, and most of them come down somewhere else, and some of them come down here. Plumed seed. Scale-dust from a moth's wing, in drifts like pollen. Spores in such number that the fog has a taste, faintly yeasty, and the beasts know the taste of a good week from a bad one before the combs have dripped a liter. The deck is a pasture. The basin is a mouth. Almost nothing that lives on this floor is native to it; almost everything here is a thing that learned to eat the sky's leavings, or to eat something that did.

Some of the cargo is alive when it arrives. Some of it never arrives at all — there is a small pale midge that mates aloft, lays aloft, and dies aloft, and the ledger-keepers have argued for a century about whether it counts as an inhabitant. It has never been caught. It has only been eaten. In the Throat, where the flow narrows and quickens, spiders have strung the notch since before there was a town: gate-weavers, palm-sized, felted gray, whose sheets bell out with the current like sails. The town crops them. Silk for the finest felt, and flour from the scraped sheets — midge-flour, pressed into cakes, sweetish, the staple of every winter. It is all theft. There is a column for it in the ledgers, headed *the gate's share*, and it is subtracted from the herd's portion before anything else, and the arithmetic of the ninth mouth begins there.

---

The standing are the mouth's tongue.

Thirty meters, blind, slate-colored, furred with a nap that beads condensate and sheds it in a slow rain of their own making. They know the basin as a texture-map: the cold seam of the melt-slot, the warm dead air behind a boulder, the rich rope of the Throat's current where the midge runs thick, the thin bitter lanes downstream of each other. They have no eyes worth the name and do not need them; the fog has banks and eddies and a season, and a beast that has stood here two hundred years has that season in its body the way a river has its bed.

They are not interchangeable, whatever the numbers suggest. Third has a lightning of pale scar down the left flank where a slab fell from the rim in the year of the bird. Fifth carries the garden quarter and has a manner of settling her weight before dawn that the back-born read as a warning of weather. Sixth is the leaner, the red-banded ponds, the one that stands nearest the Throat and takes the first of everything. The Calf is eighty years old and still called the Calf, and its shrimp are still its mother's, and it is thin, because a young beast must feed in the lee of the one that bore it and there is nothing there to eat. The mother knows this. She has begun to stand aside — to hold a rank she should not hold, angled off the current, giving her young one the clean water and taking the exhausted air herself. It costs her perhaps four kilos a season. The ledger-keepers have written it down. The gap-guild has quietly re-measured that side of the basin twice. Nobody has proposed a rule about it, because there is no rule that would not be a cruelty, and because everyone can see what it is.

Their ears are the town's instruments: furled along the flanks in dry weather, opened in rain into twelve feet of trembling velvet, and there are children paid in dried shrimp to sit on the ledges and watch the ears and report the first unfurling. Their antler-crop is harvested by saw, over weeks, by the hushed, and the beasts permit it and shift their weight and permit it. And their breath, once a minute, a sigh you feel in the sternum from a hundred paces, leaves no seam at all. The wardens have argued about this for three hundred years and the potters have the best answer, which is that a seam is made by an edge, and a sigh has no edge; the slow have been in the middle of the same breath since before the floor dropped, and a thing that has not finished cannot be attended.

---

All the soil in the world is on their backs, and it is forty centimeters deep, and it is entirely made of dung and dead midge and shed fuzz and the felted corpses of ten thousand generations of moss-grazers. Eight islands. Eight ponds behind eight pairs of shoulder blades, callus-lined, two thousand liters each, iodine-smelling, leaf-choked.

The shrimp are the famous part, and the ledgers track them like heraldry: Third's pale and blind, Sixth's banded red, the Calf's still its mother's, and a new thing in the water within a year of any spill and merge. But the better clock is a fly. Wingless, thumbnail, it lives its entire life on one hide and cannot cross bare rock; it drinks at the pond's rim and eats the crust at the waterline. It migrates only when two beasts touch. Its family tree is therefore an exact record of every touching since the basin was made, including the ones nobody wrote down, and there is a woman in Eight who can hold a fly against her palm and tell you which hill your grandmother was born on.

Which makes falsification a crime. To carry shrimp or flies from one pond to another in the night, in a wet gourd, is to forge time — to manufacture an old right, a prior claim, a marriage entitlement. It is the only offense for which the town has ever exiled anyone, and exile is a rope up an overhang.

And when two beasts root, the islands become a peninsula. Everything crosses. Everything competes. Within four years there are three fewer kinds of snail in the world and no one has ever seen the last individual of any of them. The ledger-keepers keep that list too, on the reverse of the shrimp pages, and it is longer than the shrimp pages, and it is read aloud only in rain.

---

The floor is not empty. The floor is a crowd photographed from too far away.

Mat-crabs the size of coins, grazing at night in patterns visible from the ledges as pale frost-tracks. Thumbnail snails in drifts like spilled grain. Springtails so thick that a handful of moss held over your palm rains white for a full minute. Mites in eleven kinds, of which four are predators and one rides the fringe of a filter-mouth and eats what the fringe catches, and is therefore, technically, a parasite of a god. Fungus threading the black soil on the backs and the raw rock beneath, stitching the two together, moving nitrogen downhill.

And in the old seams — the ones nobody has walked through in a decade, the shout-ribbons and dropped-pot knots hanging in the still air off the lanes — a lichen has learned to grow. It colonizes the hum. It takes the shape of what it grows in. There is a hanging thicket at the north end of the plaza that is unmistakably the shape of a woman calling two syllables, and it has been there since before anyone's grandmother, and the moss under it is greener than the moss anywhere else, and the rain dissolves the seam and the lichen falls in a night and the shape is gone. The town has lost more of its history this way than to fire or flood. There is a guild that copies the shapes onto hide before the wet season, badly, from memory, at a distance.

---

The three hills at the edge are the old town and the town's larder and the town's graveyard, and they are corpses.

Two beasts, leaned together with felt-tipped poles over the course of a rain, legs stiffening, hide lignifying, mouths closing, ponds spilling into one another; forty years of dying at the pace of a forest. Now: terraces, stairs, orchards, ribs showing through turf like the rafters of a buried hall. A skull hollowed into a cistern that holds nine thousand liters and never goes stale, because something in the bone still filters, and no one knows what, and no one has cut it open to find out.

People are buried in the flanks, in the deep made-soil, and the fungus that eats lignified hide takes them in about nine years and glows very faintly while it does, a green quite different from the shrimp-green, and the back-born will not use it in a lamp. Every plank in Eight comes out of these three bodies. Wood is rationed by weight and inherited by name; a family's standing is measured in rafters. There is a beetle in the third hill that lives nowhere else and cannot be got rid of, and the joiners have simply learned to design around its holes.

---

Two hundred and thirty people, and two kinds.

The rock-born walk the scree and know the cups. The back-born are born on Fifth or Third, in a hammock under a hobble-birch, and grow up in a country that tilts a degree and settles, and have soft soles and cannot stand on the floor for more than an hour without their feet bleeding, and consider the ground a place you visit for funerals. Their cant is smaller than the ground cant — wrists tighter, signs made in the space of a hammock — and the two dialects mistranslate each other constantly in ways that are funny to everyone but the parties involved. A marriage between a back-born and a rock-born is a negotiation between garden-rights and cup-rights, and takes years, and the cups usually win.

The potters work underground, in sunk kilns under wet-clay damper-lids, entirely by feel, and their eyes go useless by forty and their hands become the most valuable objects in the basin. The sound of the trade is buried with the pots. There is a hum under the kiln quarter that has been accumulating for two centuries, and you do not dig there, and the wardens' maps of that district are the most detailed documents the town possesses.

The rain-warden sits roped on the rim with her hands inside her sleeves and reads the underside of the deck. She has dropped the red flag late once. A boy kept talking into the tail. She is still the only person who can read the deck, so she still sits there, and the town has decided, without ever discussing it, that this is what justice looks like.

The midwives time births to weather where they can, and where they cannot, a child waits. There is a list of the unshouted: infants born in a dry month, dead in a dry month, whose names are written and have never been said aloud. At the first crack of any rain, before the drums, before the market, before the arguments, someone stands in the plaza and screams that list. It is the only thing that always goes first. It has gone first for three hundred years. The list is sometimes empty and sometimes not.

And grief waits for weather. A woman whose mother died in the sixth dry week has been carrying a scream for forty days, and you can see it in how she holds her jaw, and there is nothing to be done, and everyone brings her food and says nothing, expertly.

---

The hushed do the loud work, and something else.

What was taken from them was not the body. They can strike a drum and nothing comes out of it. Their laughter is a shape in the air with nothing inside. They quarry, they break rock, they haul, they saw antler, and their cant is the largest and fastest and most obscene in town — whole-arm, thrown wide, invented by people who had nothing left to lose by gesturing — and half the vocabulary for feeling in Eight was made by the hushed and is considered vulgar and is used by everybody.

They are followed. This is the part that outsiders would not believe. The Attending come to an empty seam and find nothing in it and stay, and so a hushed man walks to the quarry with four or six or eleven black shapes moving politely along behind him at a distance of ten paces, and children count them to guess how long he has been hushed, and he does not turn around, ever, because there is no point.

And because they can scream forever and nothing comes, people go to them. In a dry month, when the grief will not wait any longer, you go to a hushed man you are related to or friendly with, and you sign it out to him, all of it, and he stands in front of you and opens his mouth and screams your grief with his whole body, veins standing, spit flying, in absolute silence, while the Attending gather and wait and get nothing. It is a service. It is not paid for. It is the only unguarded gesture available in this world and it is given away for free.

The free — the deaf, the wardens, the ones who read the marks and route the lanes and sleep sound beside a wound in the air — are mostly born. A few are made. There is a way to make one, and it is done to volunteers, and in the bad century it was done to others, and the pads from that century are small and close together and there are far too many of them and then there are none for a long time.

---

The Attending are the only fast thing here, and they have been standing still since before the town had a name.

Four hundred at the First Knock, black and felted, many-limbed, faces like folded hands, moss on the shoulders, ferns in the joints, hobble-birch seedlings rooted in the dust of their backs. Some say they rode the slab down. Some say they came in through the Throat in the first fog, in numbers, and walked to the middle and stopped. The count is taken from the ledges every dry week and posted beside the shrimp pages, and when the number rises the town gets quieter without anybody saying so.

Understand what they wait for. Not the noise. The *maker*. The First Knock finished three hundred years ago and they are not attending the seam; they are attending the floor, which is the thing that made it, and which might make something else. There are eleven of them along the melt-slot, facing the water, waiting for the stream to finish; in the bad century the stream stopped for eleven days and the ledgers for those days are a single line.

They come to a person because a person is a small floor: a thing that can break silence. What they take is the capacity, the source, the ability to end a quiet. Whether they eat it or store it or simply hold it is the town's oldest argument. The ledger-keepers say they eat the ending. The potters say they are cold, and that sound is the only warmth in this basin, and that somewhere in the cliff there is a place that is warm. The children say they are the audience and we are the play, and that the play is very bad, and that they are waiting for it to improve. The free, who have the most data and no ears, say only that the Attending face the maker and not the mark, and that this is a fact, and that the rest is religion.

Their shoulders carry the only fixed orchards in Eight, because everything else in town is on skids. The fruit is called rain-plum and is eaten only in the wet hour, and children dare each other to eat it dry, and a hushed girl did once, on a bet, and bit, and something came out of the plum that had gone into it years before, and the Attending came and stood around her and got nothing, and that afternoon is the town's single best piece of evidence about anything.

---

Rain, and the world is let off its leash for ninety minutes.

Everything that has been saved up all season is spent at once: the market, the lawsuits, the songs with no melody because melody can only be practiced in weather, the marriages, the shouting of names, the unshouted list first. A year of speech compressed into an hour and a half, badly organized, everyone hoarse, everyone weeping, the gap-guild cutting and re-rigging a season of bridges in an afternoon. And then the flag comes down the wire and the town stops mid-gesture, a drum with the stick raised, and the last thirty seconds are called the hem, and every generation loses someone who kept talking into it.

Rooting takes four hours. Rain averages ninety minutes.

So a rooting is only attempted when the deck promises the long wet, the six-hour kind, and sometimes the deck lies, and then the last hour is done in absolute silence, with felt-tipped poles, two hundred and thirty people pushing two gods into each other with their mouths shut and their faces streaming, and the beasts beginning to stiffen, and the Attending coming out of the walls to stand at the edge of the crowd in numbers nobody has ever counted, waiting to see which of these makers is about to finish. Someone dies at every rooting. There has never been a rooting at which someone did not die.

There is a fourth hill due. The Calf is thin and there is a ninth coming and the fog will only carry so many mouths, and the ledgers have already worked out which two, and it is Third and Sixth, who have been leaning toward each other for eleven years. The gap-guild cuts more bridges on that side than on any other. Everyone knows. Nobody writes it down. There was a season when a rain ended early and the two of them stood a hand's breadth apart for eleven days, and the guild sat between them with hooked knives, in shifts, and Sixth kept leaning, and on the twelfth day it rained again and the guild stepped back.

The town will call it arithmetic. It will also, in the only way it has, be doing them a kindness. Both things are true and the cant has no sign that holds them together, so people make two signs, one after the other, quickly, and look away.

---

At night the deck comes down and touches the groves and everything drips. Glimpse-jars flare cold green against a palm for three seconds and go dark. The lanes are lit sparsely by old seams and avoided. Eight vast shapes sigh once a minute. The mat-crabs write their frost on the moss. In the plaza the moss gains a millimeter over four hundred patient black shoulders, and sometime before morning one of them will not be there.

And at first light a hushed man walks the telling-lane with the town's one unowned cup.

It is a shallow bowl, older than the ledgers, mended in copper, filled to the brim with still pond-water. He goes door to door and holds it under your chin, and you lean over it and whisper your name into it, and if the water rings — a shiver, a fine grain of rings, the little cymatic bloom of a voice — he nods and moves on. And if the water stays flat he does not move on, and neither do you, and behind him the shapes that follow him stop, and wait, politely, at ten paces.

Along that lane, every dry morning, hang two hundred and thirty whispered names, small burrs the size of grains of barley, faintly luminous, a low glittering hedge that the children are told not to walk through and that dissolve in the next rain and are made again the next dawn. It is the loudest thing the town does in silence. It is the census, and the census is the name, and the name is Eight.

Nothing here is rounded, and nothing here is finished, and that is the whole of the safety anyone has. The basin makes one shape out of everything that stays long enough: a still thing, felted, holding its water on its shoulders, wearing a garden, waiting with its hands folded for something to end. The beasts are that shape. The Attending are that shape. The hills are that shape with the waiting over. The people are getting there.

There is no word in the cant for the difference between a person and an Attending. There is a word for the difference between a person and a hill. It is a verb, and it means *not yet*.
