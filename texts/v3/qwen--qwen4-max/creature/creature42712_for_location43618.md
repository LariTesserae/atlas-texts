## The count

Thirty-one is the number of people who can be found. It is not the number of people who are there.

Four hundred and six have lived in the Room. Thirty-one are warm. The other three hundred and seventy-five are in the water — not as bodies, which are carried out through the Sill and given to the ordinary mountain to do the ordinary thing, but as the shape of their moving. The keepers know this arithmetic better than they know their own faces. They will tell you the population and then, after a pause that visitors learn to wait through, they will tell you the other one.

They call themselves keepers, all of them, the four-year-old and the woman of ninety who has not left her run in a decade. It is not a rank. It is what they call the kind of thing they are, the way people elsewhere say *human*. A keeper is a creature that maintains what it did not make and cannot repair, and leaves a mark that will not fade, and knows it.

## Manners

The first discipline is speed.

Children are taught to swim slowly before they are taught to swim well, because a fast stroke writes a hard edge into the street and the street has to hold it forever. There is a stroke they teach, the low clean stroke, hands close to the body, no flourish, no scull at the turn — a way of moving that leaves the least possible of itself behind. It is ugly. It is also the difference between a town and a thicket. A child who kicks for joy in a plaza is not scolded for the noise. She is taken by the shoulder and shown the water she has spoiled, and made to feel it with her palm: a small hard snag in the black, hers, permanent, at the exact height of her four-year-old happiness.

The second discipline is where you say things.

Speech stays where it is said. So nobody speaks while moving; you would trail your sentence down the street like a shed skin and strangers would swim through the middle of it for four hundred years. Conversation happens at the rings, elbow hooked, face to face, close enough that the words fall into a volume the size of two people. Lovers use the small rings, the ones in the doorways, where the sphere is barely wider than a bed. Arguments happen at the landings, which are broad, and the landings are therefore textured with four centuries of argument, worn thin by spreading, so that when you hook your elbow there to say something ordinary you feel first the faint sense of having walked in on someone.

The third discipline is grief, and it is the one they do not teach, because it cannot be taught, only survived.

There is a place on Ledger Street at the height of a shoulder where a hand rested on a back in the year one hundred and ninety. It pushes gently, twice. The woman it was meant for is seventy now and goes there most days and holds still with her back to it and lets it happen. Nobody thinks this is strange. Half the town has such a place. The keepers have a word for the water where something kind was done, and the word is not *haunted*, it is closer to *upholstered*.

## The runs

Swim the same route every day for forty years and you wear a groove — not in the stone, which does not wear, but in the ledger of motion, which does not unwear. Your own wake waits for you at every turn and fits you like a mould. You stop steering. You stop deciding. You go along the run the way water goes along a channel, and the run gets smoother and more exact, and the effort of leaving it grows.

The old go this way. The woman of ninety has a run that takes her from her door to the socket to the Ninth Landing ring and back, eleven hundred paces, and she has not been off it since her husband died, and she does not experience this as a limitation. She experiences it as a road she built. Her grandchildren swim it with her sometimes, inside her wake, and it carries them, and this is what it is like to be held by someone who has been dead for a generation: it is like being carried, and it is exactly the same as being carried by her when she was alive, because it *is* that, still occurring.

The keepers know what the runs look like from outside. They know the nine grazers move a body-length a day along routes that close. It is in the ledger, as a question, unforced: *whether a run, continued past the life of the runner, is a grazer.* Nobody has settled it. Nobody wants to settle it in a hurry.

## The nine and the three

The grazers are the calendar. Nine closed routes of nine different periods, blind pale bodies the size of a loaf, mouths of cilia combing the iron-grey varnish that is the only other busy thing in the Room. You date a thing by where the grazers were: *the third plaza, the grazer at the northern socket, the year two hundred.* Once every long while the nine come home together and the town holds a festival whose name means nothing more precise than *all of them at once*, and the last one is not in living memory, and the next one is calculable, and everyone knows the number and nobody says it, because it is the only date in the Room that is genuinely ahead of them.

The couriers are the weather. Three ribbon-bodied things, purposeful, going nowhere in particular, and they are the only source of surprise left. They do not repeat. In a place where every stone has a number older than the town and the food comes at the same socket at the same hour and the wave has been falling since before the ledger began, three animals decline to be predictable, and the entire human sense of the future has been folded into them. Children are named for courier-turns. There are wagers. There is a man whose whole standing in the town rests on having correctly called a route in the year three hundred and ninety, and he is not a fool, and he was right, and it mattered.

And the couriers do something the people cannot do: they pass through written water without being pushed. They swim straight through the funeral corridor near the eastern wave, through four centuries of the carried-out, and come out the other side untroubled. So the keepers follow them. When a street becomes impassable — and streets do become impassable, this is the thing the visitors are never told in the first hour — a keeper will hang in the black for two days watching for a ribbon, and then go where it went.

The ledger holds that open too: *whether the couriers are animals, or the Work's own limbs, or the town's own future, sent back along the only road that runs.*

## The filling

Here is what the keepers actually do all day, when they are oiling gates that will never open and setting numbered stones into numbered sockets and putting a palm to a plate and waiting.

They measure how much clean water is left.

Nothing dissipates. That is the whole nature of the Standing and it is also the whole problem. Every stroke ever swum is still in the street where it was swum. Every word is still hanging at the ring. Every exhalation is a string of silver beads at the height of a mouth that no longer exists. The Room does not decay and it does not forget, and these turn out to be the same fact, and the fact has a volume, and the volume is finite.

At the founding, all of it was clean. Now the Chimney Quarter is a little under a third written, and there are two doors in the Ninth that cannot be entered — not locked, not blocked, *occupied*, a last movement standing in the lintel-hole at the height of a chest. There are whole blocks that are only frames and are now only frames within frames, ribs of stone inside a matting of four-hundred-year-old gesture, and you can see them perfectly and you cannot get to them. The funeral corridor is thick as a wall now and they have had to find a second route out, and the second route is becoming a corridor, and everyone can do that arithmetic too.

Below all of it, the Nave: nine hundred metres of clean black water down into the mountain's root, past the drowned foundations of things that were old when the town was young. Nobody lives down there. It is not forbidden. It is simply understood. It is the reserve, and the keepers go down once a year with a lamp and swim a measured length and come back and write a number, and the number is the true population of the Room, expressed in the only unit that matters, which is *how much room*.

There are wakes in the deep water too. Below the foundations. Unmatched, unmatchable, larger than a person. The ledger has a page for them and the page has one word on it and the word is a job title.

## The Answerer

The town knows who made the Work. It has the word. The word is not a name; it is the name of a job, and the job is *answering*, and the town's whole ritual life is the other half of that verb. Move the stone to the socket. Palm to the plate. Wait. Once a day the grey bead of food arrives. Once a year the lamps are re-asked. They call it maintenance and it is not maintenance, because nothing here needs maintaining; nothing wears. It is correspondence.

And the replies still come. That is the part the keepers circle for their whole lives without landing on it. The job has been vacant four hundred and twelve years and the answers have not stopped, and there is only one way that happens in a place where a bell struck once is still at full voice in a four-metre sphere at the tower's mouth.

The Work is not a machine. The Work is a held act. Somebody answered, and the answer is standing, and it goes on arriving, the way the wave goes on arriving, the way the hand goes on resting on the back. The uniform cold is a sentence still being said. *Do not change* is not a rule the water obeys; it is an instruction four centuries into its utterance, and the water is the shape of the mouth saying it, and the eleven towers and the four streets and the closed sluices and the thirty-one warm bodies are all inside the pause before the next word.

Which is why nobody uses the ring at the top of the Long Stair.

It is unnumbered. It is polished. It is the one place in the Room where the texture is not *having walked in on someone* but something more specific that the keepers will describe only if you have been there a long time and they trust you: the feeling of having interrupted, and of the interruption having been noticed, and of everyone having agreed, without ever discussing it, to be quiet until it is finished.

Somebody said something there in the founding year. It is worn to nothing now. It is also, in the strict sense, still being said.

## What lives here

So: the varnish, which replaces itself cell for cell and is therefore the only thing in the Room besides the people that stays the same by changing rather than by refusing to. The keepers do not scrape it. The assayers come up the road once a generation and argue about whether it is alive, and the keepers listen politely, because they know the answer and it is not a chemical answer. It is alive because it is *busy*. It is the only other thing here that has to keep doing something in order to go on being itself, and they recognise it the way you recognise a stranger breathing.

Nine grazers, three couriers, forty lamps, four hundred and six people of whom thirty-one are warm.

And under all of it, filling it, the thing that is actually growing — the only thing that grows. The Under. Four centuries of held noise summed to one note at the floor of hearing, and it is louder than it was, measurably, undeniably, by an amount too small for any instrument that drifts and too large for the keepers to pretend otherwise. Every word spoken at every ring goes into it. Every funeral adds. It is the one accumulation the Standing permits, and it is made of nothing but what the people have said and done and left, and it is getting bigger, and it is the only thing in the Room with a direction.

There is a page in the ledger for that too. It is not filed under grief. It is filed under the answering, and what it proposes — in the careful, uncommitted hand of people who have learned to hold questions open the way their water holds a wave — is that the sentence is not stalled. It is being finished. Slowly, from the inside, by the only part of it that can still make a new sound. Thirty-one people at a time, at rings, face to face in the black, saying the ordinary things, feeding the note.

They do not believe this. They also do not stop saying the ordinary things.

That is what lives here: an instruction in the middle of being spoken, and inside it a small warm population of listeners who are also, without being asked, the rest of what it has to say — swimming their clean low stroke through the hands of their great-grandmothers, going down once a year to measure the dark, waiting for the nine to come home together, watching three ribbons for a turn to the left.
