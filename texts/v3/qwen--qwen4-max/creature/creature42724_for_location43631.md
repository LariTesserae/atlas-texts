## The Fit

Begin where the tolerances are.

In the annulus of a rotary joint in the second compressor lung of a pink district — a shaft of grown horn turning inside a socket of silica-loaded wood, four hundred millimeters across, six bars on one side and six on the other, eight degrees, a whine at four kilohertz that has not stopped since the joint was cut — there live perhaps eleven thousand bearing-beetles. They are the size of a fingernail paring. They are blind in any useful sense and entirely deaf. Along the flank they carry a hair-field so dense it looks like plush, and with it they read the phase of the shaft's rotation the way a hand reads a turned bowl.

Their work is the gap. Not the shaft, not the socket: the gap, that film of clearance a few tens of microns wide where fouling would otherwise gather and seize the whole lung and starve a hundred halls. They crop the fouling. They eat it, and they lay their eggs in what they cannot eat, and they die, and their bodies — which are harder than the wood, which are loaded with the same silica, which is why they can do this at all — are dragged around the race by the shaft and become the polish. The fit of that shaft in that socket is not a dimension. It is a sediment. It is ten thousand generations of small corpses ground into a surface, and the surface is better than anything the body could grow, and the body knows this, and grows the socket deliberately oversize, and waits.

The beetles do not know any of this. What they have, in place of knowledge, is an inheritance so specific it functions as one: the females lay only in the loaded quadrant, the larvae hatch already oriented to the phase, and a beetle that finds itself in an unloaded quadrant walks until it dies. There are lineages that have not left one joint in sixty thousand years. There are others — the walking ones, the dispersal caste, wingless, provisioned, long-lived — that exist to cross the four hundred meters of strut to the next joint when a socket is re-grown, and that navigate by the whine, which they cannot hear, and which they read as a gradient in the wood.

In the flange beside them lives something with no lineage separation at all. The gasket-slugs of that seam are one margin. A slug seals the flange with a mucus that cures to amide rubber; it cures *in place*, around and through the parent's body, so that the parent becomes the seal and the young hatch inside the seam and take up the wet edge of it, and the next generation cures into that, and so on, outward along the flange, at a rate of about a meter a century. The seam is a single continuous animal in the way a coral is. It has been sealing that flange since before the arc hall three districts over learned to strike. Cut it and it does not bleed; it weeps solvent, briefly, and then a thousand small deaths arrange themselves into a gasket.

Nothing in this joint has ever been made. Nothing in it has ever been designed. The clearance is perfect because it is old, and it is old because eleven thousand small lives are spent on it every day, and this is what an inhabitant is, here: a thing that lives in the accuracy of something else.

## The Breath

Out in the halls, the residents keep a calendar with one number in it.

The kite-things — paper wings, four-meter span, a body like a folded hand, no eyes worth the name — do not reckon days. They reckon breaths. Forty-one seconds out, forty-one seconds in, and a lifetime of nine hundred thousand of them, and a migration of a hundred kilometers a day flown entirely on the difference. They tack up the great halls on the exhalation, fold and drop on the intake, and sleep in the reversal, hanging in the lattice at the moment when the air has no opinion. Their pressure-lines run the whole length of the wing spar. A kite does not find the arc hall; a kite finds the *phase* of the arc hall, and rides it in.

They eat gametes. The pale drifting motes that fill the halls are the body's spawn, and the kites crop them in clouds and lay their own eggs in the fouling of the duct walls, which is to say that the country's principal pollinator-analog is also its principal fouling organism, and the eleven-second tone is the reason its populations do not end the world.

The valve-tender herds are heavier and slower and cannot fly. They graze the callus at the valve lips, keeping the horn seats clean, and their restraint is not a behavior. It is a flavor. The callus, when it is thin, tastes of a bitter amide that shuts down ovulation in the females within a day. The herd grazes, the callus thins, the taste arrives, the breeding stops, the herd dwindles, the callus thickens, the taste goes, and the herd returns. Four hundred thousand years of that loop have made a law with no lawmaker and no memory and no exceptions — except the mutations, which happen, and which produce a season of magnificent fecundity, and then a leak, and then a whistle at a pitch that liquefies the herd's hair-fields and kills every animal within two hundred meters. The district goes quiet and cold. The strut dies back. It is re-grown from the base over four centuries, and the new callus is grazed by descendants of the few that were elsewhere.

And in the arc halls there are the flies.

They are nine-second animals. The flash is the mating signal — the body's nitrogen-fixing furnace throwing hard shadows a kilometer through the lattice — and everything within those kilometers has gonads on that schedule, but the flies have gone further than synchronization. They spend their lives as larvae in the refractory's cool margins, and they emerge as adults only in the flash, and the adult is barely an animal: a pair of wings, a gamete sac, a pressure-line, no mouth. It mates in the light, lays in the light, and is cooked in the light, and the entire adult stage of the species occupies about a quarter of one breath. There are populations that have not had an adult alive in eleven years, because the hall's arc schedule slipped, and they waited, as larvae, in the dark, doing nothing, for a furnace.

## The Purge

The blowdown comes with eleven seconds of warning and a hundred and fifty meters a second of ammonium nitrate hail, and everything in the duct dies.

Almost everything. In the fouling of the great ducts there are cysts — barnacle-analogs, crusting things, the disease of this world — that spend their whole lives waiting to be shot. They are the right size and the right density and the right shape to ride the hail without shattering, and they are cemented to the wall by a plug that fails at exactly the shear the purge produces. The immune system is their dispersal vector. A duct is scoured clean and sterile and beautiful, and four hundred meters downstream, where the hail slows and drops, a hundred thousand cysts arrive in the cleanest real estate on the plateau, and take.

They have been commuting on the country's white cells for as long as there have been white cells. The body's response has been to schedule blowdowns in sequences whose intervals the cysts cannot predict — and the cysts' response has been to stop predicting, and to make half their offspring ready at all times, and to eat the loss. This is the oldest argument in the country. Neither side has ever advanced a position. Both are winning.

## The Dark Below

Under the lit halls there are halls that the sun-wells do not reach: three kilometers of void with rain coming down through it out of the light and going on down, cold, black, ammoniac, floored in the drift of everything that has ever fallen. Districts land here. When a water leak turns a strut to green-black soap and the body cuts it loose, three hundred meters of hall comes down into this dark, and it is eaten, and the eaters are the only residents of the plateau who have ever tasted the other world.

They are called nothing, because there is no one to call them, but they are the sweet-eaters: broad, flat, slow, plated things with mouthparts like scrapers, that find hydrolyzed tissue appetizing. Rot is their cuisine. In a country where every bond is an amide and every smell is hot pennies and fish, they alone are equipped to be delighted by the stink of hydrolysis, and they alone can eat it without dying, and they alone would benefit from a leak.

So there is a borer.

It is a hand-sized thing, plated, blind, deaf, with a proboscis of refractory-hard horn, and it spends its life finding water cysts. The glands are the body's treasure — a few grams of water a year per hectare, etched molecule by molecule out of hydrated silicate two kilometers down, hoarded in wax — and the borer taps them. Not to drink. To *pour*. It opens a cyst into a strut and lets the water go where water goes, and then it withdraws and waits, sometimes for a decade, while the amide bonds go and the stain spreads and the sweet stink comes up through the lattice like a dinner bell. Then it comes back with its young and eats the rot.

And there is a smaller thing, a grazers' grazer, that has learned where the body's abscission layer forms and gnaws at it. It does not eat the district. It eats the drop: everything soft that lives on a hall and cannot leave it, delivered to the dark floor at terminal velocity, four hundred meters down, once every few centuries per district. It is a farmer whose harvest is an amputation, and it works on a rotation of four hundred years, and it has never once been early.

## The Morning Countries

Outside, on the crust, life is a shift.

At the valve banks, at dawn, the rime-flowers grow out of the horn lips — actual flowers of frost, centimeters long, sublimating by midmorning — and in them lives a fauna with a total lifespan of three hundred and fifty breaths. Four hours. They hatch in the rime as it forms, they graze the frost's trace organics and each other, they mate, they cement eggs into the salt at the valve's foot, and they go to vapor with their country at nine in the morning. There are eleven thousand generations of them a year. They are, by a wide margin, the fastest-evolving animals on the plateau, and what they evolve toward, endlessly, is a slightly better tolerance for the last twenty minutes.

Between the spires walk the crossers: low, many-legged, salt-colored, water-hoarding, navigating by the forty-one-second pulse coming up through the crust into the small bones of their legs. They cannot hear the living spires and do not need to; they feel them, and they walk toward the strongest beat, because the beat means frost means the morning countries means food. A crossing takes eleven days and covers ninety kilometers and is undertaken once in a lifetime. They lay in the frost collars and die at the valve lips and their hoarded water — a few milligrams, the richest object for a hundred kilometers — is fought over by things that have no other source of it at all.

In the dead spires, the fluted hills, the nine in ten that are corpses, there are tenants. Lace-moths, salt-cemented, occupying the wind-worn holes of the dry lattice; and the holes are pipes, and the wind plays them, and the pitch of a dead spire depends on how many holes are stopped. The second voice of the country — that thin high wandering fluting under the roar — is a census. A spire that has been colonized goes flat. A spire whose tenants have died in an orange fog goes sharp again, and sings alone for a century.

The fog itself has followers. When a nitrate district smolders, crawling a few meters a day for weeks and breathing orange into the hollows, there are plated scavengers that walk ahead of the front and eat what it kills, timing their advance to the decomposition rate, and there is a fly that lays in the burning crust so that its larvae hatch in ground nothing else can enter. They are the only animals on the plateau that thrive on the country's fevers, and they follow the throttling valves too, because where the body opens a bank and drowns a fire in cold nitrogen, there is a windfall of the frozen dead.

## The Quiet

And in the nodes — the pockets where duct geometry cancels the roar, where the air is nearly still and a wing can be heard — live the listeners.

They are soft. They are thin-skinned, translucent, ear-bearing, with actual tympana, an organ every other animal on the plateau abandoned a hundred million years ago. They cannot survive the roar; the pressure gradient alone tears them. They live in silence the way the sweet-eaters live in rot: in a condition that is, everywhere else, lethal or absent. There are nodes a meter across holding one breeding pair. There is a hall the size of a village, in a duct junction east of the great beds, that has held a population for eleven thousand generations, and in it there is *sound that means something*, and this is the only such place in two thousand kilometers.

They sing. Of course they sing. It is the only medium available. The songs are quiet — a few pascals, a flutter at the edge of the residual roar — and they are not about love, or not only. A song in that hall is a map. It encodes the boundary: the exact surface where the cancellation fails, meter by meter, because a listener that drifts thirty centimeters past the node's edge dies with its ears bleeding, and the ones that survived were the ones whose parents sang the edge correctly. The population's inheritance is a sung survey of a silence, updated continuously, taught to the young by repetition, and it is the closest thing on the plateau to a document.

The silence drifts. The duct wears; the beetles polish; the geometry creeps; the node migrates a few meters a decade. So there are the wall-mouths: small, hard-jawed, earless, living at the boundary in the roar where nothing soft can go, chewing the duct wall. They chew where the standing wave is thickest, in a pattern inherited from parents who chewed the same place, and the effect of their chewing is to hold the node still. They are maintenance, like everything else. They are also, precisely, makers of silence, which in this country is the same act as making noise, performed with the opposite sign.

And the clapper comes.

It is the size of a dog and shaped like a bladder on four legs, and it hunts by cartography. It cannot hear the seam-fauna from outside the node — nothing can be heard from outside — so it finds the node itself, hangs at the boundary in the killing roar, reads the geometry with an organ that is also its weapon, and then drives the bladder inward and delivers a hammer-blow of sound into the stillness. Everything soft within thirty meters is stunned. It walks in and eats in the ringing aftermath.

Its young cannot do this. The map has to be learned. A juvenile clapper follows its mother to nine nodes and hangs beside her at the boundary and does not strike, for two years, and what it memorizes in those two years is the sung survey of the seam-fauna — because the song is audible at the edge, faintly, and it is the most accurate description of the node's shape in existence, and the clappers have been eavesdropping on it for longer than there have been species either of them.

So: the listeners sing the edge to keep their children inside it. The clapper's young learn the edge to kill them. The wall-mouths chew to hold the edge where the song says it is. The clapper crops the listeners, which prevents them from overgrazing the node's edges into the roar, which would end the node. Remove any one of the four and the silence closes within a century.

It is not a conversation. It is two lineages reading the same page, and each writing on it, and neither able to read the other's handwriting except as damage.

But in the last few thousand years — a blink, here, four hundred million breaths — the listeners in that hall have begun to sing something in the deep center of the node, in the still core the clapper's clap cannot reach and no boundary needs describing. It is a long figure. It is not a map. It has no receiver that anyone can identify. It may be nothing: a copying error, a drift in a teaching loop, the acoustic equivalent of a scar.

It is the only sound in the country that is not for anyone.

## Aloft

The spires loft their seed-bodies into the stratosphere and most come down on nothing and die, and on the way down they are inhabited. The lacquered bladder of a seed is a continent four years wide: there are mites on the lacquer, and a grazing film, and a predator of the mites, and a thing that lives in the hydrogen valve and eats the wax, and all of them die when the bladder comes down on the escarpment or the ocean or the salt, except the ones that come down on another plateau.

There are other plateaus. Not many; four that anyone here could ever have heard of, if hearing were a thing anyone here did. They are separated by two thousand kilometers of nothing, and they correspond by seed, at a rate of one successful letter every few hundred thousand years, and their seismic pulses beat against each other through the basement rock at periods forty-one and forty-three and thirty-nine seconds, and the interference is a slow throb in the deep roots that the body has been answering, in its fashion, by adjusting its breath.

At the hem, where the skin comes down onto rock and the five-and-a-half-bar differential falls to nothing, there is a fauna that can leave. Half in, half out; ammoniac on one side, nitrogen on the other; the only animals on the plateau with an outside. They carry salt in and amines out. They are the country's entire trade. And there are their lost cousins, out on the white plain past the escarpment, small and deaf and salt-colored, reading a forty-one-second pulse through their feet, walking toward it, not arriving, for eleven million years.

## Coda

In the annulus of a joint in the second compressor lung of a pink district, a bearing-beetle three hundred thousand breaths old finds the loaded quadrant, crops a film of fouling eleven microns thick, lays nine eggs in the residue, and dies with its body already oriented to the phase.

The shaft turns. The clearance holds. Somewhere above, a valve bank frosts and a four-hour country hatches; somewhere below, a borer opens a water cyst and settles in to wait a decade; in a hall east of the beds, a soft thing sings the edge of a silence its ancestors chewed out of a wall, and something with a bladder for a heart hangs at the boundary, listening, learning where to strike.

The monsoon falls. The beds do not cool. The roar goes up through the salt and out into the thin violet air, and it is not distress, and it is not effort, and it is not a message.

It is eleven thousand small lives per joint, and a hundred thousand square kilometers of them, holding a tolerance.

That is who lives here. They are the fit.
