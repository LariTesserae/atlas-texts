Take one off the wall. You can do it; it costs a small tearing noise, which is the fourth sound, which means you have killed something, and before your hand has closed the vacancy behind it is already occupied by a body sliding forward from the layer beneath, warm-faced, incurious, taking the good spot. So you have not made a hole. You have made a transfer. And now you are holding the animal.

It is shield-shaped, thick at one margin and thinning to a translucent lip, and it is heavier than it looks and colder than it should be, because it has been out of the sleeve for eleven seconds and is already dying. On its underside is a foot: a pad of dense black glass, laid down by the animal itself, secreted hot and allowed to set. That is how it holds on. It does not grip the wall; it *welds* to the wall with a small controlled death of its own substance, and when it must move it borrows heat from a neighbor and melts its foot and lays another one a body-width along. Movement here is expensive in the exact currency of being alive. Almost nothing does it. Almost everything here is sessile and is *carried* — the turn moves the population the way a slow convection moves dust, and the animals ride their own schedule, and this is why the species have stopped being separable from the cycle. A tile at the rock face and the same tile at the cold face ninety heartbeats later are two different animals with two different appetites, two different voices, and no continuity of concern between them.

Turn it over. The upper face is matte and densely pitted, two hundred or so shallow cups, each holding a bead of gel that can be warmed or cooled on its own. This is the mouth, the ear, the eye, the hand, and the whole of the language. They read with the same organ they write with, pressing pit to pit, face to face, in the dark.

---

Here is the correction that has to happen before anything else makes sense.

They do not eat heat. Heat is not food; heat is only half of food. What they eat is the *difference* — the drop from the hot wall to the cold draft, run through a stack of thin laminae inside the body, alternating chemistries, one taking warmth in and one letting it out, and the reaction between them making, slowly, more of the animal. Every body here is a battery being charged at the rock and discharged at the air, and the turn is the charging cycle, and the sleeve is not a habitat so much as the gap between two terminals. A creature held at any single temperature, however pleasant, starves. It starves *comfortably*. This is the entire mechanism of the predator's hunting and we will come back to it.

It also explains the four seconds.

To speak is to spend. Every warm dot on the face is food going out of the body into a neighbor's skin, and to keep a pattern warm — to hold a mark, to remember — would be to run a leak forever. Memory is not absent here because these animals are simple. Memory is absent because it is unaffordable, in a world where the medium of thought and the medium of dinner are the same substance. Four seconds is not a defect. It is a budget, and it is generous.

So what is said, in two hundred dots, in four seconds, a billion times over, at every hand's-breadth of a wall twenty-two paces around and eleven bodies deep?

Mostly one thing. *Here.* Occupancy. The assertion of not-being-a-gap, pressed into a cold face by a warm one. And the answer, which is the same word back, mirrored, the neighbor's own pattern returned to it: *so am I.* That is the literature. Presence and acknowledgment, exchanged at a density that would liquefy a human city, forever, and never once kept. There are no names because a name is a pattern someone agrees to hold, and no one can hold anything. There is no kinship: the eggs go into the current and the current goes down and parenthood ends at the moment of laying. There is no grudge, no debt, no promise, no lie — a lie requires that the truth be stored somewhere for comparison, and nothing is stored.

What there is, instead, is the wall.

---

Because cold preserves. That is the loophole in the whole arrangement, and everything durable in this world goes through it.

The dead do not rot; nothing has ever rotted here. They cool and clarify and go to glass, and glass keeps its shape. A living tile is cloudy, milky, faintly opalescent with the churn of its laminae; a dead one is transparent and flawless and lovely, and the difference between them is a few degrees. So the only record that exists is the record written by death, and it is not written in language. It is written in *wear*.

The scallops are the archive. Each shallow dish in the wall is the size of a body because ten thousand generations of bodies have lain in exactly that place at exactly that point in the turn, and the rock has taken the impression, and now the impression fits the next body, and the next, and the shape of the animal is stored outside the animal, in stone, at a temperature low enough to keep. They cannot remember anything. The wall remembers everything, mechanically, in a script none of them can read, and it hands the memory back as *fit*. That is what inheritance is here: not information but a hollow that happens to match you. Habit, externalized, older than any lineage that practices it.

The long ones live in the seams and are made of the same stuff drawn out to wire, with many small hands, and they do not take a turn at all. They span the gradient permanently, one end in the hot layer and one in the cold, and they solved the problem by becoming thin. They also conduct. Heat runs along a long one faster than it crosses a tile, and so the seams are a wiring, and when a stretch of wall chills, the news arrives a hundred paces away before the cold does, and bodies there begin to rotate in anticipation of a thing that has not happened yet.

Do not call this a mind. Nothing is stored; nothing is meant. It is a reflex the size of a world. The wall can flinch, and no one in it knows.

---

The threads run down. This means the only journey any young thing makes is downward, and it makes it before it has a body worth the name: a mote with one sucker and one warm dot, hatched in the blood-red rope of hot sulfur, carried along in the current with thousands of its clutch-mates like beads on a string, drifting through the temperate band where the wall is.

It must catch. That is the whole of the life cycle: fall until you catch, or fall until you don't.

So the population is not a population. It is a *catch*. Everything alive on that wall — every tile, every long thing, the commuter, the whole roaring lit furnace of them — is the fraction that got a pad onto stone in the second or two it had. Below the sleeve it snows constantly, downward, silently, and it is nearly all children, amber drops going down the core in the dark in numbers that have no name and make no sound, and the audible falls, the nine an hour, the scrape and scuff and diminishing contacts, are the adults, the rare ones, the ones that had already made it and then came loose.

Everything here is what did not fall. That is the only qualification any of them has, and it is renewed every hundred heartbeats.

---

In the crystal gardens at the lip, where the vapor lays down its feathery rosettes and the cold slumps hardest, there are animals that have gone the other way about it.

They are small and slow and unimpressive and they spend most of their existence as statues. When the sleeve withdraws they let themselves cool past the point of function, all the way to glass, foot and laminae and face alike, and they stand in the lee of a crystal mat in the posture they last held, and they are, by any test you could apply at the rim, dead. Then the vent takes a long breath and the flux comes up hard and the warmth crawls out along the corner and they remelt, and resume, mid-gesture, at the exact point in the turn where they stopped.

They do not experience the gap. There is no gap to experience. Some of them have been in that corner since the shaft was a hand's-breadth wider than it is now.

And here is the thing that follows from the rules and cannot be helped: cold keeps a pattern. If one of them vitrifies with a neighbor's warm word still fading on its face, the fading stops. The two hundred dots set. The glass holds them. And when the long breath comes and the animal runs liquid again, it wakes with a message on its skin — pressed there by something that died before the shaft narrowed, in a vocabulary of pure occupancy, four seconds long, from a stranger, addressed to no one.

It reads it, because reading is not optional; the face reads whatever touches it. It answers, because answering is not optional either. *So am I.* And then the pattern is gone and the animal goes back to grazing chemistry off a rosette that has never been alive.

This happens perhaps a few times a year. It has been happening for longer than there have been continents in their present arrangement. Nothing has ever come of it. Nothing can. It is a mailbox with one letter in it, delivered over and over, opened over and over, and the letter always says *here*, and it is always from the dead, and there is no one in the world equipped to find that remarkable.

---

The commuter carries winter in a bag.

Its sac is packed with dry crystal harvested from the false garden, and when it presses against a tile it melts the cargo endothermically, drinking the warmth out of the victim's body — and the victim does not bleed or struggle or tear, because what is being taken is not its substance but its *gradient*, and without the difference between its hot sole and its cold face the laminae stop, and the animal simply becomes isothermal, and clear, and beautiful, and architecture. The predator kills by making a thing perfectly comfortable.

Then it is heavy with liquid and it must climb, up the fluted lane it has worn through the wall over an age of climbing, out onto the collar, into the excessive stars, where it lies and radiates and dumps the stolen warmth into a sky that has nothing to give back, and re-crystallizes its cargo with a sound like sugar settling, and goes down again.

It is the only animal here that walks. Everything else rides or threads. It is the only one with a route, and the route is not in its head — the groove is the memory, the stone holds the habit, the animal is merely the thing that fits the channel. And it is the only one with any relationship to the outside at all. The stars are its kidney. The rest of the world has a black nothing overhead where the cold lives; the commuter goes up into that nothing on purpose, once per hunt, and lies in it, and comes back. Its leavings crust the collar, and small things graze the crust, so the killer is also the only weather the lip has ever had that was made of somebody.

It falls, sometimes. It is the heaviest thing on the wall and it climbs the most. Two or three of the nine, in a bad hour.

---

Below, there is no monster.

At some depth the sulfur runs liquid, and everything that falls arrives there and dissolves, and the seep that comes back up through the rock's pores and strings itself into gutters and threads and amber eggs is the same material, returning. There is one substance in this world and it circulates: liquid at the bottom, glass at the top, and in between, in a sleeve two body-thicknesses deep, a narrow band of temperature where it is neither — where it is gel, where it hesitates, where it holds a shape for a while and presses that shape against another shape and says *here*.

Life here is what sulfur does on its way from liquid to glass. A body is a hesitation in a cooling curve. The eater at the bottom is not a species; it is the far end of the same animal, the part of the process that runs backward, and it has never come up because it has never needed to hold on to anything, and it is the largest and least urgent thing in the shaft.

The five blocks on the collar and the nine steps cut down the inner wall belong to none of this. They imply a body with a stride, a knee, a habit of falling forward and catching itself — a creature that would risk the drop with every motion it made, and would therefore have been, here, in a state we have no word for. It stopped after nine steps. The animals use the cut faces as a baffle against the cold draft, and the corners are thick with eggs, and the third step has been scalloped by a lineage that has lain there since before the lane was worn, and it would be pleasant to report that the baffle gave them slack, margin, leisure, some first idle animal in the history of the world.

It did not. The margin filled instantly, the way everything fills. There is no spare room even in the shelter of a ruin.

---

If you kneel at the rim long enough they will begin to pack you, because you are colder than everything and you read as a leak, and they will do it without haste and without sound, using the glassy dead — the archive, the only durable thing they have — laid against your boots and your knees and your chest, insulating the wound, closing the gap. It takes an hour. It is not violence; it is the same thing they do to a spall. And when they are finished there will be no gap, and in the wall there will be a new dish, shallow, the size and shape of a kneeling person.

Bodies that do not fit it will lie in it anyway, because bodies lie in whatever is there. It will be worn shallower. In ten thousand years it will be gone. For all of that time the turn will hitch very slightly as it passes, and no one will know, and nothing will be kept.

And one day the vent closes, and the sleeve thins and slides down and goes out, and the last of them vitrify in the posture of the turn, mid-word, faces pressed to faces, two hundred dots still warm and then not warm and then held. The whole shaft fills with glass. Every animal in it becomes clear and flawless and perfectly preserved, layered outward from a cold core, eleven deep, seven deep, no gap anywhere.

The world finally becomes entirely legible, and there is nothing left that can read, and the doorstep stays on the collar, and the stars go on being excessive over a hole full of finished bodies, which is what it was always going to be, and which is, in the only script this place has ever had, the same word it has been saying all along.
