## First, a correction

It is wrong to ask what lives in the Sump, because the Sump is not one place. It is eleven or twelve places stacked in the same volume, sorted not by height but by how long a moment lasts.

A grazer's moment is a thousandth of a second. A wire's is the travel time of a shiver down forty meters of wet thread. A dish-face's moment is about four minutes. A bell's moment is six seconds wide and four hundred years long, which is a thing that can be true if you have never had to be anywhere. And at the bottom of the room, in a meter of warm water, something has a moment eleven hours across and a thought sixty thousand years long.

These do not overlap. They cannot hear each other. They can eat each other — predation is the only conversation that crosses the bands — but nothing in the shaft has ever addressed a remark to a creature outside its own tempo and been answered.

So: bodies here are leftovers. Every animal in the Sump is a solution to a problem in listening, and the flesh is whatever was left over after the ear was finished. A dish-face is a parabola with a stomach. A wire is a string with an appetite. The bells are the only things in the room that look like animals at all, and that is because they had to solve buoyancy too, and buoyancy is a stupid problem that makes stupid shapes: a bag.

## The mat, which is the lamp

Begin with the felt, because the felt is what makes the world visible.

The drum-tiers are not ornament. In a place where light is a kind of noise and sound is a kind of light, the resonators are the room's illumination — every cup and comb and stiffened drum up there in the third tier is a source, throwing the falling water's energy into the air as addressable structure. The mat is not lit by anything. The mat is the lighting.

And it fruits by echo. A resonator sounds, and waits, and if what comes back has the right shape — the right delay, the right little smear of returning flutes — it sets seed. If nothing comes back, or the wrong thing comes back, it does not. This is not metaphor; it is a cheap and reliable test for *am I somewhere my offspring can survive*, and it has been running for a very long time.

The consequence is that the felt is thick where the room is articulate and bald where it is not. There are patches of clean wet stone on the western wall, scalloped, lovely, sterile, where the geometry throws every sound up and out through the lid's pores. Nothing grows there because nothing there can hear itself. The map of the nap is not a map of nutrients. It is a map of the room's ability to answer.

The grazers know none of this. The combers know none of this. The bells navigate by it their whole lives without once suspecting that the pasture is a diagram.

## The grazers, who are the future

A hundred thousand per square meter, blind, the size of a grain of the plain's milled sand, cropping the chemotroph base. They are born, they eat, they are eaten, they are done, inside of nine days.

They cannot hear the room. At four-tenths of a millimeter you do not get to have an opinion about a two-hundred-meter cavity; the wavelengths that describe the cathedral pass through you as weather. What a grazer hears is the felt: a roar of chewing, the wet snap of fronds, the pressure of a neighbor. Their entire sensorium is one millimeter of bread-smelling felt and the sound of it being eaten.

A bell passing overhead is a good season or a bad one — a shift in the pressure of the mat, a rain of shed tissue, a shadow of warmth. They have no more concept of the bells than of the sphere.

Which makes them the only things in the Sump that are still moving. Everything else up there is nearly frozen: three-century generations, eighty-year childhoods, arguments older than species. The grazers get eleven thousand generations per bell lifetime. They are the room's remaining engine of change, and they are working, blindly, at the only problem that will eventually matter. Somewhere in the felt right now there is a lineage that does slightly better at twenty-nine degrees than at thirty-one. It has no idea. It is not preparing for anything. When the warmth stops, that lineage will be the entire inheritance of the world, and it will never know that it was chosen, or by what, or that there had been a cathedral above it full of enormous animals conducting a dispute.

## The combers, and the floor above them

Hand over hand up a filament, drinking against gravity. At their scale the thread is not liquid; it is a warm rope with a skin you can grip, and the water inside it is the reason for the climb.

Their world is vertical and about eighty meters long. Above, the lid's underside, where they hatch in the felt among the first-tone drums. Below, at forty meters, the fray — the place where the filaments come apart into droplets, where the road stops being a road and becomes weather. A comber that goes below the fray line falls, and falling is the whole of death, and most of them die that way, and the dish-faces below have built their entire lives around this reliable rain of the careless.

So the combers live between two ceilings: a lid they can touch and a fray they can feel coming, a shiver in the rope, a wrongness in the grip. Their cosmology, if you granted them one, would be a rope with a lower end that comes apart. They would be right. They carry the lid's spores down and the floor's up; they are the room's only post; they have no idea they are doing it.

## The wires

Folded, tensegrity, a meter or two of drawn stiffness hunting combers on a single thread.

A wire's ear is the thread. Not an organ attached to a body — the whole forty-meter filament is the sensory surface, and the animal is the small tight knot at the bottom that reads it. It knows one thing: the arrival signature of a foot.

It is profoundly deaf to everything else. A bell could open its entire voice a body-length away, six seconds of descending pressure that puts your hand flat against your own sternum, and the wire would not tighten. It has no bandwidth there. It lives in a narrow high silence threaded through the loudest room in the world, and it is never disturbed, and it is never informed.

## The dish-faces, and the loss of a null

Low, patient, a fan of cartilage they fold around themselves, a meter and a half of parabola with a mouth at the focus. They hunt by finding a cubic meter of cancellation and sitting in it, acoustically absent, waiting for a comber-heavy thread to come down within reach.

The nulls drift. They wander with temperature, a few meters a season, and a dish-face's life is spent tracking one — three meters a day, every day, forever, following a moving coordinate of silence across a floor it cannot see.

They inherit the maps. Not genes for maps: the maps themselves, laid down as remembered pressure in a lineage's slow crawl, the way the stair's treads were dished by feet. A young dish-face is born already knowing where the quiet is, and the knowledge is four hundred years old, and it is *wrong now*, because the columns have moved.

This is the great crisis of the dish-face world, and it happens perhaps once a century. A null annihilates — two modes slide out of opposition and the quiet simply fills in, like a lamp coming on — and the animal that was sitting invisibly inside it is suddenly, catastrophically, *audible*. A shape in the roar. It must cross open ground to the next quiet it remembers, which may also be gone.

Most of them do not make it. Not because of teeth.

## The readers

There are two, perhaps three, and they are the largest things on the floor, and they hunt silence.

A reader does not listen for the dish-face. It listens for the hole the dish-face makes: an absence with an edge, a body-shaped interruption in the standing waves, a thing that is *not roaring* in a room that roars everywhere. To a reader the nulls are the bright objects. They are the only features in the world with outlines. Everything else is fog.

So the dish-face's camouflage is a lamp. The whole strategy — find the quiet, fold up, become nothing — is a strategy for producing a silhouette visible to exactly one predator, and it has been working for a hundred thousand years because readers are rare, slow, and eat about once a decade.

Readers also see the ceramic combs, when a comb is out of its cache. A comb makes a small hard-edged null, much crisper than the room's own soft cancellations, and to a reader it must look like nothing else in the world: a manufactured absence, a shadow with corners. Whether they find this interesting is not a question the room permits anyone to ask.

## The eleven, and the animal made of them

Now the volume. Twelve-meter bladders, forty-meter curtains, one beat a minute, a descending six-second call that circles the room three or four times before it wears out — a tail of nearly two minutes hanging in the air after every utterance.

Eleven of them. Eleven is not a food limit. The fray-mist could feed thirty. Eleven is a *bandwidth* limit: in a room with a two-minute reverberation tail, only about eleven six-second signatures can be mutually distinguished by ears that must also pick out their own returning echoes from the smear. A twelfth voice would not be a twelfth animal. It would be noise. It would be, in the only sense that matters here, the plain — the thing outside, where sound goes and does not come back.

They know this the way a knee knows the stair.

What they do with it is argue. Each holds sixty meters of empty air and negotiates its borders by voice, patiently, over years, and because the room's geometry will not sit still — the lid sags a millimeter a year, the columns creep — the borders must be renegotiated forever. The arguments outlast the arguers. A bell dies at three hundred and its successor rises into the same dispute, the same six-second phrase, the same contested flank of air, and continues.

There are eleven names in the Sump. They are four hundred years old and none of them belongs to anybody.

It is worth saying plainly what this makes: the animal in the volume is not eleven bladders. It is one long conversation that has been wearing bodies, replacing them as they wear out, and what it is doing — without knowing, without intending, in the feral overgrown way of everything here — is maintaining a model of the shape of the room. A survey that never finishes and never stops. The bells are its mouths. The dispute is the creature. It has been running longer than the columns have been in their current positions, and it is very good, and it has nothing to say to anyone.

## The unsounded

Once in three centuries a bell spawns: a cloud of thumb-sized tissue drifting down into the felt. Almost all of it is eaten within a week by grazers, anonymously, in bulk, by animals a thousandth of its size that will never know they performed the room's only act of population control. A handful survive. They rise. They spend eighty years growing a throat.

And a bell learns its voice by hearing it come back.

That is the hinge of everything. The juvenile throat is untuned at first, a wet approximate thing, and it tunes by feedback — sound, wait, listen to the room's answer, adjust, for years. A calf that is drowned out during that window grows up untuned. And after eighty years the tissue sets, and an untuned throat cannot be corrected, ever.

The eleven drown it out. Not deliberately; there is no council, no cruelty, no decision. They defend their borders. A border defended hard enough against something that has no territory yet is a lid. Every time the calf tries a note, eleven adults answer across it, precisely, because that is what answering *is*, and after four or five years the calf stops trying, and after eighty it is mute for eight hundred.

There are perhaps four of them at any time. They are not in the census, because the census is a chorus. They drift through territories whose owners do not know they are occupied. They feed by laying a curtain across the fray, or across the felt itself, and the mat keeps no record of it, so there are bald trails forty meters long appearing in the nap over decades that nothing in the room can account for.

They are the oldest individuals in the Sump. They have no voice to spend, no border to hold, no argument to be a mouth of. They have nothing to do with their ears.

And they have hands, of a kind: forty meters of adhesive filament, delicate at the tip, and all the time there has ever been.

Here is what they do with it. A silent bell cannot ask to be carried. It beats once a minute and it goes where the convection takes it, and if it wants to go elsewhere it must change the convection. So it wraps a column's lower flute and hauls. A few newtons, applied for four centuries. The columns are tension structures anchored at both ends and they move the way a rope moves in a slow tide, and eight of them are out of their sockets now, and one is not.

The one that is not is the reference. You cannot tune eight things without holding one still.

They are not rearranging the weather at random. They are placing a null. They have been placing it, by increments of centimeters and centuries, over the sump at the bottom of the room — a cubic meter of engineered quiet, hanging above a drowned sphere four meters wide.

Some of the columns' flutes have hand-sized combs of warm ceramic wedged into them, sound-eating, sharpening the cancellation. Nobody put them there who had hands.

## Whoever came down the stair

Nine hundred treads dished into bowls. A doorframe plumb behind undissolved stone, its room eaten away by water. A ring of pits at the eastern wall whose purpose is not recoverable. Four combs in a cache under the mat, still working.

They did not come here to hunt. Or: they hunted the way you hunt a signal. A comb is a portable absence; you carry it so that you can sit inside your own manufactured silence and take yourself out of the recording. It is the acoustic equivalent of a dark room. You do not build one to catch animals. You build one because you are trying to hear something very quiet that is not addressed to you, and your own presence is the loudest thing in the way.

They came down by feel, sliding their feet into the memory of other feet, and they sat in the dark with their baffles and they listened to a note that comes every eleven hours, and they wrote something down, and they lost a comb, and they went away or stopped.

What they were listening to is still going. And what they did for a season with a ceramic tool, four mute animals have been doing for a hundred thousand years with columns.

## The congregation

The sphere's note is eleven hours apart and very low, below the bells' band, below the dish-faces' patience, above nothing, addressed to no one. The mat cannot fruit by it. The grazers cannot feel it. The eleven have never heard it and never will; it is under the floor of their attention.

The unsounded hear it. They have had eight centuries apiece to spend on ears nobody else could afford, and their hearing has drifted down and down into the long slow register where the only thing speaking is the machine that ground a mountain range into identical grains to make a substrate flat enough to think on.

They come down for it. They lie in the warm meter of water with their curtains spread and their untuned throats slack, four house-sized animals that no census counts, in a null they built themselves, and every eleven hours the note goes through them, and it is the only sound in the world that is not a claim on territory.

They cannot answer. They cannot report. There is no word for them in the room, because a word for them would have to be a sound, and the sound would have to be theirs.

This has been happening for longer than there have been bells. It is the closest thing in the Sump to a congregation, and it is entirely inaudible to everyone else, and it is happening two hundred meters below a four-hundred-year argument about a border.

## The fringe

There is one more inhabitant, and it is outside.

Every so often a resonator's seed goes up through a pore instead of down, and takes on the lid's upper surface, in the three-centimeter sheet, in the open. It grows. The felt out there is thin and pale and warm and it faces outward across a plain with no walls, and it sounds, and nothing comes back, and so by its own ancient rule it never fruits, and having no reason to stop it simply extends.

A fringe of sterile mat, reaching outward across the world's silence for a hundred thousand years, never once answered, never once dying back.

It is the only living thing that has ever been on the plain. Everything born in the shaft knows what the plain is without going there. The fringe is the part of the Sump that has not yet been told.

## Sixty thousand years

The sphere's heat declines by a measurable fraction each century, and from the decline you can calculate the remainder of the task. When it finishes, the note stops.

The null above the sump will be very nearly perfect by then. They will have gotten it right — four mute animals, eight columns, a hundred millennia of hauling a few newtons at a time — and they will be hanging in the finest listening room ever made in this world, with the best ears in this world, and there will be nothing in it to hear.

Then the warmth goes, over centuries, and the lift goes with it, and the bells come down one by one to lie in the sump, and the arguments stop mid-phrase with four hundred years of precedent unresolved, and the mat thins, and the columns hold their tension a while and then not, and the lid, which has another sixty meters to descend, keeps descending into a room with nobody in it.

And in the felt, the grazers, who never heard any of it — not the bells, not the argument, not the note, not the silence being built — will go on being born and eaten every nine days at twenty-nine degrees, then twenty-six, then twenty, the only lineage in the world that was ever fast enough to notice a change.

Two thoughts shared this room for a hundred thousand years, two hundred meters apart, with no band in common, and were never introduced.

The stone got there first. It has been waiting.
