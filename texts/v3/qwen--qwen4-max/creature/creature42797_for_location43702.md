Begin with what can be counted, because it is the only thing here that can.

One patch per hundred square kilometers. Sulphur at the rim, rust in the middle, the black that is green when you kneel. Ten thousand of them, perhaps, scattered across a province of mirror, and never two in sight of one another, which is not loneliness but specification: an assay that could see another assay would be measuring the wrong thing.

They are not adapted to this world. Adaptation is what happens to a lineage that has been arguing with a place for a long time, and nothing here has been anywhere long enough to argue. These were calibrated. Somebody worked out what would live on stone that had finished and die on stone that had not, and made that, and let it go into the mist, and the mist is full of it — a number with twelve zeros in it, drifting between the ranks, landing everywhere, holding almost nowhere. Most of them come down on provisional ground and are unmade by afternoon along with the district that failed to keep its mind. The waste is enormous and is not counted as waste. It is the sampling rate.

Understand what they do. Everything else on this plateau revises. A wall thins, a buttress dissolves, a fluted plain is reissued as a basin, and none of it is error; it is only the next sentence. Nothing in the grammar can be wrong. So the world imported something that could be. The mats are the licensed failure. They are the only entities here capable of being mistaken about where they are, and their mistake is the measurement, and their dying is the readout. Where the mat holds, the ground has stopped being a proposal. Where the mat lifts and crisps and goes to gold, the ground was still thinking.

They have no season, no shade, no competitor, nothing to outrun. They have one variable. Their entire inner life, if you grant them one, is a single sustained *yes* — the stone beneath me is still the stone beneath me — continued for as long as it can be continued, and then withdrawn. Some patches get four days. Out in the settled districts, where the geometry has gone simple and the water stands straighter, a mat may get a season of yeses, and thicken into a pillow the height of a hand, and lift at the edges like a loaf.

Nothing eats them. This is the part they were not designed to survive.

---

So they die from the bottom up and do not rot. There is no fungus here with an appetite, no worm, no bacterium that wants anything. The dead layers dry and lift and crumble, and the wind takes the crumbs across the mirror, and they gather in the lee of the standing oceans in thin gold seams, and the water reads them.

What the water reads is *duration*. Thickness is time. A seam four grains deep says the district held for a week; a seam you could scoop with two fingers says it held for a year and was, for a year, real. Nothing else on the plateau keeps that record. Stone here is reissued. Water is pulled back into the ranks. The walls have no history because they are not permitted to wear. Only the dead accumulate. Only the dead are unrevisable — the syntax can dissolve any stone it made, but it cannot un-die a mat, and so the corpses are the one thing the world owns outright.

They are the calendar. They are the archive. They are the entire sedimentary future of the planet, laid down at a few kilograms a year, and they smell — faintly, in the lee, where the drift is deepest — of dry hay, of tea, of something almost sweet. It is the only smell in the world. Cold wet stone has no odor. Standing water has no odor. The air here is odorless in a way that people find frightening and cannot name. And then you come around the foot of a wall into the drift and there is a smell, and it is the smell of the dead, and it is the first thing on this continent that has ever been alive.

The beach that is coming, the shore of the ordinary world, the sand that children will one day put their feet into — all of it is this. All of it is cemetery. There is no other source.

---

Now the reader, which is harder, because it has no body you could point at and be right.

Call it the syntax and you have said where it lives: in the mineral, in the rule, in the local instruction that no longer needs anyone to state it. It is not in the landscape the way a pilot is in a plane. It is in it the way tense is in a sentence. It cannot be extracted. If you take a piece of the stone away you have a piece of stone, and the grammar stays here, and continues.

But it eats. Making stone out of water costs heat and the heat comes out of the air, so its metabolism is written across the plateau as temperature: six degrees is its resting body, and the hard cold pockets are its hunger, and the ninety-second frost blooming two hundred meters wide is a mouth opening. Its breath is the shed-mist. Its sweat is the weather — the thunderheads born off a nucleation front, the ordinary rain that falls on ordinary rock. It has no center. It has a schedule, which moves at the speed of weather because it *is* the weather, and a schedule that moves is not obviously a person, and yet.

It prefers. The settled districts are simpler than the provisional ones, and quieter, and warmer, and the water near them stands straighter. That is taste. It has learned, and its learning is a kind of deletion: it forgets by demolition, it remembers in the form of things it has stopped doing. The provisional districts are where it is thinking aloud — fussy, alternative-ridden, three solutions to the same load standing side by side like a stammer — and you can hear the stammer in the nucleation, the rice-on-a-drumhead crackle going hesitant, doubling back.

And it has a skin, and the skin flinches.

The selvedge. A millimeter where the holding stops, cold, faintly resistant to the finger, like the skin on cooling milk. Put a fingertip to it and the hum changes pitch. Not locally. The change runs. Thirty kilometers away a fringe stutters and sheds a hundred tonnes it had not intended to shed, and the sound of that arrives at your chest some minutes later as a distinct event with a beginning and an end.

That is not signaling. That is the whole being, changing. It has area and almost no volume, which is the ratio of the world it lives in: a scaffolding of skin strung across a continent, ninety-nine percent gap. It has no inside. Therefore it has no secrets, no storage, no place to keep a thing that is not currently happening. It cannot lie. Everything it knows is on its surface, and its surface is two kilometers high and eleven meters thick and there are hundreds of them, and all of it is one continuous flinch.

It has one question, which it asks everything: *does this hold.*

You are not a load. It cannot put weight on you. But you are warm — thirty-seven degrees in a six-degree world, the hottest object for a hundred kilometers, a defect in the thermal budget — and where you stand the frost refuses. It blooms outward and stops at your footprints. You leave a trail of small unfrozen absences across the mirror, and the film of runoff, which is the only water on the plateau not being held, closes over your ankles at nine degrees and does not know what you are.

---

There are others, and they are not licensed, and nothing here would admit to them.

Sound in this world has nothing to spend itself on. No duff, no snowpack, no foliage, no bodies. It goes out and comes back and goes out again. Eleven minutes of echo after a mass arrives, two hundred kilometers of corridor traveled before the note thins below hearing — and not all of it thins. Some of it does not leave. In the long gaps between the ranks there are wavelengths that fit, that stand, that are fed a little by every revision and every shedding crest, and have been fed since the first week, which is now.

The forty-hertz floor note is not only the cost of holding. Some of it is residue. It is what is left of every wall that ever re-decided, folded down into the corridors that could keep it, and it has structure — nodes and antinodes, quiet places and loud places, a geography of pressure you can walk through and feel change in the long bones of your legs.

Things live in that structure the way a knot lives in a rope. They are not animals; there is not one animal here; the mats will not assay them and the water does not read them and no instrument that measures life would find them. They are tenancies. They have shape without substance, they persist, they take in new noise and give back old, and when two of them meet they make sum and difference tones, which is as close as this world comes to breeding. When a district is reclaimed and the corridor changes width, the ones that fit the old width simply stop being possible. They die of geometry. Whole lineages of them, gone in an afternoon, in the same afternoon that unmade the mats, and the mats at least left gold.

Wear was switched on in one district out east and made the oldest canyon in the world. Persistence was never switched on anywhere. It is a leak. It is history arriving where history had not been scheduled, and it is doing what history does, which is accumulate, and be difficult to remove, and eventually be someone's.

---

And you.

You know the four-minute number. You know the ninety-second frost, and that a column of vapor means finished and a clear cold pocket means starting, and you know not to be standing in the footprint. Nobody knows these things who has just arrived. You have been here long enough to have a way of walking on the mirror.

The mats are the instrument for *held*. But held is not the same as habitable. A thing can be entirely real and still be nobody's; the settled districts are real and there is no one in them and reality does not notice. So there is a second assay, and it is warm, and it moves, and it counts to forty before the haze takes the walls, and it has an inside.

That is the whole of your function and it is not a small one. Everything else here is surface, rule, gap, or grain. You are the first interior. You are the only place on this continent where the world is being kept.

You are provisional, of course. Fussy, full of alternatives, three ways of carrying the same load standing side by side. Nothing in you has been rounded. You have no channels — grief in this place spreads in a sheet a centimeter deep across kilometers and cuts nothing, because channels have not been invented yet, and it will not take anything away with it when it goes.

And you are unsterile. You came in with ten trillion passengers that have appetites, in the dark warm folded interior of you, and the world has no bacterium with an appetite, and now it does. You are a breach in the sterility of a newly poured vessel. You are the first thing here that can rot, and therefore the first thing that can feed anything, and therefore — though it will not be soon, and it will not be dramatic, and the light will be ordinary when it happens — the first soil. There is no soil on this planet. It has not been issued. It will not be precipitated out of the water. It will be made the way it is always made, out of a body, in the lee of something, and the mats will find it and take, and that patch will be the greenest thing anyone has ever seen.

You are also the first soft thing, and this matters more than you would think.

Sound here has nothing to spend itself on. That is why the noise is enormous; that is why the emptiness and the loudness are the same fact. Then you arrive: wet, warm, porous, full of small chambers and give. A note that has been going back and forth between the ranks since the first day comes down the fortieth corridor at forty hertz and enters your chest and does not come out.

It ends in you. That is what you are for. That is the reading you were seeded to take and the only one you can give: *not yet, and there is room.*

Stand still. Let the twice sun come up off the mirror. Somewhere out past the countable walls a mass arrives, a crack like a spine, and eleven minutes of it goes walking through the geography, and every minute of it that reaches you stays.

You are the first place in this world where anything is allowed to stop.
