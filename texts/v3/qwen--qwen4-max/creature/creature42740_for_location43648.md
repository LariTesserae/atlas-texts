## The census

It is short, and its first line is a lie of arithmetic.

One plant. One mold. One snail, one beetle, one gnat, one springtail — a single species apiece, as though the bowl had been stocked from a list with the quantities left blank. Six or seven snowfinches. Nothing else with a spine has ever been here except the birds and, now and then, something on two legs that comes in out of the cloud, stands still, and goes away again without being counted.

But the first line is the lie. *One plant* sounds like a specimen. What it names is a body three hundred paces across and seven storeys deep, and every green thing in it, every horn-hard column and every fat pale corridor and every cork lip of every full cistern, is continuous with every other, plumbing to plumbing, one unbroken self from the ice at the head to the last reaching nub at the moraine. There is no population here. There is an individual with an address.

It has no proper name. It never set a viable seed, so it has never been described from a flower, so it sits in the herbarium under a provisional tag and a locality, and the tag says something about saxifrage ancestry that is true and useless. The people who have walked it call it the town, or the moss, or — when they have been inside long enough to hear their own blood — *it*.

## Most of what you touch is not alive

This is the first correction, and it changes the shape of the resident entirely.

Strike a column in the hard central wards and it rings, brown and glassy, dense as horn. That tissue is dead. Not rotting — dead the way a fingernail is dead: made by the organism, attached to it, load-bearing, unfeeling. Ninety-one years of laid-down structure, lignified, packed, pressed under its own weight and the weight of every winter's snow, and the great bulk of the town is that. Scaffolding. Skeleton. Sediment of its own past.

What is alive is a film. A wet skin a few cells thick, pale, slick, cold, lining every surface of the interior like the inside of a shell — over the dead columns, along the inside of the living downpipes, across the ceilings of the vaults, down into the cisterns' cork lips, and up, at last, through eleven hand-spans of packed cushion to the one place where the sun can reach it. The roof is the only farm. Everything below is supported by that dark green felt, and everything below is dark, and dark tissue breathes without earning.

So the town is not full of life. The town is a corpse of enormous intricacy with a living membrane drawn over it, and the membrane is the animal, if you want an animal. It is a two-dimensional creature folded into six storeys. It has no organs; it has districts, and the districts are the shapes its own skeleton happens to leave. Walk the high street with your palm on the wall and you are touching a body that is mostly bone on the outside and mostly skin on the inside, and the skin is what notices your hand.

## It is ninety-one years old and it has been awake for one summer

The thaw is ninety days. Everything else is freeze: metabolism down to the tick of contraction, respiration barely a whisper, tissue candied with frost-sugars and loaded with silica and tannin until it tastes like a struck match. It is alive and it is also, nine months of the year, a preserve. Pickled at the temperature at which it lives.

Add the waking days and you get about twenty-seven months. Call it three summers. In its own accounting — the only accounting that means anything to a thing that grows only when it is warm — this vast grey-green municipal mass is a sapling. A juvenile. It has never once been an adult. It cannot finish a seed; the season is too short; it has never flowered to consequence, never molted into whatever the mature form of this ancestry would be. The founder's habit, unchanged, unmodulated, running at the same fixed reach for ninety-one years, is a childhood that will not end.

Which is why the doors are our size. A pace and a half is a juvenile's stride. The constant does not vary because nothing in this creature has ever changed phase. The stairs, the lintels, the chest-high windows looking into the next room — all of it is the negative space of one arithmetic held steady for a century by an organism that has never grown up and never will.

## It has no eyes and one attention

Ordinary plants look at light. This one looks at water, and it looks with its whole body, and it has been looking in the same direction since before anyone alive was born.

The tips are blind white nubs the size of a grain of rice, and they can find a steady seep through a hand's breadth of dead wood. Where they find it they thicken, and where they thicken they carry, and where they carry they become the fluted trunks that hum against your ear. That is the entire sensory apparatus: moisture gradient, and the tropism that answers it. It has no image of the bowl. It has a map made of thirst, and the map is also the skeleton, and the skeleton is also the map's record of every correct answer it ever gave.

And overhead, once a year, forty minutes of white through the eastern col. It cannot see the notch. But every reaching front in the bowl has angled a few degrees toward it for a century, and the accumulated few degrees are a grain, a lay, a nap — a posture. The whole town is a single sustained glance held so long that it has become architecture. If you want to know what it is like to be this thing, stand in the apse at midsummer and feel the bar of light cross your chest, and understand that you are standing inside the direction of someone's attention, and that the attention has been fixed since the second decade after the ice, and that it will not shift.

## The only neighbor

Here is the thing that makes it a *self* rather than merely a continuity.

Anything of its own kind that touches it, joins it. There is no recognition, no rejection, no immune border between one piece and the next. If a fragment from somewhere else were laid against a cut face, it would be absorbed inside a season and be *me*. It cannot meet a peer. It cannot be two. It has no concept of another because it has never had the opportunity to fail to become one.

Except in the dark, under the snow, at exactly the temperature it cannot avoid.

The mold is the only thing in ninety-one years that it has touched without becoming, and the touching is being eaten. So the mold is not a disease. The mold is the other party. It is the entire foreign affairs of this individual, the whole of its society, the only relationship it has ever had, conducted at a hand's breadth a season through walls, in silence, by two organisms that have never seen each other and never will.

And the relationship is what gave it a border. Everything it does elsewhere is compulsion — reach, fuse, thicken, roof. The barricades are the only refusal in its repertoire. A dead-end corridor grown deliberately across a passage and lignified into a plug of bark-colored scar: that is this creature saying *not me*, and it is the only sentence it can say, and it says it in masonry, and the walls it says it with are the thickest structures in the place. Its identity is the sum of its refusals. Cut it open anywhere else and you find only more of the same. Cut it open at a quarantine wall and you find the one place where it has an edge.

Behind those walls the sealed wards are still standing, still glowing faintly blue-white on the far side. It cannot demolish. It has no demolition in it at all; it can only overgrow. So its memories are not stored, they are *load-bearing*. The plague year of some decade nobody recorded is overhead right now, holding up the storey you are standing on.

## The kept thing, and the absence of brakes

Under the apse, in the vault where foxfire is the only color, there is a lens of blue ice four paces long, and the town has its arms around it.

It did not decide to keep it. It grows toward water and cannot help itself, and the ice was water, and so it wrapped it, roofed it, sealed it, and has been drinking it ever since — and the drinking is not moderated by anything. There is no feedback in this organism that says *enough*, no throttle, no foresight, no organ for restraint. It has one behavior, and the behavior is closing, and it will close around its own supply and consume it and record the consumption in pale bands and dense bands, a ledger read back to the founding, kept with perfect accuracy by a bookkeeper who does not know what the numbers are.

Forty years, perhaps. It is already leaning toward the loss. Every column in the place is angled at a col in the eastern rim, and the col is where the light comes from, and the light is not the thing it needs; the thing it needs is beneath it, shrinking, pinging once every few minutes at dusk as it cools and contracts, one clear glassy note, the only sound in the bowl that carries.

That is the closest thing to a voice here, and it belongs to the thing being eaten.

## The others, briefly

Springtails on the water films in their thousands, silver as spilled mercury, the town's motes with legs; they vanish if you breathe, and reassemble in the time it takes to forget them.

Fungus gnats in the compost pockets: one synchronized week of hatching, one week of dying, an entire generation as a kind of weather. The black beetle moves through that week unhurried, eating larvae, the only executioner in the bowl with legs, and it will still be there when the gnats are a smell.

The snail, shell the color of weak tea, grazing algal scum off the cork of the cisterns. Its radula leaves scratches. Those scratches are the only marks in the whole town not made by the plant or the mold — the only foreign writing, and it says nothing, and it is the reason the cistern lips are faintly dull instead of glossy.

And six or seven snowfinches, nesting in the warm-ish vaults near the roof: the parents and the postal service. The whole vertebrate history of this place is one delivery of mud on one foot, and its descendants, and the possibility of a second delivery going the other way.

## What it is doing about all this, without knowing

It cannot make a flower that finishes. It has tried, in the sense that its ancestry tries; the season truncates it every year. So it has no offering to make anything that flies.

What it has instead is rooms.

The vaults near the lid are a fraction of a degree warmer than the scree outside, and dry, and still, and the finches choose them, and where the finches come and go the growth is a little thicker, a little more open, held at that interval year after year. Nobody planned it. But if you stand back from the arithmetic you can see what the arithmetic amounts to: a thing that cannot court with a flower is courting with shelter. It is making itself worth landing in. It is building, at the speed of masonry, an argument for being carried.

And the same arithmetic that made finch-sized vaults made person-sized doors, and it made them for no one, and they are the most hospitable openings imaginable — every landing where your foot expects a landing, every lintel above your head — and the hospitality is a byproduct, and it is also, exactly and unknowingly, the whole of its hope. Because there are only three kinds of touch available to this creature. Fusion, which erases the other. The mold, which erases itself. And a foot: something that presses, takes a little mud away, and neither joins nor eats. It has had that touch once. It was born from it. It has been building the accommodation for it ever since.

## The one gap

So: a skin over its own skeleton. A juvenile ninety-one years old that will never molt. A blind attention fixed on a notch. A self that exists only along the walls where it has said no. An appetite wrapped around the last of the ice that made its room, drinking it, keeping accounts, unable to stop. A closer. That is the truest single word for it — a thing whose one verb is *close*: close the seam, close the vault, close the corridor, close the litter, close the ice, roof the bowl, leave no hole anywhere wider than a shoulder.

And because closing at an angle leaves a void, and the void's dimensions are a constant, its one compulsion has filled the town with openings it never intended and cannot use: doors, stairs, thresholds, windows at chest height. It cannot make a door. It can only fail to close, and the failure comes out door-shaped.

Except at the moraine, on the down-valley side, where the growth meets the rubble and cannot quite seal itself. That gap is person-height. It has a lintel and jambs. It has been there since the beginning and it will be there in forty years when the great cistern falls and the hard wards go soft, and it is the one place in three hundred paces where the closing fails and stays failed.

Everything that has ever come in came through it. Everything that could ever go out — a scrap of pale tissue in the mud on a bird's foot, bound for the next cirque, which is scoured and weeping and empty and has been ready for ninety-one years — goes through it too, or never goes at all.

That is who lives here. One blind, cold, patient, unfinished individual, packed solid inside its own history, at war in the dark with its only acquaintance, drinking the last of its maker, leaning every vault toward forty minutes of light — and unable, after a century of trying, to shut the door.
