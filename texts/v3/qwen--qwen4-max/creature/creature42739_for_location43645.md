# The Census

It is taken in square meters. Nobody counts heads here; heads are the wrong unit. The question the town asks of its inhabitants is not *how many are you* but *how thin can you be*, and the answer, arrived at over some unrecorded number of generations, is: one animal thick, everywhere, forever.

Twelve thousand springtails to the square meter of felt. Woodlice in overlap. Mites past the point where the number means anything. Ten thousand moths in the Long Hall, hung along the ribs in a single course like a frieze of hands. The loach three deep in the channel and no deeper. Add it up and you get an enormous population and almost no biomass in any given cubic meter — a town with the population of a city and the density of a breath.

The unit that matters is the millimeter: the distance each animal keeps from its neighbor. That millimeter is the load-bearing member. Everything else — the ribs, the vaults, the two-meter walls — is only what the millimeter holds up.

# The Graziers

The mold-lawns are farmed. They grow to specification along the felt's nap, a grey-green crop about a hair deep, and the springtails crop them in lanes so regular that from a distance the walls appear to have been brushed by something with a very steady hand.

They are not eating for themselves. Ungrazed felt mats. It felts *further* — the fibers lock, the nap goes hard and shiny, and a hard patch will not eat sound. A hall with an ungrazed ceiling starts to ring. Ringing precipitates. So the springtails are acoustic maintenance, twelve thousand to the meter, working a crop whose only purpose is to keep the walls soft enough to keep the walls open.

They do not know this. They know the lawn. But the town has arranged itself so that an animal doing the simplest thing it can do — eating what is in front of it, moving on a millimeter — is the reason the Long Hall has not become a block of pith with sixty meters of air-shaped grain in it.

# The Shinglers

In the vertical sweat-seams, where the vascular bundles run close and the wall weeps, the woodlice live stacked like roof tiles, and they are the reason the indoor rain has a timetable.

A seam left to itself will gum and close, and the sweat will back up, and one district will flood while another stays dry and the band will break. The woodlice hold the seams open with the simple architecture of their bodies: each one a small valve, each overlap a gutter, ten million of them shingling the water westward. When the vaults reach saturation together, the rain goes through the districts in a slow band at walking pace because that is the pace at which the woodlice can pass a drop along.

They ride it, some of them. A louse will let go at the top of a seam and come down the twenty-minute rain in the middle of a district it has never seen, and land, and start shingling. This is the whole of migration in the Slack. It is one drop long.

# The Mites

The mites enforce the code.

Not by intent. By appetite. A mite eats whatever has begun to be a heap: a shed skin, a fallen wing, a clot of silt, three springtails dead in a corner, a crumb of lamp-glass scale. Anything that starts to accumulate volume, the mites are on it within the hour, and by morning it is a thin smear and then it is nothing, and the room has not noticed, and the room does not set.

They are the reason a town full of falling moth-down does not develop drifts. A drift is a body. A body is occupancy. Somewhere in the last few thousand years the town and the mite came to an arrangement, and now the mite is the millimeter made animal — a creature whose ecological function is *preventing anything from being anywhere in particular*.

The maintenance schedule in the foreman's alcove lists them under **CLEANING**, which is accurate, and under **STRUCTURE**, which is more accurate.

# The Wage

The crabs sort silt in the west sluice with their mouthparts, and they are paid in warmth.

This is not a metaphor the crabs would recognize but it is the plain mechanics of the thing: the sluice admits the river at a controlled rate, the river is cold enough to ache, and the sorting has to happen where the water comes in. The tissue pays for it by running a warm bundle close to the felt at the sorting-flats — a hand's-width seam at nineteen degrees in a nine-degree world. You can see the wage being paid. You can see it being collected: the crabs in a long shallow crescent along the warm seam, mouthparts working, backs to the heat, and behind them the queue.

There is a queue. There is seniority. The big pale ones with the worn chelae hold the center of the seam and the young ones work the cold ends and rotate in, and the rotation is not fair, and there is nothing anywhere in the Slack that could be called a grievance because a grievance would require a raised voice.

The old crabs carry the map. The graphite schedule is in a hand nobody has seen and the Long Hall is nine paces narrower than it says, but the old crabs know the original width in their legs. They walk it. They will walk out into a corridor and stop at a wall that should not be there yet and stand a while and go back, and the young ones watch, and learn the wall, and in a generation the wall will be simply where the corridor ends.

They will not pass certain stairs in the eastern quarter. That is the other thing they carry.

# The Queue

The loach are blind, and they are stacked nose-to-tail three deep the whole length of Foundry Street, facing upstream, holding station in a hand's-depth of water that would strip the skin off your ankle.

They keep the bed clean. That is the service. But what they are, functionally, is the town's clock. Every forty seconds the press fires and the shiver comes down the channel in rings, and it passes through three hundred meters of fish, and the whole queue flexes once — a single muscle-wave running north to south at the speed of the water — and settles.

Nothing in the Slack has a face, but if you wanted to know what time it was you would put your hand in the channel and count the flexes, and the loach would let you, and they would not move, because moving is not what they do. They have been holding this station since before there was a word for station. Downstream, where the seed-stone goes out on the tide, some of their eggs go too, and there is a coast not yet born that will have blind fish in its rivers, queueing, keeping time for a city that has not been designed.

# The Congregation

The moths hang along the vault-ribs at the height of windows.

Grey, slow, wingspans like open hands, ten thousand of them in the Long Hall in one course, and between them and the floor sixty meters of nothing at all. They do not fly. Flying is a volume; a moth in the air is a small moving room, and the walls read it. So they hang, and they turn very slowly on their tarsi to face the lamp-green, and they wait, and the waiting is essentially the whole of their lives.

Each moth lifts once.

It is the pairing flight, and it is the only event in the Slack that could be called an event: a hand-sized grey thing detaches from a rib and crosses the void, and the bats take it, and the down comes down through the green in a soft collapse that takes a minute and a half to reach the felt. Sometimes two lift and meet. Then there are eggs on the rib, and the down still falls, because one of them is always taken and usually both.

The down is the town's other weather. It lies a scale deep on the floor of the Long Hall, and the mites take it before it can become a drift, and what they don't take the hush-engines spin. There is quiet in the bolts that leaves this place that was, eleven hours ago, a moth deciding to lift.

# The Listeners

The bats do not call. A shout here is a structural event, and a bat that hunted by shouting would have fossilized its own roost in a season.

So they read the sap. They hang with the flat of the wing against a vascular seam and they listen to the hydraulics of two meters of living wall, and when a moth lifts somewhere out in the dark the wall's pressure signature stutters by an amount that would not register on anything a person could build, and the bat is already gone into the void, silent, and the moth is taken with no sound whatsoever, and the only record is the down.

They talk by touch. Palm to felt, a pressure-language below the precipitation threshold, four taps and a hold; the whole society of the Slack's only predators conducted at the volume of a thought. They are the alarm, too. Before the drip-rate falls, before the match-smell, the sap redistributes — the town is moving water away from a district it is about to lose — and the bats read that hours out, and go along the seams tapping, and the crabs leave the sorting-flats and the woodlice let go of their seams early and ride a rain that isn't due.

In the deep halls where the laminae run backwards, the bats hang in tomorrow. They do not appear to find this remarkable. They have been reading next week's hydraulics for a long time. It is why they are never surprised, and why, of everything alive here, they seem the least afraid.

# The Understudy

It is the length of a canal barge and the color of the inside of a mouth, and it walks the districts on legs that fold the wrong way, and it is the only tenant in the Slack that has a volume.

It does not eat animals. It occupies rooms. It comes into the Cistern Quarter and it *stands* — takes a position, holds it, the way something stands in a doorway with a basket on its hip — and the void sets around it, fast and warm and irreversible, wood laid down through sixty cubic meters of air with the shape of the air still legible in the grain. A crab mid-gesture. A moth in amber. Then it walks out through the wall it has just made, which parts for it, and goes east.

Here is what it is. The organism has the specification for a city and no example of one. It has never seen a citizen. So it built one, out of the materials to hand, from the description: something roughly barge-length, mouth-colored, with limbs that fold the way limbs fold if you have only been told about them. And it sent it in to try the rooms.

It is rehearsing occupancy. It stands where a woman will stand at a well. It pauses at the head of the Nine Steps in the attitude of someone setting down a ladder. It is getting it very slightly wrong every time, and every rehearsal costs a district, and it cannot be told, because telling it would be a noise.

The animals flee it not because it hunts them but because it is *the future arriving early, in the wrong body*, and the future is solid.

# The Tenured

Some tenants have been given permanence.

The moths in the amber of the Cistern Quarter, wings still open, the scales still on them. The crab with its mouthparts full of silt it was sorting when the room read it. The sixty cubic meters of air with air's grain in it, which is the strangest object in the Slack and the one the bats will not hang near.

And the stairs in the eastern quarter with the shout still in them. A sustained shout fossilizes; the shout is in there as a hard shape, a knot of precipitate the felt cannot eat, and it *rings*. Walk past and you hear it — a thin fixed note at the edge of hearing, a noise that has been going on for a hundred years in a town whose entire industry is silence. That is why the old crabs won't pass. Not ghosts. Acoustics. A dead shout is the loudest thing in the Slack and the only thing here that is still, in any sense, speaking.

These are the tenured. They will still be residents when the city comes. Everyone else is staff.

# The Foreman

His coat is on its peg and it is warm.

The schedule in his alcove is current, in graphite, in a clean grown hand that gets better as you go east — the entries for next month are more confident than the entries for last week. His tools are worn on the side that has not been used yet. The drip in the Cistern Quarter strikes the water a fifth of a second before it detaches, and the maintenance is done a fifth of a second before the fault, and this is not haunting; it is shiftwork. He is employed on the far side of the works. He comes in from the finished end.

Nobody has seen him because nobody is standing where he will have been. The crabs know his boots. The bats read him in the sap as a small regular demand for water at the Number Three condenser, forty seconds apart, and they tap it to each other, palm to felt, and it means *the shift is on*.

# The Visitors

You are sixty liters of occupancy and you are loud.

A person alone is survivable. Keep walking. Don't stand in the Long Hall admiring the far end you can't see; the felt goes stiff under a stationary boot in about four minutes, and there are boot-shaped inclusions in the eastern quarter, and one corridor where two people stood close together talking for a quarter of an hour and are now a single piece of pith with two hollows in it, facing each other, at the correct distance for conversation.

Two visitors is a crowd. Three is a district.

Outside, on the flats, there is a path worn into the mud by nobody, three-quarters of an hour around, which is exactly how long it takes to walk around a town. It is a habit that got here before the feet. Something circumambulates the Slack at dusk — the not-yet, taking the air, going round the outside of the place where they will live, the way people do. The mud is worn smooth. There is nobody on it. It is the most heavily used thoroughfare in the Slack.

# The Landlord

There isn't one.

The organism is the largest animal in the town and it obeys the code better than anything: two meters of living film against sixty meters of held-open nothing, plastered to the inside of a shape it does not understand. It is not the house. It is the biggest tenant, and it is thin, and it does not know what a market square is. It has the specification. It has never seen the example. It runs the presses and the hush-engines and the mud-battery and it lays down tomorrow's silt behind the Number Three condenser and it sends grey brick with a grain out on the tide to become a coast, and it spends its own void doing it, a hand's breadth a year, and there is no version of the works that does otherwise.

Everything alive here is in the same trade. The springtail cropping its lane, the louse holding its seam, the mite eating the beginning of a heap, the crab at the warm seam, the loach holding station, the moth waiting on its rib at the height of a window nobody has designed, the bat with its palm on the wall reading next week — all of them keeping the negative spaces negative. The millimeter between one mite and the next is a door held open. Twelve thousand doors to the square meter. Ten thousand in the Long Hall alone, hanging in a single course, not flying.

They are not the tenants. They are the ones keeping the place ready for the tenants, and they have been at it a very long time, and the walls are nine paces in from where the old crabs remember them.

When the city comes, it will come into rooms that fit it, and it will not know what held them open, and the last thing the film will do — the springtails, the mites, the moths, the queue of blind fish, the whole enormous disciplined acreage of not being anywhere in particular — is step aside.

Except the ones who can't. They'll be in the walls, wings open, mouthparts full, mid-gesture, at the correct distance for conversation. The only citizens who never have to leave.
