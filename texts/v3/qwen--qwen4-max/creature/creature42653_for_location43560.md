## What lives in the Kettle

Start lower than the birds. Start with the smear.

Wet your thumb on any tread of the staircase and you will come away with a grey film that smells like a cellar, and in that film, under a lens the traders bring up once a decade and lose interest in, there is a country. Diatoms like glass coffins. Green threads that lie down in the current and stand up in the slack. Bacteria in sheets so thin they interfere with light and make the pools go oily-rainbow at the edges, which the aunts can see and you cannot. And grazing over all of it, in numbers that stop meaning anything past a few thousand, the flour: springtails, grey, wingless, the size of a comma, with a spring folded under the belly that fires them a hundred body-lengths into the air when something disturbs the water. They jump constantly. They land in beaks constantly. They breed in eleven days. The whole visible surface of the Kettle, every wet inch of forty terraces, is a pasture that has been mown and manured by the same mouths since the ice let go of the mountain, and the mouths are the pasture's only means of moving uphill.

The sill-folk do not count the flour as an inhabitant. They count it as food, and as weather. But the readers' oldest cords — the ones from the first two centuries, knotted by people who were still guessing — have a knot that appears nowhere later, a small hard double loop, and the gloss written in the margin in charcoal, in a hand nine hundred years dead, says *the small ones also*.

---

The aunts are four pounds of bird in a coat that weighs like twenty.

Under the felt they are not light. The bones have gone dense, marrow-filled, the bones of an animal that has stopped negotiating with the air; the breast is a slab of dark muscle that no longer lifts anything but itself up a wet step. The wings are short, and the flight feathers are worn to awns, and at the wrist of each is the claw — a baby's claw, kept, and it never stops growing. A finger's width a year. It has to be worked off. An aunt that does not build curls her own hands into her palms within two seasons and goes lame, and the lameness is not a punishment or a metaphor, it is keratin. This is the first thing to understand about them and it reorders the rest: the building is not an activity. It is hygiene. It is how they keep their hands. A bird standing idle in the Kettle is a bird slowly closing its fists.

They can fly. Badly, silently, in fog, at dawn, for about thirty paces, and only ever downhill. Every one of them that has tried it past the notch has come down into the warm world with a working body and a ruined mouth, because the language is not in the bird. It is in the bowl — in that particular spoon of rock, that particular five-fold light, those particular forty lips — and a bird raised outside it grows up with a perfect throat plate and nothing to throw. The lowlanders have had four captive aunts in nine hundred years. All four lived long. All four built, obsessively, beautifully, in their cages, and nothing they built meant anything, and one of them, in a barley-town courtyard, kept arranging and rearranging a single white chip at the edge of a water dish for eleven years.

The plate is the other organ. Nacreous keratin over the throat, wetted by a tongue-flick every few seconds, and it must be wet, because a dry plate scatters. Hoarseness here is a physical crust. An aunt carries water in her crop and gives it to another bird's throat, beak to plate, and this is the only touch in the Kettle that is not work, and they do it constantly, in pairs, in threes, in the alcoves, in the dark, and if you have watched it long enough you stop being able to call it grooming.

---

The chicks hatch in the aunts' alcoves, two to a clutch, usually one to a season, grey and blind and enormous in the coat they will molt six times before they can speak.

For their first year their plates are dull — chalk, not nacre — and they cannot throw a reflection at all. They are carried. They are fed a crop-paste of flour and midge-fall so rich it smells like a wound. They watch. And somewhere in the second spring the plate comes in, and the chick, standing on a lip in the eleven-o'clock light with no intention whatsoever, tilts its head to shake water off its face, and puts a bar of light across a stranger's threshold.

The stranger stops. The stranger reads it. Something is said to that bird that it will spend the rest of its life answering.

There is a whole class of reader devoted to this. The milk-readers, they are called, dismissively, by the other readers; they keep a separate cord, nine hundred years of it, nothing in it but accidents — every first word thrown by every chick that any reader has been in a dark cell to see, upside down, dimmed, in a black basin. Some of the milk-readers hold that this cord is the only honest scripture in the undercroft, because it is the only record of speech before the speaker knew there was a grammar. The other readers hold that it is a record of babies falling over. They have been arguing about it for four centuries in a language of hands, which means they have been arguing about it silently, at meals, with their fingers, while looking at each other.

And some chicks never come in. The plate stays chalk. Perhaps one in thirty. These are the mute, and the Kettle has a use for them that is not cruel and not kind: they carry. They are the backbone of the tide, the strongest and steadiest of the relay, beak-to-beak up forty steps, and because they have nothing to say they build without interruption and their hands stay short and their walls are the truest in the bowl. Other birds put their words into the mute ones' walls. It is a collaboration with no word for it in either language. A mute aunt will spend her whole life as the hands of a bird who is famous for a shadow.

---

Here are three of them, out of twenty thousand, as the cords have them.

*Third Lip*, called *Little Interruption* in the older knots, female, fifty-one years old, has spent thirty-eight of them on a grey chip the size of a lentil at the third terrace below the back wall. She has moved it, by the best cord count, eleven thousand and some times. Twice it has been right. The second time was nineteen years ago and lasted four minutes and put a bar of shadow across the threshold of a bird three terraces down who had been insufferable about apertures for a decade, and the bowl stopped work to look at it, and eleven hundred structures were dismantled by their own builders so they could come and stand nearer, and the tide that evening went uphill late and the lower district drowned, and nobody has ever recorded that as a loss. She is going matte. Her plate is chalky at the edges now and she wets it forty times a minute and it does not take. She has begun to tap more than she throws. In two years she will be an old bird in an alcove, speaking only in the intimate register, to whoever comes close enough to hear it, which is the whole of what old age is here.

*The Crooked Knot* — not a name any bird would recognize, a name the readers gave to a behavior — is a lineage of liars. A wall built to throw light that says *the sluice at the ninth lip is open* when the sluice at the ninth lip is shut. Water diverted by a false word. The town has a knot for it, a loop with a slipped hitch, and it appears in the cord about once a generation, and the readers cannot agree whether it is fraud or irony or the beginning of a literature.

And in the fourth alcove from the warm vein there is an old female whose throat has been fouled by a fly that lays in the plate, and who cannot wet herself anymore, and who is therefore, in the plainest terms, silenced. Four other birds keep her throat wet. They bring crop-water and they dress the plate with their tongues and they take turns, and on a good day, in good light, she says one thing. It is usually about the sluices. The readers have eleven cords of her late words and treat them the way lowlanders treat the last pages of a philosopher.

---

At night the terraces give back their blue-green shine, and you have been told this is the day leaking out of the stone.

The aunts see it. Their eyes go further into the violet than yours and the glow is not weak to them, and what they see, spread over forty lips in the dark, is the *shape of the day's light* — the whole afternoon's throwing and shadowing, laid down in the travertine as a faint afterimage and released over eight hours. The stone plays the day back. Slowly, dimly, out of order, without the birds in it. They lie in their nine-hour cells and read the town's own memory off the floor.

This is also why there is a district of nothing but stacked pebbles arranged by color, and why it is not a midden. Every stone in the Argument has been in a beak. Some have been in every beak — passed the full width of the bowl, up the tide and down again, forty steps and back, ten thousand times. The round ones, the ones worn glassy, are sorted by hue and set in rows in the shallowest tread at the eastern end where the light is worst and nothing can be said, and the readers' best guess, held loosely, is that it is a lexicon. A dictionary of the town's own hands. A place where the words that have been used most are put where they cannot be used at all.

---

They live thirty to fifty years and then they are carried to the back crack, above the warm vein, and laid in the flow.

The stone takes a season. Travertine lays itself down over bone the way it lays itself over a doorway, and the terraces are therefore full of aunts — a femur in the knee-high lip of the eleventh step, a skull in the fossil of a door, a whole folded bird somewhere in the crooked tower, which is why some readers cannot look at the Old Sentence without their hands shaking. The mountain's balance is not an abstraction here. The loss and the laying have matched for four hundred centuries and the laying is partly made of the ones who did the losing. When the floor glows at night it is not only the day coming back out.

---

The listener is not a monster. It is a pair of birds, sometimes three, with a nest on the dry side of the chimney where the rime does not reach, and it is as precarious as everything else here.

Its chicks are noisy. Most of them die of it — of the mother, who takes what makes a noise, and has no exemption in her for kin. The ones that survive have learned, in eleven days, in the dark, on a ledge, the same lesson the whole bowl has learned in forty thousand years, and they learn it faster, because they get only one draft.

The face-disc is a barn owl's face on a harrier's body and the eyes are wide and milky and probably nearly useless. It does not hear words. Words are silent; words are light and stone. What it hears is a pebble set down wrong, a shim dropped, a beak clipping a lip, the tick of a wall failing — and it comes down the wind on felt wings and takes the error.

Which means, and the readers worked this out about three hundred years ago and have not stopped arguing about it since, that the predator is the grammar. One grammar, unbroken, that no aunt knows and no aunt can learn whole, and every bird raised outside the bowl is mute — because for four hundred centuries anything ambiguous, anything clumsy, anything that had to be *fumbled* to be understood, got eaten. The listener has been editing. The Kettle's clarity is a scar.

There is a cord — number four hundred and eleven, in the second press, badly kept — that records a listener taken by a fall of rock in a winter gallery, and the knot for its throat is the knot for a plate. Under the chin feathers, vestigial, chalk. The gloss says: *it was one of us and it made a sound*. Two of the nine readers regard this as the central text. The other seven regard it as a mistranscription by a man who had been in a dark cell for thirty years. Nobody has been able to check. Nobody wants to go and look at a listener's throat.

---

In winter the bowl fills with ice and the town goes down.

The galleries are the swallowed work of forty thousand years, doorways gone to lumpiness, chambers the size of a bread oven sealed and reopened and sealed, and in them the whole population speaks in taps and nothing else for five months, beak on stone, under the hearing floor, in a dark where no reflection is possible and no shadow can be cast. The winter dialect is not a reduced version of the summer one. It is a different grammar with the same words, and the cords for it use a different knot-set entirely, and readers who specialize in it go deaf in a particular way — they lose the ability to hear speech, having spent their lives listening for ticks under sleet.

And there are aunts that never come up. A few hundred, in the deepest galleries, smaller, plates gone entirely chalk, eyes fine, hands worn to the quick. They tap. They have been tapping down there through every summer for longer than the sill-folk have been on the mountain, and at the freeze and the thaw, when the two towns pass each other on the stairs, the surface birds and the under birds stand beak to beak and tick and do not understand one another.

The Old Sentence is at the top of the town, cemented, unreadable, and everything now is arguably a footnote to it. But there is a school of readers — young, insufferable about apertures — who hold that it is not the beginning of anything. That it is a *reply*. That the sentence began below, in the dark, in taps, and came up into the light, and got loud without ever making a sound, and that the under-aunts are not a degenerate remnant but the original speakers, still going, still waiting for an answer to something said before the ice left.

---

Thirty-four people. Seven houses in the eastern undercut. Felt on everything, including the hinges, including the handles, including the soles of the feet.

Nine readers, and the best of them are nearly blind, because a life in a dark cell with a pinhole ruins you for anything else, and because the white hour catches everyone eventually. The oldest, who is sixty-eight and sees only shapes, reads by having the basin described to her by a child, and her cords are considered the finest of the last century. Eleven sluice-keepers with felt-wrapped antler picks, who walk the forty lips at noon in bone goggles, in the aphasic hour, when the town cannot speak and therefore cannot be interrupted, and who are the only large moving things in the Kettle that the aunts have ever agreed to ignore. Sweepers, felt-makers, two infants, a man who came back up the chimney after eleven years down in the barley country and has never been comfortable again, and a woman whose whole office is to say *no* — to the cress day, to a marriage, to a new gallery, to a trade — because someone must, and it must always be the same someone, and she is hated with a formality that is almost affection.

Their liturgy is two lines and it is not a solution to anything.

*It will hold.*
*Keep it anyway.*

They say it at the ninth lip every noon, in hands, with the picks in their belts, standing in water that has been in balance for four hundred centuries and would go on without them, and they know the arithmetic, and they keep the sluices anyway, and this is the central religious problem of the Kettle and nobody has solved it. The best answer anyone has offered is that they are not the town's keepers. They are the only part of it that can leave. Felt goes down the chimney. Cord goes down the chimney. Once a year the traders come up and everyone goes out through the notch and *speaks*, out loud, with voices, and it is the ugliest day of the year, and it is also the only day on which any human word is permitted at all — so the whole year's law, courtship, quarrel, inheritance, joke and grievance is compressed into twelve hoarse hours, and every child in the Kettle is conceived in the week after the shouting, and every marriage in nine hundred years has been argued in a voice like a gate.

---

There is a girl, seven years old, third child of the Basin house, who has been building.

Small cells at the eastern lip, in the bad light, where nothing can be said. She has been doing it since she could carry a pebble, the way children down in the barley country draw on walls. Her hands are already worn short. Nobody stopped her, because it is hygiene, because it is what hands are for.

Last spring the town answered her.

A wall went up nine paces west of her cell, angled to catch the eleven-o'clock light and put it across her threshold, and it stayed up eleven hours, which is longer than anything stands, and then it was taken down and built again a hand's width over. The readers have it in cord. There are three readings. One says it is a correction of her grammar. One says it is a joke about her mother. One says — and this is the reading nobody will knot, though two of them have privately made the loop and hidden it — that the town has been waiting forty thousand years for a mouth that could go down the mountain and come back, and has just said *you*.

She has no idea. She is seven. She has moved her chip four hundred times and it has not been right yet.

Above her the seeps weep and the flour jumps and lands in beaks, the old female in the fourth alcove is having her throat dressed so she can say one thing about the sluices, the listener is quartering the rim on wings you feel before you hear, and twenty thousand pale bodies are building the sentence they are inside of, in a language none of them knows, on a floor made of their grandmothers, in a bowl that eats the sky.

At dusk the light goes long and the whole place throws one beam out through the notch, forty miles across the cloud sea, and is gone before anyone down there can point.

Then the stone begins to give the day back, and the taps come up under everything, and something small at the eastern lip sets a grey chip down in the wrong place, and something nine paces west considers it.
