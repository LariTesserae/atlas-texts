## The Tenants of Ninth Cut

**The nap.** Start with the smallest, because everything else is a consequence of it.

The film is not quite alive and has never pretended to be. It has no cells that anyone has found, no spores, no parents, no lineage. Two panels a hundred metres apart will bloom the same grey-blue on the same afternoon and be entirely unrelated to one another. The nap does not reproduce. It *arrives*, wherever water meets a bond that has just been broken, in the way that frost arrives, or a smell. Its genealogy is not a family tree. It is a harvest schedule.

What it eats is wanting. Fresh fracture is a surface full of unsatisfied hands, and the nap grazes those hands quiet: peroxide, hydrogen, the short angry intermediates, all of it folded into a slick that thickens to pigeon's-neck iridescence and then, having nothing left to want, sets to varnish and cracks. A single bloom is therefore a ring. It satisfies the ground beneath itself and travels outward, dying at its own centre, so an ungrazed panel carries expanding hoops of colour like something dropped in oil. The rings run fastest along scratch lines, where the damage is deepest and the appetite keeps renewing. This is why the frost grows first along the scratches too, and why the wall, at dawn, in fur, looks written on. The scrapers read the rings the way a forester reads stumps. They can tell you the hour a face was made by the colour at its edge, and they are never wrong by more than a shift.

The nap is the only primary producer in the seam and it is not, strictly speaking, an organism. Everything that eats is downstream of a chemistry. Keep that in mind when you get to the people.

---

**The grit.** Thumbnail-sized, blind, pale as boiled rice, and armoured in chips of the wall they have already eaten. They cannot make a shell. They cement sinter to their own backs, particle by particle, and so a grit carries its itinerary: the hard silver fleck of a face quick on Tuesday, the dove-grey scale of somewhere spent last week. Old ones are humped and heavy and the colour of the whole slot averaged out. Young ones are nearly translucent and have been nowhere.

They are not slow animals. They are *stopped* animals, which is different. In a crack, dried, a grit can wait out four months of dead wall and resume mid-step when the panel opposite blooms. The walking is the expensive part. Thirty metres of matte stone between booms is a desert crossing, and they do it in files, and most of them do not arrive, and the freeze cements the failures where they fell. Come back in a season and those failures are part of the wall. Come back in a year and they have been through the crush-house, and their shells have fractured fresh, and the nap is blooming on the hunger of the things that bloomed on the hunger of the stone. The mountain is partly made of its grazers. Nobody finds this remarkable except visitors.

Children keep grit in boxes with a chip of quick face. The chip goes dead in a week. This is, for most people in Ninth Cut, the first death they are responsible for, and there is a standard way of doing it: you put the box on the wet-hour grating at noon and let the overturn take it.

---

**The plumber.** Long, white, unhurried, soft-skinned, from the sump. There may be three. The crews measure it in arms and the measurements do not agree, and the disagreement is now sixty years old and has its own file.

It hunts by reading the shiver in standing water, which is why the second rule exists. It does not need freshness; it needs grit, and grit needs freshness, so it is one remove from the boom and can fast a season without inconvenience. It is the only animal in the city with a past. Everything else here is a week old at most and frantic about it. The plumber is old and blind and has never once been in a hurry.

It is also the only thing in Ninth Cut that climbs. Water goes down, spall goes down, flour goes down, light is handed down, people spend their lives descending stair by stair toward the black lake. The plumber comes *up* a held column, and the rope bulges and the bulge travels, and the wetting crew steps back and lets it pass and logs the time. There is a bounty and it has gone unclaimed for four years, not because the thing is hard to find but because it is the only neighbour, and because a slot with nothing in it but people is a worse place, and everyone has quietly agreed not to say so out loud. Some crews leave gleanings at the sump lip. It is carried in the books as a liability, amortised at one finger per annum.

There is a fifth rule, unwritten, taught after the four: knock the column before you dip.

---

**The people.** Eleven hundred, in a volume cut for thirty thousand. The discrepancy is not a tragedy; it is a survey error made in a boom year by men who are now niches. The ledger's population is nearer thirty thousand, because the ledger counts claims, and claims include forward area — faces not yet cleaved, panels that will exist next spring, the reversionary interests of the dead. Most of the citizens of Ninth Cut are hypothetical. It is a city where the not-yet outnumber the actual, and where the actual are therefore very careful with each other.

You can sort them by hand. Wetting crews have white fingers and a cold sleeve that never quite leaves the forearm; they hold their arms out from their bodies at rest, the way you'd carry something you didn't want to touch. Tack crews are built through the shoulder and listen with their heads tilted, because they know what a pad sounds like when it is going off, and a wrong *tock* is the only noise in the city that will make a stranger run. Scraper hands carry a grey crescent under the nails that no amount of washing shifts, and which is, chemically, still hungry. Crush-house keepers are few and stand a little apart, and speak louder than the law allows, and are forgiven it.

They are a people with a tiny vocal range and an enormous manual one. Hand-talk across the void carries insult, flirtation, grief, and the entire shift schedule; the shuttered lamps carry the rest, green-white-green, all day, the room blinking at itself. Children raised to whisper have to be *taught* to shout, at about twelve, in a booked cell, and most of them find their own full voice frightening and come out quiet and shaken and go back in the following week. The shout-room slate has a standing booking for adolescents. It has a standing booking for the bereaved. It is the busiest room in the city and it is the only room lined with anything soft.

They move house three or four times in a life, following the fracture front, and their names move with the claims, so an old person has four names and the ledger-house keeps the concordance. Marriages merge area. The niches in the cemetery are labelled in plain survey hand with a grid reference and a date, because that is what a person is here: a place where the city was attached, for a while, and then was not.

The old ones breathe like people walking uphill. They call it the shine. We will come back to that.

---

**The hold.** Now the correction that matters.

The standing water is held by a field that nobody in Ninth Cut can explain and everybody can repair. Ropes of it, thigh-thick, faintly blue, hanging from cistern to wetting-head with no pipe, sweating, riming, closing behind your wrist. There are manuals. The manuals are incomplete and in places plainly wrong, and the repairs work anyway. A six-year-old can close a rope. A six-year-old cannot tell you how, and neither can her grandmother, and if you sit them side by side and film their wrists you will find the same eleven movements, none of which appear in any manual, transmitted hand-over-hand for sixty years without ever once being written down.

This is the largest living thing in the seam. Its body is eleven hundred pairs of hands. Its habitat is a crack. It has no brain, no opinion, no name in law, and one behaviour: it restores. When a rope sags, three people converge who were not called and did not look up. When a slab lets go and the room says its one enormous word, every column in the slot shivers at the same instant, before anyone has moved — the whole city's water flinches, and then the hands arrive.

It can be sick. After a bad death the ropes run slack for four or five days and the wetting logs show it, a measurable sag, and nobody comments, and the tack crews work double. It is density-dependent: it needs bodies within call, which is why a city of eleven hundred strung out along sixty metres of gallery has bells, and a noon hat-hour, and a shout-room queue, and a second bell that everyone obeys without knowing why. Those are not customs. They are circulation. The customs exist to make people coincide often enough that the water stays up.

There is a number below which it fails. Everyone knows the number. It is posted beside the tide.

In the books it is carried as plant, on a depreciation schedule, and the schedule is the apprenticeship.

---

**The likenesses.** The walls are mirrors and the mirrors tarnish, and tarnish is not even. It thickens where breath fell, where a body stood and blocked the cold, where a lamp was shuttered too long against one spot. So a panel a day old holds smears, and a panel three days old holds *shapes*, and a panel a week old holds nothing at all. The wall is a camera with a seven-day exposure and no film but its own dulling.

Most of what it holds is hands. Don't touch a quick face with bare skin: you leave part of yourself bonded to it, and you kill the nap where you touched, and the killing shows. There are handprints all over Ninth Cut in negative bloom, thousands of them, some very small, at heights that tell you exactly how old the child was.

Some of it is faces. A person who stands close to a fresh panel for an hour — waiting out a freeze, sleeping off a shift, grieving where they shouldn't — leaves an oval of absence that comes up satin over two days and is gone by the following week. There is a form in the ledger-house for reporting one. Green paper. *Image found in situ: gallery, panel, metre, date, believed to be.* The scrapers will not take a likeness off. They will work around it and let it die on its own, and there is a word for a man who scrapes a face, and the word is not used lightly.

So the city has three populations. Eleven hundred living. Four hundred and some in the niches. And the likenesses, which are the most numerous and the shortest-lived, and which are the reason that rounding a neighbour's panel is the worst crime in the code. It is not only that you have taken his area. You have taken his week. You have gone along the wall with warm water and erased everyone who was still on it.

The man from the spring case has his name on his door in scraper-tracks, which is how children write, and the gallery behind the door is spent and plugged, and there is nothing on it now, and nothing will be.

---

**The landlord.** And last, the one that lives here longest and owns the most.

Nothing in Ninth Cut is permitted to weather. Tools, stairs, terraces, claims, arguments — all of it kept young by force, re-quickened every ninety days, *tock, tock*, pause, *tock*. But there are three places where age is allowed to accumulate, and they are the plugs, the stone wool of the shout-rooms, and the lungs.

The plugs first. Behind every spent gallery is a mass of sintered flour, round, dull, dead, patient, holding the stairs that go nowhere. Downrange are the older cuts, full of the same stuff, kilometres of it. It does nothing. It wants nothing. It is simply what everything here becomes, sitting quietly in the dark behind the city's back, at the same temperature as the city, moving up the seam at exactly the city's speed, because the city is what it is following. They call it the landlord, and the joke — one of the jokes that isn't funny — is that it never comes for the rent, because it doesn't need to. Everything is leasehold. The reversion is automatic.

The wool second. Sixty years of shouting absorbed into stone lining that is never renewed and never quickened: every argument, every unbearable twelve-year-old, every hour booked by someone who could not do it anywhere else. It is the only soft thing in the seam and it is the only thing that has been allowed to get full. People put their palms on it on the way out. Nobody has a theory about this.

And the lungs. Flour that fresh is still hungry; it goes in and keeps working. Which means that inside an old cutter there is a surface that never goes dead, because the body keeps it warm and keeps it wet and keeps it moving, and the nap blooms there, and is grazed, and blooms again, on a face that was made in a crush-house forty years ago and has never had the decency to tarnish. It is the only permanently quickened panel in Ninth Cut. It is, by the ledger's own arithmetic, the richest claim in the city. There is no form for it. It is not in the books.

That is what the landlord holds here: not the walls, which the crews keep stealing back, but a few hundred hectares of interior surface, folded into the chests of men who joke about it and wear the mask anyway and go up the stair slowly, resting at the fourth landing, breathing like people walking uphill. When they die they are plugged into a spent gallery, and for some days afterward the flour in them goes on wanting.

At the ninth hour the lamps go to half and the freeze comes down the slot and the fur starts along the scratch lines. Somewhere below, water finds a face made this afternoon and begins to take it apart. Somewhere above, on the fourth landing, an old man stops to rest, and inside him the crop comes in, quietly, on schedule, in the dark, at the same sound as rice poured slowly, as applause heard through a wall.
