## The teeth

Start with the smallest mouth, because everything else here is built out of it.

A scraper's shell is not limestone. It cannot be. The water on this plain is hungry — carbon dioxide out of the caves, sulfuric acid where the sulfide meets the air — and a calcite shell would go the way of the mountains, in solution, in a season. So the scrapers build in opal. They take the silica that the chert knuckles give up grain by grain and they lay it down in ribbed, translucent plates, faintly banded, the color of weak tea held up to a lamp. When a scraper dies its shell does not dissolve. It falls into a fissure and lies there with a hundred thousand others, and the fissure floors are white with them, and in the deep galleries the old compacted drifts of them have gone to chert.

The knuckles standing proud of the pavement are the same stuff. They are older scraper-shells, or the shells of things that were shaped like scrapers before there were scrapers. Every blade on this plain is cut from the compressed dead of the animal it is used to open.

The teeth are iron. The pasture is iron and the scrapers pull it out of the film and lay it down in ribbons of goethite, black, harder than the opal, harder than anything else that gets made here. The ribbon wears out at the tip and grows from the root and the worn rows drop away, and a fist-sized scraper sheds a thimble of tooth-meal in a year, and there are more scrapers on this plain than there are grains in a thimble, so the clay pockets are full of it, grey-black, faintly glittering, the only abrasive in the world.

This matters more than it sounds. The plug in a stop-house is a ton of dressed limestone that has to seat into a limestone throat well enough to stop air. Stone will not grind stone into that fit. It is done with tooth-meal, wetted, worked with a chert lap for weeks, and the last eighth of an inch of a good plug is cut by the shed teeth of the thing the plug's owner eats.

The whole inherited craft of quiet rests on a grazer's worn-out mouth.

## The seam

Pry the lid off a fissure and the city is arranged in layers, like a argument.

At the top, air and sweet rainwater. Two feet down, the sour water: salt, old, nine degrees, sulfide-heavy, come up from the flooded storeys. Where they touch there is a seam a finger thick, and in the seam the film is not a film but a felt, rust and yellow and white, thick enough to hold a print. Everything in the fissure is positioned with reference to that seam. The scrapers crowd its upper lip. Below it, in the sour water, live the threaders.

A threader is a worm the length of a forearm and the thickness of a bootlace, and it does the one clever thing anybody does here. It lies across the seam. Its tail is in the sulfide and its head is in the oxygen and it pumps one to the other, all day, for years, and inside its body the bacteria that can live on that transaction make sugar out of nothing at all. It is not eating. It is plumbing. It anchors its tail with a crown of a hundred rust-gold filaments, and those filaments can be cropped without killing the animal, and they grow back in a season, and they are the only soft fiber in the world.

Anchor-silk. Four hundred crowns to a cloak. It is spun by the old, who cannot hear it but can feel it, and it is the closest thing here to a treasure that is not made of stone or silence.

The threaders thicken the seam by working it, so the film is richest where they are densest, so the scrapers are densest there, so the listeners hunt there. Every good seep on this plain is good because something underground is pumping.

And the phosphorus. Limestone has almost none; the red residue has a little, apatite out of the vanished mountains; bone has a great deal. A human body, gone down a shaft and picked over by the black threads, is worth about a year of thick film in a yard of fissure. So the dead are fertilizer, and the burial shafts are named and inherited, and the richest seeps on the plain are the ones over the oldest graves. The families that hold the good plugs hold the old shafts too, and the two wealths compound, and have been compounding for a long time.

Raiding here is not cattle-raiding. It is lifting bone out of somebody else's shaft to spread on your own seep. There is a word for it that means both *theft* and *planting*, and no way to say one without the other.

Children learn water before they learn the chord: drink from the block-tops, from the rain pools, never from anywhere the food grows. Never drink where there is food. It is the first sentence anybody says to a child with a rod against a jaw, four taps and a pause, and it is repeated at every seep for the first ten years of a life.

## The slow ones

The combs make no sound because sound is a thing that gets you eaten, and because they have nothing to say.

They are barometers with legs. The fan is a pressure organ as much as a filter; the fringes swell and stiffen as the air thins, and a comb knows the indraw is coming a full day before the cairns change their tone, and it starts walking then, a hundred yards a day, three legs like wet bone, arriving where the fog will be. They have been right about the weather for longer than there have been people to be wrong about it.

Inside the fan, in the fringe-bases, they brood. The spores they filter are not all digested; the black threads' spores must pass a comb's gut to germinate at all, and the young are fed on the ones that come back out. A comb is a walking nursery for a fungus that cannot travel any other way. The pasture needs the trees. There are no trees.

They are safe from everything. They put down almost no weight per foot, they have no voice to falter, they never hurry, and to a listener's dish or a blank's reading of the stone they are simply not there. The largest visible animal on the plain is invisible to every predator on it, and its whole strategy is to be too slow to be interesting.

When one dies — and they die on something like a schedule, a hundred and some years, standing up, the fan coming down over a season like a hand closing — it is a public event. People walk two days to a dead comb. The hide is tenting. The gut is cord. The legs are the best acoustic material in the world: hollow, straight-grained, dry, ringing. Every talking rod on the plain is a piece of a comb that stopped.

You do not kill one. There is no law about it. There is no need for a law about it. It would be like cutting down the last of something.

## What silence is for

The listeners have a dish where a face should be, and they hunt by the hole. Ten thousand scrapers rasping evenly; one faltering; the dish finds the falter; the stilt legs come in.

Except the scrapers clamp, and the clamping spreads, and the hole grows faster than the listener can walk, and by the time the dish arrives there is nothing in the hole but silence, thirty yards of it, and every scraper in it locked down and unfindable and gone.

Two things have come out of this.

The first is the gleaner. It is a listener lineage that has let the dish shrink to a nub and put everything into its forelegs, which are long and pale and split at the tip into a fringe of feelers. It does not listen for a falter. It puts a foot down *wrong on purpose*, casts a hole, and then walks into the hole it made and takes the clamped scrapers by touch. Silence hides you from an ear. It does not hide you from a hand, and a clamped scraper cannot run, because running is the thing it has given up. There are seeps on the eastern plain where the gleaners have worked so long that the scrapers there clamp for a heartbeat and slide, clamp and slide, a stuttering, shuffling, ugly compromise that costs them a third of their feeding.

The second is the false ring. Scrapers on the well-hunted seeps have begun to pause together — not in fear, at random, in ragged synchrony, so that holes open across the film with nothing inside them. Rings of quiet with no predator at the center. The gleaners walk into empty hole after empty hole. The listeners' dishes swing at nothing all day.

So the hiss at the top of the world's sound is not a hiss. It has a grammar. It has holes in it that mean *there* and holes that mean *nothing*, and the people who have learned to look at sound read the difference the way you read a face.

The blank is the third thing, and it is not an answer to any of this; it is a refusal of the whole conversation. Low, mineral-colored, voiceless, reading the stone through its belly. It does not hear the scrapers and does not need to. It takes the loud and the deaf and the clamped alike.

You know a listener is coming because the quiet moves toward you, a crescent, an edge, a wavefront.

You know a blank is near because a hole appears with no edge at all. Nothing spreads. A patch of the plain simply stops. Children are taught the two shapes before they are taught to piss in a fissure: *if the quiet comes toward you, run. If the quiet is just there, you are already too close.*

## Ears, voice, hands

A life here has three parts and everyone knows which part they are in.

Children are the ears. They hear the fliers' band, they hear a cairn's third come in under the Steady One at four miles, they hear the difference between a ring and a crescent. A household's hearing is a depreciating asset and the phrase for prosperity is *we still have ears*, meaning we still have children under fifteen.

Adults are the voice. They travel in the indraw, they trade, they shout wastefully, they marry, they hold the plugs.

The old are the hands. Air-hearing goes in the thirties, as it goes everywhere, and what is left is the stone. They lean on the pavement with their rods and their jaws and they talk for hours, and they spin anchor-silk, and they read the tally slabs with a fingertip because the old strokes are shallower than a rain-comb's runnel and no eye can find them. The last few years of any panel are sharp and legible; everything before your grandmother is tactile. History here is something you feel with your hand and cannot show anybody.

Speech itself has no vowels. Vowels are high and airy and the roar eats them; what survives the journey through a jaw and twenty feet of limestone is low, clicked, pitched, intervalled. Meaning lives in the gaps between taps and in the ratio of one drummed pitch to another. A person's name is a rhythm. A family's name is a note. A full name is both, and you can put a stranger's entire identity into their teeth in about two seconds.

You give the note first. Always the note first, because the note tells the other jaw whether you are kin.

Marriage is forbidden inside your own note and inside its octave, which is to say the incest taboo here is a consonance rule, and the arguments about it are arguments about tuning, and there are families on the western plain who have not spoken for six generations over whether a fifth is close enough to be a cousin.

And speech needs one stone. A fissure breaks the transmission dead — a grike six inches wide is a wall you cannot talk through. So a household is a block, and a conversation is an arrangement of bodies on one block, and to talk across a fissure you must lay a rod over it, and who may lay a rod, and where, and when, is the whole of local politics. There are speaking-stones at the boundaries between families, neutral slabs, and to drum at a man from your own block is the worst thing you can do to him short of lifting his mother out of her shaft.

Two families cannot marry on a block that will not hold them. Weddings are, first, a search for a table of limestone big enough, and the famous ones are named after the rock. *They were married on the Long Stone.* Divorce is stepping across a fissure and taking your rod with you.

Everyone carries a noisemaker. Everyone. A rattle of opal shells on the wrist, a clapper of comb-bone, something that ticks when you move, because a person who has gone quiet on this plain is either a predator or dead and there is no third reading. To be silent is the one obscenity. The deaf wear the loudest. Hospitality begins with putting a rattle in a guest's hand.

And there are people who have taken the rattles off.

They exist. They are known about. They live at the edges of the plain in the rubble where the blocks are broken and no rod will carry, and they have understood exactly what the blank has understood, and they can go anywhere on this plain unmarked, and they do. Nobody hunts them. You cannot find them. The families' whole defense is that everybody knows the names of the ones who have gone quiet, and that the names are drummed at every gathering, note and rhythm, so that if a hole opens with no edge in it, everyone within thirty miles knows whose it is.

The poor sleep in the roar with opal shells jammed in their ears, because a shell that fits a scraper's foot fits a human ear canal, and it is the only quiet they can afford. It costs them their hearing early and it costs them the fear-hole, and they are taken more often. The rich sleep behind a ton of seated rock and can hear a child's voice at fifty. That is what the money buys. Not comfort. The ability to still be listening when your grandchildren are born.

## The bead

There is a thing that lives in people's heads here and it is not a metaphor.

It comes out of the film — a larva the size of a grain of tooth-meal, taken in raw at the seep, which is how everyone eats at the seep, face down, because the film is where all the vitamins are and cooked film is nothing. It encysts against the jaw joint. It lays down a bead of opal about the size of a peppercorn, and it grows a fine tether of tissue into the fat behind the ear, and it makes the stone loud.

That is what it does. It couples the jaw to the inner ear. Rod-speech works because of it. The clicking of your neighbor's teeth arrives in your skull as something almost like a voice, and it would not, without the bead.

The price is the air. The tether stiffens as the bead grows, and the air-conducted world goes grey and then goes away, over about fifteen years, and everyone here has known since forever that people go deaf in their thirties the way teeth go, and everyone here is right, and nobody until very recently had a reason to wonder whether it had to be that way.

It passes at the seep, and it passes another way. Heads together, jaws on the same stone, mouths close, twenty years of conversation. The posture that the bead makes possible is the posture that transmits it. Intimacy is the vector. You cannot talk to your children without giving them the thing that will let you talk to them and then take away everything else they will ever hear.

When a person dies, the bead is taken out first, before the body goes down the shaft. It is drilled and set into the end of a rod. So the rod you put your teeth against is comb-bone from an animal that died standing up, with your grandmother's ear at the far end of it, and when you drum your family's note into the pavement you are doing it through her deafness.

Some people go the other way. They stop wearing the shells, they seek the roar, they sit at the throat-mouths in the outbreath for hours with their hands flat on the block, and they let it come fast. These are the readers. By forty they hear nothing in the air at all and by forty-five they can feel a listener's footfall through a hundred yards of pavement and tell you which leg it favors. They cannot hear a shouted warning. They can feel a fear-hole open under their palms like a cold spot.

They are fed by everyone and they belong to no household and they are the only people who go down into the throats.

## The chord is a list

Because someone has to check whether the notes are alive.

A valve booms every twelve or fifteen seconds, and the boom is not a signal. It is the pump. The chambered body draws a slug of water and air through itself with each stroke and filters it, and the note is the sound of the animal eating. Silence is starvation. That is why the plugged one took nine days.

The readers go down with a lamp of rendered fat and put a jaw or a palm against the wall and count. Twelve. Twelve. Twelve. Alive. And they come up and say so, and the family that holds that throat is confirmed in its holding for another generation.

What they have been reporting, for four generations now, is a slow subtraction.

The plain is lowering. A fingernail's thickness a century, and the water table with it, and the upper galleries dry out one after another, and a valve whose chambers have gone to air dies as surely as one that has been plugged, only slower, and with nobody to blame. The dry high galleries are full of them: house-sized mouths going down into bodies of ribbed opal, empty, scalloped, dead since before the tally slabs. There are eleven notes in the taught chord of the western plain. Four of them are cairns now. Two more are cairns that were built so long ago that everyone has forgotten they are cairns.

The fillers are the masons who cut a cavity and a lip to replace a note that has stopped. They are the most skilled people alive and they are not entirely respectable. There is an old argument, never settled, about whether a made note is a lie — whether you may navigate by a stack of slabs pretending to be an animal — and the practical answer has been yes for so long that the argument is now a thing you say to apprentices to make them think.

The Missing Mile cannot be filled. Nobody can build a cavity that big or a note that low. And the family that plugged it — new to the western plain, four generations ago, unable to tell a hole from an animal — did not die out or get driven off. Their shaft went down beside the corpse. A body that size is a hundred years of phosphorus in one place. The film there is a finger thick and glowing and has been for four generations, and their seep feeds half the western plain, and they hold three good plugs, and their children have ears into their fifties.

Everyone eats a little of it. Everyone hums the gap.

## What the valve is

Here is the thing the readers know and the fillers argue about and nobody has proved.

In the flooded galleries, at low water, there are fist-sized limpets with a hole in the apex of the shell. Opal, ribbed, tea-colored, banded exactly like a scraper. They clamp to the wall of a throat and they do not let go, and if you take one off it dies, and if you leave it, it grows, and it keeps growing, and it does not stop for three hundred years, and its shell lines the throat and its body goes down into the rock in chambers and its radula becomes a filter and its silence becomes a boom.

The valve is a scraper that never unclamped.

The whole chord — the low D of the Home stack, the third from the north, the Steady One that everybody hates because it sits in the band everybody needs — is made of animals whose species' entire defense is to stop, and hold, and go quiet, and be invisible. The fear response of a thumbnail grazer, extended past the end of fear, past the end of a life, into geology, is the loudest thing on this plain and the only thing anyone can find their way home by.

And the technology of the people is the same move. A ton of dressed limestone lowered onto a lever to make a hole stop speaking. They have built their houses out of the scraper's flinch. They have made rooms of it, and wealth of it, and privacy, and the only place where a voice can be heard, and once, four generations ago, a family new to the western plain did it to something enormous because they were afraid of the noise, and it died of being made invisible, in nine days, and everybody within thirty miles heard the chord break.

There is a rule about the fist-sized ones with the hole in the apex. It is not a law and it is not a story. It is operational, and it is taught to children at the low-water galleries with a chert blade in their hand: if it comes off, eat it. If it does not come off, leave it.

They still walk the Missing Mile in the indraw, when the floor drops and you can hear a footstep. The readers go out there more than anyone. They lie down on the block with their jaws against the stone and their hands flat, and they drum the third — the note that is not there, the one the children learn as a note and not as a gap, because you cannot teach a hole and you cannot navigate by an absence.

They put it into the pavement with their own teeth, through their grandmother's bead, at the right pitch, and wait the twelve seconds, and put it in again.

That is the whole of the practice. Nobody claims it does anything. The film is thick there and glowing and the children have good ears, and the plain goes on saying its one long syllable, and somewhere under all of it a thing the size of a hill is either eating or is not, and the only way anyone has ever found out is to lie down on the ground and be quiet, which here is the most dangerous thing a person can do.
