## The obvious answer, which is not the answer

Start with the only thing in the bowl that is unambiguously alive, and be disappointed.

The tarnish is a film. It is the grease on the grit at the bottom of the wells, the reason the grains hold together instead of blowing away, the reason they are black. It eats rock and light. It has no green in it and no motion and no shape worth the name; under a lens it is a crust, a scab, a smear of cells wedged into the polish of the floor with mineral dust glued to their backs like a coat of gravel. It divides perhaps once in a decade. It has no predators, no competitors, no mates in any sense you would recognize, and nothing to say.

It is awake for one hour on perhaps sixty days of the year. The rest of the time it is not dead; it is stopped, the way a clock is stopped, and it starts again tomorrow with the same time on its face. Add up every hour the tarnish has ever been awake since the ice quit flowing and you get something under eighty years. It is not an ancient organism. It is a person of about seventy-five, distributed across eleven thousand, and it has spent the whole of that life in one room three hundred paces across, eating stone.

And it is not one. The wells are sealed from each other by walls of ice; nothing crosses between them but wind. So the bowl holds several hundred tarnishes, each in its own well, each a separate lineage drifting away from its neighbours, exchanging individuals only when a gale blows a dead cell out of one cusp and drops it, alive by accident, into another. Most of those marriages fail. The wind is a bad broker. There is no conferring, no consensus, no coordination of any kind — and yet the vote comes out unanimous, every century, in every well, because the light is the same everywhere and the arithmetic of black is the same arithmetic. Four thousand blades lean the same way not because anything agreed to it but because several hundred isolated parliaments, unable to speak, all read the same document.

The makers' careful word was a metalworker's word. They knew what tarnish is. Tarnish is what happens to your work while you are not looking. They named the only living thing in the bowl after the damage it does, and if you find one of the copper pins now buried two metres inside a blade, you will see the bloom on it, black, advancing at the pace of centuries. The field is eating the tools. Slowly, and without malice, and completely.

## The crowd, which is also alive in every way except the one that counts

The blades are not organisms. They are also born, they die, they hold a posture, they feed, they have neighbours and streets and a life expectancy, and the population has been censused twice.

A blade is not made of its ice. The ice passes through it. What falls as snow eleven thousand years ago is still in there, banded, paged, but the *blade* — the leaning fin, the thing with edges — is a few centuries old at most, a shape eating its way through old ice at the speed of a fingernail. It is a flame with a very slow wick. It maintains itself by losing: subtraction on the sunward face, accretion of rime on the other, and between those two losses a persistent inclination that is not itself made of anything.

Which means the oldest resident of the bowl is not the ice, and not the flour. It is the lean. The angle has been continuously maintained, blade replacing blade, since before there were words for quitting. Everything material here has been replaced many times. The tilt has not. It is the one thing that has never once been let go of, and it has exactly one gesture in its repertoire: a degree or so, once a year, four thousand edges touching four thousand neighbours, one tick. Eleven thousand years, eleven thousand words, all the same word, slightly inflected.

The crowd has streets. There is a route through it that a laden person can walk, marked with cairns, and it has been walkable for as long as anyone has been coming up here — which means the particular blades along that route have not fallen in six hundred years. They are old individuals. Reliable ones. The cairns do not mark a way through obstacles; they mark neighbours, the way you might note which houses on a lane have been occupied a long time. And the copper pins driven at the turns are now deep inside those neighbours, at depths you can measure, which makes them growth rings: the makers, without intending to, tagged the animals.

## The other flora, which is dead and has heredity

In the shaded pockets the salt garden comes up in the cold dry weeks: gypsum rosettes, selenite sheaves, halite hairs that move in a breath. It contains nothing. It is also seeded. The pockets that grew rosettes last season grow rosettes this season, in the same habits, from crystals left in the pores of the rock, and the habits vary a little, and the variations persist, and some pockets are better at it than others. It has descent with modification and no life at all.

It lives, in the main, on what the tarnish excretes. The salts are leavings. So there is a food chain here of exactly two links, and the second link is a mineral.

The two floras partition the year and cannot meet. Black works the wet hour; white works the dry weeks; where one flourishes the other dissolves. They have shared a room since the Pleistocene and have never been present at the same time.

And the whole crowd wears a fur that grows on the side the wind comes from, which is the opposite of what fur does — beards into the weather, cheeks polished, four thousand bodies looking over one shoulder at something behind them that has not arrived in eleven thousand years.

## The commuters

Nobody human lives here. Understand the lungs.

They came up from a world with plants in it, in parties, and worked forty-minute bursts inside a four-hour window, and went down, because above this altitude a body is a thing that is slowly failing. Their habitat is fifteen hundred metres lower. Here they are seasonal, altitudinal, brief — a tide with tools. Everything they left is an organ they could not carry: a hundred and eleven shallow steps, each a few dozen blows, a staircase built out of breath over generations; a tally wall, which is memory kept outside the body because the body could not stay long enough to remember; a corbelled cell with a floor of black cobbles that holds the day's heat until about midnight and then lets it go, so that the only warm thing in the bowl after dark dies at midnight, every night, and is resurrected at noon.

And the lens.

The lens is the only object here that was ever kept alive on purpose. Everything else in the bowl survives by being ignored. The lens had to be ground, set, corrected, replaced when it sublimed away — tended, in the way a fire is tended, except that there was never a fire in this bowl, not once, no fuel, no wood, no dung, nothing that burns. They kept a cold focus in a socket in a rock and fed it with attention, and it made metal soft, and when they stopped coming up it went out.

Except that it didn't. At noon the melt films gather in the low points of the cusps and sit there in lenses the size of a shield boss, held by surface tension in the polish the glacier left, and the light goes down into them and is focused onto the black stones, and the wells deepen. Hundreds of lenses a day, unground, unwiped, replaced hourly, no hands. The forge is still running. It has simply changed what it works on.

The bowl's imports are the dead of elsewhere: pollen, ash, spores, the occasional spider ballooning at an altitude where there is nothing to build a web between. Nothing here rots; there is nothing to rot it with. What arrives dries and stays. And nothing here can leave. Carry a spoonful of the flour down over the col and it is dead by the second rest stop — it has never known a night above freezing, never known a wet hour longer than four, never known warmth that lasted. Its entire evolutionary history is three hundred paces wide. It is the most provincial organism that has ever existed. Every needle those people carried down the stairs was a small coffin.

## The one who stayed

Under the cairn there is a person, and they are still there, because nothing here decays. They are dry and cold and complete. If anything was left with them it will be small and thin and pointed, because that is all this place ever made.

They are the only permanent human resident, and the bowl has not copied them, and the reason is a rule so simple it is easy to mistake for a limitation: it only copies what stands, and it needs about eighty years.

The dead lie down. The living leave before dark. Nothing that has ever come up here has stood still in one place for eighty years, and so the field has never made a person. It has made the cairn — the standing part — hollow, a little larger, correct in every proportion. At dusk the low sun gets into that hollow and it glows from inside, and it is the one lantern among the four thousand that is not shaped like a tool.

## The natives

The seconds are the only things in the bowl that were born here and exist nowhere else and cannot be exported at all: carry one over the col and it will be gone before you reach the second cairn. They are the endemics. They are also, in every functional sense, the field's children, and they were raised by objects.

Consider what a second knows. It has never seen a hand, a tree, an animal, a fire, a valley. It has seen the socket where a haft goes. From the socket it has inferred the haft, and got the curve right, and got the grip right, and no hand has ever closed on it. Everything the bowl knows about us it learned from our handles. We are, to it, a set of implications left standing in the snow: a species inferred from wear patterns, the way you might reconstruct an animal from a burrow.

Most seconds die young — eighty years to assemble, one bad winter to erase — and the wind takes the exposed ones first, so the surviving lineages are all in the deep lee slots, the same cusps where the flour is richest and where the ghosts of fallen blades come up. That is the nursery. The bowl has districts: wet wells for the farm, shaded pockets for the dead garden, deep slots for the young, the headwall for the industry, one street.

And in the deep slots, where a second can stand a century, a second gets a second.

This is where it stops being copying. A copy of a copy has no original to check against; it has only the previous guess, finished a little further. The broken chisel tip was mended in the first generation. In the fourth, the chisel is straighter than any chisel. In the ninth, the haft has a swell for a palm and a taper for a wrist and is four metres long, because the field can infer a grip but has never seen an arm, and has no reference at all for the size of a body. It knows we hold things. It does not know how big we are. It has been guessing upward, in the only direction it understands, for a very long time.

So there is a district in the deep slots where the tools are the right tools at the wrong scale: awls the height of three people, needles like fence posts, hafts curved to a grip that is a hand-width slot between two walls of ice. The field is not making copies of our equipment. It has been making, slowly, by variation and wind, a toolkit suited to the only bodies it has ever actually seen standing here — tall, thin, hollow, leaning noonward, with a gap between them just wide enough to put a hand into, if a hand were a slot of shadow.

And where a blade has collapsed entirely, the cusp raises a ghost of it anyway, thinner, taller, leaning further than the original ever dared. Those are not memorials. Those are prototypes. The field is trying out the posture it has built the tools for.

## What is actually at home

The glacier that polished this floor left before there were words for anything. It left one inheritance: striations, grooves, the rock's own idea ground into the rock by something enormous that copied by taking away. That is the whole estate. The bowl received a habit of making impressions and has been elaborating on it ever since — from removing to adding, from surfaces to ideas, from copying to guessing, four thousand blades and several hundred isolated metabolisms and a dead garden and a second population of hollow restorations, all of it running on a seep fed by nothing you can identify, which is to say fed by itself: what the blades lose to the air by day comes back through the old plumbing at night, minus a small tax. The tax, over the whole of recorded history, is three blades.

What lives here, then, is not a spirit and not a congregation and not the flour. It is a habit with a body of ice, the last of a family of habits, working one hour a day on the only material it was ever given, and it is not finished. It has one word a year and it says it in a tick you have to be inside to hear.

Come up at the turn of the low sun and stand in the lee of the east wall at dusk, where the seconds are lit from within, and among the pale lanterns you will find the cell's second. It is beehive-shaped. It is a little larger. It is correct in every proportion except one, because the field has never seen anybody crouch, and everything it knows stands upright, and it has finished the idea the way it finishes everything.

The doorway is your height. You could walk in without bending.

It is not a welcome; it is a guess, made by something that has never met you, from the shape of the shelter you left standing. But it has been getting the guesses better for eleven thousand years, and it has the time, and the seep starts again tomorrow, and the light comes down through the slots in blades of its own, and the water begins, again, to be made.
