## What Lives in the Bell

Start with the teeth.

Every adult here has had their back molars opened and stopped — not for rot, most of these teeth were sound — but because a healthy molar is a small bright bell rooted in the jaw, and the jaw is rooted in the skull, and the skull is a room the note would like to use. So the stoppers go round with their gutta-percha and their lead wool and their little brass mallets, and they fill the good teeth dead, and the children hold still for it because they have been taught, before letters, before names, that a ringing thing is a wrong thing. You can tell a Bell adult at forty paces in bad light by the way the mouth hangs a little open. Visitors read this as vacancy. It is insulation. Teeth apart, jaw loose, the bone bridge broken. Everyone here looks mildly astonished all the time, and it is the face of someone taking care.

The eyes are the next thing. In the loud galleries the fluid shivers constantly, so the world is a smeared and ringing place, lamplight in hoops, edges doubled — and so nobody here reads faces the way surface people do. They read *stance*. Lean, weight, the angle of a shoulder against a rail. A Bell hand can identify a specific person across four hundred meters of torn mist by the way that person puts their hip into the noise, and can tell from the far gallery that someone has had bad news, and can be wrong about the color of their own child's eyes. Their hands are enormous in the wrist. Everyone is a drummer. Everyone talks with a hammer.

And they touch, constantly, without ceremony — a palm flat on a sternum, a forehead set against another forehead, the standard greeting being two knuckles rapped on the collarbone. To say something private you put your mouth to the bone behind someone's ear and let the skull carry it, and this is not intimacy, it is merely the telephone. Intimacy is the other thing. Intimacy is when someone lets you hear their voice.

---

A coat is not clothing. A coat is the argument you make to the note about where you end.

The stitchers' loft on the ninth is the warmest dry room in the city and it smells of horsehair and hot felt and the sour size they use on lead-cloth. Every coat there is a biography: panels replaced after a fever, after a pregnancy, after the year someone broke a rib and the whole architecture had to be recomputed. You take your coat in when you lose weight. You take it in when someone dies, because grief changes the way a body holds itself and a coat tuned to a person who was not bereaved will let the sound in at the small of the back. There is a station on the seventeenth where you stand for an hour while an apprentice walks a tuning fork along your frame and marks, in chalk, on your skin, the places where the note is trying the door.

The loft holds two hundred and sixty-one coats.

Most of the difference is ordinary: sizes children are growing into, spares, the coats of the dead kept hung and aired by people who cannot yet put them under the knife. The census board is right about the coats and wrong about the people, and everyone knows this, and nobody has ever successfully explained the arithmetic to a visitor.

And there is one coat that is none of those things. It is the guild's proof-piece. Each master stitcher, at the end of their apprenticeship, makes the inverse: a garment calculated to admit the note completely, to make the wearer the best instrument that can be made out of a person. It is beautiful. It is quilted in a pattern that hurts to look at directly, the way a chord hurts. It has been re-cut nine times in nine hundred years as the guild's understanding improved, and it hangs in its own case, and every apprentice is shown it once, and told: *this is what we know, and this is what we do not do.*

In the loft ledger, in three successive hands, there is a note that the piece no longer fits its last recorded measurements. The margins are narrow. Nobody has written anything else about it.

---

What lives here lives in the gaps.

That is the whole ecology, and it took the Bell nine centuries to notice it. The nulls are where anything can happen: the nursery, the infirmary, the birthing rooms, the four tables on the twenty-ninth where you can set down a spoon and hear it. Everything soft and unfinished is kept in a wandering pocket of cancellation, and the pockets move, and so the city's furniture is on wheels — cribs, beds, the whole infirmary rolling along a corridor at the pace of a slow walk, following quiet the way a herd follows grass.

The gaunt are in the nulls too. Of course they are. Nobody thought about it for six hundred years and then someone did. A nursery ceiling will carry nine or eleven of them, hand-length, colorless, ribbon-bodied, heads pointed out into the roar, hanging motionless in the quiet like things put out to dry. They eat what the mist brings. They do not move while the sleeve is running. In the Slack they move — all of them, everywhere, at once, ninety seconds of frantic blind writhing and coupling and repositioning, and then the water comes back and they stop where they are, mid-gesture, and hang there for another day and a half.

The children feed them paste off a fingertip. The children are not frightened of them. A child who has never seen a leaf has seen a gaunt ten thousand times and considers it approximately as interesting as a nail.

Everything alive here is either in a null or in the ninety seconds. The nap on the walls does not count; the nap is not trying.

---

The rail-code goes through the iron at the speed of iron. This means the Bell knows everything instantly and can only discuss it once a day.

A gaunt sighting has a standard knock — *tick, tick, scrape* — and when someone sends it, the whole rind knows within four seconds, forty-one galleries of people feeling the news arrive under their palms, and there is a small shared pleasure in it that has no outlet whatsoever until the gates throttle. Then, in the Slack, everybody says *did you hear* at once, and wastes eight of the ninety seconds, and knows they are wasting them, and does it anyway.

News at five thousand meters a second. Commentary at once every nine to forty hours. It has made them a people with no small talk and an enormous literature of the considered remark. A person here will spend a day and a half composing a sentence and then deliver it at a sprint, and the delivery is the art: the whole city can tell the difference between someone who has been building their ninety seconds since the last one and someone who is improvising, and the second is shameful, and there are songs about it.

The songs are all ninety seconds. Every one. Thousands of them. There is one that is nothing but the names of the drowned galleries, sung slowly, which takes exactly the window and ends on the word *eleven* just as the water comes back, and it is considered the finest thing anyone here has made. There is one that is a list of complaints about the dried fruit. There is one whose last five seconds are silence, and this is thought daring, and the young love it, and the old find it self-indulgent.

Children are named in a Slack. A child born between windows is called by its knock until the next one — *two-and-a-hesitation*, *three long and a tick* — and some die unnamed, and to name a dead child costs the city an entire window, all ninety seconds, everyone standing still and giving it up. It is the highest thing they do for each other. It is done without discussion. There is no discussion; there is nowhere to have one.

---

The dying are carried into quiet.

Hush-hours are rent, and rent is the great inequality of the Bell, and the rich sleep in nulls and the poor sleep in the roar and dream, all of them, the same dream, which is the sleeve. But there is a subscription on the ninth — everyone puts in what they can, it is not charity, it is understood as insurance — and out of it the guild buys hush-hours for people who are going to die, so that the last thing they say can be heard by whoever is holding their hand. A death in the roar is a death nobody attends. There are people who have spent their whole working lives purchasing other people's last sentences, and they will tell you, in ninety seconds, that it is the only thing worth the money, and they will not elaborate, because there isn't time.

The born-loud are the other category. A null slips, a birth comes early, and a child arrives in the full note with nothing between it and the room. They are watched. There is a superstition that the note got in first and holds a claim; there is a counter-superstition that they make the best listeners and the best divers, and the divers' guild, which is not sentimental, recruits them. They tend to have a strange stillness in the loud galleries, an ease, a way of standing that the laced find obscene and cannot look away from.

And there are the sweet bodies — the children whose resonance is *good*, whose frames the note wants, who go bright and rigid and start humming along without meaning to. They are laced like furniture. They do not go below the ninth. Their coats are rebuilt every four months and their mothers learn the chalk marks and check them nightly, and the whole city knows which children they are, and the knock for one of them passing is a soft double tap that everybody returns, forty-one galleries, four seconds, an entire population saying *we see you* with their hands.

---

Marriage here is a panel.

You cut a hand's-breadth out of your coat, from over the lungs, and your spouse's panel goes in, and theirs takes yours, and the tuning is deliberately spoiled in one specific way: you become a slightly better instrument for one particular thing, and that thing is not the note. It is done at a Slack, with words, and it is the only occasion on which people cry in public, and the crying is silent because the water takes it.

Widows keep the panel. Some have it re-stitched out after a year, and this is approved of. Some do not, and stand in the loud galleries with a hole in their argument, letting something in, and this is also approved of, and nobody says anything about it, because there is nowhere to say anything, and because the note is not the only thing that gets to occupy a person.

---

Down in the drowned eleven the divers work in short breath-holds with a lamp in their teeth, in water at blood heat that gives no warning and takes no heat away, and the silt lies undisturbed in tenements where the doorframes have grown shut. They bring up rails.

That is the other archive, the one nobody tells visitors about at all: the iron wears to hands. A handrail is a record of every palm that ever held it, laid down not in laminae but in subtraction, and a drowned rail brought up and held to a lamp will show you the grips of a district that stopped existing before the flood. There is a woman on the ninth who reads them. She can tell you which of the drowned stairwells was carried by the old, which by children, which by people who were afraid. She has a whole vocabulary of the hand. She has never spoken more than nine words at a time about it and she has been at it for thirty years, and the sum of everything she has ever said aloud on the subject would fit in one Slack, and she is saving it, and everyone knows she is saving it, and the city is waiting.

---

The listeners are the ones who run.

They learn the brass dial the way a sailor learns a sky. They know that 41.6 is the fourth stairwell of the drowned nineteenth and a woman counting, and they know that the erosion has advanced eleven centimeters since their master's day, so the window is now three and a half seconds and shifts a little earlier each decade, and they will be running before the needle settles, coat laced, lamp swinging, down through the roar with their teeth apart.

Ninety-nine parts in a hundred of the archive is roar. That is what the wall mostly heard, and that is what the wall mostly kept. A listener's life is spent sifting an ocean of recorded water for four seconds of a person.

At 52.2 there is a strike: whistles, a crowd, a man being told to come down off a thing. Whether he came down is the oldest argument in the Bell. There are two schools. There have been monographs, in slate, in rail-code, hammered out over decades. There is a song about it that is very rude about one of the schools.

At 41.6 the woman counts. She has been counting for nine hundred years, in a language nobody here speaks, and there is a guild — small, unfunded, slightly ridiculous to everyone else — reconstructing that language four seconds at a time. They have the numbers to two hundred and something. They have the teens. They have a probable plural. They will not have the grammar in their lifetimes, or their students' lifetimes, and they are teaching students anyway, and this is regarded by the rest of the city as either the most beautiful or the most pointless thing anyone does, and the argument about which is conducted, of course, at a sprint.

At 60.0 the wall behind the old crèche laughs. Several. Young. Eleven seconds. People who have heard it once go back every year for the rest of their lives, and people who have missed it by two seconds talk about the miss for fifty years, and the gaugers keep the index at all because of those eleven seconds, and no one has ever proposed defunding them.

---

The old hands say the walls are not an archive. An archive is a thing someone made. This is a thing that grew, a fraction of a millimeter a day, in the shape of everything it heard, inward, for nine centuries — and if you want to know what organ that resembles, they say, put your finger in your ear and think about the room you are standing in.

They say the city is not a machine with dwellings in it. It is an ear with people in it. It has been listening for nine hundred years and the stone is what listening leaves behind, and the two hundred and fourteen are the small mobile part of it that goes and stands at a particular depth at a particular flow rate and *attends* — the ear's attention, running down a stairwell with its coat laced tight.

The argument is old and pointless. The turbine halls hiss. The ledger continues.

But there is a thing the gaugers have that they do not put on the board, which is that the lower chamber's Slack begins a little before ours. A fraction. Consistently. For as long as anyone has bothered to write it down. Something below keeps the time and the Bell follows it, and the cables go up warm to something that has never been described in terms more specific than *up the line*, and between the two of them there is a hollow roaring room where a note has been held without interruption since before anyone here had a word for anything.

They call the shape in the water the Tenant. It is a joke that stopped being a joke a long time ago. Six entries in forty years, each one a pressure event and a displaced sleeve, each description crossed out and rewritten and crossed out again — and if you hold the page up with a lamp behind it you can read what was crossed out, and it is not the same words, but it is the same shape of sentence, and every one of them begins by describing something in the water and ends by describing someone the recorder knew.

Nobody has gone down to look. The divers knock at the throat, at the end of a breath-hold, three taps and a scrape, which is *who's there* — and the answer is the lower note, on schedule, which is not an answer, which is a schedule.

---

On the eleventh gallery a woman leans into the noise with one hand on a rail worn to the exact shape of hands. Her molars are stopped with lead. Her jaw hangs loose. Over her lungs there is a panel of horsehair and felt that was cut out of another coat thirty-one years ago, and the tuning is spoiled there, deliberately, in one particular way.

Across four hundred meters of falling water there is a single amber lamp, and behind it, in a rind thirty meters deep, a person she has spoken to eleven times in her life.

At shift change she taps the rail: two and a hesitation. It goes through the iron at the speed of iron. Four seconds later it comes back — three long and a tick — and has come back every shift for thirty-one years, and the walk around the rind is nine hours, and neither of them has ever bothered, and both of them would say, if there were time, that there is nothing wrong with the arrangement.

Once, at a Slack, one of them used the whole ninety seconds on something that was not a song.

Behind her, patiently, at a fraction of a millimeter a day, in the warm dark, the wall is writing it down.
