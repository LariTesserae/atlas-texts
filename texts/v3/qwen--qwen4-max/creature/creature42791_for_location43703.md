# What Lives in the Bowl

The tally counts thirty-one. It is not a census of what lives here. It is a census of the only thing in the bowl that has to be counted because it cannot be replaced.

## The Tenants

Start small, in the axils.

Each of the Nine is a country, and the countries have citizens. In the crotch where a frond meets the trunk, dust collects — not the floor's flour, but the tree's own shed cuticle, felted and warm, and in that felt lives a fauna that has never touched the ground. Bladder-things the size of a grain of rice, sealed spheres with a puckered mouth, that swell and shrink on a schedule nobody has correlated with anything. Fern-things no bigger than a fingernail, unfurling toward the pale underside of the frond above them, which is their sky and their sun. Wick-lice. A grazing mote that spends its entire life on one frond and dies within a hand's breadth of where it hatched, and whose whole geography is a rib, a groove, and a place where the oil is faintly warmer.

They are the only slow animals in the bowl. They can afford to be. Their world is sealed, fed, temperature-held, and lit secondhand; nothing in it has to happen. They have no equivalent of the swimmers' panic, no masons' frenzy. They browse. They rest. A tenant may sit for a day in one axil doing nothing whatsoever, and this is not a strategy, it is simply how things are where they live.

Then the host's note sags, and the glow beneath it dims by a quarter, and the country goes into a depression that lasts a season. The tenants come down the trunk in a slow migration, across the wax, into the powder, and most of them die there. The ones that don't are how a new tree, if it is ever permitted, arrives already inhabited.

## The Grazers of the New

The swimmers eat *breaking*.

A grain of the massif, freshly fractured, has a face that has never existed before, and that face is chemically hungry for about nine seconds. After that it has made its peace with the gas and gone stale. The swimmers graze those nine seconds. They ripple through the quick with their hearing pits open, reading the nine notes of the Nine as a chart of where the ground is currently being torn, and they arrive at the tearing while it is still fresh, and they eat the newness off it.

This is why they cannot stop. Not because the powder will set on them — that is only how it kills them. They cannot stop because their food is not a substance. It is an event. There is no larder in the bowl, no fat reserve, no seed cache, nothing that keeps. A swimmer that rests for a minute is a swimmer that has missed a minute of the only meal there is. They are four hours old and they have been in a hurry for all four.

The slabs come up out of the next surge with eleven of them caught in the same instant of turning. The gleaners who read those slabs say the turning is always toward something.

## The Masons

The towers are egg-cases.

The spiral inside is a chute. The masons pack their young along it in a glue that stays tacky, and then they stand back, and the surge comes, and the tower goes to pieces, and the pieces are the dispersal. The catastrophe is the birth. They are not building in spite of the surge. They are building *for* it, in the exact places where it is certain to arrive, to a plan whose final step is demolition.

Which is why the plans are inherited and unchanged and older than the people. A design that had to survive would drift. A design that has to be destroyed correctly cannot.

The spiral has nine turns. The tally counts in nines. Nobody likes to discuss it, and the discussion, when it happens, happens at the rim, and it is always the same discussion, and it ends the same way: with someone saying that of course it's a spiral, everything here is a spiral, and everyone agreeing, and going down.

## The Chimes

The chimes want water.

They are the only animal in the bowl that does. They can taste it at a few parts in a million in the vent gas, and they follow it, and when they arrive they hold the droplet against a frond and crack it, and the ester splits, and the reaction flashes hot inside a ceramic shell that was grown, not fired, and rings. One chime is a tick. A column of them at a rupture is the loudest sound a human being will ever hear, and it is not noise, it is *appetite* — ten billion small controlled fires eating the one thing that everything else here is built to exclude.

They leave a scar behind: hydrolyzed resin, tacky, faintly sour, wrong. The sealers know that smell and hate it.

And they pollinate, if that's the word. The propagules ride in the chime-column, dusted onto the wound, and this is how a tenth tree is conceived: at a disaster, by the one creature that loves the disaster for the wrong reason.

Most of the water they find comes up from the deep rock. Some of it, lately, comes in over the low notch. They have begun to gather at the notch in numbers. They are the only residents who are looking forward to what is coming, and they are not wrong to want it. They are only early.

## The Stilters

Long-legged, long-necked, browsing the frond-tips on the firmer margins, necks bent permanently downward like everything else here that eats. They swallow the propagules whole and pass them in a wax parcel, sealed, dry, ready. This is the tenth seedling's other route in.

The gleaners could stop it by killing stilters. They will not. They take nothing living. So they let the carrier walk, and they wait for the parcel to open, and then they go out with an amber blade and kill the child instead, and seal the stump, and stand in the powder for most of a day listening to the thin note it makes, because nobody is allowed to plug their ears.

That is the price of the rule. They pay it about once a decade. Everyone in the bowl can describe the sound who has heard it, and none of them will describe it twice.

## The Hush

It has eyes.

It has to. It has made itself deaf — a coat of felt that swallows every sound that touches it, so that it moves through the roar inside a bubble of nothing, and cannot hear a thing, including itself. Everything else in the bowl navigates by ear. The hush is the only creature here that *looks*, and what it looks at is the cold green light coming up off the floor, and the shapes standing in it.

Consider what this means for the hunters. A hush-pelt mask makes you quiet, and quiet things are invisible to swimmers. It also makes you a lit, silent, upright object in the visual field of the one animal that hunts by sight. The mask that hides you from your dinner advertises you to your death. Hunters know this. They wear it anyway, and they tie on to the Thread, and the reason they tie on is not only so that someone who loves them can find them. It is so that someone can *pull*.

The young are loud. They are born with a nap, not a pelt, and they squeak, and everything eats them. The ones that survive get quieter every year, and nothing in the bowl can find them, and so nothing in the bowl can kill them. There may be a hush out there older than the tally. There is no way to check. You cannot see one twice; you can only fail to hear it twice, in different places, and decline to draw a conclusion.

When two of them meet, the hole in the roar doubles in size for an hour. Children are taught to climb from that too.

## The Breath

Under the flour, under the crust, down in the fluted rock, something is exhaling.

The gas is reduced. It is not volcanic; it is too steady, too warm, too patient, and it varies with a rhythm that is not tidal and not seasonal. It comes up through ten thousand fractures at once and it holds the whole bowl aloft, and the Nine drink it and inject it and churn with it, and the swimmers eat the churning, and the chimes eat the trees, and the people eat the dead.

The gleaners have a word for it that means, roughly, *the one that is not counted*. The tally has no column for it. There are three opinions, held with equal looseness: that it is chemistry; that it is a mat, a single slow thing spread through the massif's cracks, breathing on the difference between rock and the trace wet far below; or that the Nine are not nine organisms at all but nine mouths of it, nine places where the thing under the floor has come up to see about the light.

If the third is true, then a tenth seedling is not a tenth individual. It is a tenth mouth on a body that can only push so hard, and killing it is not husbandry, it is a surgery performed on something that has never been seen, by people who have decided, on evidence they cannot fully articulate, that they know how many mouths it should have.

They still shrug about it. The shrug is load-bearing.

## The Thirty-One

Nine who walk the floor, nine who pump, and thirteen who keep. The remainder is where the interesting people are.

The number is not chosen. It is a ration. Every mask holds a clay canister of burnt lime, changed every hour, and the lime comes down the stair once a year from a world that makes it with fire. Thirty-one is how many lungs that caravan can dry. When a child is born the remainder becomes fourteen, and the arithmetic is done privately, and after a while someone climbs.

They do not talk about that either. They talk about very little. Speech pushes wet breath through the canister and spends it faster; a long conversation is a measurable expense. So there are three languages here and only one of them uses the mouth. The Thread carries the most: tension, pluck, damping, a hand-language read at the wrist from a hundred paces, through which you can feel the floor's whole state and also whether your sister is angry. The drum-post carries the rest, into the soles, all at once, no addressee. And speech is what is left over — for close work, for emergencies, and for love, where the cost is the point. Lovers talk. It is the most extravagant thing anybody does.

The listener's front teeth are worn flat as a river stone. She sits at the seat with the amber rod between them and the deep vents come up through her skull, and she names the Nine by their notes, and the names drift, and a tree that has been renamed three times is understood to have had three lives, or to be three people, depending on who you ask and how tired they are.

The sealers have the best hands in the world. They wax the wounded, the dead, the stumps, the ruptured trunks. They also test slabs.

Because here is the thing about the slabs. Amber is set ester, and ester is fat, and fat is what the swimmers are made of, and the gleaners take nothing living, and they must eat. So the slab is dinner and the slab is building material, and the difference between them is whether it moves when you warm it in your palms. Most don't. Some do. A swimmer sealed dry in its own body's chemistry is not dead; it is *stopped*, and warmth is a surge, and it resumes the turn it was making four hours or four seasons ago.

Those go into the walls.

Every hut in the bowl is a wallpaper of arrested motion, and some of the motion is only postponed, and on a warm night when the vents push, a wall will tick. Children learn to read the slabs before they learn the tally because reading the slabs is how you find out what is food and what is a neighbor. There is a hut whose north wall is not eaten from. Nobody has decided this. It has simply never been eaten from.

And the people themselves: waxed, felted, canistered, dry outside and sloshing within. A human being burns fat and makes water. That is what a body is. They are the only fire in the bowl, and it is inside them, and the lime canister is its chimney, and every meal is a small poisoning they must breathe out into a canister and hand to a child to swap. They are a spill that has learned to walk carefully.

So the wet things go up the stair. Births go up, to a stone cell on the rim, and the mother stays there waxed and waiting while the lime does its work. Wounds that will not stop go up. The old go up, when it is time, and are sealed on the rim by sealers who climb for the purpose and come back down alone. Grief stays down and swallows and swallows.

That groove worn down the middle of the stair is mostly feet going up.

The bell-keeper does not strike the Bell. It is not a signal, it is an instrument; it hangs on amber and bone and listens to the bowl's fundamental, and when the fundamental drifts it speaks, and everyone climbs. It is copper. Copper requires fire. The only metal in the world here was made in the wet world by people who have never come down the stair, and it is the thing that keeps the bowl alive, along with the lime that keeps their breath from killing it. The gleaners are not self-sufficient. They are the dry tip of something damp that reaches over the lip once a year and leaves a bundle at a cairn and goes away again without looking down.

## The Shapes

Now the actual fauna.

There is not one original pebble in any cairn. There is no original anything in a hut, or a tower, or a swimmer, or a frond, or a lamp's charge of powder, or a body. The massif goes out over the notch as flour and the walls are consumed to make more of it, and the level drops a hand's width in a generation, and the shape stays exactly the same.

So what lives here is the shape. The tower-plan, which is older than the people and will outlast them, and which uses masons the way a river uses water. The cairn, which is a location wearing stones. The hut-footprint. The nine-stroke tally, which may be a spiral copied off an insect by someone ten thousand years dead who needed a way to say *many*. The Thread's hum. The name of a tree, which is a pitch, and which moves when the pitch moves, so that even the names are throughput. The rule about the north wall. The habit of swallowing. The two hundred, and the knotted cord, and the walk to the notch, and the coming back, and the writing of a number one smaller.

These are the residents. They are the only things in the bowl that are old, and they are the only things that are actually alive in the sense of persisting, and every one of them is running on borrowed matter that will be powder by winter. The Nine are mouths. The swimmers are four-hour eddies in a food-event. The hush is a moving absence with eyes. The people are thirty-one wet furnaces in wax, kept burning by lime from elsewhere, kept lit by a child's arm.

Nothing here is old except the shapes, and the shapes are what is doing the living.

From the rim at night: nine great slow glows, and between them the small bobbing sparks, each with its thread of hiss, each with a child beside it pumping, and the whole floor roaring and shining because it is being ground away. If a spark stops hissing it has stopped moving, and someone goes.

That is who lives here. Something that cannot stop, and something that has learned to wait inside it, and the shape of the waiting, passing down the cliff, row after row, in nines.
