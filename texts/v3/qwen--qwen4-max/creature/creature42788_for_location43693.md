## The count

Sixty-two, and the number is old. It has been sixty-two, give or take three, for as long as anyone has kept the count, which is the whole eight hundred years, because the count is kept as a song and the song is sung to the note the ice makes. There is no way to sing it wrong without the whole bowl telling you. Children learn the number before they learn their own hands. When someone dies the verse is not shortened; the name is moved into the older part of the song, where the names have no ages attached to them any more, and the sum stays the same, and this is not a lie, it is an accounting method.

Nine households. Nine sweet pans, one to a household, and the households are named for their water as much as for their streets. Sixty-one people who belong to a water, and one who does not.

Divide the doorways by the people and you get seventeen and a fraction: seventeen roofs apiece, seventeen swept floors, seventeen lamp niches, and one of them the place where a person actually sleeps. This is the labour of their lives. Not building — building is a season's work, one cross-street a generation, and the ice does the excavation — but keeping. They sweep. The dust of this country is not dirt, it is ground stone, flour the glacier left behind, sharp as filed glass, and it comes off the polish in the wind and settles in the rooms, and every room in the city is swept every nine days by somebody whose knees know the floor of eleven hundred rooms the way a tongue knows teeth.

The sweepings are carried to the seams and poured in. There is no soil in the bowl and there never has been; the flour goes into the wet green and by the following spring it has become animals. Nobody here has ever seen a handful of earth. They have made ten thousand cartloads of it and it has all been eaten.

There is no midden. Eight centuries of habitation and no rubbish heap, no shard, no broken pot, because there is no pot; what is not stone or antler or copper goes into the cracks and is consumed. If they vanished tonight the city would look finished. That is not an accident. That is the point of them.

## The lamps

From the rim at dusk you can read the population, and you read it in the dark.

The lamps are lit in the empty doorways. Eleven hundred niches, eleven hundred small greasy flames, and the rooms with people in them are the black ones. Sixty-two dark windows scattered over a mile of light. The rule is older than the reasoning: a lit door means *this one is ready*, and a dark door means *this one is spoken for*, and when a woman dies her doorway is lit that same evening and stays lit, and when a child is born a lamp is put out and the household's district goes one shade darker, and the neighbours on the far side of the plaza see it and lift two fingers.

The lighting is done by children between nine and twelve, seven of them, each with a district and a pole taller than themselves and a hundred and sixty doors to get through before the fog goes blue. They run. It is the only running that happens in the bowl. They start at the lip, in the moss canyons where the doorways are furred and half-swallowed and the wicks are damp, and they work down the long avenues that follow the scratches, and the last of them, the youngest, finishes at the ice foot in the mist and the roar with one lamp on the newest lintel in the world, a doorway with nothing behind it but polish and a smell of ground stone, and stands there a moment because you can feel the ice on your face from that street, and then goes home along the wet hide runways with the pole over her shoulder and her footsteps coming back off the west wall a heartbeat late.

The grease in the lamps comes out of the pan that is black-green like bottle glass. Rendered on hot stones, it stinks, and it lights, and it waterproofs the hides, and the household that holds that pan is rich in the only way anybody here is rich.

## The bodies, and the hands

Small people, wide-handed. The knuckles split in the same four places on everybody, and heal, and split; you can tell a stranger from a neighbour by the pattern of the scars, which is to say you cannot, because there are no strangers. Their teeth are worn flat by flour. They are cold in a mild, unremarkable, permanent way, the way you are cold in a cellar, and they do not mention it, and they sleep curled with their backs to the ice regardless of the season, all sixty-two of them, in the same posture, in seventeen rooms each.

They shout close to the ear, without anger, a warm blast against the side of the head that visitors — there are no visitors — would find startling and then intimate. Across distance they use the hands: flat palms, fingers, a full language, unhurried, with tenses and jokes and a polite register, developed for architecture, so that its metaphors are all levels and loads and spans. To disagree is to put two palms out of true. To love somebody is to hold the flat hand under, bearing. Grief is a hand showing that nothing is resting on it.

Twice in a generation the note under the ice drops a third and goes hollow, and every person in the bowl stands up where they are, and the hands stop. All of them, mid-sentence, mid-sweep, the creel-woman with the sedge half-looped. It is the only silence the city has: not the roar stopping, but the language stopping, sixty-two people holding their hands still like dropped tools while the floor of the world finds a new route for its water. Then the old note comes back and they sit down and finish the sentence and never speak of it, and the song is still in the right key, and that is what the relief is: not safety, but the count still fitting.

## The one who was not taken out

Every child, around the seventh year, is walked out to the middle of the floor and made to stand until they notice the roar. Most cry. Within a month they cannot hear it and never will again.

One child in each generation is not taken out.

Nobody chooses this cruelly; it is drawn by lot from the year's births, and the household that draws it is condoled with and given a moss-cut out of turn. That child grows up hearing it. All of it: the grinding under the hiss, the slow beat where two echoes disagree, the thing the rest have agreed to call the quiet. They live at the ice foot, alone, in the newest quarter, where there is one coin of lichen per plaza and no green and no neighbours and the noise is a hand on the sternum, and they are fed by rota — a hide bucket of sweet water and a mustard cake left on the step every third day by whoever's turn it is, and the deliverer does not knock, because you cannot knock in that street, and does not stay.

Their work is to hold the note. When the pitch goes hollow, they are the only one who knows exactly what has been lost, and when it returns they are the only one who can say *that is it, that is the true one, sit down* — and the city sits, because it sees the hands of the person on the newest street come up and go flat and steady. There is a word for them. It means, roughly, the one still listening. It is also the word used for a taut rope.

They are shown the fish twice: once at seven, in place of the walk to the middle, and once when the previous holder dies.

## Names, which are addresses

A person's age is a street. You are of the Fourth Cross, of the Soaking, of Ramp Two; you were born in the generation that laid it down, and everyone can place you within fifty paces and thirty years by hearing you introduced. To say your grandmother's name is to name a canyon of moss in the old quarter, a doorway with willow scrub over it, a place where the roar is only a rumour and the sedge grows thick enough to cut. Genealogy here is a walk. The dead are all up at the lip, laid in the deep seams, taken by the green in a season, and their names are the names of the streets you go to for bedding and rope, so that a family's history is literally the district you harvest, and a very old lineage is a very tall thicket.

And the children are named ahead. A child born this year is given the name of a cross-street that is still under the ice — a street that will be laid out by their grandchildren, fifty paces behind the last, on stone that has not yet been uncovered. There are eleven hundred doorways and there are names in the song for doorways that do not exist yet, in the dark, under a hundred feet of blue. They are furnishing the names too.

## What the pans are

The pans are not a resource. They are the neighbours.

Thousands of them, each sealed, each with its own floor of flour and its own constitution, and no fish or beetle or fry in any of them because nothing can travel between; fifty feet apart is two countries. The households know them the way you know families: this one is sweet and reliable and a little proud; this one tastes of iron and is generous in dry weather; this one will cost you a day of cramps and is not to be argued with; this one holds its water through a fortnight of sun and has never been explained.

Nine are drunk. One is mustard, and is skimmed with antler blades and pressed into cakes and dried on hot stone, and is bread. One is bottle-glass black-green, and is oil, and is light. Two are rust, and are medicine and pigment, and the doorways of the empty rooms are striped with them at the lintel so that from the plaza you can see which generation built which. The pale pink one up near the ice, the colour of watered blood, is not touched. There is no prohibition. Nobody wants to.

The tasting of new pans is a profession. There is one taster; she is fifty-odd, of the Long Quay, and she has cramps most of the year, and she is honoured in a way that is slightly embarrassing to everybody, and when she dies the knowledge of nine waters goes with her and has to be built again from scratch by somebody else's gut.

The rest of the food is the seams: sedge hearts, fern crosiers, willow bark scraped and boiled, and the springtails. You lay a wet hide over a crack and shake the willow and lift it, and the hide comes up moving, and you scrape it into a trough and heat it with stones, and that is the protein of a nation. Eggs from the wall nests, two per nest per year, by rule, no more, because of what the birds are.

Sixty-two is not a failure to increase. Sixty-two is what a mile of stone and forty feet of jungle per crack will carry. They know this. The hundred households they build each generation are not for their children. They have never once expected to fill a street.

## The others

By weight and by number the bowl belongs to the springtails, and everyone would agree with this if you asked. Beyond counting in the moss and none anywhere else, a foot deep in the saturated seams, the surface moving like liquid when you breathe on it: the only livestock, the only crowd, the only thing in this country that happens in numbers.

There is one pale moth. There has always been one pale moth. It is understood as an office rather than an animal; when it is not seen for a season people say the moth is between holders, and when a moth is seen the children are told, and it is the same moth in the way that the note is the same note.

There are perhaps twelve small white birds in the three walls, and they carry the seed, and they are therefore the masons. Not symbolically. The order in which the cracks green is the order in which the birds fly, and the city's gardens are a record of eight hundred years of birds' decisions, and the households watch the nests the way elsewhere they would watch a master builder's yard. The birds are named. There are more names for birds than there are birds, inherited, and the surplus names are held against a good year.

And once or twice a season something with feet goes along the rim. Never down. It leaves prints on the wet polish up there, three toes and a drag, and it is never seen, and the word for it is a verb: it means *to pass along the top*. There is a stair cut — no, plucked, the ice plucked it — at the northeast corner that goes up to the rim and ends, and a lamp is kept lit at the head of it, and it is not an offering, exactly. It is the same gesture as the eleven hundred lit doorways. It is *this one is ready*. They assume it has its own arrangements. They assume, insofar as they think about it at all, that it is also waiting, and that its waiting is not their business.

## The tenant

The ice is not a feature of the landscape. The ice is the household that stepped out.

It pays rent. One or two black trunks a decade, sound, seasoned past any seasoning, so hard the adzes ring — timber that grew upstream before the bowl existed, handed over at the snout like a thing left on a step. Beads of copper in the polish. Two paces of new street a year, three in a great one, a doorway's width in a lifetime, excavated and swept clean and left ready. And once, four generations back, a fish: perfect, silver, of a kind nobody has seen alive, dried now in a stone box in the old quarter, in the keeping of the oldest person, shown to every child at seven.

Because here is what the fish proves, and what the children are shown immediately after they have stood in the middle of the floor and heard the roar and cried: that under the ice there is water that runs in a line. The one river, going down a shaft on the ice's back and out of the blue cave, not made of any rain that fell here. Water that has learned the trick. And where water runs in a line, things swim. There is a country under this one where the ordinary rules apply, and it is a hundred feet down and it is roaring, and it is the loudest thing in the world, and no one can go there. Their word for river is a word out of stories, and it is also the word for a thing that leaves.

Some of the children argue the ice made the fish as a joke. The adults do not correct them, because the adults are not certain it wasn't a letter.

So: the drains. Every street has drains, kerbed, graded, cut by the ice's own plucking and finished with antler wedges, and they have never carried a drop, and they are swept every nine days along with the floors. Eleven hundred doorways, roofed and dry, moss beds made up in the empty rooms, a creel and a bucket of sweet water left in the newest house of each cross-street where the ice will find them. A city built by sixty-two people for a hundred households a generation, against the return, because the scratches underneath run the other way and everybody can read them.

They do not describe themselves as the owners. Ask a woman mending a sedge creel in a doorway at the far end of ten minutes of empty finished maintained city who lives here, and she will lift two fingers to you and go back to it, and if you press her she will say, in the shout that is not angry, that they are the ones keeping it. Not the tenants. The keeping. The sweeping and the lamp-lighting and the drains that drain nothing and the song with the count in it and the child up at the snout in the mist with one flame on the newest lintel in the world — all of it is a house being held open, room by room, for whoever comes down the bowl next, so that whatever lives here then will not have to start.

The rain stops around four. The fog stands, the sun comes through it sideways, and the whole floor lights up in thousands of separate flat brightnesses, none of them connected, all of them reflecting the same cloud. A girl crosses the great plaza with two hide buckets on a yoke and her footsteps follow her a heartbeat behind. Eleven hundred lamps come on in the empty rooms and sixty-two go dark. Under the ice the river booms its one note, and one person in the newest street hears it, and up at the snout, in the dark, by nothing anyone would call a tool, two paces of stone are being uncovered.

The water lies where it fell. It has not yet decided where to go. Neither, in a manner of speaking, have they.
