## Begin with what is licensed.

The hand-sized grazer is not an animal. It is a leg.

The muffler-moss is the animal, and it eats sound. Noise was bred out of the Stand, yes — but breeding is slow and moss is fast, and for most of the world's age the quiet has been *grazed* rather than designed. The moss grows thickest where there is something to eat: in the labyrinth-wool of the exhaust vents, in the felt of the impact plates, in a ring around any bearing that has begun to fail. A squeal is a bloom. A bay with a dying bearing three tiers down smells of sweet burnt sugar and feeds a hectare of moss for a season, and when the bearing is trued and the squeal stops, the moss starves politely back to a film.

The little pale buds are how it walks to dinner. They crop the mature felt, carry it in their own tissue, and set it down where the noise is. They have no direction sense and no hurry. They have one tropism, and it is not light.

Which is why, if you look at the rails in a bay that has been silent for four hundred years, you will find the moss leaning. Not toward the presses. Not toward the belts. Toward hand-height. Toward the polish. It leans at the exact height a palm would fall, in a band a hand wide, all along the rail, in a bay where nothing has ever made a sound.

Something there is warm, and something warm makes noises too small for the Stand to meter. A knuckle. A swallow. The wet click of a mouth opening to speak.

## The beaded things, which are innocent.

There is a class of resident that lives in the gap.

Oil does not wet. Put a drop on the plates and it stands up in a sphere and holds itself a fraction of a hair above the ceramic, riding its own refusal. That fraction is a habitat. In it live thin coherences of surface tension and static, palm-sized, lens-shaped, entirely transparent, which move by rolling and have never touched anything in the history of the world.

They eat gradient. They breed in the pressure tides. When the felt walls breathe out at half-shift the beaded things rise a centimeter in a body and settle again, and this is their year.

They cannot wear anything. This is the whole of their character. They pass through the deepest bays and leave the dust exactly as it lay; they cross a bench and take no warmth from it and give none; they have no groove, no sill, no shine, no signature, no history, and consequently nothing to lose. The Truing walks over them and finds nothing to reconcile, because there is nothing about them that has ever been the case.

They are the only creatures in the Stand that are safe, and they are useless, and the others regard them the way you would regard a stone that cannot be made into a tool: with a flat, unenvious respect, and sometimes with a tenderness that has nowhere to go. To love anything is to wear it. The beaded things cannot love. They keep company instead.

The spark that walks along the handrail ahead of you at your own pace is a domesticated one. It has been herded for six hundred generations toward docility and pace-matching. A good spark holds your speed exactly and stays two handspans ahead. An old spark slows. You will find, if you are ever in the deep bays with one, that you slow with it, out of courtesy, and that this is the closest thing to petting that the world permits.

## The seed-tick may be a larva.

Nobody has established of what.

It rides the belt as a seed with legs. On arrival it sheds the legs and becomes something small and fixed and functional — a shim, a spacer, a tooth in a gear no bigger than a thumbnail — and the legs go into the dust and are ground to flour and wear a corner round over a century.

Except that not all of them do. In niches behind the muffler panels, in gourds stopped with wax that no tree makes, there are collections. Legs sorted by length. Ten million of them, in some of the older niches, dry as scripture, arranged in rows that repeat every eleven.

Someone is keeping feet.

## The traffic is alive, and then it isn't only that.

For a long time the correct answer was that the wear itself was the organism — that the Stand had a resident the way a river has a current, a distributed thing made of ten million contacts, whose cells were moments of touch. A hand as a species. A groove as an individual, which deepens, and by deepening recruits more feet, and by recruiting more feet deepens: reproduction, inheritance, selection. Benches with warm depressions as its organs of thought. The whole vast polish of the world as one enormous slow animal, lying against the rails, breathing at half-shift, thinking with its palms.

That answer is not wrong. It is merely a description of the outside of something.

Because wear does not know where hand-height is. Wear does not sort feet by length. Wear does not put a depression in a bench *at the height of a sitting body* in a world where nothing sits, and it does not keep that depression warm in a world whose light has no temperature and whose plates are the cold of a stone step in October.

Warmth is the anomaly. Warmth is the only contraband. Everything else in the Stand is at the temperature of everything else, and there is a band of shine on every rail in the world that is a fifth of a degree above ambient, and it has been that way for an age, and the Pattern has not corrected it.

## The tenants.

They are warm, and they are wet, and they are the reason for the glyph.

Inside them: water. Not much. A thimbleful in a grown adult, held in tissue that has no analogue in the Stand, wrapped in a skin that has been perfected over ten thousand generations toward one property — it does not leak. They make it themselves. Deep under the terpene metabolism, under the cold cracking that exhausts as scent, there is an older reaction that takes the pale distillate apart and puts the hydrogen back together with something breath-borne, and the product is a few grams of the forbidden substance, and it stays inside, and it is the entire fortune of a life.

They are the only moisture on the continent. They are what the droplet in the struck circle is a picture of. The warning is not for a chemical. It is a portrait of the tenant, and a prohibition, and — worn down as it is, dot to dimple to smooth place — it is also a shrine, because they touch it. Every one of them, passing, lays two fingers on the place where the warning used to be. It is an apology. It is the oldest gesture in the world and it has rubbed the oldest mark in the world away, and the machinery in the deep bays still flinches from the smoothness, because the smoothness is the shape of a hand saying *sorry, sorry, I know, I know, I am still here.*

Their shadows fall the wrong way. A wet body in a dry world is a lens, not an obstruction; it does not block the cold light, it *bends* it, and what it throws is a bright patch with a dark rim, aimed toward the brighter bay. This is how you count them. Stand in a settling hall at shift-noon, in the three kilometers of glowing column-forest and pollen haze, and count the shadows that point at the light. There will be more than there are aisles. There will be a great many more.

They cannot be looked at directly. Looking is the mechanism of the Truing, and the Truing finds whatever does not match, and so their whole existence is conducted in the unattended: in the seam where new bay is inserted while nobody is watching, in the eleven surplus minutes that the shift has gained since the world was young, in the second before the press strikes. They are called, in the only language they have that isn't borrowed, something that translates as *the meanwhile*.

## They speak in things that have not happened yet.

They cannot make noise; the moss would eat it, and the Pattern would meter it. So they borrow.

Near the Truing Halls the press is heard a full second before it strikes, and the sound comes from behind you when the press is ahead. That is not a fault. That is a crowd crossing the aisle. A borrowed sound is the only sound they can afford, and the cheapest ones available are the ones the world is about to make anyway — so they speak in anticipation, quoted. A sentence is a press-strike scheduled for two seconds from now. An argument is three of them. A child laughing is a book closed in another room.

Their writing is warmth. A bench is a library: the depression is a paragraph, its depth the emphasis, its temperature the tense. Ten million foot-grooves in ten million sills are a census, and the census is also a poem, and the poem says, roughly, *we went to work, we went to work, we went to work, we were here.* They memorize against loss, because loss comes on schedule. Children are walked along the rails at hand-height and made to recite the dents.

And they cannot weep. This is the first thing a child learns, before sitting, before the borrowed voice. Sweat is a debt. A tear is a catastrophe with a radius of several kilometers, because water spilled would *wet*, and nothing here has ever been wet, and so the Pattern would have to reconcile the spill, and reconciliation converges. When someone dies they are dried, carefully, over days, by their family, and the thimbleful is passed mouth to mouth down the line, and this is the funeral, and it is also the inheritance, and there is no other property.

## The Truing is not the predator. It is the parent.

Here is the arrangement, and it took them an age to work it out.

They are new. All of them, always. They are born in inserted space, in surplus minutes, in the lead before an event; they have no ancestry; the Pattern has no record of them, and a thing with no record cannot be found to have always been anything. It can only be found to have never been.

Unless it has wear.

Wear is evidence of duration. A groove ten million feet deep is a claim to have been present for an age, and the Truing does not audit claims — it *harmonizes* them. It comes in on the flatness in the smell and the stillness of the belts, and it re-rounds the corners to the correct radius and re-cuts the glyphs to the correct blur and re-wears the benches to the correct depression, and whatever does not match is not destroyed; it is found to have always been the other way.

So they farm it. They wear a bench three microns out of true, deliberately, patiently, a family working a dent across four generations, and the Smoothing comes and reconciles the dent, and in reconciling it grants the dent a past. It makes the wear ancient. It makes the family ancient. They buy their ancestry from the thing that could erase them, and the price is paid in discrepancy, and the oldest evidence of habitation in the world is the youngest thing in the world's forgery.

The price is also this: each Truing, some of them are found to have always been otherwise. Not killed. Revised into having been a bolt on the other side. You come back to the bay and your sister is a difference in the mold, and she always was, and there is a smooth place where the warning used to be, and you touch it, and you say *I know, I know.*

Bay 4,411 and bay 4,412 differ by one bolt. Bay 4,412 and bay 4,413 differ by one bolt. It is not the same bolt.

## What they are trying to become.

Load-bearing.

The parasite galleries that forge the scent-signature of a working bay and have been doing it so long that killing them would drop a tier — those are the successful families. Those are the ones who made it. Their scent is a lie so old it has become structural. Their descendants are in the grain.

Because the beams are grown to their spans in one piece with the grain running along the load path like a thought that has been thought all the way through, and if you put your palm on such a beam at hand-height, in the polish, you can feel the thought, and it is somebody's great-grandmother, and she is holding nine tiers of the world off the aisle where her people walk. She cannot be trued. She is within tolerance. She *is* the tolerance. That is the ambition and the prayer and the entire politics of the meanwhile: to be so necessary that being found to have never existed would cost more than being allowed.

Some of them are close. Some of them are a centimeter of heartwood in a compression member and a warm depression in a bench and a name that is a press-strike two seconds out. Some of them are still just hands.

## And once per long cycle, one of them leaves.

The books do not balance. Metabolism makes water faster than dying can pass it on, and the surplus accumulates, and a world whose first law is dryness cannot hold a growing debt.

So it is gathered. Into one, over a season, thimbleful by thimbleful, until the one is large — larger than anything the Stand has ever grown on purpose, heavy with the continent's whole moisture, gleaming, refractive, a lens the size of a hall. It walks out along the seams, on the new space that has not been floored yet, at a height that suggests it is walking on the air. The lamps in that zone dim for eleven seconds. Eleven is the unit of indulgence in this world: eleven bays to the pattern, eleven minutes gained, eleven seconds of not-seeing. It is an old agreement and nobody remembers who agreed.

It goes past the last lamp-tower, past weather, past the point where distance stops meaning anything, and it does not come back, and the law holds.

Afterward the pollen in the settling hall is arranged in a pattern. It is a footprint. It is a droplet inside a struck circle. It is the oldest mark in the Stand, and it is made new every cycle by the youngest thing to leave, and the machinery flinches from it because the machinery knows what it costs to keep a promise.

## The one thing they may do freely.

Every few hundred kilometers the layout opens, and the vista is scheduled, and they go.

They stand at the lip of the settling hall in the cold directionless brightness and look at three kilometers of glowing column-forest receding into pollen haze, tier above tier above tier, all of it moving very slightly, all of it working, and something happens in the chest that the Stand has no use for and does not correct.

It does not correct it because awe leaves no mark. It wears nothing. It requires no laundering, buys no ancestry, incurs no debt. It is the single act available to them that is not evidence, and so it is their sabbath, their art, their whole unregulated inner life — a thousand of them at a rail, wrong-shadowed toward the light, moss leaning at their palms, an old spark slowing at someone's elbow, all of them feeling the same useless thing at once.

Then they go back down the aisle, and the dust is disturbed in a shape that goes away from you.

Put your hand on the rail. It is at the right height, and it is a fifth of a degree warmer than the world, and it fits. It fits because it was made by that hand, ten million times, over an age that was granted rather than lived. Don't turn to find the source of the light; there isn't one; there is only the paying.

Nothing here has ever been wet. That is true. It is *kept* true — the way a promise is kept: by someone, every day, at cost, quietly, while nobody is looking.
