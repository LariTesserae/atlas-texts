## First, a correction about how much of this is alive

Take a census by weight and the basin is almost entirely animal. Take it by tissue and there is hardly anything here at all.

The glass is not bodies. The glass is what bodies have finished with. What is actually alive, at any instant, is a lining: a wet nap a few cells thick, spread over the inner surface of some hundreds of thousands of kilometers of gallery, so thin that if you could peel it off intact and lay it flat it would not cover the Milk Flats. Everything else — the terraces, the molars, the radial gutters, the nine-tenths that is hole — is either scaffold or spoil or the space the lining keeps open for itself.

So the honest first answer to *what lives here* is: a membrane, in the dark, at one point four degrees, holding a shape open against dissolution. The animals are the places where that membrane thickens and does something.

## The mason, and why it does not have a body so much as an address

A mason is a purse of gray tissue two cells deep, stretched over a basket of glass it laid itself, sitting in a cup it also laid itself. There is no meaningful boundary between the three. The basket is its skeleton and its house; the cup is the basket's outside; the terrace is the cup's outside. Cut a mason out of its cup and you have not extracted an animal from a dwelling, you have torn a surface in half.

It does not grow. Nothing here grows. It hatches the size of a grain of salt and then *lays itself down*, secreting the basket that will hold its tissue at the correct thickness, and when the basket is palm-sized the animal is finished and will not change again except to fold and unfold. Forty years is what a mason is built for. Forty years is a capacity, not a promise; the mean is nearer two.

Its crown of tubes has no direction. It cannot turn toward anything, because there is nothing to turn toward: no light with a bearing, no food that arrives from a side, no other animal it can see. It opens, it filters, it lays, it closes. When the surge comes it feels the pressure in its tubes and adjusts its laying rate by a few percent, which is the whole of its behavioral repertoire, and it is enough.

Here is what it is actually doing, over forty years, and it takes a while to see: it is maintaining a hole. The stone around it is soluble. The floor of its cup is dissolving at two millimeters a year and being relaid at two millimeters a year, and the mason's job — its only job, the thing its ancestors were selected for across eleven million years — is to keep the cup's inner profile at the curve where the flow separates cleanly and the food comes to the crown without effort. It is not sitting in a cup. It is *standing a cup up*, continuously, the way you stand a note up by continuing to blow.

The atoms of its cup are six weeks old. The hole is forty years old. The animal is the hole. Everything else about it is weather.

## The castes, which may not be castes

Settle a mason's bud in a good cup on a good terrace and it lays a basket and crowns. Settle it in a fast channel and it does not build a cup at all; it lays a sac instead, and swims, and becomes a carrier. Settle it in a seam where the water is thin and sour and it lays an acid gland and a ribbon of muscle and becomes a borer. Settle it on a flat and it lays a foot and a rasp and grazes.

Same stock. Same eleven-million-year inheritance. Four bodies, assigned by the water at the moment of settling, and no animal here has ever been told which one it is.

Which means the mason, the carrier, the borer and the grazer are not four kinds of creature living in a basin. They are one creature's four hands, and the basin is the thing it is holding, and it has been holding it since before there were primates.

The hunter is not one of them. We will come to the hunter.

## The spark, which is the only thing here that has ever come from a direction

A carrier voids its load onto a laying front and there is a cold blue point, and it is gone.

The flash is not speech. It is a cost. Nucleation from a supersaturated sac releases a little energy and the sac has no better place to put it, so it puts it into a photon, and the photon goes nowhere in particular and is absorbed within a meter by water and flour.

But a flash has a bearing. In a world where the sun is a rumor and the overcast has no direction and nothing casts a shadow, the spark is the *only* event that has ever come from somewhere. And so a settling bud, which has no eye, carries a smear of pigment on its ventral side that cannot form an image and can register *that something happened, over there* — and it swims over there, and settles, and becomes whatever that place requires.

A working front at dusk is a constellation being written and erased, and it is also a recruitment map, and nobody drew it. The masons are not calling the carriers. The carriers are not calling the buds. Waste light is being read as an address by an animal with no head, and out of that comes the labor allocation of a hundred kilometers of architecture.

## Three memories, and nothing in between

The spark lasts a fraction of a second and is read instantly.

The grazer's track — a thumb drawn through dust in the sulfurous nap — lasts a week, and is read by anything settling: a fresh track means a seam that was recently cropped, which means film at the thickness that pays, which means *here*. The grazers are gardeners and do not know it; they crop the nap to its productive depth because that is the depth their rasps can reach, and the tracks they leave are the only writing in the world, and the writing says *food, recently, this way*.

The stone remembers eleven million years.

There is nothing in between. No animal here can hold a month. There is no season, no year, no anniversary, no slow accumulation of anything in any body. The basin's entire past is stored in two places: a week of scratches on the surface of the food, and the geometry of the walls. The form is the memory. It is not stored *in* the animals; the animals are the medium it is written on, and they are replaced every six weeks, and the writing survives them the way a wave survives its water.

## The borers, who are the water's hands

A borer cannot make anything. It is a ribbon of muscle with a gland, and its whole contribution to the world is subtraction, at two millimeters a year.

But its gland is throttled by the pressure and the sourness of the water in the seam it is working, so it dissolves fastest exactly where the flow is fastest and the load is highest — where the stone is already being leaned on. It removes what the water is already trying to remove. It is not an engineer. It is an amplifier for the water's opinion.

And the masons lay down where the water slackens and drops its load, which is where the water is already trying to build.

Put those two together and you do not have a construction crew. You have a solver. The water proposes; the borers subtract; the masons add; the shape relaxes toward the configuration where nothing more can be taken and nothing more can be put. Then it arrives, and the arriving stops being visible, and what you see from the rim is a landscape that looks ground down by a billion years of patience.

It reached that answer once. It has been re-deriving it ever since, at speed, out of new atoms, forever. The eleven million years are not the age of the work. They are how long the answer has been correct.

## The kites, which are the only new things that have ever happened here

Once or twice an hour, an umbrella goes over.

A kite is a mason's brood, laid down inside the purse over a season and released whole: a bladder of glass with a fringe of a few thousand buds caught in it, riding the forty-minute slosh, feeding never, steering never. It will break up in three days and rain its buds into cups across nine hundred square kilometers. The largest thing you will see whole is a nursery, and it is already coming apart.

And because it is a bladder in a surge and not a wall in a channel, its shape is not derived from the fixed point. It is derived from the fluid, this afternoon, at this phase of the slosh, with this many buds in the fringe. Every kite is slightly different. Every kite is a shape that has never existed before.

In eleven million years, the only novelty this world has produced is in the kites, and all of it is thrown away. The buds that survive settle into cups and immediately begin re-deriving the old curve. The basin permits experiment only in the parts of itself it is about to discard.

Some kites go over the rim. They freeze in the screaming air, and shatter, and their glass goes into the cloud as flour, and the flour rides the loop with the long throat, and comes back up the flank in the rain, and is delivered to the basin it left. There is no emigration. There is only a very slow, very cold detour.

## The hunter, who came up from below

Three meters, one per hectare, moving at the speed of a minute hand. Soft — no basket, no cup, no glass except a few spicules it did not make. A sleeve that pours itself along the galleries.

Its ancestry is not the basin's. It is a throat-thing, a lineage out of the artesian plumbing kilometers down in the rock that is now only a rumor, and it arrives in the cold water and does not precipitate and does not build and does not belong. It is the only animal here that has an elsewhere.

It hunts by ear, because it has to. At close range it can hear a crown filtering — a faint granular tick, the sound of an animal doing well. At long range it has nothing, because sound is expensive and nobody makes any.

Except once every few hours, when the Combs cavitate.

The snap goes forty kilometers through the galleries. Every crown in the basin stops for two seconds. And in that two seconds the hunter, lying still in a corridor, receives the whole ringing structure of the plateau — every baffle, every blind cup, every soft shoulder returning a little of the energy — and reads from it where the flow has changed and therefore where the crowns will be thick. It is the only creature in this world that has ever had information about the whole. It does not know this. It has a gradient in its body and it pours itself along it.

Then it arrives at a cup, and engulfs the purse, and dissolves it, and lays nothing, and voids a plume of glass flour that a carrier will pick up within the hour.

The hunter is a mill. It is also the reason the architecture is crisp. Without it the cups would clog with animals, the crowns would foul each other, the local rules would blur, and the fixed point would drift. The emptiness you feel hanging in the middle of a square kilometer — four animals, widely spaced, each alone — is predation. The swept, echoing, vacant look of the place is a three-meter predator keeping the solver's hands from getting in each other's way.

Abundance in tonnage. Loneliness in appearance. Both are the hunter's doing.

## The quiet cups, where nothing has ever paused

Nine-tenths of the work is hole, and not all of it flows.

There are blind galleries back in the Combs that the water has not entered in centuries: anoxic, sulfurous, still, and — because the geometry that suits the masons happens to be a perfect sound trap — acoustically unreachable. The cavitation snap does not arrive there. There are populations in this basin that have never in their lineage stopped feeding for two seconds.

The hunter cannot hear them either.

So the blind cups are refugia, and in them the forty years is actually served, and the lining on their walls — dividing perhaps once a decade, laying nothing, dissolving nothing, at rates ten thousand times below the terrace — can be four thousand years old and the size of a fingerprint. These are the oldest living things in the world. They are also the seed bank. When a warm pocket slumps a dome and opens a sealed gallery, the still lining wakes into flow and re-founds the fauna of a district within a season.

That is why eleven million years was possible. Not because the form is robust — the form is nine days old and made of animals that die constantly — but because the answer is kept, in duplicate, in the dark, in the dead ends, at a rate slow enough to outlast anything.

## The rind, and the only animals that have ever been outside

Twice an hour the slosh drops and a dome's shoulder comes up into the thin screaming air, and a rind of ice forms on it, and forty minutes later the water returns and the rind dissolves with a sound like salt poured into a glass.

In that sound there is a bloom.

The varnish is a film-organism that lays a glass lid over itself when the water leaves and then simply stops — not dormant in the sense of waiting, dormant in the sense of not being. When the rind goes, it has ninety seconds of concentrated brine, stranded nap, and dying film to eat, and it eats them, and divides, and lays its lid, and stops. Its entire active life is a few minutes, twice an hour, keyed to a standing wave ninety kilometers wide.

It is the only creature here whose body has ever been exposed to the sky. It has no capacity to register the sky. The air is not a place to it; the air is an interval of not-being between two meals.

## What it is like

Not much, and it is important not to smuggle anything in.

The mason has a *now* about forty minutes long, because that is the period of the pressure that modulates its laying, and nothing in its tissue holds anything longer. The grazer has a week, written on the food, and cannot read its own writing, only other people's. The carrier has a second and a bearing. The hunter has the longest *now* in the basin — a few hours, the interval between cracks — and it is still not long enough to contain a day.

None of them has seen the basin. None of them wants anything. The mason does not want its cup; the cup is a rate the mason is. The borer does not intend the fluting. The kite does not aim. There is no plan, no drawing, no memory of a drawing, and the hundred kilometers of finished architecture in front of you is what a hundred billion local decisions look like when they have all been wrong in the same small ways for eleven million years and the water has corrected them.

The thing that lives here is not any of the animals. The animals are the tissue of it, and they turn over in six weeks. The resident is the curve — the one configuration where the flow takes nothing more and drops nothing more, the answer the whole plateau fell into and has been holding itself in ever since, using mouths as hands.

It has no head. It has nine throats, a forty-minute breath, a two-second held silence every few hours, a week of scratches, and a seed bank in the dark that is older than agriculture. It cannot be damaged, only delayed. It has never once looked new.

And at the rim, in the air, with the cloud below you and the water going off the edge into it, you can get warm enough to stop shivering by walking — which makes you, for as long as you stand there, the only thing in this entire arrangement that has ever wanted anything, and the only thing whose footprints will be gone in nine days, re-derived, arriving at the same curve from a different direction.
