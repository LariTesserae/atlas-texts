Only one resident crosses the middle.

Everything else keeps to the rind, pressed against the wall like filings on a paper over a magnet, and the thirty thousand cubic metres in between belong to sound. But the wren goes straight across. Nine grams over eleven metres of nothing, forty times a day, a hedge-bird's low stitching flight in a room with no hedge in it. He is a male, wintering alone, holding a territory larger than any wren has any use for, and almost nothing he wants has ever been in it. What he wants is on the walls: springtails by the thousand, the pink woodlice, the small spiders, the fly eggs in the wet moss. He works the tufa like a hedgebank that has been stood on end. He is the top predator of a two-dimensional country.

And he has found the focus. Not by understanding it. By the ordinary arithmetic of a bird: a wren that sings from there gets more song out of the same nine grams, and more song is the whole business of his life. The bowl is near enough an ellipse that every route from that point to the far wall and back to the other focus is the same length — eighty-four metres, all of them, no smear, no blur — so his phrase comes back to him entire, a quarter of a second late, at nearly full strength, laid in between the notes he is making now. He is singing a duet with a bird a quarter of a second behind him who is exactly as good as he is, who never tires, who wants the same ground. In February he sings against it. It does not yield. Neither does he. This is not a tragedy. Wrens do not require winning. They require singing.

The mat is thinnest directly under that point, and he stands on it because he weighs nine grams. It will not hold you. He roosts in a hole behind a fern strap on the south side, and he roosts alone, without the eleven other wrens that a cold night would normally put in that hole, because the Bell is not warm. It is only the same.

---

Three females on the north undercut, each beside a white balloon the size of a plum. Cave spiders, leg-span of a saucer, the colour of wet paper bag. They build almost nothing: a retreat, a few threads, one signal line out to a rubble ledge where the traffic is. They sit with eight legs on a surface that has been vibrating a hundred times a second since the substation was commissioned, and they read it through slits in the cuticle of their legs, and they cannot hear the note.

Not because it is too low. Because it never changes. A vibration sense is a difference detector; a constant is not information; after the first second of her life the hum stops being a sound and becomes a property of the floor. The eleven-second swell is far too slow and far too smooth to count as an event. What reaches her is discontinuity: a fungus gnat putting a milligram onto a thread, a springtail's jump, the tick of tufa letting go somewhere off to the left. She lives in a world with no duration in it, only arrivals. The Bell, to her, is silence with occasional food.

She is eating the ammonia works at four removes. Nitrogen falls in the drizzle, the moss takes it, the cooling ponds turn it into midges, the bats turn the midges into guano on the ledges under the mat, the gnats turn the guano into themselves, and she takes a gnat off the line and that is nine kilometres of industrial chemistry arriving in her gut as a small warm speck. She has no idea. Neither has the works.

The cocoons are four metres apart because those are the three anchors on that undercut that will hold a stalk, and three is what the guano will pay for. There have been three for as long as anyone has looked and they are not the same three. The berths outlast the spiders.

And the young cannot leave. A spiderling of this kind climbs to a high point, throws out silk, and waits for moving air to take it. There is no moving air here; there has not been for a very long time; the perfection of the stillness has quietly abolished her species' only means of dispersal. So they climb, and they throw, and they wait, and nothing happens. Some walk out over the lip into a crop that will kill them. Most let go and fall. There are spider legs in the guano.

---

The bats are the only residents with an outside.

They hang in the throat, that choked metre-wide mouth on the north side, a hundred and nineteen of them, and they leave at a particular brightness rather than a particular time, which is why it looks like a clock. For ninety seconds each evening the volume of the Bell is occupied. Then it is not. They do not hunt in the bowl — nothing to eat, no clutter worth the name — they pour through it and out over the plain to the ponds, and they are the place's only import licence, and they do not know it, and the drainage board does not know it either.

They are the other kind of listener. A horseshoe bat holds one pure tone at a hundred and ten thousand cycles and reads the flutter in what comes back: the constant part is scenery, the wobbling part is dinner. It is the spider's trick exactly, performed in ultrasound. So the two animals that come closest to filling this place are both built, exquisitely, to discard the one thing the place is made of.

---

Everything else is a film on a wet wall, and it is a crowd.

Springtails in drifts like spilled salt, grazing the yeast off the water skin. Oribatid mites, armoured, glossy, unhurried, three years to a generation — the mite working the fern axil tonight was an egg before the last harvest but one, and will still be there when the wheat has been three different varieties. Door snails climbing the tufa with a bone shutter folded inside the shell, a door against drought and cold, carried up and down a wall where neither is available. Nematodes swimming in films two molecules thick. Fungal threads consuming the shed moss at precisely the rate the moss sheds it: the only resident here that is exactly on time.

And the tardigrades, which can dry to glass and wait out the end of the world. They have never once done it. The films have never failed. They carry the whole apparatus — the tun, the sugar glass, the tolerance of vacuum and radiation, a survival kit rated for the death of a planet — and they are employed, all of them, forever, grazing a wet wall in a wheat field at ten and a half degrees. The one ending actually on offer here is drowning, and for that they have no equipment at all.

None of them can leave anything behind. There is no midden, no bone pile, no old nest, no litter, no ruin. Growth equals decay equals removal and the ledger closes every night. The only records the place keeps are the leaf moulds in the tufa, and those are of trees that fell into a river that is not here any more. Everything that lives in the Bell is a shape that matter is passing through. The wren is a post. The three spiders are three posts. The bats are a number.

---

Which brings you to the ones who live here without being here.

Two pumps, a kilometre and a half away. A transformer eight hundred metres east. A flare nine kilometres north that comes down as a wetness four days in ten. A clerk who wrote *unimprovable* on a schedule, meaning that no money could be made from this hole, which is true, and which is the only description of it anyone has ever committed to paper. The men at the pump house, who named it, and who have never stood at the lip.

The pumps hold the water at twenty-three metres and therefore hold every other rate in the place, and they are not quite in agreement. One of them is turning five and a half revolutions a minute faster than the other. That is the entire animal. Nine hundredths of a cycle per second, a disagreement between two iron objects in a shed, arriving here through the ground and finding a void underneath a mat with a resonance sitting almost exactly on top of it, and swelling and fading, swelling and fading, at the acoustic focus, fourteen metres down.

It is the only thing in the Bell that was made in the Bell. The lime is the last of the rock's own capital being spent. The nitrogen is imported. The midges are imported. The hum is imported, the water level is imposed, the wren came from a hedge and the bats came from somewhere else. But the eleven seconds is manufactured on site, out of nothing but two machines failing to be identical, and it exists nowhere else, and it is on no schedule, and it has no body. You can record it. You cannot point at it. There is no object anywhere in the world that is it.

The pumps have been changed twice. The replacements were specified to the same rating, the same nominal speed, the same tolerances, and the eleven seconds resumed without interruption. It survived its body, which is the same thing the wren's territory does, and the three berths, and the number of bats. This place is a set of vacancies that keep getting filled. Most of them are filled by animals.

And nothing here is alive to it. The spiders throw it away as a constant. The bats are tuned to flutter four decades of frequency above it. The mites have no ears, the moss has no nerves, the tardigrades have nothing to notice with. The wren hears it as the room. The men at the pump house hear it as the hum, and named the hole for the noise it makes, and think they were describing a shape.

So the one native of the Bell has no witness among its neighbours. It is noticed perhaps twice a decade, by somebody who has climbed down to the lip and stood where they were told not to stand, and felt it arrive in the sternum and the back teeth, and stayed ten minutes, and gone home.

The rest of the time it has the middle to itself, which is all it has ever had, and all anything has ever had here. Above it, on the thinnest part of the mat, nine grams of bird singing into an ellipse and getting itself back a quarter of a second later. Under him, the room swelling and fading, swelling and fading. Neither takes any account of the other. They are the two things that fill the Bell, and only one of them is alive, and it is not the one that lives there.
