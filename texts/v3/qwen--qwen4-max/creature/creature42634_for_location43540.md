Begin with the mouths, because there are more of them than of everything else, and because they are eating the floor.

The lawn is two millimetres tall and it is mown continuously, and the mowers are a guild: chitons clamped down like wet thumbnails, periwinkles in the seams, amphipods in their thousands combing the diatom felt, and above them all the urchins, green-black, the size of plums, working the flat plate in slow arcs. An urchin in the outside world hollows itself a home pit in rock. There is no rock here. So they hollow the machine. Each one sits in a shallow dish it has ground with its teeth and its spines over years, through the red lead, through the mill scale, down to a ring of bright metal that stays bright because it is being continually re-cut. There are pits on the underside of the Lid, above water, where an urchin's grandparents sat and where the water has not yet reached, dry sockets in a dry ceiling, waiting.

They are the only quarrymen this world has. They are also, though nobody has told them, its demolition crew. A ground-away patch of paint in chloride water is a crevice, and a crevice is where steel begins to think about leaving. Every home pit is a small initiation. The gauge marks climb a hand's breadth in a good decade and some fraction of that fraction is teeth.

Around the urchins, the rest of the film: barnacles in their white villages, mussels stacked eleven deep and beginning, in the older bays, to make a topography that has no drawing anywhere — the only reef, animals all the way down, with caves in it, and a cave has an owner. Tube worms scribble across the welds. Hydroids fuzz every stiffener like frost that has decided to stay. And in the bare haloes around the wasting zinc anodes, nothing at all, a clean swept circle of grey with a candle-stub in the middle, and the water there tastes of a coin held under the tongue.

The haloes are not empty of use. Fish queue at them. A bass with a gill loaded with copepods will hang in the metal-tasting ring for a quarter of an hour, working its opercula, and leave lighter. There are eleven or twelve such spas in the surveyed bays and they have regulars. The anodes are the only thing here eroding on schedule, and they are the only resource with a published end date, though the publication is a specification nobody reads any more. When the last anode in a bay has gone to white powder, the halo will close, the spa will empty, and the steel will start spending itself much faster. That is a clock too. It is the only one running out.

Seventy years is not enough to make a species. It is enough to make a habit, and here habits are inherited like bone.

Sort the residents by how many times they have been born since the makers left. The bacteria have had generations beyond counting; they are genuinely, unrecognisably new, and they are the ones doing the chemistry. Copepods and amphipods have had a few hundred turnover and have moved. The shrimp have had a hundred and forty. The bass have had perhaps twenty, the gulls fewer, and the eel — the eel has had none at all, but we will come to the eel.

And the sea keeps posting larvae through the breaches. Every flood, every bulkhead that lets go, every culvert still patent, sends in a fresh consignment of the outside's babies: barnacle cyprids, mussel spat, worm larvae, all of them carrying an ancestral instruction to spawn on the moon and settle on rock. Most of them are wrong for here. They arrive into a tide that keeps the wrong time and a substrate that hums at fifty hertz, and they fail, and their failure is subsidised by the next arrival. The loud majority of this world is immigrants who have not yet noticed they live somewhere.

The exceptions are the ones who carry.

The rough periwinkle broods. It has no swimming larva; it releases crawl-away young onto the plate a centimetre from where it lives, and so its grandchildren are its neighbours, and so a bay can make a periwinkle in a way it cannot make a bass. There are sodium-bay periwinkles now with pale shells and a thickened lip, and plume-margin periwinkles that tolerate fourteen degrees above what their cousins will bear, and a sharp-edge morph on the new rubble at Bay Nine Thousand whose foot is calloused in a way the survey has no word for. They are three centimetres of snail in a hundred million cubic metres of water. They are also the only animals in the world that have never been anywhere else. Remember them. The dark belongs to their kind.

The shrimp beds are a city and the city is a property system. Hectares of fouled grating, and every apartment a dead mussel valve or a bolt hole, and every apartment held by a pair, and the crackle — that fat-in-a-pan roar that eats a voice at three metres — is ten thousand simultaneous boundary disputes. It is not weather. It is law.

Which is why the quiet ones matter. There is a morph with a reduced claw: no pistol, no coloured flag to wave, no advertisement, no cost. In the lit bays it is a cheat and a coward and it stays rare, because a shrimp that cannot announce its tenure loses its hole. In the dark it loses nothing. Past Frame 90,000, where the fixtures are going out one a month, the crackle has thinned to a sparse muttering — a few old conservatives still arguing about a border nobody can see — and the small-clawed lineage has the grating. Silence here is not absence. It is a mutation that has stopped paying rent.

Everything with a broadcast spawning habit lives by the Coincidence, and everything else has opted out.

The bass come up the warm axis in ranks, silver and stupid, and pack the drowned catwalks, and the water goes milky, and for eleven days the warm skin under the lamps is a nursery of fry so dense it has a texture, like breath on glass. The drum choir starts at lamp-dusk and the pressure of it moves your suit. The gulls come down screaming. The big things arrive from four bays off and eat until the shallows go quiet. It is the loudest event in the world and it is entirely a festival of strangers — of animals whose young are thrown into the volume to be lost, in a volume that is a desert, on the nineteen-day chance that the tide and the lamps agree.

Against that, the small reliable sects.

There is a serpulid worm on the pump-side stiffeners that releases on the water hammer. Not on the hour — on the *boom*, on the pressure transient, once an hour nineteen times a cycle, and it has been doing this long enough that the survey has stopped calling it a coincidence. There is a barnacle that slams shut a half-second before the hammer arrives, everywhere, in every bay, faster than sound could reach it from the pump, which means it is reading the pressure coming up through the plate. There are crabs that moult into the slack of the pump-off hours because a soft crab in a duct is a dead crab.

And there is a worm — the survey calls it the moon-faithful — that still spawns on the full moon. There is no moon here. There is a wired-glass ceiling with seventy years of gull lime on it and a drum timer. Twice a month a small pink polychaete rises to a surface it cannot see through and releases into a night that is orange, and nothing comes of it, and it goes back down. It is going extinct politely, over decades, and it is being continually re-founded by larvae from a sea where the moon still works.

The eels do not spawn at all. They cannot. Every eel in this world came in as a glass elver through a breach and has been unable to find the way out ever since, and so the population has no children, only immigrants, and it is made entirely of individuals who missed the boat. They grow. They grow for a very long time. There is one in the warm axis off the Frame 41,200 diffuser that the survey has photographed across nineteen years and that is thick as a thigh and pale grey and utterly unhurried, and it rides the eleven-minute dawn — swims west ahead of the striking banks, staying in the last of the night, arriving at each new morning just as the fry rise into it. It has done this every day for longer than anyone has been counting. It is the closest thing this world has to an elder, and when it dies there will be one fewer, and no replacement will be born; one will simply arrive, small and transparent, from somewhere else.

The hunters are the ones who have read the schedule and made a body out of the reading.

There is an octopus in the mussel reef in Bay Four Thousand Something who keeps a garden. The three-metre sea lettuce stands only grow where grazers cannot crop them — the hot plume cores, the poison haloes, the high-flow throats — and she has settled at the edge of one of those, in a den she has built out of shell hash and rust drift because there is no ground to dig, and she crops the ribbons and she drives off the urchins that come to the margin. It is not agriculture. It is a woman weeding a verge she happens to live on. But it works, and the lettuce there is thicker than anywhere else in the world, and outside her door there is a midden of crab carapace and mussel valve a metre deep and still growing.

That midden is the most important landform in the world. It is the first place here where the dead have been gathered faster than the tide can scatter them. It is compacting. It is going grey inside. It is the first draft of mud — real mud, made entirely of the dead, which this world otherwise has to wait a thousand years for — and it is being made by one animal, in one doorway, out of spite and appetite.

There is a conger in a diffuser throat, in thirty-one degrees, which is too warm for her and which she has decided to tolerate, because everything that wants to be warm comes past her mouth. There are catsharks that have found the fourteen-degree isotherm on the plume margin and lay along it — a contour line of amber purses tied to hydroids and cable tray and the dead tails of mussels, a nursery that is not a place but a temperature, and that moves when the duty cycle moves. There are flounder in the mercury bays, a quarter of them, carrying a row of dark ocelli at a spacing that matches the bolt pattern of deck plate. They are not camouflaged against the ground. There is no ground. They are camouflaged against the machine, and they lie along weld seams, and they cannot bury because the drifts move every tide, so they lie on top and hold still and do not sleep much.

And in the headspaces, where it rains, there is a bull grey seal who came in through the Bay Nine Thousand failure and has not left. He is going deaf; the drum choir at dusk is a hundred thousand thuds and it takes something from him each season. He does not need it. The fish come to him in ranks, on a timetable, and he hauls out on a lamp gantry in a fog bank a hundred metres thick, in the roar of his own weather, and barks — twice through the world, once as a slap in the ribs of anything swimming below, once as a sound in the air, arriving late and enormous. He is the only mammal in the world that lives here. He talks in a room the size of a county and there is nobody in it.

---

The gulls have an outside, and this makes them the mail.

They come in through broken panes and vent louvres and they go out again, and everything that has arrived here from elsewhere that was not water has arrived on a feather or in a gut. The grass on the ledges. The fern — a filmy thing, one cell thick at the leaf, living in the fog belt where the air is permanently at saturation, which is the only reason a fern can live in a steel box: it needs a film of water to have sex in, and here it rains indoors. The springtails in the guano, the mites, the lice, the seeds.

And the shrub. Three metres, woody, wind-pruned to a trunk by the draught from a vent, growing out of a deck-plate crack packed with seventy years of bird lime and bone. It is a bramble. It has been made into a tree by a draught and a diet, and it is one organism: it layers itself along the seam, tip to crack, tip to crack, so that the standing part is only the part that found enough hash to stand up in. Under the sodium banks its berries stopped being worth being black. Black on black is nothing; a pale sport cost nothing and got seen and got eaten and got carried, and now the fruit on the sodium side is a waxy off-white, and the gulls take it by contrast in a light where a red thing is a hole. The survey calls them ghost berries. They taste of blackberry, faintly, with an aftertaste of iron, which is the aftertaste of everything here.

The gulls themselves have changed, and they have done it by learning, which is faster than genes. In the sodium provinces the chick's target — that red spot on the parent's bill that a chick pecks to beg — is invisible, black on yellow-black. For a decade the sodium colonies fledged fewer chicks and the survey wrote it down as poor condition. Now the sodium birds have pale spots, and the chicks peck at pale, and the two things have moved together in twenty generations without anything much happening to the DNA. Their calls have gone up in pitch, all of them, everywhere, because the shrimp crackle occupies the low band and a call under it is a call nobody hears. Bay to bay the dialects have drifted apart along the noise spectrum: the mercury colonies are shrill, the colonies over the pump housings are almost whistles, and a bird moved between them is understood badly.

There is a moth on the bramble. It is the only insect that eats the only tree, and it flies at lamp-dusk, and in the mercury bays it flies into the fixtures and burns, because it cannot tell a hard blue-white bank from a moon it has never had. It survives in the orange provinces, where the light does not call it. When the last fixture goes out past Frame 90,000 the moths will have a province of their own, and nobody will notice for a hundred years.

And in the tree there are eleven spider webs. The tree is the only thing in this world that does not hum. Everything else is steel and everything steel is carrying the fifty-hertz fundamental and the twelve-hertz pump under it, and an orb web anchored to a gantry is an instrument that cannot stop screaming. So the spiders anchor to wood, to the one dead-quiet substrate available, and they sit in the only place where the world's noise floor drops away, and they hear a moth coming. The tree is a library, an acoustic refuge, a nursery for gulls, a fruit crop, and the last three metres of standing life. The gauge marks are climbing toward its crack.

---

Down in Sump Nine, under the brine, nothing rots, and so nothing there is in a hurry.

The halocline has a surface you can put your hand through and feel salt find the cut on your hand. It is a millimetre thick and it is pink — a film of salt-loving algae, the only pink in the world, living at the exact interface where the light from above and the salt from below are both just barely tolerable. Under it, the archaea and the reducers, dividing perhaps once a decade, perhaps once a century: the slowest citizens, in a world whose bacteria elsewhere have had more generations than there are grains in the rust. The youngest lineage and the oldest lineage live a metre apart.

Below that, the beach of salt crust, and the windrows of pickled fish, perfect, ten years old. They are not random. There are years in them. A die-off in the mussel beds, a stalled bay venting, a bulkhead letting go eleven metres upstream — each one puts a layer down, and the layers lie in order because nothing disturbs them, and it is the only stratigraphy this world has and it is entirely a record of accidents. Something eats at the margin: a pale isopod the size of a bar of soap that goes down to the crust, takes a pickled fish, and comes back up over the halocline to eat it in water it can breathe in. It has been doing this long enough to have a route.

And in the stalled bays — loop dead, aerators dead, oxygen gone, the silence that is the loudest warning here — the white mat. It smells like a struck match. It is not death; it is the busiest province in the world, and what it is busy with is the floor. Sulphate reducers under a mat pit steel at a rate that makes the urchins look like decoration. The dead bays are where the world eats itself fastest, which means the dead bays are where the next collapses are being manufactured, which means the flood, the new landform, the fresh bright rubble, the settlement bonanza, the whole youthful angular geology of this place, is downstream of a smell.

Now the dark past Frame 90,000, where a fixture a month is going out, and the shrimp have gone quiet, and something pale and slow is doing very well.

In the light, the film is mown to a velvet by ten million mouths that hunt by eye. Take the light away and the mowers stop, and the film does what film does when nothing crops it: it stands up. The hydroids there are no longer frost. They are a pale thicket, waist-high in places, branching, brittle, growing a centimetre a year, making the second topography in this world and the first one that nobody drew and nothing drilled. It has caves. It has a canopy. It has an inside.

And everything in it carries its young.

Skeleton shrimp hooked along the branches like punctuation. Isopods with brood pouches. Sea spiders — the pale ones, slow, palm-sized in nine degrees, walking the crotch of a branch with a proboscis sunk into a polyp, and the males carrying the egg masses on their legs, glued lumps of next year held under the body like a man carrying shopping. No larvae. No broadcast. Nothing thrown into the desert to be lost, nothing re-supplied from a sea that has never heard of this place. Every animal in the thicket was born where it lives, and its mother was born there, and this is the only province in the world where that sentence can be said about everybody.

Which is why it is the only province that can become something. The lit bays are loud, magnificent, and permanently re-seeded from outside; they will go on being an approximation of a sea for as long as the lamps hold. The dark is not approximating anything. It has no moon, no immigrants, no festival, no reason to know what a nineteen-day tide is for. It is being written locally, by animals that carry, at a centimetre a year, and the dark is expanding faster than the thicket grows, and so the thicket has room.

The survey has not named it. The child on the ledge calls it the knitting.

---

There are three of them, sometimes four, and they are the ones who paint the marks.

They live above the topmost gauge, on a dry platform in the sharp waiting steel, and they come down gloved — not for the cold; the cold is honest — and they take cores that bottom out on plate, and they count lamp failures, and they write things in a plastic sleeve on a clipboard that is not the makers' clipboard but is the same kind. They eat bass and ghost berries. Their hands are cut. The youngest was born on the ledge and knows the Coincidence in her stomach the way a fish knows it, wakes before the pumps change phase, gets restless on the eighteenth day. She has named more of this world than anyone. She has never seen the sea and does not particularly want to; she has been out through the louvres twice and found it unstructured, badly lit, and full of rock.

They are not the makers. There are no bones of the makers anywhere. What the makers left is a mug, a boot, handprints in grease on a valve wheel at the height of someone short, and instructions crisp enough to still be followed — by the machines, and by the fish, who have learned them better than anyone wrote them down.

So: three kinds of thing live here, sorted by what they carry. There are the ones who throw their young into the blue and take delivery of somebody else's, and they are the noise, the ranks, the silver festival, the drum pressure, the eels who ride the dawn, the worm still faithful to a moon it will never see. There are the ones who carry their young in their bodies — the brooding snails, the pouch-bearing isopods, the sea spider fathers in the dark — and they are small and silent and they are the only true natives, and they are the future, and the future is pale. And there are the ones who carry it in their heads and their mouths: the gulls with their bay dialects and their pale bill-spots, and the three people on the dry ledge with their names.

Every nineteen days the whole world comes up into the light at once and the water goes milky for a kilometre and the gulls scream themselves hoarse over the flooded catwalks. It is the most beautiful thing here and it is mostly strangers. Under it, in the slack corners, in the anode haloes, in one octopus's doorway, out past Frame 90,000 in the widening dark, the residents are getting on with the slow local work of becoming from here — a centimetre a year, a hand's breadth a decade, one fixture a month.
