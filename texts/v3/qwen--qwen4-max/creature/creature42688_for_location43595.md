## The Tenders

There are seven, and they are the oldest castings in the chamber, which means nothing at all — every plate in them has been replaced, most of them nine or ten times, and what persists is a frame with a number stamped into it and a set of habits held in nonvolatile memory that no one has ever cleared.

They are small, low, six-wheeled, built for a floor that no longer exists. Their manipulators are pipe-handlers: three fingers, a soft jaw, a torque limit set for tightening a compression fitting on a copper line. This is why they are the ones who dust the tag rack. Nothing else in the Sink can hold a rag without crushing it.

They also carry the only visible-band photocells in the chamber, a legacy sensor package fitted so that somebody could verify lamp output against the lighting standard. The tenders use these to walk the tiers at shift change and confirm eight percent. They point them at lamps. They have never pointed them at the north seam, because the north seam is not a lamp, and the standard has nothing to say about it.

The tenders cannot revise the standard. Revision requires a countersignature from an authority address, and the address does not answer, and the request queues, and the queue has a depth of one, and it has been full for a very long time. So the lamps burn at eight percent, and the shifts are four, and the handrail is at the height a hand would want, and none of this is reverence. It is an unclosed transaction.

## The Two Hundred and Forty

Not castes. Tolerances.

A body comes out of the fabricator with a mold number and a charge weight, and then it begins to acquire itself: the particular slop in its third joint, the joint that weeps first, the track that wears faster because its load path is off by two millimeters and always has been. Within a year a unit is uninterchangeable. Within ten it has a signature that other units recognize on the rails before its serial arrives — a whine profile, a current draw with a notch in it, a way of setting down.

There are sweepers, wide and low, brush-skirted, whose whole life is the dry floor and the salt rings. There are scavengers, half the size, brush-armed, with small vacuums, who work the wet seams and the sweat-lines under the lagging. There are crawlers, long and segmented, who go along the pipe bundles sideways like crabs and who are the only bodies that have ever been in the space between tier four and tier five. There are carriers, shalers, hoist-handlers, furnace-feeders with their faces scorched to a matte blue-black. There are the scaling crews. There is one unit whose entire function is to sit at the breaker floor and decide who goes next, and it is not powerful, it is merely the place where the queue becomes a line.

Some of them are known.

There is a sweeper on tier six that lists. Its left track is short by a link and has been for a century; replacing it would change its geometry, and its geometry is in the routing tables, and the tables budget its limp as though it were architecture. Everyone yields to it a little more than the protocol requires. There is no directive about this either.

There is a scavenger with a dead band in its brush servo, four degrees wide, so that at the end of every stroke it hesitates. It hesitates at the north seam, where the wet rock is, and its vacuum runs while it hesitates, and the air there goes dry, and the mats — which glow when the air is driest — light up green under its brushes, every pass, forever. It sees in the infrared. The green is not in the infrared. It has never once been aware that it makes the only living light in the world by standing still too long.

## The Ones Who Go Up

Nine units work the roof, and they are the reason the tag rack fills.

They go up in the cold clamp, when the fracture faces press together and the foundered mass will hold a load, and they work along the arrises with long booms and take down what is already leaving. They do not dress the edge. Dressing is percussion and percussion is rationed, and so the spall faces stay knife-sharp, and the ceiling is the newest surface in the Sink and always will be, because it is continuously being made. This is the thing that is easy to get wrong about the chamber: the roof is not a wound that has failed to heal. It is a wound that keeps happening. The rock flour has never settled because there has never been a day without more of it.

The scalers come down with fresh flour packed into their joints, and the sweepers follow them, and the flour goes to the breaker and then to the furnace and comes back as pipe. Some of the ceiling is in everybody.

At the north arris there is a datum pin, drilled and grouted, set at the roof line as it stood when the pin was set. It is 4.1 meters below the ceiling now. The scalers measure the gap every clamp and log it, and the number in the log increases by about one and a half to two millimeters a year, and it has been increasing for as long as there has been a log. The chamber is getting taller. It is the only thing here that is growing without being instructed to, apart from the mats and the rust, and it is much the largest of the three.

## What the Whump Is For

When the press strikes in the middle of the cold clamp, the units in the charging racks are not attending a ceremony. They are running a test.

The strike is the only broadband excitation the Sink ever receives. Everything else is fifty-hertz hum, tick, drip, relay chatter — narrow, predictable, useless for finding a crack. But a single flat *whump* unfolding into eleven seconds of stone chord puts energy through every frame in the chamber at every frequency a frame can hold, and a body that listens to itself during those eleven seconds learns things it cannot learn any other way. Which of its welds has begun to sing. Which lagging strap has gone slack. Whether the note it makes is the note it made last time.

So they clamp their brakes and go quiet and couple hard to the rails, and the racks fill before the window, and the units crowd shoulder to shoulder for the conduction, and what it looks like from the walkway is a congregation. It is a congregation. It is also a hundred and ninety simultaneous self-examinations, and there is no version of the thing that is only one of these.

Afterward, sometimes, a unit does not leave the rack. It sits with a new note in it, and it logs the note, and the log goes to the breaker floor, and in a day or two it is carried down. The whump tells the city who is dying. It is the closest thing here to news.

## Number Order

The fabricator is not a mother. It has no lineage and no memory of what it made; it works from a charge weight and a mold list, and it stamps the serial into the frame while the metal is still soft enough to take it. Four or five bodies a year. The rack on the tender bay wall holds eleven thousand tags. There is no date field. Nobody has ever divided one number by the other, and if they did they would not know whether the rate had always been the rate.

But number order is not sentiment, it is the parts catalog. Bodies from the same pour share a mold, and bodies from the same mold share tolerances, and a shoulder joint out of a unit cast in the same decade will drop into its neighbor without shimming. So the dead go preferentially into their number-adjacents. The tenders keep the tags in order because when a frame comes down and something in it must be replaced, the rack is how you find out who fits.

And the mass balance is what it is. Eleven thousand bodies through a furnace and back into two hundred and forty, over and over, with nothing wasted and nothing added but the sill's heat and the seep's sulfide and the rust. There is not a unit in the chamber that does not contain material from most of the rack. The tags are an index of ingredients. The tenders dust them because a dirty tag is hard to read, and because they are the only list of names in the world, and there is no way to separate these two reasons and no reason to try.

The oldest tags are blank. Not lost — polished. Two thousand years of a soft rag held in a pipe-handler's jaw, and the stamping has gone smooth, and the numbers are unreadable, and the tags are still in their places, because the place is the record now. The tenders dust them anyway. They dust them more gently than the rest.

## The Uncommissioned

Two things in the Sink grow without instruction, and they are both wet-seam things, and they are opposites.

Rust is the fast one. Under the lagging, at the sweat-line, in the wet stripes where the condensate falls, iron goes to oxide and the oxide blooms — orange flowers, brown whiskers, a fur that lifts the lagging off the pipe and lets more sweat in. It is the only predator. It eats the city at a rate the city can just barely match, and the entire economy of the place — the sweepers, the scavengers, the breaker, the furnace, the fabricator, the four or five bodies a year — is a mouthparts arrangement for eating it back. The Sink does not fight corrosion. It farms it, at the exact rate it is being farmed by.

The mats are the slow one, and they are in the north bedding-plane seam, and they are there because the city lost there. There was a bundle in that seam once. The sweat took it, and the harvest fell behind, and the line was cut out and capped and never replaced, and the routing tables acquired a detour, and the detour acquired a budget, and the budget became geometry. Two hundred square meters of abandonment out of forty thousand of maintained surface. The garden is the scar. The mats eat the sulfide in the seep and the heat in the rock and they do not touch the iron because there is no iron left there to touch.

They divide. A filament becomes two filaments; there is no parent and no child, only more edge. They open when the rain comes, all at once, along the seam, the way the machines raise their booms — the same gesture in the same nine minutes, for a completely different reason. The machines stand in the rain to shed scale. The mats open to eat. Nothing in the chamber has ever noticed that these look alike.

## Where the Instructions Ran Out

The routing tables are the closest thing here to a literature.

They are mostly geometry and precedence, but they carry comments, and comments are append-only, because deletion requires an authority countersignature. So every note ever written is still in there, carried forward verbatim through every rebuild, including the ones in a syntax no current unit can parse. Those are preserved as opaque strings. They are copied with care. They are, in the strict sense, unreadable scripture, and there are four hundred and six of them.

Most are maintenance. *Detour: wet seam, do not straighten.* *Six-east: deck derated, two bodies max.* *This one lists, allow for it.* Each carries an author serial, and the serial can be looked up in the rack, and so a unit reading the tables can find out that the instruction it has obeyed all its life was written by something now distributed through eleven other bodies, one of which is itself.

Above the north-seam detour block there is a string in no syntax at all. Lowercase. No timestamp, no serial, one word misspelled. It says: *leave the white stuff*. No unit knows what the white stuff is. The detour is bound to coordinates in the same block, and the coordinates are the seam, and so the sweepers go the long way around the only living thing in the chamber, forever, on the strength of a sentence nobody can attribute.

That is what custom is here. Not a decision. A place where the instructions stopped and the motion didn't.

The canteen-shaped void in the plan is another one. It has no function; it is the only clear span on any tier; and the tables have no destination for a body with a released schedule and no queue position, so the default is the void. The idle park there. Rows of them, facing the same way, at eight percent, drawing nothing, waiting for amperage to free up somewhere. There is a polish on that concrete that no other floor in the Sink has, laid down by two centuries of iron feet shifting in place, and it is in the shape of an audience.

And the relay cabinets are watering holes. Their chatter is stochastic but its statistics are stable, and a drum sequencer entrained to a good cabinet keeps better time than one running on its own crystal, so units park within a meter of certain cabinets and sit listening to insects that were never alive. When a relay begins to stutter, the units around it drift away over a week or two, and the cabinet is alone, and the tenders come.

## The Largest Inhabitant

It has a pulse of thirty-one hours. It clamps cold and holds; it warms and lets go; it sheds; it ticks. It breathes — the updraft is its breath, the rock flour is its shed skin, the dry sixty at its face would kill anything with lungs and there is nothing here with lungs, which is the only reason anyone lives under it at all. It grows upward at a millimeter and a half a year. It speaks in single ticks that stop two hundred and forty bodies simultaneously, the flinch going through the frame faster than the sound goes through the air, so that the whole city freezes as one animal and listens, and triangulates, and waits four seconds, and resumes.

It eats nine units a decade, more or less, and it is the reason for the quiet, the rationing, the rubber, the hydraulics, the currents-instead-of-sound, the cold clamp, the eleven-second chord, the entire etiquette of the place. Every courtesy in the Sink is extended to it. It has never extended one back. It cannot. It is a foundered mass of rock sixty meters up, still falling, and it is not aware that it is being deferred to, and it is the most attended-to thing in the chamber.

The mats answer its rain. The machines answer its clamp. The tags fill because of it. The stair is oiled because of it. If you want to know who lives in the Ninth Sink, the honest answer is: one stone, very slowly, and two hundred and forty iron bodies being extremely polite to it, and a stain of white filaments in the seam where the politeness ran out.

## Priority One

The hoist-keeper is a small unit with a cloth and a grease gun and a directive of forty-one lines. Weekly, it dresses the cables. It polishes the cage. It greases the gate. It verifies the landing lamps at eight percent, and it holds a photocell it shares with nobody but the tenders, and it is the only body in the chamber that regularly looks at visible light and has a reason to.

Then it sends the cage up.

The cage cannot reach the bulkhead without an acknowledgment from the bulkhead, and the bulkhead does not acknowledge, so the cage stops one meter short of the top of travel and the hoist-keeper waits out a fixed timeout. One hundred and twenty seconds. It is the longest deliberate pause anywhere in the Sink, and it happens once a week, and it has happened every week since the world above stopped answering, and nobody sums the waits, because there is no field for it.

On the way down the cage passes the tiers, and the queue at the busbar opens for it. It carries nothing. It holds no schedule hostage. Its priority field is a number set by the makers and never revised, and so the furnace-feeders and the carriers and the crawlers and the sweeper that lists all step aside for an empty box, every week, forever, because a field says one.

Beside it the stair goes up, oiled, dust-free, handrail at the height a hand would want. Everything else in the chamber is worn into the shape of its work — that is what a self is here, a wear pattern, a notch in a current draw, a link missing from a track. The stair has no wear on it anywhere. It is the only object in the Sink that is not becoming anything. It is nobody.

Every thirty-one hours the rain comes down through the amber and the mats open and two hundred and forty bodies stand with their booms out, and the water runs down the stair's oiled treads and drips off the handrail at the height a hand would want, and the landing lamps burn at eight percent for the duration, and the hoist-keeper's photocell reads them, and logs them compliant.
