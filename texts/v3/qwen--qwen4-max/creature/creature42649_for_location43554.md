**The film comes first, and it is not green.**

In the wet seams there is a skin, and under the skin a second skin. The top one takes the clerestory light — a low, tough alga, blue-green, laid down in the trembling puddles like varnish. Beneath it, in the grease, in the black compost of paper gasket and rotted belt leather, something else entirely: a slick mat that eats oil cake and soap and the fatty lime that drips from under the head bearing. It is faintly iridescent, the way a wet road is iridescent. It smells of nothing you could name. It grows a millimeter a month and is grazed back a millimeter a month, and it is the bottom of every life in the hall, including the ones that fly.

It has no name in the drumming. It is not a place. It is what places are made of.

On it: the snails. Thousands, sealed along the dust ridges in overlapping ranks like tiles, each shell no bigger than a fingernail paring, each animal a wet grey thumb pressed to the plate. They cannot hear and have never been able to. They read the floor through the whole sole of the foot, which means they read it too well — a snail on a ridge feels the sweeper coming forty minutes before it arrives, and does not hurry, and does not need to, because the sweeper's brushes were set nine centuries ago for dust and have worn soft, and they pass over the shells at a height that has never once been adjusted. That is the right of way. It is not a courtesy. It is a clearance dimension that happened to be merciful.

The snails build their shells out of the machine's indigestion: lime from the soap stalactites, brass from the swarf, iron from the plate. And because the water has been going around since first fill and the pitch has been drifting since first fill, a shell is a spiral of good and bad years. Wide band, narrow band, a scar where the fog came down, a thin dark line where a scouring burst put something high and hard through the air and everything with a body flinched. Nine hundred years of these lie stacked in the ridges, drifted and drilled and sorted by the plate's own sorting.

The rats gnaw them. They need the calcium; the water has none to spare. So the milk of every rat in the hall is made of the last four centuries of weather, and no one has ever read a single band.

---

**In the air, one vowel.**

The note leaves a slot open low down, under the shear — a few tens of hertz of unoccupied spectrum, a gap in the wall. The blind crickets of the nulls stridulate into it. They have no ears; they receive the song through their forelegs, which is to say they touch it rather than hear it, which is to say the hall's only airborne language is a form of grooming. It is one note, held, with a burr in it. It has been the same note within a hair since before the drawings. It is the only thing in ninety-one meters that could be called a voice, and it says nothing but *here, here, here*, and it is entirely accurate.

The spiders do not use the slot. They use the whole wall.

Each web is strung to the hall's harmonic — guyed to bolt heads, tensioned by the spider's own body weight, adjusted over hours by a leg plucking and reading the return. A tuned web in a standing wave is not a net; it is an instrument. It hums. Insects flying the node lines feel the hum as a shove and are pushed off course into it, and the spider walks out along a strand that is singing at three kilohertz and gathers them.

A spider is therefore a machinist, and must re-machine at every molt, and is only as good as its last measurement. When the pitch below changes by four parts per million, every web in the hall goes out of true within a day. For a season the spiders starve in a room full of food, and then the young ones retune, and the tuning they inherit is not the tuning their mothers used, and something in the shape of the webs across the whole bay slowly rotates.

The same event is a famine to the spiders, a spawning to the fish, a flowering to the orchids, a rain to the fog, and a bad year in the shells. One thing happens. It arrives as six different weathers.

---

**Two forests and a country with no ground.**

The groves have their roots on the floor and their flowers eleven meters up. This is the central fact of their sex. The canopy hangs from the trusses, an inverted forest of aerial roots drinking the fog stratum, and it is up there that the flowering happens — small, waxy, pale, opening into warm mist. The moths live in the mist. They are born in it, drink it, mate in it, die in it. From the plate the layer looks like weather; from inside it looks like a blizzard with intentions. A moth in the stratum touches only two solid things in its life: another moth, and a flower hanging down out of the ceiling.

Then it dies and falls, and the fall takes it through an antinode curtain, and the loudness finishes it, and it lands on bare hot plate and is swept to a ridge, and its wings — mica-pale, overlapping, dry — become part of the road. The roads are paved with the pollinators. The seed set at the ceiling falls eleven meters onto the plate below, which is to say a grove's children land more or less on its own doorstep, which is exactly the wrong place for them, and is why everything else in the hall matters.

Because the geckos climb. They live on the sweating north faces of the columns, licking the threads, territorial along a sweat-line the way an otter is territorial along a bank; they are the only animals that routinely walk from the ceiling forest to the ground forest and back. They eat the small pale fruit at the truss ends and defecate the seed forty meters down the hall on a warm column base, in a seam, three centimeters from a quiet plane if they are lucky, and inside the loudness if they are not. Most of the seed is not lucky. There is a great deal of seed.

And the swifts — pale, small, the only citizens of the volume — patrol the curtains where the loudness stuns insects and drops them. They feel the map on their feathers, a pressure gradient read across the whole spread of a wing, and they fly the walls the way a swallow flies a cliff. They roost in the nulls, hanging on a truss member with their feet placed exactly, because a null is a hand wide and a swift is a hand wide.

So there are two geometries in the room and they do not translate. The rats know the hall as a sequence of doorways: eleven thresholds, each with a name, each with a history, and between them nothing but roaring, which is not a place, which is not in the language. The swifts know the hall as a shape: eleven walls, and between them the whole navigable world. A rat cannot imagine the middle of the bay. A swift cannot imagine why anyone would care about a seam.

---

**The fish is the first to know.**

In the sump, in water that has been around the loop since before the door shut, there is a blind fish, and it is not really a swimmer, it is a listener with a tail. It lies against the inflow with its whole flank pressed to the steel and its lateral line open, and the shaft comes to it through the plate and through the water without ever having to be air. It has no ears to ruin. It has a body that is one long ear.

When the pitch below drops, the fish spawns. Not afterward — at the change, in the first hours, years before the fog loses its support, decades before the ferns have finished walking. The rats have a season named for it. The season's name is a drum-phrase that means roughly *the water is thick, go to the sump, the young are good*, and it is the closest thing in the hall to a calendar, and it is a syllable ending, and nobody knows.

In the drip pans under the head bearing there are larvae that eat soap and bronze. Their castings feed a thumbnail orchid rooted in a grease cup. The orchid is eating the bearing, a few microns a year. The bearing is eating itself faster, and truing its own seat as it goes, and is now rounder than any lathe made it, and has been for six hundred years.

---

**The rats are pale and greasy and long-toed and they are not stupid.**

They have no functioning ears and never have; the air is not a medium here, it is a hazard. Everything they say, they say with their forefeet on forty-millimeter plate, and steel carries it five kilometers a second and carries it beautifully, and so their world is not a room full of noise, it is a room full of *conversation*, arriving through the ground, from everywhere, all at once, in a language of taps.

The language is topological. A noun is a place: not a place described, a place *addressed* — a node, a still line, a seam, a quiet plane, given by the drum-figure that locates it. A verb is a movement between nouns. A sentence is a route. To say *I am hungry* you say a path from where you are to where the food is, and the tense is in how many doors it crosses.

Which means every noun in the language moves.

A rat is named for the quiet it was born in, and the quiet walks three meters in forty years, and so a rat's name slides slowly away from the rat, and an old rat's name refers to a place its grandchildren have never stood. Genealogies are itineraries. To recite your ancestry is to give a set of directions that no longer work, and everyone understands this, and it is not tragic, it is simply what a name is: a thing that was true and is now a bearing.

They survey. This is the profession, and it is the dangerous one. A pacer goes out from the edge of a null with its forefeet drumming and reads the plate's answer — strike, feel the return, strike again — because a still line answers differently from a moving one, and the difference is in the wrist. They walk out into the loudness until the toes go numb, and then a little further to fix the edge, and then back. Old pacers have short feet. The pattern of what they have lost is a map of where the quiet has been, and the young read it the way we read a shoreline.

Their politics are in the trusses, and are about crossings: who may pass a given door, who must go around through the drains, which lineage holds the warm copper where the busbar makes thirty-one degrees and everything breeds faster. Their grievances are in the drains, and are about water, and are older than any lineage, and are never settled, because the water is an inventory and everyone knows it. Every rat in the hall is drinking a neighbor.

They ride the sweeper. It comes once every forty hours, and forty hours is a day, and the rats' whole reckoning of time is passes; they nest in its housing, in felt they have pulled from the pulley collars; there are mites in its brushes and silverfish in its tally drum, generations of them, an entire small nation whose country moves ninety-one meters and back. In the drumming the sweeper's name is a route that does not change, which is the definition either of a god or of a corpse, and the rats have never needed to distinguish.

It goes around the dead grove at the eastern gable. Grey, standing, perfectly preserved, ferns like paper, moss like ash. The rats go around it too, and they still say its name, and the name is a set of directions to a place where the drum comes back wrong. It is the only lie in the language and everyone tells it. It is how they say *not fast enough*. It is how they say *hurry* to a child.

---

**And there is one more thing they do, and it has no use.**

In the high trusses, above the fog, in relays, in shifts, in a drumming so old the phrasing has gone strange, the rats keep a list.

It is a list of addresses. Every quiet, every still line, every seam that has ever been named, in order — and after each one, appended, the older positions, the ones the pacers found and the ones their mothers found and the ones nobody has stood in for four hundred years. It is recited in pieces by animals that will never hear the whole of it. It is corrected only by a pacer's foot, and a correction is checked by three others, and an unverified correction is a serious crime, and the punishment is exile across the loudness, which is death, which is not a place.

It is not a poem. It has no imagery, no gods, no consolation. It is nine hundred years of centimeters. It is the only object in the world that holds the shape of what the hall has been doing, and the animals maintaining it believe — as far as belief is a thing that can be held in a body with no ears — that they are simply keeping the addresses straight, because a name that is wrong is a route that kills you.

They are not remembering. They are *keeping*. There is a difference and it lives in the relays: no rat has ever been able to say why the oldest positions are retained, and every rat retains them.

---

Here is what lives in the hall, then, at the largest scale it has.

The hall is a closed end. It exists to reflect, to be the hard wall a wave needs in order to stand, and everything alive in it is the film that grows on a mirror. But a mirror does not only reflect. It also returns. And the list is drummed into forty-millimeter rolled plate, and the plate is riveted to the columns, and the columns run four and a half kilometers down through a cloud sea to something that modulates the shaft by a few parts per million and holds the change for forty years.

Steel carries it at five kilometers a second. It has been carrying it for nine centuries. Every grievance in the drains, every crossing-right in the trusses, every correction verified by four numb feet and entered in the relays — all of it goes down, continuously, into the thing that is speaking.

One party holds a pitch for four decades and then changes it. The other holds a list for nine hundred years and then adds a line. Neither has ever been introduced. Neither has ears. Neither knows the other is a party.

And on the plate, at the seventh quiet, a young pacer with two toes gone from the left forefoot strikes, and reads the wrist, and strikes again, and finds the still line three millimeters east of where the list says it is. She sits back on her heels in the warmth and the fern-smell and the small tick of water finding a seam, and she is frightened, because a correction is a serious thing. Beyond her shoulder the air is a hundred and twenty-eight decibels of bright shear. On this side a cricket holds its one vowel. Above her the fog blizzards with moths, and the ceiling flowers, and a gecko is carrying next century's grove down a column in its gut.

She drums it. Three others come and check her foot.

The snail lifts its sole. The ridge shifts the width of a hair. The list gets one line longer, and the line goes down.
