# The People of the Held Breath

## i. What they call themselves

Their word for a person means *a going-through*. Their word for a crowd means *a channel*. Their word for a stranger means *new water* — one who has not yet learned to fall — and their word for dying is the ordinary word for *still*, the same word they use for a rope left slack, a hinge unworked, a district nobody swims.

They are not a slow people. They are a people whose emergencies take a month, and who therefore have no patience at all for the kind of hurry that makes noise.

A resting heart of thirty-one. Ninety skins if you are careful, a hundred and ten if you are lucky and never went down to the mouths. Enormous dark eyes with almost no white to them, and a way of looking straight at your face for a long time that visitors find aggressive and is not: the face is the only fine print in this country. You cannot read a mark on a tool. You can read a twitch at the corner of a mouth at ten paces, and they do, constantly, the way you would read a page. Staring is not rudeness here. It is literacy.

They have no small writing. What writing they have is big and tactile: notches the length of a forearm, knots the size of a fist, records you read with your hands in the dark. A city of people who cannot write their own names and can feel, through four hundred meters of taut cord, that the woman at the far end is lying.

## ii. The hands

Everyone is holding some end. This is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing they understand.

A child holds the house-line while her mother swims out. A man dozes with three turns around his wrist and wakes when the district's mood changes. The very old — the ones who have gone gritty and half-marble and sit in their niches with their hands folded because folding is now the only thing hands do — the very old are the most fluent people in the city. They have nothing else left. They hold the ends all day and all night and they talk, and gossip, and refine, and remember, and lie beautifully. The stone ones are the switchboard. A neighborhood's opinion passes through the hands of four immobilized women who have not moved since their sixtieth skin, and those women are not passive. They edit.

Marriage is two people agreeing to share a cord and be worn smooth on the same schedule. You can read the length of a marriage by running the line: the polish, the thinning, the splices. A splice is an anniversary. Some couples have eleven knots in their cord and are proud of every one. A cut cord accretes at the ends into two blunt knobs, and a person carrying stumps is known to be divorced by anyone who touches the line, which is everyone, forever.

And in a great many houses there is a cord tied to a dead grandmother's folded hands, because it was tied there when she was alive and nobody has untied it. People pluck it. It answers. It is only physics — a taut string does what a taut string does — and no one in the house ever says so, and the children are told it is bad manners to say so, and the adults go on saying good morning to the door.

## iii. The archive

A book is a rope.

The histories of this city hang in a low grown room off the Weir: nine hundred cords, knotted in the old dialect, each one a run of events, a lineage, a flood-mark, a grievance. And because everything that holds still gets a rind, a knot left alone for thirty years fills in and becomes a lump, and a lump says nothing.

So the archive is kept by being read. Readers come on a rotation and run the cords hand over hand, wearing the knots crisp, and the wearing is the preservation, and the wearing is also destruction, because wear is the only subtraction this country has ever known. Every reading takes a little of the words away. The floor beneath the archive is swept like a shrine.

That sweepings-dust is the finest and most abundant dust in the world. It is called *the sayings*. It is the ground-off substance of nine hundred years of things that happened.

Remember it. It comes back.

## iv. The pipe-layers

The least honored guild knows everything.

Ondu has been laying and listening for forty-two skins. She can put her cheek against a trunk-section and tell you which lung it feeds, how many people talked in it last night, and whether the resin at the ninth joint is going to fail in a year or in ten. Her ears are her capital. She has refused two marriages on the grounds that the suitor's household was on a line she did not trust.

Her granddaughter Coir is eleven and small enough for the mains. This is the apprenticeship: you go into the air pipe, on your belly, in the dark, in a tube of sealed lungwood a hand and a half wide, with a resin pot and a scraper, and you crawl the undersides of the bridges for a hundred meters and fix the joint from inside. Coir is the only person in the city who is ever alone in air. Everyone else's air is a room with people in it. She says it is like being inside a throat that is listening.

She hears everything. All the lungs open into the mains. Every argument, every ridiculous gossip, every proposal of marriage, every tax that isn't a tax, arrives in the pipe as a thin dry voice under her elbow, and she comes out at the end of a shift full of other people's lives, and her grandmother debriefs her, and this is why the pipe-layers, who are treated like plumbers, are the actual government.

A blocked line kills a district over a month, in its sleep. Ondu has walked three of them out. She says the worst part is not the dying. It is that the survivors are so quiet about it, and the doors seal so politely behind them, and in eighty years the lane is a variation in the grain.

## v. The deep-swimmers

They go down to the mouths at a hundred and fifty meters to bring up the birth-water, still copper-tasting, still hot, that seeds the seeps and makes the moss lay stone fast. They are honored, fêted, married young, and half marble by forty-five. The joints go first: a grittiness in the knuckle, then a click in the hip, then the slow beautiful terrible stiffening that everyone in this city is heading toward and that they are heading toward at speed.

Hesk is forty-eight skins and sits in a niche at the Weir with her hands in her lap and her wrists like knucklebones of polished rock. She is asked what she saw down there about four times a day. She has answered it eleven thousand times. The answers do not agree, and she stopped correcting them thirty years ago, because she noticed that people remember the corrections and not the answer.

What she says, when she is tired and honest, is that the mouths are not holes. Holes have edges. She says the water comes up *fond*. She says she has put her hand into a throat and felt the throat be aware of her hand, and that this is a thing she has no cord-pattern for, and that she has therefore spent her whole life saying it in a language that turns it into weather.

Once, in her nineteenth skin, she brought up a sealed jar with something pale in it, and set it on the sill of the physicians' house, and went home.

Nobody has ever proved that this is where the biter came from. Everybody knows it is.

## vi. The mason's animal

Curd-moss is not a plant. It has never been a plant. It is a colonial animal, a filter-feeder, a million small mouths in a felt, laying down limestone as its skeleton because that is what it does instead of thinking. The green count it among their nine kinds anyway, the way you count a grandmother among the household.

It is herded. It has strains and bloodlines and reputations: slow-white, which is patient and flawless; sweet-tooth, which is fast and coarse and will fill a weave in eighteen years and crack it in sixty; the old Weir strain, which nobody can breed anymore and which every mason claims to have. A jar of moss is the dowry. A bride carries her family's animal to her husband's seep, and the tower's skin is the marriage of two mosses, and you can tell a mixed lineage at a touch by the grain, and people do, and comment.

A moss lineage can die. It is the only death in this city that is not a person and not a district, and there is a formal cord-pattern for it, a slow triple damping, and when it goes out on the lines the whole city stops talking for a count of nine.

Here is what the animal eats: the fine shed matter of the green, and the spores, and — preferentially, greedily — the dust of use. The grit of feet and hands and rope-wear. The mason's animal eats the only substance in the world that has been taken away from anything.

Which means a busy lane feeds its walls. The center stays clear because it is swum; the sides fatten because the swimming makes grit and the grit feeds the moss and the moss lays stone. A street here does not narrow. It becomes a throat. Two hundred years of traffic and the gap between the towers is a round bore, and then a narrow bore, and then a hairline, and then it is grain.

## vii. The last thing that fits

Sesha is a thin-way swimmer. Her guild is paid by the district to keep alive the routes nobody wants.

She swims the ninth lane of Low Rungs twice a day. It is a bore now, shoulder-width, ribbed with the ghost of a hand's-breadth lattice, and she goes through it on her side with one arm forward like a woman threading a needle forty meters long. In eleven years, the guild's estimate, it will be head-width. In thirty, nothing. She is the last thing that fits, and she knows it, and she has a joke about it that she plucks so often that the pattern has worn smooth.

The lane has eleven dead women at eleven doorways and no living residents. Sesha holds all eleven cords. She is the entire population of that neighborhood's conversation. She reports the weather to a woman who has been stone since before Sesha was born; she asks a lintel-holder's advice about a boy; she has, over twenty years, developed a private dialect for the ninth lane that nobody else in the city can read, full of abbreviations for people who cannot answer.

Twice a year she goes deeper, into the parts that have already gone to grain, and puts her hand on a doorway she can feel but not see — a pocket in the loaf — and counts. This is not sentiment. This is the census. The city's dead outnumber its living by a factor nobody has ever agreed on, and Sesha's guild is the only body that has ever tried to count them, and their figures are disputed on the cords at all hours by people who have never been down a bore in their lives.

## viii. The six

Kettle, Tallow, Amity, Sump, Nine, and Bell.

Blind, pale, ox-sized, mild to the point of saintliness, cropping the lamp-meadows on an eleven-year rotation that the meadow-wardens enforce with a fanaticism outsiders find comic until they see the meadow. They have districts. They have a sort of citizenship. There is a cord-pattern that means *Kettle is in the west shallows* and it is broadcast, unprompted, several times a day, because people like to know.

They are milked. You can only drink milk in air — in water it will not mix, it hangs there in a soft white fist and then goes up, and children are scolded for trying — so milk is a lung-food, a thing you take sitting down with your family in a dome that smells of resin and crushed leaf, and it is therefore associated with everything warm and domestic and slightly boring about being alive.

Bell took a biter's bite nine years ago on the left flank, and the wound did not close, and then it softened.

Nine thousand people ask after an ox. That is not a figure of speech. The pattern *how is Bell* goes out on the long lines at all hours, from the rim settlements, from the tongue-walkers, from a child in a pipe. There are people who have never seen Bell and would weep.

## ix. The wound-house

Vann has spent her entire adult life on one wound. She is fifty-one skins and her hands still work and she has not taken a marriage because the wound was more interesting, and she says this without embarrassment, and everyone understands.

What is living in Bell's side is not the biter and is not Bell. It is small. It is many. It is *soft*, and softness in a country where nothing softens is a thing that stops your breath. Vann's word for it is four years old and is already the most-used new pattern on the cords.

She has three theories and she believes a different one on different days.

The first: it came down with the birds. Feathers, feet, the outside world's ordinary invisible freight, arriving once a year at the lip for eleven hundred years and finally taking.

The second: it came up. Out of the mouths, out of the warm throats, in a jar, on a sill, in the nineteenth skin of a woman who is now furniture. New water carries new things. The country is not finished. Why should its animals be?

The third she does not put on the public lines. The third is this: nothing in this country has ever removed anything from anything, except feet. The biter is the first animal to do what people do. It learned subtraction from us — from the wear of hands, the sweep of the step, the grit under the archive. And having learned to take a piece out of a living thing, it taught the piece to go on taking. Predation is not a new animal. It is a new *geology*, arriving in the shape of a mouth.

And in the private lung behind her house, in a stoppered jar, Vann has milk that has gone thick and smells sharp and has not killed her, because she tasted it, on a dare she gave herself, in her forty-ninth skin.

She has not thrown it out. She has not told the cords. She goes in, and lifts the lid, and smells it, and puts the lid back, and comes out and is normal. She is watching a disaster or a birth and she suspects the two are the same thing seen from different ends, and she has begun to suspect that the difference is only whether you are the one who gets eaten or the one who gets to eat.

## x. The birds, and the shutter party

Three pairs, sometimes four. They come down through the fog at the saddles once a year to drink at the lip, and their arrival is the only holiday this city has that is not maintenance.

They land on the posts. Of course they land on the posts. Nine hundred years of flood-marks, arm-length notches cut into grown stone, and the birds stand on them and shift their feet and their beaks worry the edges, and the marks lose a hair of themselves a year.

The lip-watchers have begun to sweep the posts. The sweepings of the ninth post are the oldest dust in the world: nine centuries of measurement, ground away by weather that has feet.

The shutter party wants nets at the saddles. They are polite about it and they are winning, and their argument is unanswerable if you accept their premise: the wound came in on a bird, the dead are the deeds, the deeds are softening, close the door. They are the water's party. They are on the side of accretion, of the rind, of the thing that only ever adds. They have children named Wick and Feather and they will not let those children stand at the saddle next year, and the children have not been told yet.

Against them: the physicians' quiet faction, who say a country that cannot rot cannot digest, cannot sour, cannot rise, cannot make food out of anything but itself; who say we have been eating the same nine things for eleven thousand years; who do not say *I want cheese* on a public line, because it sounds insane, and because it is the whole of their politics.

## xi. The west

The tannin forest is the only antiseptic in the world.

Its water is bitter, astringent, faintly numbing; visibility twenty meters; the shed matter of a drowned woodland lying in it unrotted for millennia, and it turns out that whatever is living in Bell's flank will not live there. Vann has proved this with eleven jars and a lot of patience. Half her work has moved west. There is a grown shed among the pale trunks now with a lamp-lettuce light in it and people talking softly, and it is a hospital, the first, and the softness of speech there is no longer a mystery.

And so the dead are going west too.

It began with one household in the Grown Quarter, three skins ago, and it was the scandal of the decade. They cut the ankle-ropes at their own sill — cut them, in a city where the worst crime there is has always been untying somebody else's grandmother — and they towed their two sisters away through the upper streets, folded hands, drifting slightly, on a long cord, four kilometers, to the bitter water where nothing softens.

Moving house means leaving your dead. The dead are the deed. Nobody moves house.

Unless you take the deed with you.

There have been nineteen such towings this year. They are done at the quiet hours and nobody plucks. Four kilometers of cord and not one word on it, and every hand in the city holding an end and feeling the tow go by, the particular slow drag of a stone-shaped woman being pulled somewhere else, and nine thousand people saying nothing at the same time, which is a thing you can hear.

## xii. The dust houses

Three houses in the Grown Quarter have begun to wet the sweepings and shape them and see whether they will hold.

Nim's is the furthest along. Nim is thirty-four skins, a rope-field heir, entirely unimportant, and she has made a bowl.

It cost her four generations of her family's sweepings and a purchase from the archive that she will be paying off in cordweed until she dies. She shaped it wet, in air, in the private lung, with her thumbs — the first thing in this country whose inside was decided rather than grown — and then she carried it down and hung it in a seep for six years, and the animal skinned it, and what she has now is a stone vessel with a core of dust: a little grey-brown heart of ground-off hands and feet and rope, sealed in the same rind as everything else.

It holds water. Of course it holds water; everything here holds water. But it holds it *chosen*, at a lip she made, in a shape she thought of, and when she puts it on the sill the water inside stands slightly proud of the rim and quivers when she swims past, and it is the sea in miniature, and she knows it, and she has never once said so out loud, because you would have to say it in a lung, to people, and she is not ready.

The problem is supply. A few pinches a year, per house. And the archive has the most, and the finest, and the archive's dust is made by reading, and reading wears the words away.

There is a new verb on the cords. It means *reading for grit*: running a history hand over hand, fast, hard, not to remember it, not to keep it, but to grind it. To make clay out of what happened.

It is not a nice verb. There are people who will not pluck it. There is a reader at the archive, a stone woman of eighty-one skins who has not moved in twenty years, who has caught two apprentices doing it and who now holds the ends of the reading-room lines herself, all of them, all day, in her lap, and who is the most feared person in the city.

And the mason's animal eats the same dust. Every pinch Nim buys is a pinch the moss does not get, and the masons have noticed, and the masons are ancient and rich and have eleven thousand years of moral authority, and they have begun to say, on the long lines, in the small hours, that the potters are taking the food out of the mouth of the thing that builds the world.

They are right. That is what makes it interesting.

## xiii. The lip

Old Marl keeps the ninth post. His daughter walks the tongues.

When the sea overtops a saddle it does not pour, it walks: a standing tongue of warm water going out along a ridge, holding itself, exploring. The tongue-walkers go with it. They are the only people who ever leave, and they leave at the speed of a hand's breadth in a generation, and they come back.

She swims out along the ridge with a jar of her mother's moss and a cable of cordweed and a tethered child, and she plants the first animal in a country with no name, and comes back in a season to find it has a skin. There are four fingers of sea out there now with the beginnings of a grown thing at the head of each: a scaffold, a mark tied to it, a shrine the size of a fist. Her grandchildren will peel the forms off. She will not see it. Nobody who plants a tower sees it. This is not tragic here; it is simply the tense everyone speaks in.

And Marl measures the bulge, and the curve is getting steeper, and he can do the arithmetic, and so can about four hundred other people, and none of them are frightened, or none of them will admit to it in a lung.

Four hundred cubic kilometers, one afternoon. The first rivers. The first mud. The first sand, the first soil, the first salt, and everything downstream that has never existed here. They do not call it an ending. They call it a delivery, and they argue about the date the way people elsewhere argue about a wedding.

Vann's faction has begun to point out, quietly, that there may be two deliveries, and that the small one is already in Bell's side, and that if it goes through the ceiling-midden — kilometers of pressed fronds lying intact against the silver, eleven thousand years of uneaten rising — then the water will cloud, and the far towers of the Weir will go away, and the children will grow up not knowing what three hundred meters looks like, and there will be soil.

Soil is what you get when something is finally allowed to be taken apart.

## xiv. The house with the two sisters

At the doorway, the grandmother. Folded hands, tanned, a new skin on her shoulders this year as last, the broken thumb that makes this address this address and not the one forty meters on.

Her granddaughter swims the street twice a day so that it will still be a street next year, and holds four ends while she does it, and is thinking about a boy and about a bowl and about whether the birds will come.

Under her bare heel the step loses a hair's breadth of itself.

On the sill there is a small grey vessel with a lip that holds the water slightly proud, and inside it, held, quivering, is a mouthful of the world — and the vessel is made of nine hundred years of things that happened, ground off by hands, swept up, kept in a jar, wetted, shaped by a woman with thumbs, and skinned over by an animal that has never once been asked what it thinks.

It is the first thing in this country that was made out of loss.

It is holding water that has not yet learned to fall.
