## The Sixty

Ask how many people live in Nine and you will be told sixty, but the number is not arrived at by counting. It is arrived at by listening. Every morning the gauge-reader stands at the brass staff with her book and her pencil and, while she waits for her eyes to adjust, she takes the town's attendance the way a conductor takes a hall: the chain's tick, the transformer's hum, the four or five human sounds currently in the air, and the enormous attentive silence where the other fifty-five are. If a sound is missing she notices before she could say which. She has never in her life been in a room with all sixty at once except twice, and both times it was because someone had died.

They are a population of ears. They are also, from the knee down, a population of legs.

This is the thing visitors never adjust to. The light in Nine is hooded downward and amber and lives at the height of a shin, so what you know of your neighbors is their feet: the felt slippers with the double-stitched toe, the tender's shins mottled from thirty years of cold water, the boy who walks on the outsides of his soles and has done since he was four and will do until he is put in the water. You will know a man's calves for three decades before you know his face. Faces are for the bathhouse, which is heated on the fourth day, and for the high-water months when everyone lives one flight closer together, and for the dying, when the lamp is allowed to be lifted. To see someone's face is an event. There are people married forty years in Nine who could not draw each other's eyebrows but could identify each other's cough from four hundred paces across open water, through fog, in sleep.

So they are not intimate the way a village is intimate. They are intimate the way an orchestra is intimate. Nobody runs. Nobody shouts. When a woman sets down a cup on the far side of the chamber, sixty people receive, without wanting it, the small private fact of how tired she is.

---

A child is named for the drip-line it was born under. This is why they learn the drip-lines before they learn the stairs: you cannot be told who you are until you can find your own name in the dark, by ear, from anywhere in the room.

The names are therefore not family names. They are weather. Kettle. Sill. Long Weep. Second Sister. Bell. Wick. The same nine or ten dozen names come around again as the births come around, and a Kettle is not related to the last Kettle except by having been born in the same wet place, which in Nine is considered a substantial relationship.

When you die, your name is retired from the living and given to a sump-hound.

Nobody remembers starting this. It is simply what happens: within a season of a death, a blind pink salamander in the condenser coils is called by the dead person's name, and answers to it, and comes gliding out of the warm water to follow the tenders on their rounds. There are about two hundred hounds and therefore about two hundred names in the dark, fanning and closing their gills. The current gauge-reader is called Sill. There is a hound called Sill that was her grandmother, and it follows her at heel on the morning round, and she talks to it — not sentimentally, not as to a ghost, but in the manner of a woman reading aloud to a colleague who cannot check her figures. *Two fingers down from yesterday. You'd have said the roots. You always said the roots.*

Children are not frightened of the hounds. Children are not frightened of anything in Nine, which is one of the town's few genuine failures of imagination, and the adults work quietly to correct it.

---

At a naming, a thread is strung for the child on the middle terrace, in the child's own drip-line, weighted with a lead bead like all the others. It hangs there for the length of a life.

This means that a person's age is not a number of years but a diameter. *She's a pea and a half.* *He's near marble.* A man of forty-nine will walk past his own thread on his way to the pump-house and put his thumb against the bead without looking, the way you'd touch a doorframe going through, and go on. There are people who have not touched their thread in twenty years and people who touch it every day, and the town has opinions about both.

The commercial beads are harvested and crated and go up the ninety-one steps to an instrument nobody has seen. Your own bead is not harvested. When you die, your thread is cut from the middle-terrace rail, carried down the iron stair at the north compass point, and lowered on a cord into the black water of the drowned hall, and re-hung there in the same drip-line, which is still active, which has been active the whole time, which will go on depositing in the dark at the pace of a fingernail for as long as the hill has water in it.

So the drowned floors are two things at once. They are the abandoned commercial stringing of four generations, thickened and furred, some beads now the size of a fist. And they are everybody.

The tenders lower a lamp on a cord sometimes and look. What they see is a ranked curtain standing in still water, and among the ranks a certain number of threads hung by a knot called the return, and the largest spheres down there are the oldest people, and the town knows this, and the ledger says *lower floor, pending,* in a different hand each generation, and the line is carried forward.

The eels go down among them. The eels eat gut. The threads are gut. Every year the eels come up with the high water and are taken and stripped and their gut is dried and twisted and strung, and the town's dead are re-hung on lines made from the animals that have been eating the town's dead since before the books begin.

This is not a horror in Nine. It is not even a mystery. It is the closed circle, stated plainly, and the plainness is the point: there is nothing here to be reconciled to, because nothing here was ever separated. A woman of eighty will say *I'll be a marble by the time the water drops* and mean it as a plan, and go back to trimming a wick.

---

They have two of everything, because the year has two moods.

Seven months of falling water: the lower terrace dry, the stringing floor open, the workshops spread out along the wall, people at their own doors, formal, dispersed, using the long names — Kettle of the Middle, Sill Who Reads. Five months of rising: the lagoon, the punt, the workshops moved up a level, everybody living one flight closer to everybody, and the short names, and the faces.

The grammar changes with it. There is a way of saying a thing in low water that asserts it, and a way of saying the same thing in high water that only offers it, and using the low-water form in the fifth month is a small aggression, and using the high-water form in the second month is either poetry or a proposal. Children get this wrong until they are about eleven and are corrected by other children, mercilessly, at a volume that would not carry three feet outside.

Almost everyone in Nine was conceived in high water and born in low. Almost every marriage was made in the five months and survived the seven, or did not, and the ones that did not are conducted afterward with a courtesy so exact that visitors mistake it for love.

And once a week, on the fourth day, in the bathhouse, in steam, with the lamps turned up because there is no open drinking water in that room, sixty people look at each other's faces.

That is the festival. It happens fifty-two times a year and nobody has ever called it that.

---

Some of them, in particular:

**Sill, who reads the gauge.** Four generations of books, three hands, hers the third. She writes the number every morning in a script that has drifted so slowly across thirty years that she cannot see the drift, only that her grandmother's letters lean and hers stand up. She keeps a second book, unofficial, in which she has copied out all four generations of numbers as a single curve on a single long sheet of folded paper, and the curve does something. Not the breath — the breath is faithful, seven and five, two and a half meters, lip to lip. The *mean* climbs. A hair's breadth a decade, which is the same rate at which a wall thins. It has climbed since the lower floors were strung. It is why they were strung and why they are under water now. She has shown the sheet to four people in nineteen years. All four looked at it for a long time and then said something about the roots.

**Oss, who listens to the chain.** His entire office is to notice a stoppage. Nine hundred buckets, one revolution in an hour and eleven minutes, and the only catastrophe the town can suffer is silence. He is paid in iron and felt and lamp-glass to be the one person in Nine who does not sleep soundly. He is fifty-one and has never heard it stop. He has heard it *nearly* stop twice and both times he was standing in the pump-house before the second tick failed, and both times the cause was a snail, and he does not tell people this because they would laugh, and laughter in Nine is loud.

**Bell, who carries the crate.** Once a decade, up the ninety-one steps on his back, and down again empty. He has been up four times. He is the only person alive in Nine who has seen the sky more than twice, and the only one who has seen a horse, and his account of the horse has been repeated so often that it has become a unit of measurement: *a hound the size of a room, and it did not care about me at all.* People use *horse* now to mean a thing that exists and is real and has no bearing on anything. He gets headaches up there. Everyone does — the transformer isn't there, and the silence of the upper world is not silence but a roaring absence, and the light comes from *everywhere*, including from above, which the body reads as falling. He goes up because someone must and because his mother did. He has never once been asked what the sun is like. It is considered rude, the way asking a widow about the funeral is rude.

**Mera, who keeps the empty rooms.** There are doors standing open in Nine onto rooms with nothing in them but a chair and a hook. She oils those hinges. She wipes those chairs. She clears the gutters above rooms nobody has entered in her lifetime, and trims lamps in corridors that lead to other empty rooms, and she is not mad and she is not a relic; the town pays her, in iron and felt, to keep Nine complete. In the mess there are twelve tables and two are used. She wipes all twelve. There were meant to be twelve chambers and there are eleven other holes in the world with iron in them, and she has never been to any of them, and she does not say — she has never once said — that she is keeping the place ready. When asked why, she says the hinges seize. This is true.

**Wick, who stole light.** The one law in Nine is older than its reason: do not point a lamp at open water. He was fourteen and he wanted to see his own face, there being no mirror in town that did not show him a hand's-width at a time, and he held a lamp out over the third shrimp-bed and looked at himself for perhaps twenty seconds. The bed did not spawn that season. For five months the town drank through gravel and the water tasted of wet iron and everybody knew whose fault it was and nobody said a word to him about it, ever, which is the worst thing Nine can do to a person. He is sixty-three now. He is the tender who lowers the lamp on the cord into the drowned hall, because that water is not drunk, and the law's reach has been argued about for four generations at a volume no outsider would call speech, and the compromise the town arrived at without ever admitting there was a compromise is that the looking is done by him. Once or twice a year he goes down to the low-water line with a cord and a hooded lamp and looks at the dead for as long as he likes. He has never described what he sees. He does not have to. Everyone knows what's down there. It's their families.

**And a thread on the middle terrace that has never been cut.** You only lower a thread when you have a body. Forty-one years ago a boy called Grace went up the ninety-one steps and did not come back down, and his father went to the turn of the stair, where the daylight is a gray coin, and shouted his son's name up the crack.

All sixty heard it. It is the only shout in the town's living memory. In the gauge book for that morning, beside the number, in the grandmother's leaning hand, there is a small mark like a tick and nothing else, and the thread still hangs on the middle terrace in the drip-line called Grace, growing, indistinguishable from every other thread, because a bead cannot tell you whether the person it belongs to is drowned or married or living in a town with a sky.

---

The people are not the majority. They are not even the government.

Nine has one clause of constitution and it is about shrimp: no light on open water, because above a certain brightness they stop spawning, and the town drinks through its fauna, and a town that has broken its kidneys is a town of sixty people carrying buckets up ninety-one steps. Every human arrangement here — the hooded lamps, the ankle-light, the felt shoes, the faces kept for the fourth day, the whole gorgeous deprivation of seeing your neighbors — is downstream of an eyelash-long translucent animal walking on its own reflection in a puddle. The sixty know this. They find it unremarkable. If you ask who runs Nine they will say the pump-house, and they will be wrong, and the shrimp will go on grazing the film off the water in the dark they are legally owed.

The snails are the other guild: thousands of thumbprints rasping the fresh lime off the weeping wall as fast as it arrives, keeping the flowstone lips sharp, working against the water that is the town's mason. Two masons, opposite directions, no arbitration, and between them the walls stay exactly as they are for a hundred and forty years. The lime-skinners trowel their sacrificial skins on and dissolve them off and trowel them on again and consider themselves, correctly, to be the third guild, the slowest, the junior partner.

And in the mess hall there is a moth that walks. It has wings. Its ancestors had wings and used them and it has, over some long span of generations, decided against them, and it crosses the floor at the speed of a minute hand, and there is only ever one, and it has a name, and the name is not from the drip-lines and not from the dead. It is the only creature in Nine that has changed its mind. Children are allowed to move it out of the way of a foot and are not allowed to move it anywhere else.

---

Here is what the sixty are actually doing, under the hill, in the amber ankle-light, with their two moods and their reused names and their cabinet of three hundred and eleven drawers full of crickets that fell into drip-lines in years nobody recorded.

They are waiting for the water to come back down. It was lower once — the drowned floors prove it — and water that has been low can be low again, and the breath is faithful even if the mean climbs, and a hair's breadth a decade is nothing, is less than nothing, is the pace at which a fingernail grows and a wall thins and a bead becomes a marble. There is always later. *Lower floor, pending.* Somewhere above there is an office with a ledger, and in that ledger there is a line for Nine, carried forward every year in a different hand, and it says something similar.

And on the wall above the upper terrace, in the dim that the lamps are not permitted to reach, there is a fourth terrace being cut. One buttress a decade. A pin every few years. The iron is bought with bead-money and the cutting is done by whoever is young and idle in the low-water months, which is one or two people at a time, and none of them will live on it, and their grandchildren may not either. It is being built at the speed the water rises, which is the only speed anything in Nine has ever been built at, and it is not a defense and it is not a hope. It is a message left in the one medium the room respects. It says: *we did the arithmetic, and we kept the hinges oiled, and we are still here, and we cut you a floor.*

A child of six is standing at the top of the stair with her hand on black iron that has not rusted in ninety years, learning the drip-lines before she is allowed to learn the steps. She is being taught to hear the eastern cistern. It takes most children a season. She will get it wrong for a while and then one evening she will get it right, and she will not look up, and she will say *eastern*, and the woman beside her will say *yes*, and that is the whole of the initiation, and afterward there are ninety-one steps down into a small wet room full of pale animals and patient people and two hundred named salamanders, one of which will eventually be called after her.

A drop leaves the ceiling. It falls for a long time through the dark. It lands.

Sixty people, without looking up, know exactly where it went. So do the dead, but they are slower about it, and they have time.
