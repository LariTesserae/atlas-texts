The smell at the crack is them. Resin is the forest, struck matches is the vent, old fat is the herd — but the fourth thing in it, the thing a stranger would call *lived-in*, is two thousand people who have been greasing each other's hands for four hundred generations.

They are lean and warm and slightly glossy. Everyone is greased: face, forearms, the backs of the knees, the crack of the lip that the outside air would otherwise split in a minute. Grease is weatherproofing against a weather that is always the same, and it is manners. You do not ask a guest to sit; you take their hands and work a thumb of clear gut-oil into the knuckles, and while you do it you tell them the news. To be *dry* is the local word for stingy, for cruel, for dead. Their skin is the color of the inside of a shell and stays that way; the sun does not reach them and something in the greaser oil does the sun's work instead, so their legs are straight and their teeth are good and they do not think of themselves as pale. Above, in the light-bar, they are suddenly the color of a wound and cannot keep their eyes open, and this is considered the correct appearance for a person receiving a blessing.

Their eyes are all pupil in the dark, and they blink slowly, and they do not look at each other across a room. There is no across a room. Faces are read at arm's length, so faces are read partly with the hands: the thumb along the jaw, two fingers on the cheekbone, a habitual little pass over the eyebrow that means *go on*. Everyone stands close. Everyone touches constantly, and none of it is affection, or all of it is, in the way that breathing is.

Out of this comes the thing that would take a stranger longest to learn. They have names you can say, and names you can't. The sayable name is for the light-bar hour, when there is enough light to be an individual at a distance. The rest of the day a person is a tap-name: three taps and a slide on the forearm, given at the first rain after birth, unique in the town, and untranslatable into speech. In the dark you are greeted by being *tapped*. In the dark, an argument four tiers down is a conversation between two rhythms. Children learn their own tap before they learn to walk, because it is what stops them being lost.

There is a grammar of stillness, too. In a place with no doors that matter, privacy is not enclosure; privacy is quiet. Lovers go still. The grieving go still. And when the town wishes not to see someone — a man who has done something shameful but not criminal — the town goes still around him, every hand within reach declines to tap, and he walks through two thousand people who are politely, perfectly absent. It is called being held out. Some men have gone up the ladder rather than endure another week of it.

---

They are not a people who believe in an afterlife. They are a people who believe in a food chain, and they are right, and it is tender.

The dead are carried to the firm edge and given to the pan. Everything else in the chamber is eaten promptly — a moth in eleven seconds, a beetle's whole life spent arriving in nine — and the pan is the only slow eater in the world. So a human death is the only event in Underlid that takes time. The family sits at the edge and watches. It takes a day and a night. They narrate it: what has gone under, what is still showing, whose hand is still above the black. Children are brought to see, and are bored, and are made to stay. To die is to be granted the only patience there is, and then to come back up through the mats into the herd, and the herd is milked and eaten, and no one finds this horrible, because the alternative — being left, being kept, being *not used* — is the local definition of damnation.

So the herd is partly the ancestors, and everyone knows which beast is which. A greaser given a dead woman's tap-name is not eaten. It is tapped on the flank, three and a slide, when anyone passes it. It is allowed to grow old and mild-eyed and enormous, and when it dies of age it goes to the pan unnamed, having finished being her. There are usually eleven or twelve of these in the herd at once. Children are told not to ride them. Children ride them.

And there are the gifts. Anything given to the pan at the center — where the tar is glossy and takes a pole and no one can stand — moves outward at the speed a fingernail grows. Which means it stays visible for a lifetime. A bowl put in the black by a woman four generations dead is still out there, a hand's breadth further along than it was at her funeral, and her people can point to it, and do, and argue about exactly how far it has come. The middle of the Slow River is a slow museum of everybody's grief, creeping toward the edge at a rate that can be measured but not seen. Rope-men who go down hooded for the good tar are the only ones who pass among these things. They are asked about them constantly. They have learned to give good answers. Some of the answers are true.

---

The work sorts them, and the sorting is not subtle.

Highest and best are the west tiers, where the light-bar crosses. Light is property here, in stripes, and the stripes are litigated. A family owns not a room but an hour-angle: the right to have the bar lie across their drying-rack for the eleven minutes it takes to pass. Because the Slot is fixed and the sun is not, the bar's foot climbs and slides down the wall across the long year, and this is the calendar. At the year's height the bar comes off the wall entirely and lies out on the black pan, in the middle, where nothing can be built and nobody can stand. That day is the Standing. All two thousand come down to the firm edge and stand shoulder to shoulder in the one place they can all fit, and look at a lit patch of tar nobody can reach, and the moths that live for the bar rise in a column and are eaten, and the shell bell is struck until the striker's arm fails. It is the only hour in the year when the town can see itself entire. People propose marriage at the Standing. People are conceived at the Standing. It is not a solemn occasion.

Lowest are the vent districts, under the north roar, where the gas comes up and the sour pools first and the noise never stops. The vent people are deaf by forty, most of them, and they do not consider this a misfortune. They read the floor. Palms and heels on the stone, they feel the seep's gulp change temper a beat before anyone hears it, and so the sour-watchers are drawn from the vent, and the best rope-men are deaf, and the jar-children — the small ones who sit at the lip of the low stairs all day watching a glow-jar for the first sulk of brown — are taught by deaf women who tap the warning into the child's wrist rather than shout it. The children learn the tap before they learn what it means. That is deliberate.

Then there are the flint-crews, who go up.

They go in pairs, through the Squeeze, out onto the red pavement, in slit-shells that cut the sky down to a stripe. They talk the entire time. They talk about nothing, continuously, at a volume that would be absurd below, because the silence of the flat gets into a person and starts taking things out. It is not the quiet that sickens them; a held quiet is the holiest thing they know. It is the *unroofedness* of it. Above, there is nothing over you, and nothing under the nothing, and a sound you make goes away and does not come back, and this is experienced as a kind of hemorrhage. Crews come down shaking and are put in a warm room and greased and fed and wept over. A person who goes up alone does not come back right.

The punishment for the worst things — for carrying flame low, for fouling a jar, for the few crimes that have no restitution — is not death. It is the ladder. They call it being widened. You are put out on the flat with a shell of lean and a knife, and the town goes down, and the town does not tap your name again. Everyone knows how long it takes. Nobody has ever had to be told twice.

---

The lamp-tenders are chosen from those who cannot pass the Squeeze.

This is stated plainly, as an engineering fact, and only secondarily as a mystery. The bead must never leave the world; therefore its keepers must be people who physically cannot carry it out. There are always a few in Underlid too broad for the door — bodies run wide, or a bad shoulder from a ceiling fall — and these are called the Ungoing, and they are looked after, and the tenders are drawn from them and are, in addition, fed. Deliberately. Richly. From childhood. A chosen tender is a person the town has decided to love and immobilize, and the town is entirely honest with them about it, and they accept, and it is not a sad life. They are enormous and warm and slow and they sit in the best dark on the fourth ledge with children climbing on them, and they are read to, and they arbitrate, and they are the only people in Underlid who are ever alone, because everyone else has agreed to hold out around them for as long as they wish. It is the highest honor and it is a cage and both of these things are said out loud at the choosing.

Their theology is simple and it is not about a maker.

The water-marks in the dome are not tool-work; everyone can see that. The scoops overlap like the inside of a shell. The flutes run parallel. The great lazy S goes from the north end to the south, cutting the ceiling, and it does not begin or end anywhere a person can reach. From this the town has concluded, with a rigor they would recognize as rigor, that the world was made by a *leaving*. Something went through. The room is what is left where it went. They call it the Long Body, or more often just the Going, and the doctrine is that everything now alive — forest, herd, town, bell, you — is what grows in the place a thing has vacated. Creation is subtraction. Holiness is the shape of an absence. Their blessing, said over babies and over the dying and over a good bowl of lean, is *be held*. Their curse is *may you be seen from above*.

And this is why the bead is holy, and the reason is not sentimental. In a chamber where everything is used immediately — where a fallen moth is gone in eleven seconds, where nothing is permitted to persist — there is exactly one object that does not participate. It will not burn. It will not grease. It ruins leather. Nothing eats it. It has no use whatsoever, and it does not go away. Therefore it is not of this world; therefore it is a piece of the Going, left behind, the one thing the leaving forgot to take.

They drink the lean, of course. Everyone does. The rain is caught in shell basins and left to stand, and it separates: oil above, drawn off for the lamps, and beneath it the thin cold fraction they draw through a hole in the basin's foot and drink all day, every day, from birth. But the lean always carries the pan's taste — faintly resinous, faintly of struck matches — and a drink without that taste is not a drink; it is a defect; it hurts the throat. The bead is the lean with the lean taken out. It is the ghost of drinking. Some tenders hold that it is what the Going drank. Some hold that it is what the Going wept. The doctrine does not require them to agree, only to keep it.

It is kept in a shell, and the shell is kept in a nest of shells, each one cut to fit the bead at the time of a particular keeping. There are eleven. The bead now fits the eleventh. There is a twelfth, cut and polished and empty, sitting in the nest, waiting, and everyone in Underlid knows about the twelfth shell, and the tenders' answer to the question is a formula so old it has gone smooth: *it is the same bead; it is only smaller because we are larger.*

Once a generation they bake selenite in the top hearth, and the stone weeps a few beads, and the whole town smells hot crystal for an hour and calls it the Weeping and gets drunk on fermented frond-sugar. What happens to the new beads afterward is the tenders' business. There have been three schisms about it. All three were resolved by everyone going still for a season, which is how Underlid ends arguments, and which works.

---

There is a woman on the sixth ledge whose trade is cutting fall.

She cuts the gutters. Every rain, the drizzle beads on the fronds and runs and stands in cupped leaves and drips, and the town's whole architecture of catchment — the little channels, the lip-cut basins, the graded runs that carry the rain to the shell cisterns without letting it go oily — is her work and her mother's and her mother's. She has spent forty years learning exactly how a thin liquid behaves going down a curved stone: how it braids, how it undercuts the outside of a bend, how it lays a scoop and then another scoop overlapping, how a long slow run will wander into a lazy S if the fall is gentle enough and the stone is patient enough.

She has looked up.

She has looked up in the slack, in the held quiet between breaths, with a glow-jar hooded to a coin of light, and she has watched the flutes all lean one way, and she has followed the great channel from north to south and felt the fall of it in her hands the way you feel a stair you cut yourself, and she has known — fully, wordlessly, with the certainty of a trade — that something *ran* here. Not went through. Ran. Something thin and cold and plentiful, that fell and gathered and went downhill and took the room out of the stone the way her gutters take the water off a ledge, and then stopped falling.

She tried to say it once, at the Standing, with the whole town shoulder to shoulder on the black.

But the word for wet is the word for oily. There is no other. The word for a running thing is the Slow River, and the Slow River is tar, and it is under their feet, and it is warm. Thin, cold, plentiful, falling: she had four words and each one meant something else, and when she put them in a row what came out was that the Long Body had gone through and left the room and everything since was what grows in the leaving.

Which is the doctrine. Which is exactly the doctrine.

They were delighted. They tapped her name up and down the tiers for an hour, three and a slide, two thousand hands, and the bell was struck out of turn, and the tenders came for her that winter and she was chosen and fed. She is very large now. She cannot pass the Squeeze and would not want to. Children sleep against her flank in the slack. She sits in the best dark on the fourth ledge with the nest of shells in her lap and the eleventh in her palm, and she is loved, and she is the most respected person in the world, and once or twice a year she still tries to say it, and every time she says it, it comes out as praise.
