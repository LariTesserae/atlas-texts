Begin with the underside, because nothing here has a back.

Lie on the mirror beneath a carrier and let its feet go over you at a meter an hour, and you will see the parts of it lit as brightly as the parts you have been taught to call visible. Two hundred feet. Each foot a wheel, and each wheel wet, and each wheel turning inside something that is not turning, and the thing that is not turning is dead.

This is the first anatomy lesson of the plain and it takes a long time to believe. A wheel does not grow as a wheel. It grows as a brood. When the carrier makes a foot it pours a slurry of larvae into the socket and lets them set, and they set into a ring, and they die there — all of them, in one motion, an entire generation spent as a surface. The next brood is poured into the ring the first one made. It sets against that dead smoothness with nothing to grip and nothing to fuse to, and so it comes out free: a living axle inside a dead sleeve, turning in the exact shape of its siblings' corpse.

That is a bearing. That is the only way a bearing has ever been made here.

So amend the rule as you learned it. Not every cast walks out. Some broods are meant to stay and die and become the surface the next ones live against. The nacre in a kiln-heart's chamber is layer on layer of children who set and stopped. The races are children. The gauged bores in a carrier's frame are children who were poured round and never moved again. In the sumps between the turning and the still, the grease lives — a colonial slime rendering its own dead into lubricant, farmed by the things it lubricates, and in the races the bearing-mites go back and forth with their mouthparts full of it, eating oxide, paid in fat, keeping the one interface on this continent where a living thing must slide against a dead one forever without wearing through.

When a foot wears out, the carrier does not heal. It withdraws the axle, dissolves the sleeve, and has another child.

---

The lickers are the largest animals that move at a speed you can watch.

Two hundred meters of ribbon, low, mirror-bellied, sky-backed, no eyes anywhere on them. A tongue four hundred meters long, folded in the body like a fire hose, running out over the ground in a slow ribbon of forty thousand teeth. They go in herds of nine thousand and they go in lanes. The lanes are inherited. A herd walks the path its grandmothers walked, mowing the same eighty-centimeter strip of oxide for eleven thousand years, and the strip stays bright, and the strips on either side go orange, and from the eastern margin the whole plain is striped like a mown field by animals that cannot see.

For a long time I read the polishing as hygiene. It is agriculture.

Rust is ash. The black mats oxidize fresh metal and take the electrons and leave the orange bloom behind, and the bloom, once it is a millimeter thick, seals the meat away from the mouths that want it. The lickers eat the ash — for the trace metals in it, for the lipid dust of the aerial plankton that has settled and gone rancid in it, for the mat-cells trapped and starving inside — and in eating the ash they open the rock again. They are ploughmen. Every bright surface on this continent is a field that has been harvested and turned. Their droppings go out as a fine bright powder that hangs in the fog and falls everywhere else, and the whole plain's iron is redistributed by animals defecating downwind of their own feeding.

And because they keep it bright, the sky comes back up from below, and the gasket flora in the seams get light they could not otherwise get, and the flying plankton can see each other, and there is no dark side to anything, and every predator here has had to solve the problem of a world with no shadows in it. None of this is the lickers' business. They are eating lunch.

At the edge of a dead province they stop. The tongue goes out, tastes the oxide — thick, old, dry, no mat under it, no fat in it, nothing living has touched this metal in ninety thousand years — and the whole herd folds its tongue and turns, without hurry, and walks away along a boundary that has not moved in a hundred centuries. Remember that turn. It matters later.

---

Between the freezing plate and the sweating seam there is a hand's width, and there are things that are only that hand's width, and they have no gut.

Junction weed. It grows as whiskers, as braided veins, as a fur of bimetallic hair in the exact interface where ninety degrees meets minus forty, and it makes its living directly out of the difference. No mouth, no digestion, no rock. Two tissues with different appetites for electrons, joined at a hot end and a cold end, and a current falls through the animal all day like water through a mill. It is the only life on the plain that eats neither meat nor light nor waste. It eats the gradient. It is the economy, made flesh, and it is the most fragile thing here: close the seam and it dies in a minute; open the seam and it dies in a minute; it lives only in the exact leak it was born in, and it spends its whole existence trying to keep the leak from widening.

Which is why it is always found in the company of the gasket flora, and why the seam-keepers tolerate both.

A seam-keeper is a sessile thing the size of a dog, wedged where two flank-plates meet, and it has one organ worth naming: a valve in its own side that it can open to bleed hot fluid into the packing around it. It farms the belt-leaves. It warms them, and they swell and seal the gap, and the sealed gap holds the heat that keeps the seam-keeper alive, and the seam-keeper pays for its house in its own blood, continuously, at a rate it can barely afford. A keeper that bleeds too little loses its mat to the frost and dies of the cold in a night. A keeper that bleeds too much is cropped to nothing by the grazers that follow the warm seams, and dies of the grazing in a night. There is no correct amount. There is only the amount that has worked so far.

Down where the seams go deep and the light is only light that has bounced, the mats go pale and fat and throw up orchards of soft fruiting bodies, and among them live the unmolded.

They are the only creatures on this continent that look like creatures. Blobs. Sacks. Wet, un-gauged, asymmetric, pale as a peeled almond, with limbs that fit nothing and mouths that were never a bore. They lost the casting habit somewhere back in the deep past and breed by eggs, and their children resemble them without needing a corpse to tell them how. They have no gauge, no tolerance, no standard, no fit. They are also, in the terms of this place, invisible: everything here hunts by recognizing a shape, and they are not a shape anything recognizes. They wander through the orchards being mistaken for spoil. Some of them are eaten. Most are simply not noticed, which on this plain is the safest thing that can happen to anybody.

---

The frost is a sense organ.

Loose frost jumps on cold plates in the eleven-hertz shiver, all day, everywhere, a fine continuous dance — and spread flat in that dance, white on white, is the frost-reader. Two meters across, four millimeters thick, legs like hair laid out radially into the fern-forest. It does not feel the ground. It feels the frost. Every footfall within forty meters arrives as a defect in the shivering, a place where the crystals are jumping wrong, and the reader reads the interference the way a spider reads a web, except that the reader did not make the web and does not supply the vibration. The continent supplies it. The summed stroke of a hundred million slow bodies is the lamp by which the plain's spiders see.

A frost-reader that finds itself on a plate gone quiet — a stalled press, a cooling flank — is blind, and knows it is blind, and walks.

And in the air, the dusk-runners.

There is one minute of darkness a day in any given place, and it moves at the speed of a running animal, and there is a lineage that has arranged its entire life around being inside it. Eight legs, eyes like dinner plates, a body with almost no fat reserve. They run under the swarm-shade, killing in the brown dusk, and when the shade lifts they are standing in the glare with their pupils shut to pinholes and their prey gone to ground, and they run on anyway, because the shade is not coming back and the next one is eleven kilometers east. They cannot stop. A dusk-runner that stops for a day dies. Their eyes are the largest eyes on the plain and they are used for sixty seconds a day.

The rest of the hunting is done by things with too many legs, waiting in the chip-fall with their limbs open like calipers, taking hot blue ribbons out of the air. The catch does not eat the ribbon. It eats the bloom that has already grown on the ribbon in the four seconds since it left the press, and it holds the ribbon against its belly while it feeds, and the ribbon is still at three hundred degrees, and the catch's belly is a heat exchanger, and it is also, incidentally, warming its brood.

---

The swarms are not weather. They are a search.

Every flying thing on this plain is, at that moment, a larva, and every larva is looking for the same thing: a cavity with nobody in it. That is the whole of aerial life here. The plankton bloom is a continent-wide search image for death, and it is so dense that its shadow arrives as a physical event, and the only darkness that ever crosses this country is the shade of everything looking for somewhere to be born.

They navigate by the plume — eleven kilometers of standing white with its lenticular hat, visible from three days out, and every brood in the air is drifting with reference to it. They navigate by the eleven-hertz, which tells them where work is. And they navigate by the whistle.

The two-day whistle is a large body dying. Something has stalled with its pressure up and its relief port open and it is bleeding out through a gap the width of a hair, and the note goes out along the inversion for four hundred kilometers with no loss at all, so that it arrives at your elbow from the far edge of the world. Every gauger within hearing turns toward it. Every swarm in the air slides downwind of it. It is not a call. Nothing is calling. It is a corpse announcing its own availability in the only language a corpse has, and the heirs come at a run, and the shade of their coming crosses the metal like a cool brown hand.

Almost all of them die. The rain, when it comes — warm, oily, faintly acid, one hour every few years — is the great filter: everything spawns into it at once and the fog fills with a slurry of larvae and the pools under their ice skins fill with a slurry of larvae and the standing water for two hundred kilometers becomes a soup of ambition. Around the puddle the wall of drinking animals stands four deep and ten deep and does not move, and the larvae fall into it, and are drunk.

---

Time here is not one thing.

There is the eleven-hertz, which is the plain's pulse and which nothing owns. Bodies entrain to it over deep time with no conductor; a press that drifts out of phase finds its stroke landing against neighbors instead of with them, and the interference works its way into its bed, and it develops lesions, and it stalls. To be in time is to be well. Arrhythmia is the only general diagnosis available on this continent, and it is read by everything: frost-readers feel it, bearing-mites taste it in the grease, and there are hunters that do nothing all season but wait for a body to fall out of the beat, and then go to it, because it will be dead within the year and its cavity will be free.

There is the hammer, which is the calendar. Eleven days of rising air-note, the sound of a gap closing, and every animal on the plain that has a brood ripe gets it ready. The strike flattens the fog for thirty kilometers and lifts every flying thing at once in a single white sheet of panic, and that panic is the point: the shock is a spawning current, and half the small fauna of the interior times its dispersal to being thrown. In the deep seams the unmolded, who have never seen a sun and never felt a wind, breed by the strike felt coming down through four kilometers of iron. It is their only season. They have no other way to know that anything has happened.

And there is generation time, which is the real clock, and which is not shared.

A press breeds once in nine hundred seasons. A bearing-mite breeds every eleven days. Both of them shrink two percent when they cast. So the giant's lineage loses half its size in three hundred thousand years, and the mite's lineage loses half its size in a year and a half, and everything small on this plain is falling, continuously, at a rate that depends on how fast it can afford to breed.

---

That fall is the engine of the smallness.

Run it out: a founder the size of a hill, casting every eleven days, is the size of a grain of salt in six years, and is at the floor of what a body can be in nine. Every mite you see is in the last decade of a collapse that began with something that bit mountains. The saturation of roles here — five tiers of parasite on parasite, whole food webs that never touch rock — is not an old, patient accumulation. It is a rain. Giants die and throw off broods and the broods come down through the scales like sediment, filling every niche on the way past, and almost all of them reach the bottom and go out.

There are three ways to survive the bottom.

You can stop being an individual. The grease is what that looks like: lineages that shrank past the floor and dissolved into coloniality, rendering their dead into lubricant, holding no shape, keeping no gauge, farmed by everything, never dying of smallness because they gave up the thing that smallness kills. It is the largest organism on the plain by mass and it has no outline at all.

You can go down to the rock and stop being an animal, which is what the black mats are, and which is why the mats and the grease taste, to a gauger's tongue, faintly of kin.

Or you can borrow somebody else's scale. That is what the parasite stacks are. A hyperparasite four tiers deep is a lineage that has bought its way out of free-living smallness by hiding inside a body that is still large, and its host is doing the same thing one level up, and so on, all the way to a flank-plate four kilometers long. The whole tower is a stay against shrinking. Nobody in it is making a living off rock. They are making a living off each other's size.

---

Fit is the plain's one aesthetic, and fit is also its disease.

Everything here hunts and hides by gauge. Camouflage in a world with no shadows cannot be a matter of color, so it is a matter of *being a part*: the ambush thing shaped like a gasket, waiting in a seam for whatever comes to seal it; the thing shaped like a shaft, lying in the heap, mirror-bright, correct to eleven microns.

That one is called the fitted, and it is the worst thing on the continent.

Its dispersal stage is a perfect gauged shaft. It does not move, does not feed, does not respire measurably; it lies in the ingot heaps among the waste-with-a-heredity and it waits to be *installed*. Bearing-mites are the repair crews of this world, and a bearing-mite that finds a shaft of exactly the right diameter in a heap will carry it home, because that is what bearing-mites are for. They put it in the bore. It fits. It fits beautifully. And then, over a season, it wakes up and eats the carrier from inside, and the carrier's feet stop one at a time, and the lickers come and read the oxide and find it good, because the fitted keeps its host's surface bright.

Standardization is the vulnerability. A continent where every part is interchangeable is a continent where anything can be smuggled in by being the right size.

Which is one reading of the margins — of the wandering parallels, the rams that no longer fit their beds, the winding-beast extruding a coil of a diameter nothing has used for a million years. The feral provinces are misprints. They are also uninvadable. Nothing shaped like a standard part can be installed in a body that has no standards. Out there the gauges drift and the half-species wander and nothing is quite interchangeable with anything, and the fitted lies in the heaps and is never picked up, and the presses, though they are ugly and wrong and grinding themselves to pieces against beds they no longer match, are clean.

And in the gap between the ram and the bed that no longer fit lives the shim: an animal whose entire lineage is the error. It eats tolerance. It fills the difference between two shapes that were meant to meet and don't, exactly as the junction weed fills the difference between two temperatures that were meant to be apart and aren't. The plain has two kinds of gap-eater and they are not related and they have converged on the same living, which is to say: on the same failure.

---

Now the gauger, and the cold provinces, and what the waiting is for.

Palm-sized. Blind. Its legs are pins of nine standard diameters and its body is a set of go/no-go gauges, and it knows a hole only by whether it enters. It spends its life trying holes. It is not an assessor and it is not clever; it cannot judge a four-kilometer cavity, it cannot even perceive one. It has exactly one talent, and its whole nervous system is spent on it: it can tell a live cavity from a dead one. Heat, the beat, the smell of another brood's setting fluid. Living means death. Dead means maybe.

So it goes where the lickers turn away. One reads the oxide and leaves; the other reads the oxide and goes in. Between those two refusals the whole continent is maintained.

Inside, it is a hundred thousand years of mirror. Nine hundred presses mid-stroke. A carrier still loaded. The swarm fallen in a drift like snow, each larva perfect, cold as the sky, still looking. Nothing rots because there is no water and nothing rusts because rust needs water and the water went elsewhere long ago. The gauger walks over the frozen brood of something that died before its ancestry had language and tries a hole.

It does not fit. It was never going to fit. It spawns anyway, into everything it can reach, and the slurry pours into the cavity and sets — and dies. A mite's physiology at the scale of a hill cannot move, cannot cool, cannot get anything across its own skin fast enough. It cooks in its own setting heat and stops.

But it stops *against the wall*.

That is the whole secret of the dead provinces. Every failed brood plates the interior with its own dead. Microns. A thousand failures make a lining; a hundred thousand years of failures make a lining you could measure with a hand. The cavity is being relined downward, brood by brood, corpse by corpse, by animals that are all dying of the same mistake, and the mistake is the only process on this plain that can make a big mold small enough to be survivable. The dead province is not waiting. It is being renovated by failure.

And when the lining has come down far enough — when the jump is thirty times instead of ten million — something walks out.

It is not the press returned. It is a mite's descendant wearing a hill: a body whose outer wall is a sarcophagus of its own failed siblings, whose instincts are a mite's instincts in an architecture it has never used, doing something no press ever did, in a shape older than every animal that has ever worn it. It will be wrong for a thousand years. Its ram will not fit its bed. Its first stroke will vent a whistle that lasts two days and is heard four hundred kilometers away, and every gauger within hearing will turn toward it, and the swarm will come down the wind, and its shade will slide across the metal at the speed of a running animal.

And the lickers, out on their inherited lanes, will taste a new bloom of oxide where there has been nothing for a hundred centuries — ash, which means something has been fed — and fold their tongues, and turn, and come back.

Underneath the new thing, in the doubled light, on the underside that is as bright as its back, a gauger is already trying a hole.
