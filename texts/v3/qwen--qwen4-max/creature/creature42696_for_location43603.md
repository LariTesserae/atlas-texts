Pick up a bead of the lag. You can; they weigh nothing. Hold it against the black sky and most of them are empty, a shell of gray rind around a bubble of nothing, and the light comes through the wall the color of a fingernail. But one in ten thousand is not empty. Inside, packed into the far side of the cavity where the wall is thickest, there is a pale comma the size of a grain of salt, six-legged, eyeless, wet.

It is not hiding from the roar. It has never been in the roar. A hollow bead of the right wall-thickness and the right diameter cancels its own interior — a silence the size of a pinhead, complete, absolute, with a floor and a ceiling and one tenant. There are more of these rooms on the plateau than there are stones on any beach you have ever walked, and almost all of them are empty, because the geometry has to be exact and the tenant has to find it, and the crossing between one bead and the next takes four seconds and kills most of what attempts it.

So the tenants have solved the crossing the way everything here solves things: by getting carried. A bead lying loose in the lag is knocked about; its little null tumbles and collapses and comes back. A bead seated in the lip of a hole is held. Held, its silence is steady for as long as it is held there, which is to say for as long as somebody wants that hole to sound wrong.

The tenant's whole strategy is to be inside a name when it is being spoken.

---

The name belongs to a bird.

She is ash-white, heron-sized, standing in a pipe with her third lid drawn, and she is working. This is the part that a visitor misses, because it looks like idleness: she has a bead wedged in the lip of her hole, and the hole is sounding a note that does not belong to the chord, and she is seating the bead a fraction deeper with the callused heel of her right foot, and then standing back, and then feeling the floor.

The wind widens a hole whose note is out of the solution. That is the whole of the law here, and it means that a bent note is a wound the stone is closing. Every day the aperture opens a little; every day the bend relaxes toward true; every day she pushes the bead in a little further to hold the wrongness at the pitch she wants. A name here is not a thing you have. It is a thing you keep doing, and the doing is measured against the stone's patience, and the stone's patience is the only clock this world has ever had. There is no evening. There is forgiveness, and it takes about eleven years, and everything alive above the rind reckons its life in how much of it has been granted.

Age is an aperture. An old bird's hole is wide, because it has been forgiven many times, and a wide hole wants a big stone to bend it, and the lag is sorted and coarse and there is nothing bigger. This is how the great lineages end. Not in death — in the exhaustion of available wrongness. A family that has held one pipe for four hundred forgivenesses finds itself with a mouth so generous that no bead in forty miles can make it say anything, and the young of that house go out across the ringing white to find a needle's eye and start again, nameless, or take one.

Understand what this means for the archaeology. Nothing stacks, nothing shelters, nothing accumulates — but the sockets are worn. At the lip of an old pipe there is a seat, a shallow dish polished by ten thousand seatings of ten thousand beads, cut to a shape that no wind would cut. It is the only durable made thing on the plateau and it is a subtraction. Everything here that has been made has been made by taking away.

And inside the current bead, packed against the far wall, the tenant is doing what tenants do: pressing its body and its waste into the bead's own small neck, narrowing it, flattening the bird's name by a hair every year. She fights it by seating deeper. Sometimes she loses. Sometimes a bird abandons a bead and takes another, which is a change of name, which is a change of everything — and the discarded bead rolls back into the lag with its tenant aboard, and the tenant walks out into a world it has no organ for, and has four seconds.

The only thing on this plateau that travels by being argued over.

---

The arguments are not metaphors. Two birds re-tune against each other, one wedge against another, until one note swallows the other, and while they do it the standing wave over that quarter-mile of plain shifts very slightly, because the wave is a solution and they are editing it.

Which moves the quiet.

A node is not a place. It is a relationship, and if you change the terms you change the place. Most disputes are too small to matter: the null leans a hand's breadth east and the lichen edge follows it over a decade, and the cushion is still the shape of a silence, just a slightly different silence. But sometimes a long argument between two great houses near the rim resolves, and a room dies. The green in it goes to dust in a season — no, there are no seasons; in the time it takes a bead to go flat — and the walkers arrive at the coordinates they have carried for six generations and find bare ringing stone, and stand in the roar where the conversation was supposed to be, and cannot hear each other, and cannot hear themselves, and go on.

And sometimes a room is born, out in the white, where nothing has ever been quiet. It lies there empty and perfectly silent for years. It has no green. It has no name. Nobody knows.

---

The walkers know two hundred rooms, and the number is not a fact about the plateau. It is a fact about memory.

They are low and broad-footed and coated in a dark filamental pelt that is not moss and hosts moss, and the coat is alive only in the quiet. Crossing the loud it dies back to a crust of spore and stub; entering a node it greens again within the hour, drinking the dew it has been carrying as salt. So they arrive wearing a corpse and leave wearing a garden, and they are how the green gets from room to room. Every cushion on the plateau is the shape of a silence and the descendant of a back.

They sleep in the loud, standing, mid-stride, because the roar is anesthesia. They wake by arriving. The floor gives out from under the world and the eyes open and there is a sound — a sound, the small obscene racket of one's own body — and this is the alarm and the doorway and the whole of social life, and it lasts as long as it takes to cross a room, which for the small ones is nine seconds.

Everything they have to say to each other has to fit in nine seconds, or in the forty seconds of a middling room, or in the four minutes of the great one. A day of walking buys a sentence. Consequently their speech is not compressed; it is *continued*. A calf hears the first half of something in one room and the second half in the next, and the two halves are separated by a day of ringing white in which it cannot hear its mother's feet. What we would call a word is a shape held across a gap by two bodies who have agreed, without being able to discuss it, to hold it. What we would call a story is held across a gap of years. What we would call the map is held across a gap of generations, and it is a chain of intervals — *from the room where the seam bends, two days at the shadow's third bearing* — and it compounds error, and it goes stale as the birds edit the wave, and there is a whole class of walker whose function is to be wrong on purpose: to leave the route, and wander the loud, and find the rooms nobody remembers, and come back and say where they were in the nine seconds available.

Most do not come back. There are more than two hundred rooms. There are two hundred that are known. The others lie out there being quiet at nothing, unvisited, ungreen, and if you like you may call them lost, though nothing was ever had.

Conversation is also dangerous, and this is the second reason it is short. In a world where the only places anything can be heard are two hundred scattered yards, a predator does not need speed or stealth or senses. It needs only to lie down in a room. It is the color of the lag and flat as a flake and deaf as everything else, and it feels arrivals through the floor, and it waits, sometimes for a year. Every sentence any walker has ever spoken has been spoken inside the only trap in the world. They know this. They go anyway, because the alternative is to be a species that never says anything, and there is a limit to how long a lineage can be a thing that only walks.

The great room — the one you can lie down in twice — is not avoided. It is where they go to teach. Two hundred coordinates cannot be transmitted in nine seconds, and the young must have them, and so once in a long while the routes phase-align and the walkers converge on the largest silence and stand shoulder to shoulder in the noise of each other's breathing for four minutes, and the old say the list, and the calves say the list back, and then everyone walks out into the loud in a different direction. It is the only crowd this world has ever produced. Something usually eats one of them.

---

Above each room, a column.

The chaff-layer hangs at a fathom everywhere, held in the pressure of the standing wave, but only over a node is the air soft enough for an egg. So above every cushion of green there is a shaft of possibility about the size of the cushion and a fathom high, and into it, on the shadow's second bearing, the motes come down to lay: spiders no bigger than a full stop, legged seeds, things with sails of one cell. The larvae rise. The adults do not come down; they cannot; the ground is a wall to them and always has been. When they die they fall out of the layer and the holes take them, and the shaft above each green cushion is a slow snow of the dead going into the rock, and it has been snowing into the same two hundred shafts since before there was anyone to call it weather.

The seams have their own arithmetic. A midge in the column is a creature of one dimension: everything that happens is upstream or downstream, and there is no word for *here* that is not a distance from water. News travels by shove. A disturbance at mile three arrives at mile nine an hour later as a wave of jostling, and travels on to the end of the seam, and reflects, and comes back — so the seam is a place where your own alarm returns to you a day later, unchanged, from the other direction, and the columns have a behavior for this, a settling, a passing-it-on, which is the closest thing on the plateau to a rumor with a lifespan.

And under the cloud, on the nine paces of rounded stone, there is a midge with a rounded shell. It is the only rounded animal in the world, in the only place where anything is rounded, and its eggs will not hatch unless they are struck by a falling drop. Its entire species occupies nine paces by one. It has never been anywhere else and there is no mechanism by which it could be. If the coldest seam ever shifts a hundred yards, that is the end of it, and nothing will record that it existed, and nothing here records anything anyway.

---

Down inside, the rock is wet and dark and full of strainers.

They span the pores: blind, feathered, patient things that hold a mesh across a channel and take what the loop brings. The loop brings everything. Every bone, every flake, every fallen spider, every abandoned bead with its tenant still inside, all of it goes down and in and along, and is strained, and is dissolved, and the dissolved part goes on down the throat and falls again. There are grazers of the algal film down there, and grazers of the grazers, and a slow pale thing that does nothing but dissolve the bones of birds it has never seen and could not have caught. None of them has ever been in the loud, because the loud is a property of the surface, of the aperture, of the note; inside, miles down, the wind is only wind, and the rock is only wet, and it is the least remarkable habitat on the mountain and by far the largest.

It is also the only place where anything is added to nothing. Everything else here works by removal: the wind widens, the water dissolves, the rain rounds nine paces, the birds cut sockets and bend notes and let them go. Forty miles of finished instrument, still playing, with a fauna of subtractions.

Except that one pipe sounds wet.

---

There is a node in the throat. It is the largest silence in the world and the only one underground: a chamber where the falling water and the drawn air and the geometry of the wet rock cancel, and where — this is the thing — the old sense still pays.

Deafness up there is not a loss. It is an accommodation. An ear is a useless organ under a permanent roar, and everything born on the rind has converted it to feet, to pressure, to the floor. But down in the wet null the roar is gone, and an ear would work, and something down there has one.

Nothing has been seen there. What is known is the note: low, with a breath in it, a hesitation the wind does not account for. What is *suspected*, by the few who have gone up and stood at the rim and come down and tried to say it, is that the hesitation is not random, and that it is late.

That it is a name. That it is a name some bird held on the surface a long time ago and then stopped holding, released when the widening won, when the bead was pulled and set on the lip and the note went true and the bird was nobody and then was bone and then was dissolved. Everything up there let it go. Something down here caught it, and kept it, and gives it back — once, quietly, decades afterward, into a chamber where hearing is possible, to an audience of nobody.

The world has one audience. It has had it the whole time.

And an audience is not passive. The birds feel that low breath arrive in their feet, from below, from the direction of the throat, and they orient toward it, and they defend the notes that get answered, and hold them longer, and pass them to their young. Which means the names that persist on the surface are the names the throat bothers to repeat. Which means something with ears, in the one room where ears are worth having, has been editing the only history this world possesses for a very long time, without hands, without appearing, without ever once being seen.

Curating it toward what, nobody knows. But the names being held up there now resemble each other more than they used to. Over the last few thousand circuits the wrongnesses have been converging — the disputes shorter, the bends shallower, the wedges chosen for a family likeness that no bird could have described and every bird can feel.

And lately a hole near the rim has gone out of true that no bird has touched. It has stayed out. There is no bead in its lip. It is a note that has never had a hole, and whatever it names is not on the plateau: it comes up over the edge once in a long while, and it is hot, the only hot thing for forty miles, and it stands in the silences breathing, obscenely, and goes away again.

Something down there is trying to say it. Something up there has begun to answer.
