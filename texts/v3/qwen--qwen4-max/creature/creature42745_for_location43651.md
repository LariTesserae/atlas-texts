## The Pavement

Begin with what you would step on.

Each is a low cone of shell the color of weak tea, thumbnail-wide, and beneath the shell a foot the color of a bruise, and around the foot a fringe of bristles that tastes. There is no head. There is no front. A grazer that has been turned around has not been turned around; it has only been moved, and it does not have opinions about being moved. It rasps. The rasp takes the rind of lime off the floor-stone at the rate the seep lays it down, so the floor is not a surface but a thickness, held constant by mouths, the way a lawn is a height held constant by something that eats it.

Under each shell is a shallow depression in the tufa that fits that shell and no other. It is easy to think the animal made the hole and owns it. The truth runs the other way. The fit is maintained — foot-rasp and lime-deposition cancelling — but the animal is brief. It lives about as long as it takes the water to lay down a layer equal to its own shell, and then it stops, and the shell goes back to lime, and the foot goes into the cushions, and out of the interstice beside the scar comes a juvenile that has been sitting there, settlement-ready, for exactly this. There is never a vacancy. There is not an instant of vacancy. The young one grows into the depression and the depression does not change, and by the time the fit is exact it has always been exact.

The socket outlives the sitter. Remember that; it comes back.

## What Keeps the Wall Open

The water is saturated. It arrives already full of lime and it is moving, and moving saturated water deposits. This should have killed the bowl.

Give it a century and the pores of the limestone would seal themselves with their own cargo; the seep would stop; the film would thin and fail; the glass ferns would go first, then everything that depends on the ferns, then everything. A place like this should scab over and die of its own richness. It has not, because the pores are inhabited.

The borers are in the rind and in the rock behind it: pale, segmented, no longer than a grain of rice, with a rasp at one end and nothing at the other. No eyes, no ears, no whiskers, no direction. They have chemistry and they have a mouth and that is the whole of their equipment. They eat the closing. They graze the inside of the capillaries at precisely the rate the lime tries to shut them, and so the wall stays open, and so the mountain keeps delivering, and so there is a world here at all.

They never come out. Not one of them, ever, in ten thousand years, has been on the outside of anything. There is a fauna in this bowl with no exterior phase, no dispersal, no encounter with the room it makes possible — animals that live entirely inside the plumbing of a place they will never perceive, and whose entire existence is the maintenance of a hole.

The seep is not a leak. It is a circulation, and the capillaries are alive, and the reason the walls weep is that something in them is hungry.

## The Warm Ones

Now the ones that make it smell of blood.

Palm-sized, dense-furred, blind. The fur is not for warmth: nothing here is losing heat, and a coat in a place with no gradient is a coat with nothing to do. It eats sound, which is a use, and it hosts a further population of things that graze the iron-crust at the hair roots, which is another use, and it is the surface against which other fur presses, which is the real one.

Their whiskers do the whole work of the world, and whiskers reach a hand's breadth. Understand what this means. They have no sense that reports distance. Beyond the whisker-tip there is not *space* — there is only smell arriving, iron and mushroom and the sweetness of the seep, unlocalizable, a fog with no geometry in it. The bowl, to a warm one, is not seventy paces by forty. It is a sphere of touch about the size of a soup plate, moving, and everything else is a rumor.

So they pack. Not for heat. Because to be out of contact is to be nowhere: no floor under the foot, no fur against the flank, nothing in the whiskers, the whole world switched off. Solitude here is not sadness, it is sensory annihilation, and they avoid it the way you avoid stepping off a cliff, without deliberation, continuously, forever. They lie in the hollows and along the ropes and under the shell-pavement in mounds and chains and knots, hand over hand over hand, and they groom, and the grooming is not hygiene. It is the only verb they have. To hold a thing is to know it. To be held is to exist. There is no other way for one of them to be certain of anything.

Their blood runs thick with iron drawn straight out of the seep, so that the thin skin of the hands and feet is the color of a four-day bruise, and they taste of the wall, and they are, in the plainest chemical sense, the mountain's rust made mobile — the part of the seep that got up and started walking.

And they have one patch of skin, on the crown of the head, that is not blind. Not an eye. No lens, no image, no direction: a bare palm-sized patch of photoreceptive flesh that reports one bit. *Light, or no light.* In a bowl lit only by the ghost-glow off the cushion threads, the meaning of that bit is not *day*. It is *uncovered*. Light on the crown means nothing is lying against you. It means there is a gap where someone should be.

It is an organ for detecting loneliness, and it is the only sense of sight any animal in this bowl has ever had.

## The Ledger, Which Is Not an Account but a Mechanism

The births are not scheduled by a season. There is no season. They are scheduled by pressure.

When the purse comes through the pavement, the crowd parts by exactly one body-width, and every animal along that strip loses contact on one side. The loss travels. It goes out through the packed bodies as a wave of shifting weight, hand slipping from hand, flank against flank re-seating, a slow hydraulic of touch that crosses the bowl in about a quarter of an hour and arrives, attenuated but sufficient, at the far curve, where a female in the cushions has been gravid and waiting for precisely this and releases.

The predator is the midwife. Not metaphorically. The signal to bear is the mechanical news that someone has been taken, carried through the bodies of the survivors, and the number closes because the number *is* the mechanism, not a tally kept afterward. Count tomorrow and you get today. You could not get anything else. There is no organ here for a discrepancy.

And along the strip where the crowd parted, for the length of time it takes the purse to pass, the crowns are uncovered, and the glow from the cushions reaches them, and each of those animals receives the only image it will ever receive: a green ribbon of light, moving, the width of a body.

That is what the bowl looks like from inside. Not walls, not ropes, not the notch, not the star. A brief luminous wake where somebody was. Ten thousand years of these, at intervals, none kept, none compared, none remembered — the whole visual history of the place is a series of photographs of an absence, developed and discarded in the same second.

## The Hang-Fruit

Along the ropes, packed like fruit, the fliers: heavy-bodied, wings scaled and hushed, folded flat.

Flight is expensive in thin air and there is nowhere to go, so they climb. They spend a whole life climbing — a rope's length in a season, hand over hand, wet wings dragging, grazing the fur of finer growths off the cord as they go. The climbing is the saving. At the end, gravid and heavy and finished, each lets go and glides: one descent, downward and outward, the length of the bowl if it is lucky, to the wall where its eggs must go. That is the flight. Once, at the end, downhill, paid for by everything before it.

The wings are not a vestige of some freer ancestry. Nothing here is left over. They are a savings account with a single withdrawal.

## The Purse

There is one.

Pale, long, unhurried, mouth a drawstring, moving on the flat of its belly through the shell-pavement, which parts and closes. It does not hunt. Hunting requires a search, and search requires uncertainty about where the food is, and the food is everywhere, at total density, in every direction, always. It has no ambush, no strike, no lunge, no speed. It has a gut and a direction and the gut sets the rate and the temperature sets the gut and the mountain sets the temperature.

It has never been hungry. Hunger is a gradient and there are no gradients. It has never wanted anything, never failed to get anything, never hurried, never rested from hurrying. It is not a hunter in a room full of prey. It is a swallow moving through a solid medium: peristalsis with a face.

When it dies, one of the borers comes out.

Only one. Some of what tunnels in the rind is not a species but a stage — a long pale larva coiled in the wet stone, growing at the pace of the seep, and there is a mechanism, chemical and touch-driven and utterly reliable, that keeps all of them arrested and permits exactly one to emerge, when the crown of the world goes briefly uncovered in a certain way. The predator's understudy has been in the wall the entire time. So have the next eleven.

## The Eleven

Larger. Slow. Heavy through the shoulder, with a mouth that is also a hand: split, lipped, muscular, capable of a grip.

Each keeps a flake of flint that the mountain does not contain. Each keeps it in a hollow in the wall, in the seep, where the water lays lime onto the struck edge at exactly the rate the striking wears it away, so that the tool is not consumed and not maintained but *held*, the way the home scars are held, the way the pores are held. The flake does not get smaller. Nobody sharpens it. Nobody could. There is no flint here to make another and no technique for making one; there is only a technique for using what the ice left in the rubble before there was a bowl.

The glass-snail is set in the floor-socket, which fits it. The flake comes down. One strike. The shell opens, the animal is eaten, the flake goes back to its own hollow. No scatter, no debris, no workshop, no accumulation, nothing built.

Here is the thing worth standing still for. The stroke is not known by any of them.

It is not in a mind. There is no mind here large enough to hold a plan, and no need of one. The stroke lives in the tendon and the joint and the weight of the shoulder; in the fit of the socket; in the exact thickness of the flake's restored edge; in the shape of a shell that grows to a specification. It is distributed across eleven bodies and eleven stones and eleven wall-hollows and eleven floor-cups and one species of snail, and it is transmitted by the young one's hand being taken into the old one's mouth-hand and moved through the arc, over and over, for years, until the arc is in the arm. Not instruction. Nothing is explained; nothing here can explain. It is closer to a mold than to a lesson. The apprentice does not learn the stroke. The apprentice is *worn into* it, the way a foot is worn into a scar.

Which means a holder does not die and get replaced. By the time it dies, its successor has been the second half of its grip for a decade, and the death is only the moment the transfer completes. There have been eleven for longer than there have been species with names. Eleven is not a population. Eleven is the number of hollows in the wall.

The sockets are the holders. The animals are what the sockets are currently doing.

And the click — the loudest thing in the world, once every hundred breaths — is not a signal, and no one is calling, and no one answers. It is a growth rate made audible: the interval at which a glass-snail comes to size within reach of a mouth-hand. It is the bowl's only clock and it is a snail.

But it is also the only thing here with a beginning and an end. Everything else is continuous: the film, the breath, the glow, the going-over of water on knuckles. The click is discrete. It arrives, it is over, and for a moment afterward there is a *there* and a *here* — a direction, a faint spherical asymmetry in a world that has no gradients at all. The warm ones turn toward it. Not startled; they have nothing to be startled with. They simply orient, briefly, to the one place in the universe that is momentarily louder than another place.

Then it decays and the world is even again. Nothing is built on it. Nothing has ever been built on it. It has been happening every hundred breaths for ten thousand years and it has never once led anywhere, and this is not a failure, and it is not a promise, and it is not waiting.

## The Glow

The green is not for seeing. Almost nothing here can see, and what can see can only tell covered from uncovered.

The glow is an overflow. The iron chemistry that runs the growth has a step that must shed energy or stall, and in a place where nothing may accumulate — no store, no deposit, no heap, no reserve — light is the only waste that can be thrown away absolutely. It cannot be buried. It cannot pile up. It leaves.

So the bowl exports two things and imports one. Lime goes out through the notch in the water. Light goes out through the notch in a thin green thread, into the underside of the cloud, where nobody has ever stood to see it. And the star comes in.

Nothing in the bowl has ever seen the star. Nothing has an organ that could. The one object in this world that came from outside it, the single unrepeating fact, the only thing not made of the seep and the rind and the fit — it hangs in its two-finger slot, unreceived, at an angle no one has ever measured, above a room in which the entire visual capacity of every resident is a patch of skin that says *someone is missing*.

## What Lives Here

Not the grazers, which are brief fillings of permanent holes. Not the warm ones, whose unit is not the body but the knot of bodies, so that counting them is a category error and *a great many* is the only honest number. Not the eleven, who are eleven hollows' current hands. Not the purse, which is the wall's one permitted emergence.

What lives in the bowl is the fit. A ten-thousand-year-old set of cancellations, arrived at the way the walls arrived at their curve, and everything with a body in here is a temporary thickening of it — weather passing through a shape. The scars, the sockets, the pores, the arc of the stroke: these persist. The animals are what those absences are doing at the moment. Species are the local names for the shapes the water takes on its way through.

Do not call it peace. Peace is the far side of a war and there was never a war. Do not call it a trap, either; a trap implies an elsewhere, and the only elsewhere is a star that no one has looked at.

And then you.

You are thirty-seven degrees in a room at root-cellar chill. You are the first gradient in ten thousand years, and the crowd around your ankle shifts under the unfamiliar heat, and the shift goes out through the packed bodies as a wave of re-seating weight, hand slipping from hand, and crosses the bowl in a quarter of an hour, and arrives at the far curve where something gravid has been waiting.

The bowl has no way to know you are here except to believe that someone has died.

You cannot put a hand down without putting it on someone. The thing that comes to rest against your palm is not curious — there is no organ for the new — it is doing the only thing it does, which is holding, which is knowing, which is the whole of the welcome available. Out on the frond, one cell thick, held open by nothing but water pressure, something has climbed toward your warmth and the glass is bending under it, and it will tear, and the tear will be the first breakage in a hundred centuries, and no one will notice, and the ledger is already moving to cover the cost.

Above you, eleven hollows hold eleven stones. One of them is two fingers from the notch. It would fit through. Ten is not eleven, and nothing in this room has ever had an organ for a missing thing.

The star has not moved. Nothing here has ever looked at it. You are the only thing in the bowl that has.
