## The Forty-One

The ledger has three columns and only the middle one has a heading.

The middle column is names, and it is forty-one lines long, and it has been forty-one lines long since the winter of the long knit, when it was forty-four. Nobody in Sixteen says the number forty-four. The tally-keeper recounts the column every thaw, out loud, at the lip, with the whole town standing in the thin snow listening to a number they already know, and this is not a census. It is a calculation about breath. Six or eight weeks of sealed air, forty-odd pairs of lungs, forty lamps eating what the lungs eat: the arithmetic is done with lamp flames and headaches and the taste at the back of the throat, and it comes out at forty-one, and if a child is born in the dark then somebody goes up.

The first column has no heading. It has a mark like a thumbprint, once per tide, and a sub-mark for the hard winters.

The last column has no heading either. It has one stroke a year, made in the same hand, always in the same place on the page, and the keepers call it the back column when they have to call it anything.

---

They are the ones who did not follow.

That is the whole origin. There was a family that wintered near the hole when the hole was new, and there were relatives who moved with the herds, and one autumn the relatives went and the family put a lamp in the wound and stayed. Everything else — the terraces, the flues, the bathhouse, the six hundred swept beds — was built by the relatives, in the loud weeks, by people who then walked away again. Sixteen is not a monastery. It is a house where most of the household is away, kept by the ones who could not be bothered to leave, or could not bear to.

So they are named for where they sleep. Bench, because her landing is directly above the drowned bench you can see on a still day. Second Flue. Guano. Long Stair, who is a small woman, and finds this funny, and has found it funny for nineteen years. The addresses are the names and half the addresses are underwater, which means that when a keeper says who she is she is saying where her mother's house used to be.

Given names exist. They come out in sealed time. In the six or eight weeks of absolute dark, when you cannot see a landing or a terrace or anybody's face, they call each other by the names they were given as infants, and it is the closest thing to nakedness the town has, and at thaw they stop, immediately, in the middle of sentences, and go back to being addresses.

---

Their hands are glazed. Sweetwater does not permit rot, and it does not much permit healing either: a cut stays pink and clean and open for a season, edges neat as a scored rib, no pus, no smell, no closing. A keeper of thirty winters has a lifetime's small bright cuts on her fingers like a map of everything she has ever touched badly. They shake hands by pressing the backs of the fingers together, palm to palm being unpleasant, and they can identify each other in the dark this way, and by smell, which is the real name.

Every district has its smell and every body carries its district. Tannery is sour-green. Rendering is tallow and singed hair. The bone-yard is chalk. The guano gallery is ammonia and it is a punishment posting and everybody knows who has been punished. To wash is to go briefly anonymous, and the bathhouse — snow-melt, never sweetwater, heated by the shared flue — is therefore a slightly disreputable place, and people come out of it looking sheepish and smelling of nothing, and are greeted by their own families with a moment's genuine hesitation. *She still smells of the tannery* means *she is homesick*, and it is said of a woman who has lived four hundred paces from the tannery for eleven years.

The felt keeps a thumbprint for a week. This is the town's postal system. You press a thumb into the wall beside a door and the shape of your presence stays there for seven days, and anyone passing can read the wall by hand and know who called, and how hard, and whether they waited. There are walls in Sixteen that are nothing but a rolling week of thumbs, and there is a boy whose job is to read them at dawn and carry the news, and there are two people who have been conducting an argument in a wall for four years without ever once being in the room together.

There are no dogs. Dogs come at tide with the six hundred and are tied at the lip. There was a dog in the third tide and it barked, once, and the whole chamber heard it inside their own chests, and then it swam.

The pipes talk. Smoke runs in the dead rhizomes and so does speech: put your mouth to a cold flue-mouth on the fourth terrace and you can hear a household on the second, breathing, arguing about a strap. The children who crawl the flues to scrape them are the only people who know everything, and they grow out of the job at about eleven, and are then watched very carefully for a year.

---

The trades, briefly, because they will tell you briefly:

**Tally**, who has the knife. The blade has been sharpened so often it is a third of its width and shaped like a tooth. It is a pen. Iron scores the ribs and never cuts flesh or living tissue, because a notch is a word and a cut is a wound, and the difference matters to something. Her right ear is white and shiny and has no feeling in it at all, from sixty years of pressing to cold ribs, and she presses it there anyway, in autumn, and listens to the crowd in the other valley, and then tells the town how much to salt.

**The discouragers**, two, who do not agree. They prune with antler wedges and tearing, never blades, and their work is a language of refusal: this root-run may not go here, this one may go a little, this one we would prefer to become a stair. The stair on the north face has been consenting for eleven years and is nearly a stair. There is a rib that came up through a warming-hut in the second tide because a discourager said no in a way the plant took wrongly, and the hut is still there with the rib through it, and it is used as a store, and nobody has ever repaired it.

**The tuner**, who keeps the drips in pitch. The hundred drips are the map; each district has its figure — the bone-yard in threes, the tannery a low knock and two ticks — and she carries antler shims and gut and a little mallet and goes round adjusting the walls so that the room continues to say where it is. She is blind above the fog and sighted below it, which is not a metaphor, it is a thing about her eyes. When a wall shifts and a drip changes note, the map lies until she finds it, and there is a word in Sixteen for a person standing perfectly still in the middle of a room they have lived in all their life.

**Hook**, who salvages. Hauls with his legs. Never leaves a line overnight, not for any reason, not for a boot, not for the iron if it fell. He has one story and he tells it only in sealed time and it is not the same story each time.

**The hand**, who goes down. Greased, tethered, one adult, once a year, to see that the eleven doors are shut. She comes up sweet-smelling and says nothing, and the nothing is not modesty, it is the terms of the job, and she retires at thirty and her hands are the glossiest in the town.

**Raft**, who feeds the glow. The foxfire on the drowned raft is the second light and lamp-fat is dear, so the raft is a light-farm: bone-meal, scrapings, the right amount of the wrong thing. She knows what makes it brighter. There is one thing she does not feed it, and she has never said what, and she has told her successor, who is nine.

---

The plant lives there. That is not decoration.

It is the first resident and the landlord and the walls and the water and the calendar, and the keepers do not worship it, they *negotiate* with it, in the manner of people living in a house owned by a difficult aunt who is also, in some sense, the house. It has no name. Tally's first column exists because you cannot write a ledger with no first column and you cannot write that word down either.

What it does, from the keepers' side of the arrangement: it holds the cavity open. It plumbs the sweetwater up. It knits the lid shut in hard winters and opens it in thaw, and it does this on its own schedule, and the afternoon in which you decide to be inside or outside is the only say you get. It grows one rib a tide. It listens, or something listens through it. It grows the twenty-fourth rib pale and tender a season before the noise, and it lets a woman put her ruined ear against it and hear a crowd coming across forty miles of nothing.

And it eats. Nobody says this above a murmur either, but every keeper knows what the sweetwater is, and knows what went into it in the last tide, and drinks it, and salts with it, and renders in it, and bathes in the melted snow instead, and has done all of this since childhood, and does not find it remarkable, and would find it remarkable if you asked.

---

The drowned are residents too, and they outnumber everybody.

Two hundred and nine, by the last lamp-count, which was six winters ago. The count before that was two hundred and eleven. Both numbers are in the ledger. Neither is corrected.

The town is twenty-seven years old, which means the drowned first generation is not ancient, it is *recent*: there are three keepers whose mothers are down there in the ranks, head-upstream, a hand's width from a stranger, perfectly kept, eyelashes intact, in a doorway with the lamp still lashed to its post. The lower quarter is also the town's warehouse. Rope, boots, gut-line, a bench, antler, an axe-head that is not iron. You may take a thing from the drowned quarter but you must lower a thing of the same kind, and the rule is not about fairness. Nobody will say what it is about. Hook does the exchanging. He does it fast.

You do not say a name over the water. Not the name of a dead person, not your own given name, not the name of a district. This is the first thing a child learns and the last thing an old person forgets.

In the fifth tide one door stood open. Not forced. Open. The bench inside had been moved a hand's breadth to the left. The town weighted the whole quarter under felt and shingle and has not salvaged there since, and the story of who saw it is told differently by every one of the four people who could have seen it, and Tally has written down all four versions, dated, because that is what she is for.

---

The sorter is a set of verbs with no noun attached to it.

It sorts. Small to large, head-upstream, none upside down, none tangled, a hand's width between. It corrects: a body lowered badly is found later in the ranks, properly. It returns: a mitten, dry; a gaff, handle-first; once, a boot, laced. It takes: a line goes taut sideways, and everyone stops working, forty-one people standing absolutely still on four terraces in the dark with their hands out, and the felt eats the sound of their breathing, and then the line comes slack, and whatever was on it is not.

The keepers do not speculate. This is the single most disciplined thing about them. They will tell you the plant is a person if you press them, they will tell you the ribs hear the lemmings coming, they will argue for an hour about whether the fog layer is thicker than it was — and about the sorter they will say *we haul fast*, and change the subject with a competence that takes years to acquire.

No child swims. There is a boy of eleven who can, in the shallows, secretly, and he is the most dangerous person in the settlement, and two other children know, and the fact that they have not told is the first adult thing they have ever done.

---

Then there are the selves that only exist in sealed time, which is to say there are two populations wearing the same forty-one bodies.

The town that is sealed is not the town that is open. Voices drop by a third. Given names come out. The story cycle has to be finished — it is a single story, seven nights long, and it has been added to every seal for seven tides, and there are passages in it that are obviously about the lemmings and passages that are obviously about something else and nobody living knows what. Lies told in the dark are binding; this is not a moral rule, it is a practical one, because you cannot see a face and therefore cannot check, and so a thing said in the dark is simply true until thaw, and people have been married by it, and unmade.

Air goes sweet and thick. Lamps shorten. Heads ache in a particular place. The roof makes its small continuous tearing sound, like a scab lifted very slowly, for weeks, above your head, while you sleep.

At thaw they come up blinking and cannot judge distances, and for the first week nobody in Sixteen is permitted to decide anything. There is a keeper appointed to say no — to every request, every trade, every proposal, every *I'm going up to the plain for an hour* — and the appointment rotates and is hated and obeyed. Some never fully recover the distances. The sealed children, born in the dark, are the best drip-readers in the town and cannot throw a stone straight, ever, and there are four of them, and they are the ones the tuner is training.

The oldest woman is called Ground. She is eighty-one and she is the person who saw it happen: she was a girl and the plain dropped a hole, and she is the reason the town knows how young its wound is. She lives at the top and does not do the ladders now. Her account has changed slightly at every telling for sixty years and Tally has written down every version, all of them, dated, in the middle column, under a name.

---

Sixteen has six hundred people. It has them for a day and a half, every three or four years.

The tide-kin are the relatives who kept moving. They come down the ladders with their kits, four days' walk from everywhere, because everyone within four days' walk knows what the noise means, and they fill the beds and the three rendering floors and the bathhouse and the whole second-level street of warming-huts, and they are loud, and they smell of outside, and they bring news.

News arrives at tide. That is the news cycle. A joke told in one tide is still funny in the next and is retold, carefully, with the setup intact, to people who heard it three years ago and have been keeping it. A death out on the plain is grieved twice: once when it is told, and once, worse, at the next tide, when someone who did not hear it the first time hears it now.

The keepers are strange in the crowd. They stand at the edges of their own house. They cannot follow two conversations. They go up to the lip at the height of it and stand in the wind that arrives with no news, and then come down and go back to work, because thirty hours is thirty hours.

And afterwards the beds are swept. All of them. There is a keeper whose entire trade is the empty beds, and she has a list of families, and against most of the names is a tide-number, and against one of them is a tide-number three tides old, and the town has twice proposed giving that bed away and twice been told no, by her, in a voice that ended it.

---

The animals are residents in a way the six hundred are not.

Felt-voles, pale, generations deep, living in the wall-mat since the founding, tame enough to take fat from a thumb, eaten in hard winters and counted, always counted. Stoats that stay fat and insolent for a season after a tide and then go. A fox family in the lid, above the town, whose diggings the keepers plug and re-plug, and whose kits they can hear through the ceiling in the quiet, which is the only sound in Sixteen that anybody is glad of. An owl at the slot for a month, posted, indifferent, temporary. A raven at the lip that has learned a cough — a specific cough, a woman's, a keeper who died in the sixth tide — and does it, at intervals, from above, and everyone in the chamber stops, every time, and no one has ever suggested killing it.

The children live under the fog. The fog layer sits at chest height, flat-topped, and below it the air is clear, so a child of six walks through a different room than her mother does: lit from beneath by forty orange beads, floor and shins and the black shine of the water, ceiling of white. They have games in there that adults cannot see the board of. They put their hands through the fog from below and are told not to.

And the swimmer. Grey, nose up, unhurried, coming across the black sweetwater toward the only unfrozen thing in the world. It arrives in the cold months, drinks, hangs about the shallows for a while, goes. It has never taken a keeper. The lowest door is left unshored, and has been since the second tide, and the shoring of it is the one job that is never assigned. In the winter of the long knit it did not come, and the back column has a gap in it, and that is the winter the ledger went from forty-four to forty-one, and nobody has ever put those two facts in the same sentence.

Tally makes the stroke. One a year, same hand, same place on the page. She does not head the column. She has been asked.

---

Go to the edge at dusk with your face down and you will hear a room, and the room is fuller than the count.

Forty-one, and the plant that is the room. Two hundred and nine, sorted, in ranks, with the doors shut and one door not. Six hundred who are not here and whose beds are swept. Four children under the fog. A raven with a dead woman's cough. Something with no noun that returns mittens dry. A crowd in another valley, audible in a rib you could dent with a fingernail, coming.

Ask a keeper who lives here and she will tell you forty-one, and mean it, and be telling the truth. Then she will look at the water for a while. Then she will say, *and the roof* — and go back to work, in a house where you can watch a man hammering sixty feet away and hear it happening inside a pillow, and where forty-one people all not saying something at once is a sound you can hear.
