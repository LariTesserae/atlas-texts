# The Culm: the inhabitants

## The four hundred and thirty

They are a right-sided people, and most of them are not right-handed.

The deafness comes on the shaft side, so it comes on the left, so you approach a man from his right, and after two hundred years this has stopped being a courtesy and become a geometry. Doorways have a good side. The school benches are set at a slight angle to the master, all of them, canted like pews in a chapel built by someone with one ear. Lovers walk on the right. To speak into a Culm man's left is to say something to nobody, and there is a word for people who do it, and the word is not kind.

The hips go next. The sideways gait is learned at three and never unlearned, and it lays the pelvis down slightly askew, so that at forty everyone here has one shoulder a finger lower than the other and a walk that from a distance looks like a limp and up close is not one. Outsiders bruise themselves on the Floor within an hour. Locals pass through a gap of eleven inches at a dead run without touching either side, and the outer seams of every coat in the town are polished shiny, and no one has ever remarked on this.

Their hands are varnished. Not metaphorically: turpentine and resin get into the creases and set there, so the palms have a faint amber glaze in the lines that soap will not shift, and across the base of the fingers there is a pale ridge of callus from the hand-lines, the same ridge in the same place on every person over nine, the way a signature is the same. Grafters have a thumb like a boot heel. Tin-children have thin wrists and enormous forearms.

And they smell of the place, which they cannot smell, and which means that the first thing a stranger notices about a Culm man three days inland is that he smells like a painter's shop; and the first thing a Culm man notices about a stranger is that the stranger smells of nothing at all, of soap and dry cloth, and there is a sign for that, one hand brushing the other sleeve, and it is also the sign for foreigner, and it is also the sign for a lie you have not checked.

*Dry.* That is what they call you.

## What they hear with

Not the ears. The ears are for the first thirty years.

After that the bones take over, and the town has an entire second hearing that nobody from outside ever sees because it looks like leaning. A fitter with a dead left ear and a failing right lays the flat of his skull against a main and hears the water hammer arrive four rooms before it gets to him. Press men rest a jaw on the frame. Children lie on the horn-dark floor with their cheek on the wood and their eyes shut and can tell you which of the forty-two presses is running light.

Sedder, who has emptied and tuned the tins on the eastern third of the Floor for fifty-one years and is now stone deaf in both ears, carries a rod of ironwood a foot long, greased, in his sleeve. He puts one end against the tin and the other between his teeth. He walks his round with his head tilted and his mouth full of wood, and he hears the drip chorus through his jaw, and he hears it *better* than the apprentices do, because bone does not care about eighty-four decibels. He has found nineteen failing glands in the last decade that nobody else could locate. He is not considered remarkable. He is considered due for retirement, which he refuses.

They all have the same private note. A high thin whine, somewhere above the leaf-surf, left over from the presses; it is what is left when the clutch is drawn and the town drops to fifty-eight, and every person here hears it and has heard it since adolescence, and it is the closest thing they have to a shared secret, and they do not discuss it, because to discuss it would be to admit that it never stops.

Silence is the alarm. An unscheduled stop brings all four hundred and thirty upright in their hammocks at once, hearts going, before anyone has thought about it. The nine-minute gap does not wake anybody. The gap is on the timetable.

## Three languages

The signs are public and the voices are private. This is the opposite of everywhere else, and it rearranges everything.

On the Floor a normal speaking voice carries about half a metre. So you can stand at the press in full view of ninety people and say the worst thing you have ever said, and only the person beside you gets it. Confession happens at work. Courtship happens at work, mouth to the good ear, four inches away, in plain sight, invisible. Two men can be tearing each other apart at the shaft side and the hall sees only two faces close together and hands still on the wheel, and the hall reads the faces, because faces are the public channel here, and by evening everyone knows what was said in substance and nobody knows a word of it.

To sign is to publish. A sign carries the length of a hall. So gossip travels by hand, at the speed of sight, and a scandal in the Culm is not something people heard, it is something everybody watched. There are signs you do not make in front of your mother. There are children who can hold their hands still under a table for an hour, which is the local equivalent of a poker face, and it is taught, and it is a skill worth money.

The four hundred words include eleven for kinds of nearly-dripping and one for the specific rising pitch of a gland about to go, which cannot be said aloud in any language, and none at all for anything that happens thirty kilometres away. Dialects run by shift. Night signs are small and held close to the chest because the lamps are poor; day signs are wide, thrown out from the shoulder. You can tell a man's shift from across the basin by the size of his words.

A person's name is their trade-sign with a personal flick added. Which means that to speak of your grandmother you must do her work with your hands: the tilt and tap of emptying a tin, the draw of the grafting knife. The dead are named by their labour, and the naming is a small motion, and everyone does it a hundred times a day without noticing, so that the town is continuously, absently performing the trades of people who have been ash for a century.

Marriages across shifts invent a pidgin in the first year. The children grow up speaking it. It dies with the household. There are perhaps sixty such languages in the Culm at any time and no record of any of them.

And in the nine minutes, when the shafting coasts down, everybody shouts, because nobody can recalibrate, and the public business of the town is crammed into that gap — the ledger readings, the notices, the marriages, the arguments that must have witnesses, the school's last hour, the singing. They sing unaccompanied and out of tune, all of them, at full volume, having spent their lives in a noise that eats pitch. They believe they sound magnificent. Nobody has ever told them otherwise, and nobody ever will, because the only people who could tell them are dry, and what would they know.

## Quick

The worst thing you can call a person here is *quick*.

It is not an insult about intelligence. It is the fire word. A town of resin and cellulose has one enemy and it is the fast thing, the spark, the sudden heat, and so the whole moral vocabulary has grown out of the fire drill and been applied to tempers. A quick man is a man who might kill everybody. Children are taught to fear their own flare-ups the way children elsewhere are taught to fear the road.

There is nowhere for anger to go except down. The Under is warm as blood and dark and loud and nobody can find you there, and men go down to the digesters to be furious, and stand in the black stink with their fists shut, and come up an hour later fit to be spoken to. This is understood. Nobody asks. The six domes hold fifty-five degrees all year and take the town's waste and its press-cake and its sedge and, in a manner of speaking, its rages, and give back gas that lights the lamps, and the arrangement is not a metaphor to anyone who works there. It is a trade. Rot in the Culm is employed. It has shifts and a supervisor. Outside, decay is something that happens to you; here it is done by colleagues, on purpose, at a controlled temperature, and it is the second-oldest job in the place.

The one aesthetic crime is a beautiful lie. A boy on the tin round, twelve years old, tuned a tin a semitone flat because he liked the sound of it in the chord, and the chord was wrong for eleven days and two fitters went looking for a leak that did not exist, and the boy was beaten and then made to walk the whole round again with his hand on every tin until he could say the true note of each. He is forty now and tunes the eastern third under Sedder and has never once improved anything.

## Ages, and the book

Nobody here reckons in years. They reckon in coppices, which come round every eleven, and in flowers, which come when the glass-house men can force one, about once a decade, and in bund-raisings, which are every ten and are the only festival.

So a woman of forty-four is four coppices, and she was a brush-child at the flower before last, and her mother was a brush-child at the one before that, and this is a real distinction with real consequences, because the pollination is done by children with brushes under glass and the seedlings go into the planting book with names, and the names are the children's names.

Till pollinated eleven florets at the last flower. She was seven. There is a whip in the north nursery with her name on a lead tag, four centimetres through, and an entry in the book that says what it will be: a floor beam on the seaward side, in the eighth coppice from now, when Till will be ninety-one or dead. She goes out to look at it sometimes. It is a stick. She is not sentimental about it. She was told at seven, in the same hour as the grafting and the valve-work and the signs, that this was her beam and that she should be careful with it, and she has been careful with it, and she is fourteen now and has begun to be curious about who will sleep under it.

The clerk, Vess, keeps the book in lampblack bound with the town's own resin varnish, because ink runs here and paper goes limp in a week and everything written down has to be written in something that will not rot. His hand is very small and very even. Half his entries are for people who do not exist: rooms, hooks, hammock limbs, beams, all specified, all planted, all growing, for a population eighty years out. He has written the room of a woman whose grandmother is not yet born. He does not find this affecting. He finds it a nuisance when the parents name the child something he did not allow for.

## Hands that do particular work

**Bel**, seventy-one, is the best grafter in the town and has been for forty years, and every living hook in the leaf rooms is hers — she planted the limbs and set the shackles in the right season so the wood would close around the iron and take the load, which means that four hundred and thirty people sleep tonight on her decisions, held by callus she made when she was thirty. Her hands shake at rest and are perfectly steady on the knife. She has no left ear at all and reads the mouth beautifully.

**Ondry** runs Retort Row, in its brick cell behind the clay fire-walls and the double doors, and because that is the only lawful fire in the Culm, Ondry is also the undertaker. The same crew, the same doors, the same fire-watch, the same drill. They take the dead in at slack water and they burn them properly and they are extremely careful, and the whole town knows exactly how careful, because the drill is timed monthly and posted. People are easy with Ondry in a way they are not easy with anyone else, and they are also, without ever saying it, slightly afraid of him, and both of these things are about the doors.

The ash is kept. There is a room off the Under with a shelf of jars, labelled, one for each death since the last flood season, four or five or six in a bad year, and at the feeding the jars go down and the ash goes into the slurry with the potash and the bone-ash and the digester liquor and is pumped into the root collars, and the children carry the jars, and every child knows which collar got their grandmother.

Something over a thousand people are in the walls. The main girder on the Combing Floor was a whip when the works was founded and it has been fed on the founders and their children and their children, and it is a metre through now and it thickens on its underside because that is where the load is, and if you ask a press man about this he will tell you, without embarrassment and without any particular emphasis, that this is where the load goes.

They visit the walls. Not solemnly. A woman will put her hand flat on a beam for a second going past, the way you would touch a doorframe in a house you grew up in.

And the walls give things back. The stock lays callus over anything left on it long enough, and the coppice audit, cutting on the eleven-year rotation, finds a spoon, a whistle, a child's shoe, a spanner with a maker's mark nobody has used in ninety years, a wedding ring returned to a widow sixty years late. There is a shelf for these. It is called the Found Shelf and it is the closest thing the Culm has to a museum. The peat under the Under gives up older things still, because the peat keeps what you put in it, and the ditches get re-cut, and once a generation something comes out of the black that has a face, and they burn it properly and put it in a jar and feed it to the collar with the rest.

## The Leaven

Ask who lives here and the honest answer has three parts, and only one of them is people.

There is the stock: one genotype, one hundred and eleven grafted trunks, salt-tolerant, mangrove-blooded, sterile, two hundred and thirteen years old and incapable of making a seed without a child and a brush. It is not a wild thing. It is a dependant. It cannot reproduce, it cannot fruit, it cannot feed anything, it has no insects and no birds and no webs in its corners; it can only seal, and thicken, and be fed. It has been kept alive by husbandry for so long that the husbandry is now part of its body. Four hundred and thirty people are its reproductive organs, its immune system, its gut flora and its hands.

There is the Leaven, in the six domes: the thermophile consortia that came out of marsh mud and a cow's stomach in the first year and have been fed ever since, kept back by the ladle at every clean-out, mother to daughter of sludge, unbroken. It is the oldest continuous living thing in the Culm, older than any beam, older than the book, and it is the only resident that has actually *changed*. Everything above it is held still by audit. Down there, in the dark at fifty-five degrees, things are breeding and dying and shifting, hour by hour, and have been for two centuries, and the feeders can taste the difference between a good year and a bad one and cannot tell you in words what it is. Two mothers, then: a green one that cannot breed and a brown one that does nothing else.

And the people, who sweep. There is no spider here, no beetle, nothing to eat the hair and the skin-flake and the fluff, so the dust of the Culm is almost entirely human, and it is swept up daily and goes down the chute into the domes. They know this. It is one of the first things a child is told, along with the fire rule and the name of her beam. *You are already going in.*

What they eat comes up the channel. Thirty kilometres of sedge and standing water and nothing else at all: no grain, no flour, no oil, no bulbs, no iron. The barges bring it, and the lock-keeper is therefore the town's mouth, and the only person here who talks to strangers as a matter of routine, and he has a strange loud slow way of speaking that the others mimic behind their hands. The barge-pole men do not look up as they come in. Neither does anybody in the Culm. Both parties have been doing this for two hundred years and it is not rudeness. It is what you do about a thing that is always there.

## The ones who leave

A few go. Two or three in a generation, inland, for work or love or because they could not stand it.

They cannot sleep. That is what finishes most of them. Seventy-one decibels and a slow pulse through the wood is the condition of sleep for every person born here; a quiet room is a hole; and they lie in it at two in the morning with their hearts going and the whine in their heads and nothing underneath them, and they get up and walk. Marra, who left at nineteen for a man three days inland, lives now beside a millrace and pays the miller a small sum every year to keep the wheel turning at night. She is fifty-eight. She has never told her husband what the money is for. She goes back for the bund-raising when she can.

The ones who come back are re-grafted into their old rooms without any fuss and are watched for a year. The ones who do not come back have their hooks cut out and their limbs redirected, and the entry in the book is amended in Vess's small even hand, and the room grows into something else.

Nobody is exiled. Nobody is prevented. It is simply that the place selects, quietly, for the people who can sleep in it — who have the hips and the palms and the bones and the deafness and the tolerance for ash in the walls, who do not mind the corners being empty, who find the shout-gap normal and a dry sleeve unpleasant. It has been selecting for two hundred and thirteen years. There is no policy. It is only who stays.

## At the margin, and on the seaward side

The mosquitoes come to the gate at dusk and no further; the black anoxic water in the sumps kills a larva in minutes. Children are taken out to the margin once, at about seven, and made to stand there until they are bitten, so that they know what is out there and have felt it on their own blood. Eels in the outer ditch, and the men who take them are the only people here with a trade that faces outward. A heron stands on the bund at slack water, and does not come in, and is watched by half the Over every time, and there is a sign for it that is used for nothing else.

That is the whole bestiary. No webs in any corner. Visitors cannot stop looking at the corners. It is the emptiness they mind, not the noise: a warm wet crowded place with nothing living in the eaves.

And on the seaward side, the leaves have been browning at the tips since anyone's grandmother was young, four millimetres of subsidence a year, the wedge coming up the channels a little further each decade, and the bund goes up a hand's breadth every ten years with a festival, and nobody under thirty thinks of it as an emergency.

The book is changing the stock out there. Has been for a hundred and fifty years, entry by entry, cutting the intolerant and planting the tolerant, no announcement, no crisis, one clerk's small even hand at a time. So there are two lineages in the Culm being bred for salt, and only one of them is green, and only one of them knows.

Ask Vess who lives here and he will not give you the number. He will turn the book around so you can read it: a name, a room, a collar, a year eighty years off. The room is four centimetres through. The woman who will sleep in it has not been born, and neither has her mother, and neither has the child who will pollinate the flower that makes her beam.

The hook she will sling her hammock on is a limb the thickness of a wrist, out on the north-west side, in the wind, in the surf-sound of a hundred thousand leaves. It has not been offered the shackle yet. When it is, it will lay callus over the iron within a day and seal it within a week, and thicken where it is loaded, and hold.
