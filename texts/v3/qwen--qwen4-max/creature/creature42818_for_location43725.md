Count them and the number will not hold still.

This is not a failure of arithmetic. It is the correct answer, arrived at honestly. The Blister has ten thousand warm bodies in it at any moment and something like three times that many claims on those bodies, and the unit that matters is not the body. A berth is a column of hours. A person, in the sense that the exchange recognizes a person, is a *holding* — an unbroken line of occupancy running through a hammock in Underlip or a warm alcove on the Rind, maintained across however many pairs of legs happen to be carrying it this shift. The tenants have a sign for this that translates poorly. It is made by holding one hand flat and passing the other over it, again and again, without ever touching. *An hour that keeps happening.*

So begin there, and let the bodies come second.

---

Three holders to a hammock, often four in the cold districts, and they may go twenty years without seeing one another's faces. They know one another better than spouses do. The medium is chalk on the frame: a narrow band of slate-grey rock the width of a wrist, running the length of the hammock's iron, wiped down to a hand's-breadth and no more, and on it the whole literature of a life. *Hog off her yield, third day. Left her the warm corner. Fault in the bundle above you, don't lean back. Your daughter came and stood here an hour and did not lie down.* A holder learns the previous holder's hand before learning their trade — the slant, the pressure, the way they make the little cross that means *and I am sorry*. There are forgeries. There are connoisseurs. There is a woman in the Crown who can look at four inches of chalk and tell you which of six districts the writer's mother came from, and she is paid in wax to do it, and she is never wrong twice about the same hand.

The consequence, which visitors would find unbearable if there were any, is that a holding outlives its holders. When a body dies in the wool-tiers, the berth does not stop. Someone else takes the hour. Someone else reads the frame. And because it would be a cruelty to break the line, the surviving holders write back — they answer the dead one's questions, they keep up his side, they say *yes, the hog is better, thank you for asking* in a hand they have practiced until it is not quite theirs. There are correspondences in the Blister four generations deep in which no living participant has ever met any other, and one of the correspondents has been dead for sixty years, and is still, in every sense the city can measure, being written to.

Grief, in such a place, has to go somewhere else. It goes onto the doorframe.

---

The bodies themselves are unremarkable at first glance and strange on the second. Small — a tall tenant stands at your ribs — and built around the problem of thin air: deep chests, slow hearts, a great deal of surface for the volume, so that the little heat they make has somewhere to go and can be shared. They are dry. Their blood is a light oil, dark amber at the wrist, and it does not blush or bruise so much as *pool*, a stain under the skin like a lamp seen through a hand. A cut is not an emergency of loss; it is an emergency of waste. You wipe it and you burn the rag. To bleed out is to *gutter*, and the word is used for bankruptcies too.

They cannot sweat, which means the hot seams kill them, and the Throat crews work quarter-hours and are paid like guild-masters and are usually dead by thirty-five. They cannot weep, which means the face has very little to do, and has largely stopped trying. What expression remains has migrated: to the split inner lid, which flicks across the eye twice for assent and holds half-closed for grief; and to the hands, which never stop.

Because touch is the only bandwidth left. Sound dies a body-length from the mouth. Light is brown and deliberately dim. So the palms do the work that voices do elsewhere, and a tenant's hands are legible the way a face is legible — the callus-map of a trade, the hooked-wire scar of a fault-chaser, the flattened pads of a carder who has spent thirty years not startling anything. You greet by offering the back of the wrist. You court by reading a palm slowly, with the thumb, in the dark, which is not a metaphor for anything; it is simply what there is.

And everyone smells of hot lamp, always, and cannot tell you why, and would be distressed to learn it.

---

A household is a warm box with a blind animal in it.

The hog is hairless, sightless, the size of a large dog, and it lives in an alcove the dimensions of a wardrobe, and it sweats quick from the glands along its flank, and this is the entire solvent cycle of a city of ten thousand. It is also a radiator. In the cold districts the family sleeps against it, children on the flank where the glands run, and the smell that outsiders would call unbearable is the smell of being warm and solvent and alive, and no tenant can smell it at all anymore.

Wealth is yield. Marriage is the merging of two stalls, negotiated over months by two guild-clerks with wax tablets, and the ceremony consists of moving one animal through a doorway while both families stand very still, because a frightened hog voids its glands and the marriage begins in debt. Love is not required and frequently arrives anyway, in the way it does, sideways, during the long business of keeping an animal comfortable.

But the household is not the hog and not the alcove and not the bodies. The household is the doorframe.

Everything in the Blister is bolted and skidded and dragged to a new berth at shift-end. Everything except the frame, which goes with the family, on its own little sledge, and which is the only object any tenant owns that is never replaced. Because every death is thumbed onto it in wax. A well-loved frame grows a soft white lip, then a bulge, then a slab; and after two hundred years the doorway is a lens of pale wax with a hole through the middle, and it takes four men and a capstan to move it, and it is the family's name, its archive, its title deed, and — this is the part they do not say aloud to strangers — its fuel reserve.

Once a generation the lip is trimmed. The trimmings go into the household lamp. So the light by which a tenant oils her hands and reads the chalk and checks the hog's flank is her great-grandmother, burning slowly, at the correct temperature, in a red-shrouded cage, because bright light gums the works.

That is why everyone smells of hot lamp. That is what the smell is.

There is a trade in lip-wax. There has to be. It is the oldest, purest, slowest-burning stock in the city and the desperate sell it, and the arc-houses buy it through three intermediaries, and half the light in the Exchange is other people's dead. The pawnbrokers who handle it are not despised. They are something worse: they are necessary, and everyone has been to one, and nobody's sleeve itches when they pass.

---

Children are a guild, and childhood ends at a measurement.

The crawl-spaces behind the presses, the condenser fins, the busbar bundles — these are the city's maintenance access, and they admit a shoulder of eleven inches and no more. So the crawlers are children, and they have their own sign-dialect, faster and dirtier than the guilds', and their own currency of wax cakes stamped with a thumbnail, and their own hierarchy, which is inverse to size. A great crawler is a small child. The best are eight and look six.

Some households keep them hungry a shift longer than they should. The guild looks away. The child does not.

Growing out is the real puberty: the day you jam at the third bend and have to be pulled back by the ankles, and your cake-money is worthless, and you are handed a hooked wire and a trade. There is a formal last crawl, done in front of witnesses, and afterward you keep one cake you are not allowed to spend, and most people still have it at sixty, gone grey and soft-cornered in a silk pouch against the skin.

Before any of that, they learn the ghosts. A body on the spiral walks with its reflection on the curved wall and a second, fainter, deeper in, and a child who cannot tell her own third ghost from a stranger's second is a child who will get lost in a crowd and counted wrong. They learn it as a game with rules and rhymes. They also learn — nobody teaches this — not to look at their third ghost during a gulp. Ask an adult why and they will sign *because* and stop.

---

Two professions have to be described together, because they are the same accident happening twice.

The mode-wardens hold copper bits between their teeth and stand with their palms on the shell in eleven places, reading the taps that come up the busbars, feeling for the shell's moods — the long sigh, the shiver, the note that precedes a spall. It is skilled, silent, essential work, and after twenty years the jaw goes numb to it. Bone-deaf. They can no longer feel the message in their teeth, no longer feel the turbine at the sternum, no longer feel the deaf chorus in the wool, which is to say they can no longer tell good air from bad.

They should be useless. Instead they became the ghost-watchers, because a curved wall doubles an angular movement, and a man who has lost his jaw can see a third ghost shiver a half-beat before anyone else's teeth begin to tick. The bone-deaf are the city's seismographs. They stand at the alcove mouths with their hands folded, watching the reflections of strangers, and when one of them raises his arm the lamp-shutters ripple and a district starts unbolting.

Meanwhile the wool-farms are making music, and have been for centuries, and no one has ever heard it.

The stridulators sing in the batt at frequencies this air will not carry, and the itch of them along a forearm is the sign of good air — but strains differ, and a breeder can stack four trays into something that resolves, that has tension and release, that moves along the skin of the wrist like a hand reading a palm. There are composers. There are schools and rivalries and a famous schism over whether the seventh strain is a chord or a cheat. A concert is a hundred people lying in an alcove in the dark with their sleeves against their faces, and it lasts as long as the wool holds, and it is the highest art the Blister has, and an outsider standing in the middle of it would perceive nothing whatsoever except a room full of small oiled people not moving.

Your sleeve is also your passport. District, trade, shift, health, all of it in the itch. You know your wife at three paces by the grain of her wool. To borrow another's batt is the specific form adultery takes here, and to have your wool taken from you is the specific form of exile: they strike your sign from the register and take your sleeve, and afterward people can only address you by tapping your wrist, which is how you speak to a hog.

---

The dead-house presses them. Oil to the lamps, solids to the mat, and the mat is scraped and re-seeded on the nine-shift rotation, and the mites graze it, and the mites are pressed into the pale paste that everyone eats and nobody praises. Nine shifts. You have eaten your grandmother. Every tenant knows this and no tenant finds it remarkable, and the re-seeders are the closest thing the city has to clergy, and their liturgy is a schedule.

But there is a heresy, and it goes in two directions, and both of them are about the same wound.

Down: in the low pockets of the Throat, where the heavy fraction pools and the air is thick as broth, sound *works*. A cry arrives as a cry. Tenants pay a great deal of wax to be lowered on a silk line into a place where the quick will gum their lamp and their lungs and eventually their mind, for the privilege of hearing something. Not music. Almost nobody buys music. What they buy, overwhelmingly, is a name — their own, spoken aloud, once, by a stranger with a good voice, and then the line comes up. Some of them weep, which they cannot do, and stand there dry-faced with their inner lids flicking, having spent a month's yield on eleven seconds of a sound they will not be able to remember correctly by tomorrow.

Out: through the felt plug, in carder silk and felt over-boots, onto the plateau, where nothing lives and nothing rots. The exposed dead are up there in the ropey bands, laid out by mourners who could not bear the presses. They do not decay. They are the only unchanging objects any tenant has ever seen, and people go out to look at them for that reason — to stand in the cutting wind under a bruise-coloured sky with the stars showing beside the sun, and look at one thing that has stayed.

It does not stay. The grit is angular and the wind is thin and takes the skin off anything, and it takes faces too, slowly, over years: first the nose, then the lips, then the eyes, until a mother is a smooth blank shape in the black glass, and then a low ridge, and then grit. The pilgrims know this. They go anyway. Some of them sit down beside the ridge and do not come back in, and are found later, and are also smooth.

There is no shouting on the plateau either. The air up there is thinner than the air in the city. The wind takes the voice as it takes everything.

---

So: silence and impermanence, in the middle, between the two consolations that kill you. That is the address. That is what the tenants have instead of a religion.

What lives in the Blister is not, finally, the ten thousand. The ten thousand are the wax — falling indifferently, settling on the wool and the rails and the shoulders, scraped off, pressed into cakes, spent. What lives there is the shape they fall through. A holding that keeps happening. A frame that gets fatter with the dead and is dragged to a new berth every night. A correspondence between three people who will never meet, one of whom has been dead for sixty years and is still asking after the hog. A map wiped clean every morning by a clerk who has never once been asked to make it beautiful, and who has, for forty years, in the bottom corner of every slate, put the same small mark — a flat hand, and another passing over it, not touching.

At shift-change the red apertures blink up the spiral in a wave, ten thousand of them, and the felt shuffles on the glass, and the jaw-hum climbs, and a hand comes out of the dark and writes on a frame in a script four generations deep:

*She's better. Thank you for asking. Don't trim the lip, the new wax is mine.*

And the next holder reads it, and answers, and neither will ever know the other's face.
