## The tenant

Start with one animal, because there is no other way to start, and because the city has no other way to be built.

It is a worm the length of a eyelash, in a pipe of lime it secreted around itself and will never leave. Its world is three things: the seam it settled in, the bead of stone a neighbor laid against its wall, and a column of water it combs but cannot enter. Out of the tube's mouth it puts a crown of feathers, and the feathers beat in a slow spiral, and everything the worm will ever know arrives on that spiral as a taste — a flake, a mote, a diatom's ghost six weeks dead. It has no head worth the name. It has one idea with two states. *Not yet. Not yet. Not yet. Now.*

*Now* is a shadow. On the dorsal side of each feather is a single light-sensitive spot, and its whole office is to fold the crown before the mouth arrives. The worm has never seen anything. It has been flinching from light for longer than there have been eyes to see it with. The flinch is older than the basin; it was inherited from ancestors who lived somewhere with a sky, and it still works here, in a dark where the only lights are hooks, and it is the reason this animal is alive tonight.

It spawns once. It dies in the room it was born in. The room becomes the floor of somebody else's room.

And the walls are not empty either. The grey sponge felt that lines the galleries — the anechoic layer, the thing that makes a stone city refuse to echo — is itself a crowd: nematodes by the hundred thousand in every thimbleful, mites, a scale-worm flat as a fingerprint working the felt's underside, copepods tucked in the tube-throats where the doubling-back keeps the water slow. The soundproofing is inhabited. The silence is not a condition here; it is a habitat, and it is full, and the very small live inside the quiet the way the very small live inside everything.

The pavers move between districts, which makes them the couriers, though they carry nothing. A snail the size of a thumbnail spends its entire life following dampness uphill. It does not know it is going up. It scrapes film off stone with a tongue and voids lime into the joints with a gland, and its grandchildren do the same a centimeter higher, and after ten thousand years of this the family has laid the mortar of a spire without any member of it ever having been more than a body-length from a wet seam.

## The office

At the district margin, on a terrace lip where the current combs hardest, there is a shrimp with one claw grown monstrous and useless for eating.

It is not brave. It has no fear to be brave about. It has a threshold. When the water carries a certain kind of pressure-line across its antennae — a body large enough, moving with intent, crossing into the streets — the claw closes, and the closure is a cavitation snap that goes out across four miles of warm water at fifteen hundred meters a second, and the shrimp is torn apart by the recoil and eaten by whatever it announced.

Most of them never fire. Most of them die of old age on the same lip of stone with the claw still cocked, and the district behind them never knew they were there, and was safe the whole time.

Behind them, in the streets, walk the multitude. Their cousins elsewhere escape by tail-flip; that reflex has been gone from this lineage so long that the muscles have forgotten what they were for. The ones that flipped were heard. The ones that hesitated became the city. Now they cross the terraces on tiptoe in numbers that make the stone look like it is breathing, and crabs set their claws down like glassware, and ten million snails rasp all day and all night, and the sum of it is a hiss so fine and so evenly spread that a hunter's ear reads it as the texture of nothing happening.

## The native

The listener hangs in a shaft between towers, motionless, sometimes for four days. Velvet-skinned, eyeless, its head mostly ear, the lateral line down each flank drawn out into fringed filaments that can read a shrimp's walking legs at forty meters.

Here is the thing about the listener that the listener does not know and nothing else in the basin can know: it is the only animal here with anything like a picture of the whole.

Sound gives it a map. It knows the districts by their hiss — this one dense and fine, that one thinning, a third gone grey and silent since the breath came late. It knows the felt-thick galleries as dead spots, holes in the world where noise goes in and does not come back. It knows the margin where the sentinels sit, and it has learned, in the way a long-lived animal learns, that the margin is where the food is. It carries the city entire, as a shape made of absences, and it can never enter it. It arrives, and the hands go in, and the water goes smooth, and smooth is the one surface it cannot read.

And it was born here. Its mother hung in this shaft. There is no invading fleet, no outside, no enemy from the cold sea over the sill; nothing from the cold sea survives the warmth long enough to hunt. The fortress is built against its own citizens. The listener is a native of the place it besieges, and the wall of small hands is its own neighborhood, and both of them have been here since the sills closed, and neither has relatives anywhere else in the world.

## The rich, and the thief

Down at the low corner, the mussels do not wait for spring.

They are the only independent means in four miles. Everyone else lives on a pension from the lit world, six weeks in arrears; the mussel bed eats the floor. Each shell is a pasture with a hinge on it — gills packed with sulfur bacteria, farmed internally, fed by brine that seeps up out of the rock. The herd and the meadow are the same body, a kilometer long and forty meters wide, packed so tight that a finger cannot be put between two shells, and it has no center and no head and no season except catastrophe. When the breath comes late, the sulfide climbs and the bed dies from its lower edge upward. When the breath comes hard, the lake tilts and drowns a hundred meters of it in an afternoon. It regrows from the survivors in a season, which means the bed's border is a legible memory of the last thing that went wrong.

And along the hem, in windrows sorted by the slow surf, lie the only individuals in the city.

Nothing rots in brine. A fish that strayed too low keeps its face. An eel that misjudged keeps the exact curve of its misjudgment. A raft of shrimp caught by a rising tide lies mid-step, ten thousand pairs of legs still folded under them — and this is the only place in the basin where you could learn how they walk. Everywhere else a body is eaten, dissolved, plastered over, or carried up in somebody's gut. Here the dead are permitted to stay themselves, some of them older than the district standing above them, unhurried, perfect, and not working.

The eel goes in after them on purpose. It is short and ugly and it knows what it costs: it crosses a surface that is actually a surface, water with a floor to it, four times the salt and heavy with sulfide, and the burn starts in the gills immediately. It takes a body from the windrow and comes out convulsing and alive and eats what it took.

This is the only theft in the city, and what is stolen is the past.

## The misaddressed

Once in a generation a hundred tons of whale hits the plain outside the districts, and for forty years there is a neighborhood there that is not made of stone. Hagfish knot in it. Amphipods boil off it in drifts. Then the bone-eaters arrive, red-plumed, root-bodied, dissolving the skeleton from inside with acid and borrowed bacteria, and they spawn into the current, and most of their larvae find bone.

Some find the city.

They settle on a terrace lip and begin, patiently, to quarry it, and they are doing precisely what they were made to do, in the wrong place. There is no vandalism in this basin. There is only misaddressed settlement. Below the lime line the water is already hungry for carbonate — warm, pressurized, loaded with the breath of ten trillion animals — and it eats the ankles of the towers at about the rate the crowns grow. The dissolvers and the builders have nearly the same face. Neither can tell the difference. Neither can the city. A district leans, and folds, and goes down over a decade in a slump the sponge felt swallows almost whole, and the worms are settling on the rubble before it has finished moving.

Out past all of it, on the flat soft plain, a fish stands on three stilts with its fins spread like open hands, facing the current. It is the only posture in the basin. Everything else is a wall, a mat, a plume, a furrow. This one animal has oriented its whole body toward an event that comes twice a year, or once, or not at all — the cold cataract tumbling down the basin wall, the only wind — and it will stand there through the years the wind skips. Nearby, the sea cucumbers plow their furrows and go nowhere in particular, and inside one of them, in its hindgut, a small fish lives rent-free and comes out to feed when the water is safest, because even the plowing has tenants, because nowhere here is there a bare surface, or an empty interior, or room to be alone.

And in the snowfall, when the whole basin spawns and the water becomes broth, the larvae are the only travelers this city will ever have. Almost all of them settle within a body-length of the tube that made them. A hundred thousand towers of cousins. The crowding is not just the architecture; it is the family tree, and it has been inbreeding in a closed warm room since before there were forests overhead.

## The speed

So who lives here.

Not the worm; it lives for one brood and never knows there is a tower. Not the snail, which knows only wetness. Not the listener, which holds the whole city as a map and is starving at the center of it. Not the mussels, which are a field. Not the standing wave in calcium, which is a shape with no one inside it, material running through, holding its silhouette the way a meander holds its curve while the river leaves.

Something else lives here, and it has no body, and it is very fast.

It is the interval. One snap at the margin, and crowns folding, and tube-mouths shutting, and a hundred thousand small hands withdrawing in a ripple that crosses a district in under a second — no signal, no decision, no one in charge, just ten thousand generations of flinch tuned against a native hunter until the latency came down below the speed of a mouth. That is the resident. That is the thing the masonry is for, and the felt, and the baffled throats, and the bred-out tail-flip, and the shrimp's one shot. It is inherited without being known. It is the only thing in the basin that has been getting better.

For about a minute after it fires, the water is smooth. The rasping goes on, fine as velvet. The pickled dead lie in their windrows under their own slow surf. The listener drifts, full of one shrimp, holding a map with a new hole in it. Nothing in four miles is combing.

Then the hands come out again, district by district, in the order the sound reached them, and the dark goes back to being worked by animals that will never know they were shut.
