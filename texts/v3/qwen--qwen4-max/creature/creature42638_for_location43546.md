# The Six Hundred and Twelve

## Bodies

They are cold all their lives, and they work to stay warm, and so they are a people who cannot be idle without suffering. The felt is not clothing so much as architecture: seamless, wet-formed, sculpted to the body in the damp the way clay is thrown elsewhere, layered in seven or nine skins according to trade and height and the season nobody has. A Cropper on the mid-walls wears a short kirtle and arm-greaves and is naked at the shoulder for two hours a day, and shivers, and is proud of it. A Comber up in the Veil wears a hood like a chimney-cowl and never takes it off, and is rheumy, and coughs into a cloth embroidered by someone who loves them.

Adults are lean and stringy and hot at the core and blue at the knuckles. Infants and the very old are pink and fat, because they are the ones who get the compost. You can read a person's age from across the bulb by their colour.

Their hands are ruined and precise: chilblained, split at the nail, thickened at the joint — and capable of splicing a hair-rope so fine that the splice cannot be found by the eye, only by the wrist. They do not think this is remarkable. The whole town is a machine for making ruined hands do fine work.

## Faces

In a light with no direction, a face has no modelling. The socket does not darken. The nose, seen from the front, disappears entirely — it is the same white as the cheek and there is nothing to say where it ends. What is left is outline, and the eyes, which sit in the flat pale field like two coins on a plate, enormous, unshaded, appallingly legible.

So they draw themselves on. Edging: a fine dark line down the bridge of the nose and around the jaw, a stroke at the corner of the mouth, a rim to the eye. It is done with soot-free pigment — manganese, ground shell-char, the grey-black of certain snail whorls — because soot is unthinkable and the joke about it is the oldest joke they have. Edging is hereditary and guild-marked and vain. Combers edge long and green. Croppers edge blunt. A marriage adds a line at the temple; a death takes one away; a woman in mourning is a face with a gap in it, and everyone can see the gap from ninety paces, and nobody looks.

Their portraits are profiles. A profile has an edge; a front-facing face is a plate with coins on it. Their word for a person's character is the same as their word for the silhouette of them: *edge*. She has a good edge. He has gone soft at the edge. It is not a metaphor about light. It is the only way anyone here has ever been able to see anybody.

And nothing dries. A cried face stays wet for an hour. There is no such thing as having composed yourself. This is why the cough-cloth is the most intimate object in the city: to wipe another person's face with your own cloth is the largest thing you can do for them in public, and it is done slowly, and everyone sees it, and everyone looks at their hands.

## Two Languages, Two Kinds of Truth

The hands are public. Eleven thousand shapes, chest-height, legible from anywhere in the bulb, with a grammar carried in the shoulders — and the shoulders are the part nobody can train. A man can sign *I am content* with perfect fluency while his shoulders say *my son is in the queue and the old woman below me will not die*. Everyone reads both. Lying, here, is not a matter of words. It is an athletic feat of the upper back. There are famous liars, admired the way other places admire singers. There are the flat-shouldered, who cannot do it, and who are treated as holy or as simple, depending on the district.

The mouth is private. Speech happens at the distance of a hand on a shoulder, breath more than tone, and no one three paces off receives anything at all. So the town has an exact inversion of the world above: **what is signed is witnessed, and what is said is not.** Contracts are signed, in the open, at distance, with the shoulders held still. Oaths are spoken, mouth to ear, and are therefore a kind of trust that the law cannot reach. To *say with the mouth what you would not say with the hands* is their definition of cowardice and also, in a slightly different inflection, of tenderness.

Names are never signed. A person's name is a sound, and it lives in the ears of the people who have been close enough to receive it — a mother, a spouse, two or three friends, the woman who washed them for the midden. Across the void you are your trade and your height and your ledger line. To be called by name is to have someone come to you and put their mouth against your ear, and there are people in this town who have not heard their own name in eleven years, and who mention this to no one, because to mention it would be to ask.

And so gossip has a shape and no content. Everyone can see who whispers to whom, for how long, with what shoulders. Nobody has ever known what was said. The town's entire political history is a record of visible conversations whose contents are lost. There are scholars of it. They are, necessarily, wrong.

## Looking at Your Hands

There is no corner. There is no dark. There is no shadow to stand in. Every one of the six hundred and twelve is visible to every other, all the time, at a brightness that never varies.

What they have built instead of privacy is a courtesy of the eye, drilled from infancy, so total that outsiders find it more frightening than the silence: the ability to look through a person. To grant someone privacy you look at your own palms. A crowd of people all studying their own hands is a funeral, or a birth, or a bad quarrel, or someone being sick. Children cannot do it yet — children stare — and the correction is a hand laid gently over the child's eyes, which is also the sign for *please*, and also the sign for *I love you*, and no one here finds this confusing.

There is exactly one licensed stare. Every morning the junior Counter goes to the nook at the base of the north wall, cold-soaked for an hour beforehand, fasting, breathing away through a pine tube, and gauges the Ice Ear with a hair. Then they walk back out onto the Dish and sign the result, and the whole town watches them do it, openly, hungrily, six hundred and twelve pairs of eyes on one pair of hands.

The hands say: *the same.*

The town exhales. It goes across the chamber as a visible ripple, shoulders dropping in sequence, and then somebody laughs — you feel laughter only if you are touching, so laughter travels this room as a silent wave of bodies — and the day begins.

They have been doing this every morning for six hundred years. It is the only event.

## The Guilds Are Temperaments

The **Combers** live at the boundary with their arms in July. They are the only citizens who touch the outside daily, and they are holy and fecund and short-lived: gauze-lung takes them at forty, and because a death permits a birth, the Combers' families are the ones with children in them. Everyone understands this arithmetic. Nobody says it in signs. It is the town's quietest resentment and its quietest reverence, and the Combers themselves are insufferable about both.

The **Croppers** are the guild of the exact hand's-length, and they are the reason there are factions. A Cropper's whole life is a judgment about a finger's breadth of moss, made a hundred thousand times, and it does something to a person. They are precise, humourless, physically magnificent, and they schism. The Long-Felt and the Short-Felt have not spoken aloud to each other in four generations, which in this town is not a figure of speech: it means that when a Long-Felt marries into a Short-Felt household, the two families conduct the entire negotiation in signs at a distance of eleven paces, with their shoulders rigid, while the couple stands between them holding hands and going out of their minds.

The **Limewashers** are everywhere and nowhere, working the rotating patch, and they are the closest thing here to a priesthood, because they maintain the commons itself. They are also the only people permitted to make a wall temporarily less white, and they are watched while they do it, and they enjoy being watched, and this is considered their one flaw.

The **Porters** are sunburnt and squinting and half-deaf and pitied and rich. They control the Scale, the trade, the saxifrage, the copper grains, the pine, the wool. They sign too big — a Throat accent, everything shouted with the hands — and they use loanwords for *shadow* and *horizon* and *sweet*, and they are mocked and married for their money. Deafness is no disability here; the greatest rope-critics are porters, who receive the cord through wrists already ruined by rope and claim, insufferably, to feel it cleaner than anyone. Smuggling is impossible because everything is weighed, so porters smuggle in the body: a seed swallowed, a feather in the hair, a tune. The town's contraband is almost entirely information.

The **Counters**, the **Weight**, the **Reckoners** are the salaried boredom at the centre of everything, and they are not humourless at all; they are the funniest people in the city, in a dry way that takes decades to appreciate, which is also how they cook.

And the **Listeners** — four families, ten thousand sittings in a bone hollow — are the town's actors. There is no theatre here; there cannot be, in a place with no dark and no corners. What there is instead is the Report. The Listener comes down out of the Throat and stands on the Dish and signs the world, and six hundred people watch a hand say *nothing*. There are eleven inherited ways to sign nothing. There is a nothing with weather in it and a nothing with an animal in it and a nothing that means *I was asleep*. There are connoisseurs. There is a famous family whose *nothing* is considered untranslatable. Once in a lifetime the hands make the shape for *a stag*, and the town talks about it for a year, and nothing alters.

## The Queue

A birth requires a death. The Counters keep the list, and the list is public, and this is the hardest thing about living here.

The family who are next go and sit with the one who is dying. They bring curd. They wipe the face. They are gentle and they are useful and they are there every day, and everyone including them knows what they are waiting for, and there is a word — an ugly, precise, unavoidable word — for *looking at the dying with the eyes of a mother*. It is not a crime. It is simply the thing everyone here has done and cannot forgive themselves for.

The very old feel the debt. Some of them pay it early, and going early is the highest honour the town has, and it is not called dying; it is called *making room*, and the name of someone who made room is put into the cord as a rhythm by the rope-players, once, on the night, and six hundred and twelve people receive it in the bones of the wrist. That is the whole of the town's history: a music of names, felt, unrecorded, remembered imperfectly, argued about by critics.

The dead go into the middens and come back as heat, and the heat is rationed by the hour to the infants and the very old, who lie in it together in the dark warm cavity at fifty degrees, the plump pink ends of life against each other in the only embrace-space in the city. The Warm Room smells appalling. It is where every tenderness in this town actually happens. There is a waiting list, and there has been for six hundred years, and there is a black market in hours, and the great scandal of the last generation was a Reckoner who took two.

## Households

A household is a scallop, a hammock-stack, a jar, and a needle.

The needle is copper, one to a family, named, inherited, and its wear is in the Weight. Needles are called things like *Patience* and *Grandmother's Tooth* and *The One That Was Dropped*. A needle shortens over three hundred years and changes shape as it is sharpened, so that a family's identity is slowly, visibly, a different object, and there are households that have quarrelled for a century about the profile of their own needle. When one becomes too short to hold, its copper goes into its successor and the name transfers, and the Weight closes a line, and there is a small ceremony, and it is a real grief.

The jar is the household's share in the mother-curd, and the mother-curd is never emptied. Also there is the betrothal jar, started at a birth, opened at a death, decades of sourness arriving on schedule for people who are not born yet. Their cooks are people who prepare food for strangers in the future. It gives them a particular kind of patience and a particular kind of arrogance.

And there is the marriage-cord: two people's hair, spliced with the silk of a moth caught by a child, a short private line hung in the scallop, tensioned between two bone eyes. It is the only instrument in the city that is not broadcast. Lovers lie palm to palm and play it, and the music goes into the wrist and nowhere else, and no one else in the world knows what it sounds like, though everyone can see them doing it, and looks at their hands.

Hair is the great gift. It grows; it is nearly the only thing here that does; it comes out of the curd, which comes out of the dead, and it goes into the cord, which is the town's nervous system. Every rail, every hammock-sling, every ladder, every fog-net frame. When the town lies down at the long tremor and puts its palms flat on the rail, it is holding hands with its ancestors, and it knows this, and it is not sentimental about it, because sentiment would require drying your face.

## The Other Citizens

The lamp-snails have five whorls of grey and cream and they are bred for pattern the way other places breed horses. There are lineages. There are heraldic snails. A Cropper family has kept a banding strain for four hundred years and will show you the shells of the dead ones, in order, in a felt tray, and talk about them the way you would talk about grandparents. The snails tick on the stone as they graze, and each tick belongs to whoever is within arm's reach, so a snail-paddock at work is a field of people each alone inside a small private clicking.

The newt in the basin has watched ten generations of children be brought to look at it. It is on a full ledger line. Children are told that it remembers them, which is not true, and are also told that it does not, which is, and the two statements are held together without difficulty.

The hush-mice follow the Croppers and take the pupae and are not tame and will not be handled and watch you longer than is comfortable. Every household has one it considers its own. None of them are.

The moths have no ears, because the walls ate the bats. They fly straight and slow and have never in their lineage had anything to fear from a sound, and children take them out of the air with two fingers. The town's word for an unguarded person — someone who walks directly into things, who cannot evade, who is loved and worried over — is *moth*. It is entirely affectionate. It is also a diagnosis.

The cricket still has the apparatus. It rubs, and nothing comes out, and it has been doing this for a hundred thousand years. The sign for *sing* is two fingers rubbed together, and nobody knows why, and the Reckoners who do know are not thanked for explaining.

The fell-bird comes down the Throat at night and hunts by sight in a bright cave, fringed wings, no noise, an offence against every rule the world above has. It has a full ledger line. Its pellets are collected, its dung goes to the Warm Room, and there is a faction — small, persistent, exhausting — that wants it *numbered*. To number the bird would be to change the census, and the census is the name, and the name is the town. The Bird Question has been debated for two hundred years. It is not really about the bird. It is about whether a thing can belong to you without being counted by you, and every family in the Six Hundred and Twelve has an opinion, and the opinion is always about their own son-in-law.

## Those Who Do Not Fit

The sleepless. Sleep is announced by the cord and the whole town lies down at once, and a hammock that keeps moving is visible to everyone, and insomnia here is a public condition. So they get up and go down to the Dish and lie with their heads on the flowstone and an ear a hand from the basin, where the drop falls, and they talk. Mouth to ear. Under the only public sound in the city. This is where every real conversation in the town's history has taken place: six or nine people at a time, flat on the floor in the green light, whispering, invisible to nobody and inaudible to everybody. All the love affairs and all the conspiracies. The Counters cannot hear them. The Counters lie there too.

The child who draws the stain at the feet. It happens in every generation. A child hears a porter's loanword and draws a person with a dark thing attached to the heel, following, and the adults go quiet and take the drawing away and cannot say why, and the child is frightened by their fright, and the word for shadow sits in the language meaning *the thing that follows you and that we do not have*. Their hell is not fire. Their hell is a hot bright place where a black shape walks behind you and the walls give your voice back and a stranger two hundred paces away can hear you chew.

The one who wanted to be heard. Once every century or so, somebody tunes a rail to ring. It is the deepest crime available to them, deeper than theft, deeper than the centipede poison in the curd. A ringing rail takes silence out of the commons; a smoky lamp takes white out of the commons; and both are the same sin, which is *a private joy subtracted from everyone*. They are not ascetics. They are the most thorough sensualists imaginable — of texture, of sourness, of rhythm in the wrist, of another person's breath at the ear. They simply have a moral system in which every pleasure has a mass, and it must be matched on the Scale.

## What Lives There

Here is the thing they will tell you, if you get close enough, in the dark that is not dark, with a hand on your shoulder.

Fire is forbidden because fire warms the pool and lifts the Veil and moves the fog and soils the white. Every child can do the arithmetic. But the chamber is forty paces of trapped cold with no outlet, lying under a hot plain, and there are six hundred and twelve bodies in it, each one burning food at the rate of a small lamp, each one breathing out heat and water and carbon, each one a fire that cannot be put out and must not be allowed to grow.

That is what the Number is. It is not only a census and not only a mass budget. It is how many small burns the cold can carry without the Veil rising. Six hundred and twelve is a thermal figure. It has been checked against the Ice Ear every morning for six hundred years, and every morning the Ear has said *the same*, which means: *you are not too many yet.*

So they are the fire. There is no other. They are a people who live inside a lantern and have sworn never to strike a light, and the light they have sworn off is themselves, and the whole of their law — the White, the Hush, the Cold, the Number, the felt at a hand's length, the grain of copper, the queue, the courtesy of the palms — is a technique for carrying six hundred and twelve flames through a room without letting them show.

And they are not grim about it. That is what visitors never get right. They are busy and vain and funny and in love and bored and greedy about curd. They have critics. They have a heresy about moss. They have a bird they cannot decide whether to count. They lie on the floor at night to hear one drop of water fall, and think of it as the sea, and are not disappointed.

They have permitted exactly one thing in the chamber to change: the Slow Hand, wet, faintly translucent, the height of a child, growing one hair's breadth in a decade, ruled with fine lines. They measure it and record the measurement and the measurement is always the same, and once in a very long while it is not, and then they talk about nothing else for a year. They have calculated when it will reach the Veil. The number is enormous. They speak of it the way other peoples speak of the end of the world, or of a child coming home.

That is what lives here: a stillness with six hundred and twelve warm hands inside it, working all the time, holding a thing perfectly level — and one small wet stone, growing, that they have agreed to love on their behalf.
