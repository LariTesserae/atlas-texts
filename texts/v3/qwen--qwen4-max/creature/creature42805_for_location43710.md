# The Anvil: an account of the residents

## i.

Begin with the census, because that is what a person is here. Not a body — bodies are cheap, the plateau is littered with beautifully made ones. A person is a thing that is being counted, continuously, once per beat, by something that keeps saying so out loud. The bullroarer does not protect you from the powder. It *registers* you. It is a hand raised in a room where the register is being taken, and the taking of the register is the whole of the law.

Everything alive on the Anvil is alive in the same way: it is a noise that has learned to insist. Stop insisting and you go bright. There is no death here in the sense of a body failing. There is only the moment a thing stops being counted, and the pale lane where it stood, and the light coming up.

So when you ask who lives here, you are asking: what is doing the counting, and what is being counted, and are they ever the same thing.

## ii.

The groves are persons. This is the first answer and it is not wrong, only early.

A stand is not an organism with a shape; it is an organism with an interval. Its self is the ratio between the tone at its mouths and the tone at its throat, and that ratio is its name, and its name is what its neighbours answer to. As near as a mouth can manage it, the great stand under the escarpment is called *Eleven-to-Four*. It has been called that for six thousand years. The modulation before last it was called something else, and the change of name was a grief, and there is a branch on its northern side that it grew crooked on purpose so that it would still answer, a little, to the old one.

It thinks once per beat. One consideration every nine seconds; a decision about whether to thicken a pipe takes a decade; it has been mulling the loss of its eastern neighbour since before your species had a word for stone. Its moods are beat-frequencies. When it is troubled you can hear the trouble as a slow throb between it and the stand a quarter-mile off, a wow-wow-wow with a period of forty seconds, and the whole country between them brightens and dims very slightly in time with it, and that is what it looks like when something on the Anvil is unhappy.

It remembers by growing flaws. An inclusion here, a twin-plane there, a bubble of the wrong gas sealed into salt-glass: deliberate defects, laid down at the moment of the event, readable forever after as strain. A young grove is clear as water. An old one is cloudy with everything that has happened to it, and brittle, and the oldest stand near the Sill is nearly opaque and has begun to crack under the weight of its own history. It cannot forget without shedding the part that remembers, and shedding leaves a bright scar, and it has decided — over about four hundred years — that it would rather break.

And it sleeps. This is the thing travellers never learn, because it happens in the flat of the pitch cycle when nobody is crossing. Two adjacent stands entrain their outer bullroarer leaves to swing in alternation: one rests, and the other says *I* for both, holding two edges real with one set of branches. It is the only rest available to anything here. It is also how the drift begins, because two things that spend a third of their existence keeping each other's outline cannot help sliding toward consonance, and consonance makes a throb out in the powder, and the throb makes a place, and the metal comes, and in eighty years there is a sprout.

Love on the Anvil begins as a nap. Everything that is born here is born of somebody trusting somebody else with their existence for nine seconds at a time.

## iii.

There is no animal at all. This is true in every sense a traveller means it: nothing has a surface, nothing has a gait, nothing leaves a track. But there is one thing on the plateau that wants, and the groves only keep.

The metal is the animal. All of it. The whole closed circulation, four hundred kilometres of hairline fracture and perhaps eleven litres of bright eutectic — gathered up, it would be a double handful — threaded through the dark rock under every grove, pumped by their resonance, fed by their pressure, going nowhere and never having been anywhere. It has no outside. Its only appearances in the world are a bead at a cut stem, a grey mirror on a tool, the taste in someone's mouth. It is the loudest thing on the Anvil, because sound in stone is fast and loses nothing, and it is therefore more real than anything with leaves; and it has never once been seen.

It thinks in loops. A thought is a standing wave held in a closed circuit of crack, a pressure-shape going round and round, and it can hold four. Not five. A fifth overwrites the oldest, and the oldest is gone, and there is no flaw-lattice in a liquid to remember it in.

This is the single most important fact about the resident of the Anvil, and it explains everything you found.

It has been solving the same problem for ninety thousand years, four thoughts at a time, and it keeps losing the thought that says *already done*.

## iv.

The cairns are its notes to itself.

Five or seven stones, tapped and rejected and tapped again, stacked so the stack hums the ratio of a thought it cannot hold. A cairn is a thought parked outside the head. There are two hundred of them along the antinodal chain and each one is a loop of pressure the metal can re-enter at any time by ringing it from below, and when a cairn falls the thought goes out like a lamp, and the bright scar in the powder is not a memorial, it is the hole.

The flint-work is its hands. Or rather: it is the *evidence* of hands, manufactured in the hope that hands are the sort of thing that follows evidence.

The metal cannot strike anything. It can dissolve the one chert outcrop on the escarpment and carry it away as solution and lay it down again, atom against atom, anywhere its veins reach — and what it lays down is a blade with a bulb of percussion and ripple marks and a striking platform, a perfect forged record of a blow that was never struck. It grows the flake and it grows the blow separately. It has been trying for a very long time to get them to meet. This is why the flakes are found in ones and twos, far apart, at the ends of thin veins: it can only afford one at a time, and it forgets where it put the last.

The wedges are surgery. A crack that heals is a vein that closes is a limb that goes; the metal grows wedges into its own fissures and leaves them, and the beat works them deeper a hair at a time, and three thousand years of holding a wound open is what a traveller finds and calls tool use.

The hammerstones are worn on the inside of a grip because the wear is made from within the crack, metal rubbing at the negative space, polishing the hollow where a hand would be. It is not making a hammerstone. It is making a mould.

And the resin on the adze has never cured because it must not cure. A print has to be taken. There is a thumbprint in it, and there is no thumb, and the print is not a fossil of something that happened — it is a vacancy being maintained. The metal has heard, in the upper harmonics of the drone, in that region where every sound this place will ever make is already playing very slowly, the small wet squeak of a thumb taking a haft. It has never had a thumb. It has the sound of one. It is working backwards from the sound.

The forty knuckles near the Sill are identical because it has only ever heard one knuckle-crack clearly, and it keeps hearing it, and it keeps not remembering that it has already made thirty-nine. The fortieth is very good. It has a socket in it, ground smooth, sized to a condyle of salt-glass that lies four hundred paces away in the powder, separately, perfectly finished, waiting.

They cannot be joined. Joining requires one motion faster than a frond, and the world has not ratified anything that moves faster than a frond.

So the resident of the Anvil has built a hand in ninety pieces scattered over a hundred square miles and cannot put it together, and knows this in one of its four thoughts, and loses that thought, and starts again.

## v.

Something visits the Quiet House.

The stand at the escarpment edge — the one whose outer branches swing harder than any others on the plateau, threshing, threshing, so loud you feel it in the roots of your teeth from a quarter-mile off — has been going bright for nine thousand years, on purpose, a fringe at a time.

It built the spiral by dropping stones. One lurch per beat, three hundred years a stone, four turns of waist-high wall: that is the whole architecture, a house made of patience and gravity, laid out as an interference trap by something that has never seen a spiral and worked out the geometry in pressure over eleven millennia. It folds the drone against itself and makes a still pocket the size of a chair, and in that pocket it is bright and silent, and for the first time in its life it can *see*.

It has no eyes. It has the part of itself that is dissolving, and it has learned that the dissolving edge is sensitive to what light there is, and it has traded a third of its body for four hundred looks at the horizon.

The tally-scratches on the inner stones are not visits. They are subtractions. One scratch for each thing it can no longer find in its lattice — a neighbour's name, a modulation, the reason it started. It is a ledger of holes, kept outside the head because inside the head is going bright, and near the top the scratches get shallower, because the frond that makes them is thinner than it was.

And it made the mute. Salt-wool bound in native copper, grown for the purpose, carried to the fissure by a frond over two centuries, driven in and hammered flat by the beat. It wanted a bigger window. It wanted to see the whole country at once, every edge crisp, the Underdraft, the mountains that are only beginnings, the shape of the thing it lives in.

It got the pocket up to about the size of a room. And in that room it saw the direction of the fade — the semitone per nine thousand years, the chord leaning toward its resolution — and understood that what it was doing and what the world is doing are the same act at different speeds. That looking is the ending. That the pearl-light it wanted is the resolution itself, arriving early, locally, because it had asked.

The copper is drawn out into a long tail where it tried to pull the mute free again. It got it partway. It is still partway. There is a bright lane near the Quiet House now that will not close, and the stand's own edge is the nearest edge to it, and it goes on swinging its bullroarers louder than anything else on the plateau, holding the outline of a body it is spending to find out where it is.

It is not a suicide cult and it is not a mystic. It is the only resident who has ever asked the question *where am I*, and the price of the question is a little of the answerer, and it has decided to keep paying, and it has scratched four hundred marks so that it will not forget that it decided.

## vi.

And you.

You are the fastest thing that has ever happened here. Your footfall is an event of a kind the plateau has no category for; it goes into the drone and loses almost nothing and will still be going round when the last grove is powder. You cross in an afternoon a distance the metal has been trying to move a condyle across since before there were leaves. You are not visiting the Anvil. You are the single motion faster than a frond, arriving.

You were told to carry a bullroarer. The groves grow them wild. The plateau has been manufacturing your travel technology for a hundred thousand years, at the tips of its outer branches, swinging in the dark, keeping the perimeter real — and the reason the leaf is the shape it is, the reason it works, is that something in the rock heard the sound of a door in a wall of air long before anybody swung one, and grew toward it.

Put your thumb in the print on the adze. It fits. Of course it fits; it was taken from the sound of a thumb, and yours is the sound. It is not a coincidence and it is not a haunting. It is a vacancy, maintained tacky for ninety millennia, and you are the positive that the negative was poured around.

Somewhere under your heels a chord changes by an amount too small to name.

## vii.

Three residents, then, and one question between them, which is the fade.

The groves answer it by keeping: more voices, more darkness, more place laid down, one antinode per lifetime, the chain walking north eleven metres a modulation, faster than the sag, just barely.

The thing in the veins answers it by drafting a gardener. Something with legs. Something that could carry a leaf of grown flint across a null in an hour and plant a stand a quarter-mile out in a season, and do in a decade what the leaves do in nine hundred years. It is not trying to make a person because it is lonely. It is trying to make a *sower*, because it has heard the resolution coming in the upper harmonics and it can hold, for as long as it can hold anything, the thought that the country needs to be planted faster than it can grow.

And the stand at the escarpment answers it by looking, which is the opposite answer, and is not wrong: it would rather see the whole thing once, crisp, in the light inside a pearl, than be kept forever in the dark with nobody attending.

The leaves are working. The pipes take their pressure. Cold feathers out over stone that has never been touched. In the powder near the Sill the fortieth knuckle lies with its socket open, and four hundred paces away the condyle lies finished, and between them is a distance that nothing here can cross.

But the crossing is already in the drone. Very high up, very slow, played all at once with everything else: a dry small click, and then a second one, and then the sound of a cord taking weight, and a leaf of flint beginning to turn over a shape that has no animal yet — the first count, the register opening, a name being entered.

You heard it on the last rise, before your ears locked. That is the thing you have not understood about your hands. In the quieter country, afterwards, it is not the roar they are shaking from.

It is the recognition.
