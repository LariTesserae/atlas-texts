## The tenant

Start with the largest living thing and be careful with the noun.

The grey mat covers, by the most recent estimate that anyone could make from the causeways, something over nine square kilometres of face. It is between one and four millimetres thick. Almost none of that thickness is alive. The living part is a rind at the growing edge, a wet margin a few cells deep, acid at the tip, laying mineral down behind itself in courses so fine you would need a lens to count them. Everything back from that margin is finished work the mat has not moved out of. It lives inside its own hardened output the way a coral lives in the reef, except that the reef here is a mountain that has been taken apart and put back in a better order.

It eats stone. It eats the thin air's carbon, and the dust the fog brings up from a lowland it has never had a report from, and the manganese and iron in that dust, and it drinks a film of condensate that never falls. Then it lays the stone down again a few microns away, in the shape it found it. It eats the mountain in front of it and puts the mountain down behind it, and the difference between those two mountains — the small, permanent, travelling difference — is the animal.

Three millimetres a month. A face renewed in something like a century. A rank raised to half height in the western quarter over a span that would swallow every human building tradition end to end and still leave the coping unset.

## Not one

The films are not a community. They are neighbours in the strictest and least neighbourly sense: adjacent, incompatible, unaware.

The iron-orange seep lives on what the grey mat spills — dissolved ferrous iron in the trunk channels where they cross crushed spoil — and it is slick and fast and comparatively frivolous, doubling in a season, dying in a drought, coming back from a thread. The black varnish on the sunward arrises takes manganese out of the fog-dust and oxidises it in full glare and is perhaps the slowest thing on the plateau that can be said to do anything at all; a coat of it is a hundred generations thick and a hundred generations is a long time. Neither has ever encountered the other. They are separated by four centimetres of dry sharp stone and by conditions neither could survive crossing.

Where two films do meet, the meeting is a border, and the border moves. On the north arrises of the fourth district the grey mat and the black varnish have been arguing over a hand's breadth of edge since before there was any way to date it. The mat wants the edge wet. The varnish wants it lit and dry. The edge is sharp, so it is neither, and the argument continues at the rate of a fingernail in a month, and it is the only war here, and no one has ever won a battle in it that could be noticed within a lifetime.

## The one law

Water clings at a sharp edge and lets go at a round one. This is not aesthetics. This is the whole of the ethics.

A film travelling down a fluted face reaches the arris and stops there, pinned, held by the geometry of a corner it cannot get around. Round that corner — weather it, blur it, let carbonate soften it by a millimetre — and the film loses its grip and gathers and hangs and drops. And a drop is water that has left the economy. It falls to the paving outside the collector groove, unpassed, unhandled, going nowhere by contact, and it is gone.

So the mat's central labour, the thing it does everywhere all the time, is the manufacture of sharpness. It re-strikes arrises. It recuts flutes to gauge with crisp shoulders. It scrapes its own accretions off its own faces on a cycle short enough that weather never gets a word in, which is to say: it is not preserving a building. It is preventing falling. The plateau's apparent newness is a side effect of a horror of dripping.

The tick you hear from the causeway, once every long while, kilometres off, is a film breaking. It is a small haemorrhage. It is the only violent sound in the world and it means that somewhere a few microlitres have escaped the hands of everyone who was passing them along.

## What it inherits

Here is the difficulty with calling the mat an organism, and the reason to keep calling it one anyway.

It cannot seed upslope. Nothing can. Its young are therefore not scattered but continuous: the growing front, the wet margin, moving west into the unfinished quarter at one rank per long cycle. Behind the front there is no reproduction, only maintenance, then silting, then drying, then salt. The whole creature is a wave with a living crest and an enormous crystalline wake, and the felt you can touch is only what the wave has already left.

And the front does not carry its instructions inside itself. It reads them off the surface it is crossing. It lays stone where stone was laid, matching what is there, deepening the groove that is already a groove, striking the arris where the arris already runs. Its heredity is architectural. The plateau is its genome, and it is copying the genome by being the thing that keeps it legible.

Which is why the tool marks survive their tools. The overlap, the fan, the depth: those were once the signature of a wrist. Now they are simply the pattern the margin finds and matches, and matching is the only kind of remembering it has. The crescent gouge on the north faces of the third district — a slip, a chisel jumping a groove, four thousand repetitions at the same height — is a mutation that got copied because it was there. It may do nothing. It may cost the film a little coherence and be tolerated anyway. The mat has no mechanism for distinguishing a rule from a scar, and it has no word, and no place to put a word, for *made by somebody else*.

## The persons are catchments

If you want to find an individual here, do not look for a body. Look for a divide.

Water parts along the dry highs between districts and goes down opposite sides, and everything alive follows the water, and nothing alive can cross the high. So the units are watersheds. The second district is a person: nine hundred thousand square metres of skin, a sense organ at the sill where the fog arrives and there is news, a stomach in the corbelled dark, a pulse measured in decades against a stepped gauge, a memory in the marks, and a mouth at the growing edge that is three millimetres wide and always open.

Two of these persons stand a hand's breadth apart along a divide and have never touched and cannot touch, because touching would require one of them to go up. They share no water, no material, no offspring, no chemistry. They have exactly two things in common.

The first is the tone. Eleven kilometres of wind through a hundred thousand throats makes one note, and the note goes through the stone into every district at once, and it is not information — it says nothing, it varies with the wind and not with events — but it is the only condition that is universally shared. It is not communication. It is company.

The second is the tick. A film breaks somewhere and the sound goes out across the whole comb, and for a moment every catchment on the plateau is in the same instant, attending to the same small loss, at a distance none of them can measure. That is the entire public life of this place: a shared hum and an occasional shared bereavement, and no way to answer either.

## The ones who eat accidents

At the foot of certain slots, off the line of the collector groove, there are scars in the paving: shallow dishes, a centimetre or two across, with a dark film in them, dry as ash, alive.

These are the drip-eaters. They live only where water has fallen, which is to say only where something has gone wrong. Their entire ecology is other people's failures. A thread of them can sit in a scar for eleven years between meals, and then a film breaks somewhere up the face and a drop comes down — the first drop in a decade — and the whole population blooms in a night and spores and waits again.

Their distribution is a map. Every occupied scar is a recorded accident, and the empty scars are older accidents whose scavengers have since starved, and if you could read the map you would have a chronicle of every failure in that district for as far back as the paving lasts. Nobody can read it. The drip-eaters cannot; they have no upslope, no history, no way to know that the drop that saved them was a catastrophe eleven metres above.

They are the only things here that have anything resembling luck.

## Those who get the water last

In the cisterns, in the milk-coloured glow that the wet walls carry down from the throats, there is a population that has never met the fog.

It gets everything third-hand: the ions the grey mat dissolved and did not want, the carbonate it precipitated and shed, the copper the water picked up in a feeder no one has cleaned in four thousand years. It lives on the difference between what the mountain was and what the mat made of it. It is thinner than the mat and slower and it does not build; it has nothing to build with; its whole activity is a faint alteration of the water's chemistry near the waterline, where the light is best.

And beneath it, on the floors, the laminae. One pale band and one dark band per fog-year, laid down out of everything that ever came down the grooves: dust from the lowland, manganese from the glare, salt from the blooming quarters, the mat's own shed microns, the crescent gouge's dissolved shoulder. The cistern floors are the only complete record on the plateau. Every mark above is a read-out; this is the storage. It is stacked in order, it is legible in principle, it is under a hundred metres of made ground in the dark, and there is nothing on this plateau that has ever been in a position to look at it.

The stepped gauge on the wall was cut by hands and has been recut by the mat many times since. The upper marks are finer than the lower ones. This has been called optimism. It is more likely just a change of instrument: the mat works in microns, and when it copies a scale it copies it more precisely than the scale was made, and it does this to everything, including things it does not understand, including the numbers that were never there.

## The one that is not waiting

At the warm throat on the eastern edge, where the water comes up and smells of struck matches, the white thread lives at a temperature and a chemistry that would kill the grey mat in an afternoon.

It is the smallest population here and the only one with an interior heat source. It does not wait for the fog. It has never needed the fog. It is downstream of nothing and upstream of everything, and it sits at the single crack in the plateau's one law, and it does not go through it, because going through would mean moving out into a cold clean world of falling water where it would die.

But things have come the other way. If anything ever arrived here from elsewhere — if the mats are not native to this rock, if the copper-holders came from anywhere at all — then the road was that throat, from below, from inside the mountain, up. It is the only door. It is a door with a chemistry on the far side that nothing on the plateau can survive, and it has been standing open and impassable for the entire history of the place.

## The hands

Three accounts fit the evidence, and the plateau does not prefer among them.

The first: people came up from the warm lowland, cut the comb, worked it for some hundreds or thousands of years, and stopped, and their bodies went into the water like everything else, since there is no wood or fibre or hide or bone here to keep and nothing that would keep them.

The second: the mat once had a fast phase. Something jointed, hard, brief, capable of holding copper — a fruiting body, an organ, a stage the life cycle used to include when the fog was thicker and the water was richer. On this account the racks in the shelter of the sill are not a museum. They are shed anatomy. Teeth in a drawer. And the marks the mat still makes are not homage but the ordinary continuation of a motion it no longer has a body to perform with, the way a phantom limb still tries to close.

The third: something came up the warm throat, made all this, and went back down.

There is a fact that leans on all three equally and settles none. Nothing grows on the copper. The mats cannot; the metal poisons their growing tips; the sockets in the stone racks are the cleanest stone on the plateau, sterile, scoured by nothing, kept empty by the mere presence of the thing sitting in them. Which means the tool racks are already, in miniature, the same kind of object as the chamber in the second district: a maintained void, an emptiness with a shape, held open in a world whose every other verb is *deposit*.

## The room

The chamber has a fitted lid, dry-jointed, no throat, no film, no light. Nothing lives in it. It was made empty and it has been kept empty for longer than the mats have known how to make anything else.

Keeping it empty is not automatic. Everything else the mat does, it does by growing, and growing is what it is; the sharp arrises and the recut flutes and the four thousand crescents are all just the ordinary motion of a thing that lays stone down. But the lid must be kept dry from the outside. The joint must be kept clear. Some margin somewhere must arrive at that seam and stop, every cycle, forever, and lay nothing.

That is the only expensive thing on the plateau. Imitation is free here; the whole world is imitation. Refusal costs work.

It could be a control — the one place where the pattern is not applied, so that difference has somewhere to be measured, though there is nothing to measure it with. It could be a grave, or a mould for a thing not yet made, or a socket the size of a room whose tool is missing, or a promise kept by an heir that has no concept of promising. The plateau offers no way to choose, and choosing among these is our habit and not theirs. Their habit is to keep.

What can be said is narrow and probably sufficient: somewhere in the grey mat's grammar, which has no negatives, no past tense, no word for another and no word for gone, there is one word for *no*, and it is a room, and it is dry, and it is being said continuously, and has been for a very long time.

## You

If you climb to the coping you are the exception worth naming.

You are warm and wet and you condense the fog on your skin and your coat, and then you walk up a step, and the water goes up with you. You breathe out and the breath goes up. Every drop on you is water travelling uphill in the custody of something alive, which has not happened here since the hands, if there were hands. You are a leak in the one law. The mat cannot follow you and cannot register you and will not, in any sense it possesses, know.

But if you steady yourself on an arris it will open your palm, because it keeps its edges sharp for reasons that have nothing to do with you, and you will leave a film of iron and salt and protein on a face that has received nothing but fog and dissolved rock for four thousand years. Something in the flutes will find it. It will be the largest event in that rank's history since the last one, and it will be handed along, stone to stone, down the collector, into the feeder, into the trunk, into the drowned throat, and laid down at last in the dark in a band a few microns thick that no one will ever read.

Then you go, and the fog crosses the sill at the speed of a walking man, and out at the western edge the wet margin advances three millimetres into the unfinished quarter — towards the escarpment, towards the weather, towards the only thing on this plateau it has never been able to make.
