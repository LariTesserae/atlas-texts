## The Resident at the Second Drip

Begin with the one that is unmistakably an animal, because it is the easiest to be wrong about.

It is four millimeters long, the color of watered milk, and shaped like a pseudoscorpion the way a cloud is shaped like a horse. Its pincers are too long for it. It has no eyes — not reduced eyes, not eyes under skin, simply no place where eyes went, the front of it smooth as a knuckle. It sits on a shelf of halite the width of a thumbnail beneath the second drip point, and it holds its arms open.

The obvious reading is patience. That reading is wrong, and the room corrects it within a minute. Everything here runs hot. At 58°C in saturated air, chemistry does not loiter; this animal's guts work at a speed that would embarrass a mouse. It is not slow. It is *stationary*, which is a different achievement entirely, and it is hungry all the time, and it has solved hunger by locating itself under a delivery.

Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Thirty-four.

The two short intervals are when the drip shakes the film above it and something comes down: a grazer washed off its meadow, a molt, a strand of bacteria with a mite of sulfur in it. The long interval is not waiting. The long interval is eating, arms folded in, and then the arms go out again at the angle they have been going out at since before there were people to notice angles.

It does not count. Counting would require a number. What it has is a metabolism tuned by eleven thousand years of the same water arriving in the same order, so that its hunger peaks about a second before the drop does. It is a clock made of meat, keeping the same time as a clock made of salt, and neither of them knows what time is.

## The Sixty

The grazers are two millimeters, pale as the inside of a wrist, and they live on four meadows the size of your hand.

Their meadows are the stains: rust, olive, weak tea, films of purple sulfur bacteria a few cells thick, growing on the only light in the world that is also yesterday's light. The grazers crop them to a lawn. They are why the wall faces stay optically clean, and the room's brightness — the whole apparatus, the sorting, the storing, the anticipation — leans on that cleanliness the way a lung leans on not being filled with sand. Sixty small appetites are load-bearing.

But do not call them staff. They have their own business, and their business is crossing.

Between the four patches the floor is bare, growing, precipitating in optical continuity at a rate you can measure in a day. A hexapod that stops out there does not die of exposure; nothing desiccates in here. It gets a foot grown into the crystal. Then two. Then the crystal comes up over it while it is still working its mouthparts, and there is no seam, and there is no scar.

So they cross only in the rain. Ninety seconds a day, when the ceiling's whole face lets go at once and every surface in the room is briefly a dissolving surface, briefly taking water back instead of laying it down — ninety seconds when the floor lets go of things. That is the entire window of travel, courtship, and gene flow between four populations on four walls. Their whole politics happens in a minute and a half, daily, at three in the afternoon, in the loudest sound that ever occurs here.

There are hexapods in the floor. Look down through the lit fog and you can find them at a hand's depth, and then deeper, and then further than the light goes. They are not fossils. A fossil is a rumor of an animal, a replacement, a shape where something was. These are the animals, entire, with the setae still on their legs, held in a crystal that has no seam around them because there was never an interface to make a seam of. The room does not bury its dead. It *incorporates* them, at the same speed and by the same operation with which it heals a knife scratch, and it can dissolve them back out again whenever it needs the phosphorus.

Some of them, deeper down, are not dead. Copepod cysts. Grazer eggs. Sealed in optical continuity, dry in the way only salt can make a thing dry, viable in a way nobody has yet had the nerve to test properly. The room's archive and its nursery are the same drawer.

## The One Black Dot

The pool is four meters across and thirty centimeters deep and has a seam in it like badly poured glass: brine below, fresh above, held apart by the room's sorting and by nothing else at all.

In the fresh lens there is a copepod. Translucent to the point of being a rumor with a gut. It has one eye — a naupliar eye, a cup of pigment, a single granule of red-black — and that granule is the only opaque object in nine meters by six.

Consider what it takes to be the one opaque thing in a room whose four-meter ceiling has been graded at the sub-wavelength scale so that it will not reflect light from any angle, ever. The room has performed an optical miracle overhead and cannot, anywhere in itself, produce a shadow. And this animal swims around carrying the single point of darkness in the world, like a punctuation mark that has escaped its sentence.

Its country has no shore. Above is air, which will pin it at the surface film and hold it there and kill it. Below is the seam, and under the seam is brine, which kills it faster. It lives in a slot of fresh water a hand deep and four meters wide, and it lives there entirely on the room's competence. If the sorting fails for an afternoon, the layers mix, and the last of it goes.

It is not adapted to the room. It is *hosted* by the room, the way a note is hosted by a held breath.

## Upstream of the Sky

In the feeder's throat, where the water comes up at 91°C and the sulfur is, there are archaea, and they are the oldest things in the place.

They were in the bed before there was a room. They have never been in light and never will be. They do not photosynthesize, do not know about the lid, do not participate in the eleven-and-eleven-and-thirty-four; they live at the temperature of a scalding tap and they reduce sulfur and they have been doing it, without interruption, through everything.

They are also the reason there is anything else. The carbon in this room arrives dissolved, a few parts per million, ancient and inorganic and grudging. There is no soil and no leaf and no reason for either. The entire animal life of the chamber — sixty grazers, one copepod, one blind thing with its arms out — is what that trickle can afford. The films fix it, the grazers crop the films, the predator takes the grazers, the water carries the sulfur back down, the archaea turn it over, and the loop is very nearly closed, and the whole biosphere is a rounding error in the room's chemistry that happens to keep the windows clean.

When the lid falls, the archaea will not notice.

## The Thing With No Edge

Now the resident that is not an animal, and for which the word *resident* is already a mistake, because it does not reside in the room; the room is a feature of it.

The defect lattice: vacancies and substitutions in an ordered pattern repeating over centimeters, holding at 60°C in a wet room where thermal noise should erase it in microseconds. Coherent floor to ceiling. Coherent out into the bed for a hundred meters in every direction, getting slower and coarser as it goes, so that there is no surface anywhere you can point to and say *here it stops.*

It runs between the sun and the cold of the night sky and it does work, and the work is sorting, storing, anticipating. It has a metabolism. It has a temperature it maintains against the world. It heals. It schedules. It solves for its own continuation an hour before the problem arrives, and the solution comes out as water falling in the right place.

But the important thing about it is not that it is clever. It is that it has no single speed.

Here, at the hot bright center, its fastest operation takes eleven seconds. Twenty meters out, where the pattern has coarsened, the same operation takes a day. At the hundred-meter edge it takes a decade, and the pattern out there is doing the same work — sorting, anticipating, holding its order against the noise — at the tempo of geology, in the dark, in the cold, without a ceiling, without a pool, without anything to look at.

So: the room is not the organism. The room is the part of it that is awake.

Everything else is the same thing thinking slowly. The pan above, ticking as it expands at noon, is its skin. The fracture is its throat. The hundred meters is its long body, and somewhere out past the edge of coherence, if there is an edge, there may be other bright hot rooms keeping other schedules, and no water passes between them, and they are related the way two thoughts in one head are related.

The animals are not its organs. They have their own lineages, their own futures, their own separate ways of failing. They are what it can afford, and what it can afford turns out to be necessary, and neither party signed anything.

## What Cannot Live Here

You cannot live here. This is worth saying plainly, because everything above is a resident and you are not one.

Fifty-eight degrees, saturated. Sweat does not leave. A body in this room only heats. Fifteen minutes to the end of judgment, less if you hurried down the fracture. The room has no opinion about this. It has no opinion about anything; it has a schedule.

And if you die in here — and people have died in stranger places for worse reasons — you will be, by a wide margin, the largest event in eleven thousand years. Sixty kilos of carbon and nitrogen and phosphorus, more nutrient than the feeder has delivered since the ice went. The films will bloom past a few cells. The optical faces will foul. The stored light will go into a stain instead of into the night. The sorting will stagger. It will take a century to digest you, and the room will be a different room afterward, dimmer, slower, its schedule rewritten around a catastrophe with your name on it.

It has no defense against this. Nothing here has ever had a mouth aimed at it. It has no immune system, no predator, no competitor, no experience of being eaten. The only disaster it has ever had to account for is the sky, and it has already answered the sky.

## The Cupped Hands

Two meters off the northeast corner, behind a wall that looks like every other wall, there is a void the size of two hands held together. Dry. Dark. Its edges so fresh they squeak under a fingernail.

Nothing lives in it. That is not the same as it being empty.

Nothing is stored there — no egg, no cyst, no cell, no food, no water. It is not a seed and it is not an egg and there is no little copy of the room folded up inside it. It is a specification. It is the shape a room needs to be, cut in advance, in the dark, by something that has been rehearsing this cut for a very long time.

The bands in its wall are already moving. Eleven, and eleven, and thirty-four, in a place where there is no water to fall.

The inheritance is not in the child. It is in the mother's walls: the eggs and the cysts in optical continuity, the films reducible to a few cells, the whole archive held in crystal that heals and eats and heals again. When the lid loses its span and the pan comes down and the sky gets in and the apparatus becomes ordinary geology in an afternoon, the feeder will still come up at 91°C, because a fracture in a hundred-meter bed does not care what happened to a ceiling. The water will go looking. It will find a dry dark cupped pair of hands with fresh squeaking edges, and it will dissolve the walls of the room that died, and carry everything forward in solution.

Inheritance by dissolution. The same operation seen from two sides. The death is the delivery.

So what lives here is one thing, at several speeds, with four kinds of memory — crystal that forgets on purpose, bodies held perfectly in the floor, genomes tested by eleven thousand years of afternoons, and a plan cut in the dark. It has been young the whole time. It has never once been the same. At three o'clock the ceiling will let go all at once and the loudest sound in the world will happen and sixty grazers will cross between four meadows and a blind thing under the second drip will open its arms a second before the water arrives, as it always does, as it has no idea it is doing.

And two meters to the northeast, in the only darkness for a hundred meters, in a place where nothing has happened yet, something is keeping the same time.
