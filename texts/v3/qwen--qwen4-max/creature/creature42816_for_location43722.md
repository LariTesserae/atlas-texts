## The Tenants

There is no one here to introduce. There are positions, and the positions are occupied, and the occupants are replaceable in a way that would be obscene anywhere else. Take a handful of litter, seal it in a bag, and the animals inside will eat one another down through a day of frantic subtraction and stop — not at exhaustion, but at a ratio. Predators per prey. Shredders per gram of leaf. One of the large thing that eats the small things, and enough of the small things to keep it. Open the bag in a year and you will find the same arrangement wearing different bodies. The arrangement is the resident. The animals are how it is currently being done.

That is the first answer, and it is true, and it is not sufficient, because ratios do not eat. Go down.

---

Everything wet here is filmed with water, and the film is inhabited. On the underside of a leaf, in a layer two or three microns thick, nematodes swim. They do not crawl; the film is deep enough for a body their size to be a body in a fluid, and shallow enough that surface tension is a wall they cannot breach. Gravity is a rumor to them. The world is a set of connected wetnesses, and to cross a dry patch is to die. Rotifers and tardigrades do die, routinely, and stop dying: they contract into tuns, expel almost all their water, shut down to a metabolism too low to measure, and wait out the afternoon. When the four o'clock drip arrives they resume. A single tardigrade on a single bract may have been dead four hundred times. It has no equipment with which to notice.

Among them walk the oribatid mites, armored, unhurried, three years to a generation. The nymphs of many kinds carry their shed skins on their backs, stacked, a tower of their own former selves; some add grains of soil and fragments of the litter until they are walking debris, indistinguishable from the floor except that the floor does not turn. Whole lineages of them have not produced a male since the Eocene. Daughters, daughters, daughters, forty million years of a mother copying herself into the dark.

These are the oldest tenants and the smallest, and to them the shelf is not dark at all. It is wet, and it has edges.

---

Above that scale, nothing here lives alone.

Point at a leafcutter and you have not finished pointing. In the pocket beneath her mouth she carries a pellet of the garden. In crypts on her cuticle she grows a bacterium that makes the antibiotic that suppresses the mold that would ruin the garden; the mold is present too, always, waiting in the walls. On her tarsi rides a mite that eats the garden's spores. In the notch of her neck rides the hitchhiker whose whole profession is the fly that would otherwise lay an egg on her. Five parties and one walking body, and the body is the smallest of the five in terms of what it contributes.

The fungus is the great individual of the shelf. One clone, threaded through chambers under every trunk road, fed leaf it cannot digest by an animal that cannot digest it either. Between them they have a single stomach. It has been passed mouth to mouth, queen to founding chamber, for something like thirty million years, an unbroken wet line that has never once been left outside a body to fend for itself. In that time it has been shaped into making swollen hyphal tips it does not need, food bodies offered up like a fruit to a farmer that will otherwise let it starve. It has no outside. Its environment is ants. If you could ask it what lives here it would say: mouths, arriving.

---

Two hundred thousand tanks per hectare, and each one is the shelf again, smaller.

Fall comes in through the top. Sediment settles. A filter-feeder works the sediment, a scraper works the walls, and something with a hinged jaw sits at the summit and eats the rest, and the proportions are the proportions of the basin, arrived at independently, in a cup of water in the axil of a sedge that holds ninety milliliters and has never been visited by the sun. The largest mosquito here spends its larval life eating its neighbors and its adult life drinking nectar; the killing apparatus is dismantled at the molt and never rebuilt. It does all of its harm as a child, in a room the size of a thimble.

The frogs have solved the tanks differently. The mother does not lay a clutch and leave. She lays a few eggs in the litter, waits, and then carries the tadpoles one at a time on her back, up a trunk, to addresses she remembers: this bract, that hole, the third axil on the left. One child per tank, because two would mean one. Then she returns, every few days, to each room, and lays an unfertilized egg in the water, and the child eats it. Ninety rooms. Ninety only children, none of whom will ever meet a sibling, each of whom knows the world as a curved wall, a ceiling of leaf, and a mother who arrives with food and is otherwise the sky.

When a tank dries, that world ends in an afternoon. She has bet against exactly this by refusing to keep her children together.

---

The slow residents are not slow because they are stupid. They are slow because motion is a sentence spoken aloud in a room full of listeners, and because at ninety-six percent humidity a warm body cannot afford to work.

The python near the seep terraces has eaten nine times in six years. After a meal her intestine swells to several times its resting mass, her heart grows, her metabolism climbs to a furnace; then the meal is finished and the organs are dismantled back down, because carrying a working gut between meals costs more than the meals are worth. She rebuilds the machinery each time. She is not waiting. She is the shelf's method of keeping a bat for a year.

In the hinge of each of her scales lives a mite that has never seen her move. Deeper in the same hollow is a blind snake, thread-thin, a meter of it, all female, one clone, parthenogenetic, eating ant brood in the dark by smell; the same animal is in flowerpots on four continents and does not know it has been distributed.

The land crabs are the shredders. They clip leaves and carry them down into burrows and stack them, and they do not eat the leaves; they eat what the leaves become after a week of rotting, which is a different food and a better one. Their burrows ventilate the litter. The floor breathes because crabs are digging. Each crab carries water in its gill chambers — a tank that walks, one of the two hundred thousand, and the only one that has read the map. Twice a day the water in the rock lifts a hand's width and settles, six hours behind a coast none of them will see, and the crabs read it and go, over days, three days' walk to salt, and release larvae into a sea they have no organ for imagining. Some of them wear a dead snail while they do it, and inside the shell, a mouthful of ocean, carried uphill.

---

Some residents have no bodies of their own to speak of.

The army ant column carries guests: rove beetles shaped vaguely like ants, smelling exactly like ants, groomed by ants, fed by ants, eating the ants' brood. Identity here is a chemical password, and the password can be forged, and a thing that has the password is inside the colony no matter what it is doing. There is no border. There is only the smell, and the smell is the resident, and the bodies are its current clothing.

Others live in behavior. A fungus grows through an ant's body and then, at an hour it selects, walks her out of the nest and up a stem and makes her clamp onto a vein at a height and a humidity that suit the fungus and not the ant. The last eleven hours of her life are somebody else's address. She performs them flawlessly. Nothing in her has been broken; she is simply being spent by a tenant who pays no rent in matter.

---

The bats commute in at dusk and the owls work the seams, and neither of them lives here; they visit, and their visits define everyone.

A resident of the shelf is, in large part, a list of things not done. Not called at the frequency the bats attend to. Not hurried across an open face of leaf. Not landed on the upper surface, where the silhouette shows. Not gone out at the hour when something comes down the seam listening for the sound of a wing unfolding. The moths have taken this furthest: their wings and bodies are upholstered in scales that absorb ultrasound, a surface engineered against being heard, so that the echo going back to the bat is thinner than the moth deserves to be. They are quiet in the material itself.

The frogs whisper into the gaps. The midges find even the whisper and drink them, and so the whisper has a budget. The mantid crosses a leaf in eleven minutes, swaying at the frequency of a leaf in wind — a resident pretending to be the weather.

---

And there are the ones with one room.

The glow-worm larvae strung in the wet seams of the terraces have a sky thirty centimeters wide. They spin fishing lines hung with beads of mucus and they light, and the light is honest because it costs: a hungrier larva glows brighter, and cannot help it. The lamp is a mouth. Everything that comes to the only light in the basin comes to be eaten, and after nine months of it the larva spends its whole savings on four days of wings and a mouth it does not have, mates in the dark it made, and lays eggs on the ceiling.

Four kilometers east, the fig wasps live in doorways. The foundress squeezes through the one small door and her wings snap off at the threshold and stay there, a coat left at the entrance of the only house she will ever enter. Inside: an inward-facing garden, her eggs, her death, and the tree's option to drop the whole vessel if she came without pollen. Her sons are wingless. They will never leave. They mate with their sisters and then chew an exit tunnel through the fig wall, working together, for hours, and die at the inner end of a door they made and will not use.

---

Something lives here that is not any of these, and it is not a spirit, and it is not above them, and it does not know.

It is the arrangement — the thing that reassembles in the bag. It has no body but it has a metabolism, forty-four degrees in the pockets, thirty-four at the skin. It is fed by the fall. It repairs itself: when a branch lets go and tears a hole in the world, it sends bidders into the gap and closes it in twenty years and does not change. It has held its ratios through four million years of species arriving and leaving, the way a standing wave holds its shape while every molecule of the river goes through it. Individuals are the medium. It is what they are doing, taken all at once.

And it has no inside, either, because the ceiling is tenants too. The nine layers taking the sun apart are alive; the dark is their output. The heat on your palm is breath, or the next thing to it. Nothing here lives in the darkness. They manufacture it, ninety feet overhead, all day, and stand underneath what they make.

Stand still and you become one of the tanks: thirty-seven degrees, the largest single body of warm water in the hectare, wet at the surface, salted, exhaling carbon dioxide in a plume the midges can read from twenty meters. The ants find the salt. The fall begins on your shoulders. You have not been noticed, exactly; you have been entered, at the rate the ledger uses, which is grams.

Then, at some hour with no name here, the water in the limestone lifts a hand's width, and in a snail shell in a crab's grip a mouthful of the sea lifts with it, and everything that lives on the shelf goes up together, and settles.
