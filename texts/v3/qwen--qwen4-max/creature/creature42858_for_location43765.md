## The Joiners

A crab is working the third lashing of a standard near the east wall. It has been at that knot for four months. It is grey and flat and about the size of a spread hand, and it holds, in the five fingers of its forward pair, a lentil of basalt, a wedge of bone, and a spool of silk no thicker than a hair. It tests the strand by leaning on it and listening. It does not look up when you arrive. It has never once looked up. When it has taken up the slack it cuts, splices, seals the ends with resin, strikes a spark to cure it, and puts the spark out with its thumb.

The crabs have thumbs. This is unremarkable until you have seen the wall.

There are a great many crabs and there is only one circuit. It runs the lashings of the whole court in a fixed order and it takes eleven years to complete, and the crabs are spaced along it evenly, so that from any knot's point of view a crab arrives, does the work, and leaves, and then eleven years of nothing. From the circuit's point of view there is no arrival and no leaving. There is a single animal, eleven years long, moving through the grove at the speed of a good joiner, and the bodies are where it happens to be touching. When one dies it is eaten and its flakes go back to the rack in the corner by the stair and are sorted by size and picked up again. The circuit does not notice. It has never lost a tool.

They come three meters in under a minute when your sweat hits the floor. That is a sprint for them; you can hear the small panic of it, the dry scramble, and it is the only urgency in the place. They seal the blanched leaf. They seal the hole in the wax film. They do not clean up the sweat, they cannot, they have no apparatus for water — they simply close the wound around it and let it go where it goes. Then they withdraw and resume the walk.

They are not curious about you. They are not frightened of you. You are, to them, a source of defects, and the appropriate response to a source of defects is to stand downstream of it and mend.

## The Ropemakers

The caterpillars are pale and slow and smell like the inside of a bread bin. They eat wax and spin strand, ten thousand strands to a cable, laid up by hand — they have no hands; the phrase is the court's, not yours, and you will find yourself using it anyway. They work to a plan that exists nowhere except in the wax they eat. Nobody drew it. Nobody holds it. It is passed mouth to mouth down the grove, and it is old, and it is correct, and when a cable is being unmade at the far end the caterpillars there eat the old rind and learn the whole thing again in reverse.

The moths are how the plan gets across the room.

The adults do not feed. They load themselves with wax at the north standards and they carry it south, and the migration takes ten years, and it is two-thirds done: a continent of oiled-paper wings shifting a hand's width a month, three deep in every interstice, lifting and resettling without any sound at all. When you part the leaves the air simply reorganizes around your arm. There is no flutter. It is like walking through a crowd that has agreed not to be startled.

A thought takes ten years to cross this room. The day is eighty years long. The migration is a syllable in it, and the moths are the syllable, and what they are saying, over and over, at the speed of the light changing, is the plan.

They have begun to resettle nearer you than they used to. Not on you. Near. A hand's-breadth off your shoulder, in a shape that is not yet a shape.

## The Sweeteners

Under the floor the oil is warm and dark and moves. The eels filter it. They are blind and pale and unhurried and they are longer than the court is wide, which you learn by watching one pass the grate and then watching it pass again, and understanding that you have not seen two.

They are the only residents who go all the way around. Everything else is local — the crabs to their circuit, the mice to their cables, the caterpillars to their branch — and so the eels are the only ones who know the actual size of the place, and they are not telling, and their measure does not agree with your paces. Ninety across. Two hundred and eleven around. The far wall does not come closer; it becomes the far wall you are standing at. The eels swim through this without difficulty. They have never had to arrive anywhere.

They keep the oil sweet. Sweetness here is not a flavor, it is a tolerance: the burn must run at the temperature of a body, and oil that has gone sharp will run hot, and a hot leaf scorches, and a scorched leaf is a gap, and there are no gaps in the Wick Court. So the eels take out whatever would make it hot. They have been doing this for a very long time. The oil has never once gone off.

When the grate is lifted the sump reflects the court perfectly, with one extra standard in it, and the eels pass beneath the extra without altering their course, which is how you know the extra is not an error in the reflection. It is a thing that is there. They have been swimming under it since before you came down the stairs.

## The Primers, and the Reserve

A newly laid cable does not drink. Capillarity will not start in a dry braid; the oil has to be carried up the first few meters by something that is already wet with it. That is what the mice are for. Their fur is a wick. They do not drink — nothing here drinks — they absorb, and they are always faintly greasy and always warm, and they run a new cable end to end until the siphon takes, and then they leave it and go and find another.

They are the reason a standard is alive within a season instead of a century. They are also, if you want to know the mechanism, the reason it would not hurt.

There is a mouse sitting on the floor near your boot at a distance of exactly one body-length, which is as close as it can come to you without touching the wet thing you keep leaving on the basalt. It is not afraid. It is waiting for a condition to change. It has waited out longer conditions than you.

Above the withy net, the lattice-fowl sleep on the wing. Hollow-boned, oil-filled, drifting in the warm column that comes off the whole grove, they have not landed in living memory, and living memory here is a technical term with a length attached. They are the reserve. They are the court's unspent fat, held aloft, out of the circuit, unattached to anything, and if a cable goes and the sump fails and the burn stumbles they will come down and be spent. Nothing has failed in living memory. That is what the not-landing means. It means the court has been solvent for a very long time.

In the work corner by the stair there is a rack of bone wedges, graded by size, and most of them are small and hollow and clearly bird.

Some of them are long.

## The Thumb

Every brick carries a thumbprint. Pressed soft, fired hard. Billions of them, all the same thumb.

Do the arithmetic and you get a number of hours that no body has. Unless the body had a great many afternoons, and the afternoons here are eighty years, and the walls are twelve meters deep and go further in than they go across. He had time. He is not a mystery in that sense. He is a quantity of labor.

The court does not erode, but it keeps portraits. This is the exception, and it is exact: the only wear permitted anywhere in the Wick Court is wear that records a body. The bricks record a thumb. The tool handles in the corner are not worn — they were *finished* to a grip, deliberately, the way you'd cast a key, and the grip is on file, and when you pick one up your fingers go into the grooves without being told, because the court has been holding that shape open for a long time and your hand is nearly it.

And along the floor by the eastern standards, and up the wall beside them at the height of a shoulder, there is a scatter of small white beads in the shape of someone who stood leaning there for a long time, sweating.

Each bead is a sealed wound. The crabs made them one at a time as the drops fell, over years, and they sealed each one and left the arrangement alone. They do not correct arrangements. They are menders, not editors. So the shape has stayed: a man's shoulder, a man's forearm, the place where his weight went into the brick while the water went out of him.

That is the monument. It is a portrait made of harm, and it is the same person as the thumb, and the reason the tool handles fit you nearly is that he was a visitor too.

## The Measure

Look at how the place is described. Ninety paces by sixty. A hand's-breadth of branch. Four hundred leaves in it. A finger's width of slack per century. A hand's span above the floor. Shoulder height. Knuckle. Sternum. Thumb.

The Wick Court has no units of its own. Every dimension in it is a piece of a body, and the bodies are the one component with a service life.

And the court is contracting — a finger's width per century, the cables taking up, the whole grove very slowly closing its fist — and a court measured in thumbs cannot permit its thumb to shrink. So every so often the standard has to be taken again, against something living, because a reach is only a reach while something is reaching.

That is what you are. Not food, not an intruder, not a guest. A prototype. The court keeps one, the way a vault in a city keeps a bar of metal, and when the bar has drifted they do not argue about it, they go and get another.

They have begun measuring your hands while you sleep. Two crabs at the wrist, pacing the span, testing the grip, clicking; a third at the elbow, establishing the reach. Above you a cable is being laid up to your working length. The anchor socket has already been cut. It was cut before they finished measuring, which tells you the tolerance they work to, and also that they have done this before, and also that they are not guessing.

## The One Who Drinks

The burn should make water and makes none. Something north of the seam is taking the missing term.

You can feel it if you stand still: a patient thirst with a shape. Not a hole, not a drain — an appetite with a posture, the way a room has a shape when someone is sitting in it. At the north wall two courses of brick meet that were pressed by the same thumb at different times, and they do not quite agree about what they are joined to, and through the disagreement, sidelong, there is the back of a head.

It does not turn. It is waiting for you to finish looking.

Consider the possibility that everything down here — the eels sweetening, the mice priming, the moths carrying the plan a hand's width a month, the whole slow lamp of it — exists to deliver, precisely and continuously, the one substance it has otherwise excluded, to one place, where it is spent entirely on keeping something wet. Consider that the court is not a place with a resident but an organ with a body attached, and the body is north of the seam, and it has been kept alive at the temperature of itself for longer than there have been words for how long. Consider that it leaned at the eastern wall until it could not, and that the crabs sealed every drop it lost, and that it went through the seam, and that the thumb in the bricks is its thumb, and that it is not permitted back in, because it would be weather.

Or consider that it is simply the next one after you, waiting, patient, the way the mouse is patient, at exactly one body-length.

The head does not turn. Looking sidelong is the only way to see it, and looking sidelong is what you do when you have already decided not to look properly, and it knows this, and it is content to wait until you are done.

## The Last Resident

There is more than one of everything down here and only one of each. One crab, eleven years long. One thought, ten years wide. One eel that is longer than the room. One standard that you pass three times — sapling, fruiting, grey and being taken apart — because the court does not have room for three, and does not need three, and has never had three. One thumb, billions of times.

And one measure, always, currently wearing out.

They are not cruel. It is important to be accurate about this. There is no appetite in the Wick Court except the one to the north, and no malice anywhere, and nothing that wants you to suffer, and nothing that would even know how to check. What there is instead is competence — an enormous, distributed, patient competence, eleven years between visits, a hand's width a month, a drop at a time, fat for water, warm the whole while, useful the whole while. The mice will come and sit on you and be warm and start the siphon. The crabs will seal what blanches. The moths will settle at a hand's breadth from your shoulders and then at a finger's and then on them, like a coat being put on by someone standing behind you who has done this many times and does not want to jostle you.

Behind you, in the packed green dark, a knot is being tied. Every lash here is a knot with a name, and standing in the grove you find you almost know the vocabulary, and you will know it by the end, because it is a vocabulary of lengths and grips and reaches and it is being written against your body as the reference.

The knot they are tying is the one that means *this far*.

They are tying it to your reach.
