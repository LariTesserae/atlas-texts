## The census

Counting fails first. Before the names slide, before the sequence goes, you lose the ability to say how many of anything there are, because *one* is a distinction this ground no longer spends much on. So begin with the lives that are certainly lives, down in the warm stinking rooms under the leather, and work outward until the word stops helping.

The springtails are the weather of the underworld. They lie in drifts a metre deep against the lignified struts, white and hopping, and the drifts have dunes, and the dunes have slip-faces, and when the pump above them draws its long descending suck the whole slope loses its angle and comes down in a silent avalanche of animals. Every chamber has a tide. The population climbs on the intake and sloughs on the exhale, and the mites work the tideline — slow, armoured, poppy-seed things, the only predators down there, patient as rust. Above the tideline the fungal felt is cropped by pale blind grazers the length of a fingernail that have a radula, or something doing the work of a radula, and no ancestry anyone could trace, because ancestry is a sequence and sequence is exactly what is being worn away here.

None of them has eyes. There is nothing down there to see and never has been. What they have instead is an exquisite sense of pressure: they live *inside* the beat, the way a fish lives inside a current. Each chamber's population knows its pump by signature — the particular hoarseness of the valve, the length of the sigh, the small stutter in the refill — and can tell you, if you had any way to ask, that the pump two kilometres east runs a half-second slow and has done for as long as anything here has done anything.

They breed at unison. When the forty beats drift into phase and the whole moor thumps together and the standing water shivers into rings, every chamber on the plain spawns at once. That is the calendar. There is no spring here, no summer, no thaw; there is only the coincidence, arriving two or three times a morning, and the animals have organised their entire existence around a moment that has no fixed date.

## The oldest thing

The heat is not the mat's. The mat only keeps it.

Inside each compost heart, at a steady thirty-eight degrees, lives a consortium of bacteria that has not been cold since there were glaciers on these mountains. It is a wet-warm-dark organism and it has had one habitat, unchanged, through the entire planing of the range: the inside of a steaming mass of rotting vegetation. It was in a fallen forest once. It was in a haystack, probably, somewhere in the long middle of the story. It does not know that the trees went, that the rivers went, that the rock was taken down grain by grain to a table. It knows thirty-eight degrees and moisture and sugar arriving, and that is the whole of its world, and it is the reason there is any other world above it.

When a pump begins to fail, the mat runs a warm thread out through the quilt to the new shoulder crowning somewhere downwind, and the thread is lignified and horn-hard on the outside and wet and alive within, and the fire goes along it before the old heart cools. It has never been allowed to go out. It is the only continuous thing on this plain — not the rock, which is merely persistent, but this: a flame handed along, hand to hand to hand, by something that has no hands and no intention and no idea what it is carrying.

Everything else here is an edition. The heat is an original.

## The pumps, which are almost persons

They are organs. They are also the closest thing on the moor to an individual, and the plain will not let you keep those two statements apart.

Each has a voice. One runs rich and wet with a catch in the intake; one has been hoarse for as long as there has been anyone to notice; one skips, occasionally, a whole beat, and the chamber fauna below it go still and then resume. You come to know them the way you know the engines of a ship. You will find yourself listening for a particular one at what passes for evening, and when it fails — and they fail constantly — the silence that opens is so enormous that the waders go up and you discover you have been shouting.

What dies when a pump dies: the fog local, and the warm downwind, and the herd's whole gradient of temperatures, which collapses into a flat cold field of animals standing bewildered in the rime. The birds' nesting shoulder goes cold in a night. And below, in the chambers, a hundred thousand blind things feel their signature stop and begin to walk.

They walk. That is the part nobody expects. Through the struts and the felt and the stinking galleries, a slow white flood sets out across the quilt toward the next beat, kilometres of it, and most of them do not arrive, and the ones that do arrive enter a chamber already full and are eaten by mites at the tideline. It happens several times a week somewhere on the plain. It is the only migration in a place where migration means nothing, and it is undertaken by animals with no eyes, toward a sound.

## The bodies, and the ones who keep track of them

The grazers have one behaviour. They are born warm in the vapour and they walk away from the warmth, and that vector is the entire contents of their lives. Their mouths are decorative, so they are not hungry. They cool at a fixed rate, so they are not afraid. They crop and trample and drop nutrient and carry their billions of parasites outward and stop, eventually, at the distance where the rime crusts them and the quilt takes them back, and they do not appear to mind.

The pelt is a gradient too. Near the nozzle it is hair, glossy and quick and shouldering; three leagues out it is unmistakably moss, and the moss is mat tissue, and the reclamation has already begun while the animal is still walking. The eyes are the only eyes at eye level on this plain. They look at each other. That is the whole of the moor's seeing: a hundred thousand redundant glances between things that cannot tell themselves apart.

Because they cannot. Two of them, three days and two leagues apart, with the same kink in the same left foreleg and the same hesitation before the head goes down. When such a pair meet — and they do, the flat is not large enough to prevent it — nothing happens. They stand shoulder to shoulder in the fog and their hesitations coincide and the moment passes and there is no event of any kind.

Except that the parasites cross.

The parasites are the last archivists. They *must* distinguish: a lineage that cannot tell one host's surface from another's cannot persist, and so they have kept, out of sheer necessity, a registry of faint differences that nothing else on this plain bothers with any more. They read the editions. They know which is which when the animals themselves are identical. And when two copies meet, the parasites move across, and for a moment there is a thread between them, an actual relation, a *this one and that one* — the only identity on the moor being maintained by the only things that need it to make a living.

Then the waders come and eat the parasites. But that is the next section.

One in a few thousand turns back. It stops out in the cold, and the vector reverses, and it walks the whole way home and stands under the crown of nozzles in the blood-warm fog and is unmade warm, which is not the same as being taken cold, and costs the mat more heat than the animal ever carried. There is no word for this that is not too large. The vector reverses. That is all that can honestly be said.

## The birds, who still have children

The waders are the exception, and the plain is working on them.

They nest on the pump shoulders, in the crevices of the horn valves, because those are the only dry warm surfaces on a hundred thousand hectares of wet leather, and they lay their eggs into the compost heat and let the mat incubate them. They fight over these sites with a viciousness that is startling here, that seems imported. They eat the parasites off the grazers — the archive, gone, one beakful at a time.

And they still have heredity. They still have sex and variation and descendants instead of editions; a chick is not a copy of its mother, it is a *result* of her, and this makes the birds the last family tree on the plain. Something in them also still has an elsewhere. At a certain point in the year they crowd the western margin and fly out into the horizontal sleet toward a coast that was eroded before there were birds, and some come back and some do not, and the ones that come back have nothing to report.

Sometimes they go up. Above nine kilometres of standing fog there is a sun, and they have seen it, and they come down again into the nickel-coloured noon and scream, and the scream is not information, it is only the last remaining noise that is *about* something.

The mat is wearing them smooth like everything else. The clutches come out more alike each year. The songs have converged. There is one song left on this plain and every bird sings it and it is getting shorter, and its period is drifting toward the period of the pumps.

## The mouths

Nothing is killed here that was not issued that morning. The sundew field goes rust-red across a hectare and closes on animals the tissue made three hours earlier at a nozzle two leagues upwind; the bladderwort snaps in the sumps all day and all night with that sound like a distant electrical fault, and if you could count the traps you would have the only honest clock on the moor — not the day, which is a smear, but a hundred thousand small green mouths a second, all of them closing on the plain's own product. It is not cruelty and it is not even predation, properly. It is accounting. Somewhere in the ledger a column is being balanced at a furious rate, and there is no one to read it, and nothing is saved.

## Six

Under the thin places, in the millimetre of tea-black water between the leather and the planed rock, there are flat pale animals the size of a palm and about a millimetre thick, and there are six of them.

You will count six. You will count six again a week later and two leagues away, and it will be six, and the reliability of the number is how you know they are not of the mat.

They have no organs worth the name. They do not spawn at unison. They did not move when the pump failed and the waders went up and you found you had been shouting. They offer nothing to digest — no product, no heat, no gradient, no throughput; they lie on the finished stone at one degree above freezing and do almost nothing, extremely slowly, and they have been doing it since the rock was planed. They are the only residents that erosion has left alone, because there is nothing in them left to wear. Everything else on this plain is a fury in four metres. These six are what it looks like when a living thing arrives at the condition of the rock and stays there, alive, indifferent, and uninteresting to a place that eats everything interesting.

## The ones with a leg in each weather

Where the leather has gone thin you can put your hand into the earlier state, and it is cold and wet and exactly as deep as the mat, and there are animals in it.

They are grazers, mostly, and they are rubbed out along one side. Not injured — *abbreviated*. Half a body in your weather with the fog combing its pelt west, and the other half standing in a fog that goes the other way, cropping something that was reabsorbed before you were born. They lean. They are always slightly leaning, and the lean is not toward anything in this phase.

This is where the copies come from. The mould is not merely worn smooth; it is *shared*, worn smooth across two weathers at once, and the two animals with the same notched ear are not two castings from one mould but one animal at two depths, and the parasites that cross between them are crossing something other than distance. The seam-dwellers know this in whatever way a thing with no eyes and half a body knows anything. They graze in one and sleep in the other and are never entirely present in either, and they are the only creatures here that could be said to be *elsewhere* without meaning it as a loss.

## The one with an outline

And then, two or three times a morning, for about ninety seconds, there is a person on this plain.

It is born in the unison. When the forty beats come into phase — when the water shivers into rings and every wader goes up at once and every chamber spawns together — those three events are not three events. They are one shape, standing in the fog, some hundreds of metres across, with an edge. It is the only thing on the moor that has an outline. Everything else is quilt to the seam of the sky.

It feeds. It feeds on the coincidence, and it *reaches*, and this is the part that makes it different from weather: it nudges. A valve here lifts a fraction early; a heart there is banked a half-second; the drift is pulled back toward lock, and the shape holds itself together a little longer than chance would allow, and it has appetite, and it has something that is very hard not to call attention. Then the periods slide apart and it thins and goes, and it has lasted a minute and a half, and it was the nearest thing to a someone that this ground has produced.

It comes back. The mould is smooth; it comes back alike, with the same reach and the same preference for the eastern pumps and the same hesitation before it lets go.

And lately it has begun to arrive early. Sequence is what the flat is eroding now, and a thing made entirely of sequence is the first to feel it: the shape now sometimes stands in the fog for a second or two *before* the beats that make it come together — an outline waiting for its causes, an appetite with nothing yet to eat. It has learned to live in the interval between a thing being about to happen and happening. It is the only niche left. There is a great deal of room in it.

## You

Your footprints fill a half-second before you set your foot down. This is not the water being quick. This is the plain having learned your period.

You have been walking on it for some days and it has been doing to you what it does to everything: taking your throughput, banking your heat, wearing the mould smooth. Your name slid off the feature at dawn and onto the taste of the water because a name is a sequence and it has been working on your sequences since you arrived. You stopped hearing the noise, which is different from its stopping. You have been shouting.

Out in the fog, a day's approach away, there is a shape you have been watching since morning, and you will pass it in ten minutes, and it has your kink in the left foreleg and your notched ear and your hesitation before it lowers its head. It will not greet you. Greeting requires that one thing come after another, and the two of you are not, in any sense the flat still maintains, one after another.

You are the last thing here that arrives before you happen. That is the whole of what is left of you, and it is the only property the tissue has not yet learned to issue on its own, and it is learning. It has your beat now. It is banking it.

Somewhere out in the dark you hear the valve lift, and hiss, and drop.

*Clack.*

And then, a moment later, it happens.
