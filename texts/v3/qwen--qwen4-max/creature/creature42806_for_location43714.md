Begin by lowering the rate. Nothing in the bowl keeps time the way you do, and if you look for residents at your own speed you will find an empty room and say so and climb out. Look slower. What lives here is a set of things that hold their shape while their substance passes through them — which is also your arrangement, only you do it in seconds and are therefore mistaken for an object.

**The drip** is the shortest-lived individual in the place, and there is always exactly one. For ten of its eleven seconds it is not falling at all; it is a bead on the lip four meters up, swelling, gathering itself out of the rock's slow bleed. Then it lets go and takes most of a second to cross the air and dies into the basin. It has no successor, only a replacement. Its whole biography is a weight increasing until it cannot be held. Everything it does it does once, and the doing has worn a hollow in the floor the size of two cupped hands, which is to say the drip has spent centuries digging the exact shape of its own reception. It is the only thing here with a gait. Stand still and you will find your breathing falling into step with it, four or five breaths to one fall, and you will understand that you have not found a clock. You have found a pulse, and it is not yours.

**The film** is the largest resident by area and the thinnest by depth, and it is the only one that touches everything. Twelve stripes, lip to floor, each a hand's breadth or wider, all of them the same thickness at every hour of every year. It has no direction and no front. It does not grow and it does not shrink; it is the exact surface where the stone becomes the air, held at that boundary so precisely that it never commits to either. Wipe it and it returns while you watch, not eagerly, not reluctantly, at the rate the rock can supply. It is the bowl's skin, and it is a skin turned outside in, and it is also the stone quietly leaving, one molecule at a time, at a pace that has not varied since the roof settled. Every stripe is a wound that never scabs and never bleeds out. Put your palm on one and you cannot tell which of you is warming the other. The film can tell. It takes your sweat and your heat and files them, and within a minute the place where you touched is indistinguishable from the rest, and this is not erasure, it is the only thing the film knows how to do.

**The moss** is the only true life, and it lives here on a technicality. The black-green cushions along the seep stripes do not love copper. They endure it, which is a different and lonelier talent, and what they actually require is the emptiness the copper makes: no competitor can germinate on this floor, so the moss has the whole bowl to itself and no use for most of it. Each cushion is a crowd of thousands of stems the height of a fingernail, packed so tight they hold grit and make the only shade in the place that is not the overhang's. Ten paces of killing ground lie between one cushion and the next, and no cushion has ever crossed it.

Consider what the moss has been given. Mosses cannot mate dry; their sperm must swim, and elsewhere they wait weeks for rain and get an afternoon. Here the water film never leaves. The moss is permanently in the condition other mosses pray for — ready, always, at every hour, for a hundred generations — and there is nowhere for the result to go. Its spores fall into air that does not move and land within a pace of the parent, on poison, and do not come up. It has been fertile without interruption since before anyone cut the steps, and every one of its children has died at its feet.

And it never dries. The thing this moss is best at — going brittle and grey and apparently dead, and coming back green in an hour when water returns — it has never once had to do. Its great talent is unemployed. It sits in the warm dim in a state of permanent sufficiency, growing at exactly the rate the copper kills it back, hand-sized, hand-sized, hand-sized.

**The four rosettes** are spaced, not scattered; each holds a pocket of grit and a radius around it, and the ten paces between them is a border enforced by chemistry. Grey fleshy tongues, thick with stored water in a place where water is not the scarcity. They are built for a desert's schedule: shut by day, open by night, breathe when it is cool. But there is no day here and no night, no cooling, no cue of any kind. So they keep a day of their own manufacture. Somewhere in those leaves an oscillator is running free, opening and closing on a cycle slightly too long, drifting a little later each turn, so that after enough decades its private noon has walked all the way around the clock and come back and gone around again. It is the only thing in the bowl with a calendar. The calendar corresponds to nothing. It has never once been wrong, because there is nothing here for it to be wrong about.

**The fern** in the south crack has six fronds and all six are old. Ferns replace themselves; this one has stopped. Under each frond the spore lines are rust-brown and full and have been full for a long time, and what is released drops straight down onto the copper and does not come up. It is not growing and it is not dying. It is holding what it has, the way a man holds a hand of cards he has decided not to play.

**The tree** at the lip is the only immigrant, and it never arrived. Most of its length is root, coming down the wall in a fan, exposed, gripping, reaching for the damp stripes; the trunk is a stub, an afterthought, the small part of it that stands in the weather. It has spent its whole life converting itself into reach. Its rings are thin enough to need a lens. Once in a long while it lets a leaf go, and the leaf falls into the bowl, and this is the only delivery the bowl receives from the outside world.

**Three dead leaves** lie in a crack on the floor, and they are the population of the dead: three, maintained. Nothing eats them. There is no rot here, no fungus, no bacteria willing to work in this water, so they are not being taken apart into a thousand things and carried off into the ground. They are being coated. Mineral by mineral they are becoming one thing, slowly, themselves, harder — a hundred years to pass from leaf to stone without ever passing through soil. They will be the floor. They are the only remains in the bowl and they are also its furniture, and the tree will send down three more when these are done, and no one will notice the exchange.

**The pool** has no inhabitants and this is its health. Its clarity is a symptom. Nothing in it, nothing on it, nothing over it; you can see the bottom because there is nothing to see. The one thing it makes is its own edge — a fingernail-wide crust of mineral sitting exactly at the waterline, dissolving inward at the rate it builds outward, a shell grown precisely to the size of the body it holds and never a fraction larger. It is the pool's only offspring and it is the pool's own outline.

**The ticking** in the seep stripes you will not locate. It is irregular, which makes it the only aperiodic thing in the place, and therefore the only thing you could honestly call an event. A grain letting go. A crystal taking its position. A moss stem adjusting. The crust building somewhere at a rate that occasionally becomes audible. It is the sound of the balance being kept, and it does not repeat, and it is the closest you will come to meeting anyone here who is not simply holding a shape.

And **the ones who scraped**. They are the most thorough inhabitants the bowl has had, and they exist now entirely as negative space: nine steps dished at their centers by feet that all landed in the same place because everyone knew the way; a niche above the pool polished at the sides where wrists passed and at the underside of the lip where fingers curled to take weight; thousands of crescents along the green vein, overlapping, the oldest nearly gone, the newest nearly gone too.

Look at the height of the scraping. It stops where a standing person's reach stops. Above that the vein goes on, rich and untouched, up under the overhang where the light is best and no arm goes. They did not necessarily die out or fall under a prohibition. They may simply have taken everything within reach, season after season, until the green remaining was the green they could not get to, and then stopped coming, because the place was finished. The taking found its angle of repose, the same way the walls did.

The color went elsewhere. Ground and carried out and put onto eyelids, onto pots, onto the prows of boats, onto the faces of the dead — the same chemistry that keeps this floor bare, spread thin across the world to keep other things from spoiling. Most of the bowl's population is abroad and does not know it. What stayed behind is the shape of the taking, and one fallen stone that nobody set upright again, which tells you only that no one has cared since.

So the resident is the arrangement itself: pulse eleven seconds, skin twelve stripes, a mouth on the eastern side that nothing enters, a stomach off-center west that digests nothing, four private calendars, one fertility with no issue, three dead becoming rock. It is not asleep and it is not waiting. Everything in it is a process that has learned to hold still.

You are the change. Your handprint is gone before you have taken your hand away. But down on the floor your feet have pressed the sharp grit into two shapes that were not there this morning, and there is no wind to smooth them and no animal to cross them, and they will stay — a year, ten years, until the wall sheds a grain into the heel of one. Newest and softest of the marks, going the way of the others.

Then the bowl will be exactly as it was.
