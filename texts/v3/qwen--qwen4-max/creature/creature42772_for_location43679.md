## I. The print

The first inhabitant you can prove is a thumbprint.

It is at eye height, red-brown, a centimeter and a half across, pressed into the milk-blue skin of a rib with the whorls still legible. It has been there perhaps a season. Next year the rib will have thickened over it and it will be a shadow inside the opal, and then it will be nothing, and someone will have pressed it again.

Understand what the print is for. In a world where a hand's-breadth and four kilometers have the same value, a thumb is a *known width*. If the print looks like a thumb, it is arm's length away. If it looks like a lentil, it is thirty paces. If it looks like a speck, do not step. The arrow of dots along the cable is a ruler laid into the void, foreshortening tells you the angle, and the flat hand at the junction — five fingers, a bigger standard — is for reading across a gulf.

The ochre network is not decoration and not signage. It is a survey. It is the only depth in the Under, imported from the floor at enormous cost and applied to the world in thumb-sized increments. Their word for *lost* and their word for *unmarked* are the same hand-shape.

So the people are, first, the things that make the world measurable. Call them the Askers; that is roughly what their name-sign means, two fingers laid on a stem and lifted. It is not a spoken name. Nothing here is.

## II. The body that fits

They are small and thick and slow, big-handed, with the barrel chests of people who have never once been warm. They wear felted pith in five layers, white, hooded, and their boots are felt, and their knees are wrapped, because the gravel is razor work.

Their mittens have the first two fingers cut away. Those fingers are always bare, always cold, always stained red-brown to the second knuckle, and they are the organ by which the Under is known: laid on a stem, they read slack from taut, the difference between a rope that will hold and a rope that is about to inhale. Every child learns this before it learns to walk. Elders' reading-fingers are scarred white and often short a joint.

Their front teeth are notched. A lifetime of the navigation click — soft, dull, pitched low, the only sound permitted at range — wears a groove in the incisors, and you can tell a corpse's trade from its mouth. The click is also the voice. Everyone's is different: everyone's is a name. To imitate another's click is the crime that has no forgiveness, because it is how you call someone off a sill.

Their eyes are hooded and heavily lashed and permanently narrowed, and by sixty most of them are blind — the ache takes everyone eventually, the snow-blindness accumulating like silt in a place with no silt. This is not the tragedy it would be elsewhere. Sight is nearly useless here. The blind are the finest navigators alive; they have no bad habits to unlearn. *He still looks* is what they say of a fool. When an old woman's corneas go milky and she becomes, at last, the exact color of the world, they say she has opened.

They walk with a rolling foot, heel to toe, no attack, no edge. They do not point — pointing means nothing in a whiteout — and they do not wave. They greet by laying two fingers on your sternum and waiting until you feel it.

And once in a while, at a bad junction, at a birth, at a death, one of them will take a pinch of ochre from a horn flask and blow it into the air.

For about ten seconds the red dust hangs and settles, and the near members go speckled and the far ones stay clean, and the world *snaps into distance* like a bone going back into its socket. They call it casting the red. It costs a month of somebody's descent. It is the most expensive thing they do and they do it with the flick of a wrist, and afterward everyone stands very still and looks, and nobody says anything, because there is nothing to say and no way to say it.

## III. The hush

A crying infant can start a shatter front.

This is the first fact of their domestic life and it shapes everything. Babies are carried in a pith muffle against the breast, hooded, sound-dead, the mother's own chest the last layer of damping. They are never permitted to cry aloud. Not once, not ever, and the tenderness with which this is enforced is total and constant and would look, to anyone from outside, like something unspeakable.

Their sign for *love* is the sign for *muffle*.

The lullaby is a hum conducted through the sternum, below hearing, felt in the infant's teeth. The blind grandmothers hold the babies all day, rocking, humming into them, and the babies grow up with a memory older than language of being held against something that was vibrating.

Some infants cannot be hushed. Every band has one or two. They are taken down.

## IV. What they own

Almost nothing, and all of it grown.

A blade is asked for: a wound in a cambium sleeve, a leaf-mold pressed in, and the plant lays opal into the shape you requested. It comes out sharp and is finished with three careful strikes under a felt hammer, conchoidal white blooming in silence. Blades are resharpened for generations. An old family's knife is a thumbnail of glass in a tendon haft, worn down from a hand's length by two hundred years of honing, and it is the most valuable object in the band, and it will be a stub, and then a sliver, and then a memory with a name.

The camps are hung and cut free every year or the rib swallows them. Most of the "ruins" are absorbed rooms — a doorway gone to solid opal, a platform become a lump with a hollow in it, and sometimes, in the hollow, a tendon cradle and a stone cup and the ochre handprint of somebody who did not cut free in time.

They have no permanent settlements. They have permanent *stains*.

Because a thumbprint is eaten by the rib in a season, the survey must be continually re-pressed, and so there are mark-keepers who walk circuits their whole lives renewing the prints of the dead. At the great junctions, where four routes meet, the same thumb-sized patch has been pressed by ten thousand thumbs over eight hundred years, and the ochre there is a door-sized smear, thick as a fingernail, layered like lacquer.

That is a town. It has a name, a market, a marriage-season, a reputation. It is two millimeters deep. Everything else about it must be carried in and carried out.

The only old things in the Under are shapes and habits.

## V. Kinship is a load path

You do not descend from a person. You descend from a wound.

A village is a set of scores made patiently in the right places sixty years ago, and the people who made them are in the walls, and the strut that came of it is *theirs*, and you are of it. *I am of the third cable of the Ash Fan* is a complete introduction: it says your grandmother's hands, your inheritance, your obligations, and where you may safely put your weight.

Marriage is a graft. Two families score a junction where their members run near each other, and wait two seasons, and if the graft takes and the load runs through it, they are married. If it fails, they are not, and nobody argues, because the argument would be with the fan.

Children are named for the tide they were born on. Ages are counted in darks.

The worst punishment is not death. It is to be *stilled*: hands bound, cord cut, click forbidden. Fed, led, warm, and outside language forever. There are three or four stilled people alive at any time on the whole continent, and they are cared for meticulously, and they are the loneliest things in the world.

## VI. The others

The grazer is palm-sized and six-limbed and rasps opal with a mineral radula, building its shell out of what it eats, so that its back is a miniature of the lattice — a tiny white fan, a thrust curve you could cover with a coin. It is the only native source of high-frequency sound. It is also the only other creature that reads slack: a grazer will not rasp a taut member, and the ones that misjudged are why the technique is teachable. People learned the two-finger craft by watching grazers live and grazers die. There is no word for this debt but there is a gesture, and it is used at graves.

The cistern flatworms are translucent and slow and are eaten alive, one at a time, as a medicine.

The white shrimp live in the ferment pockets, where sap pools and goes to alcohol, and the pockets are *farmed* — deliberately wounded, seeded, tended, harvested at the dark. The Under's only agriculture is a sour garden. The only drunkenness in the world happens in the polar night, in a hung camp, in absolute black, with everyone's hands on the cords.

The moths are heavy-bodied and furred and work the light columns, navigating by wells, and they congregate once, at a flowering, and that is the only crowd.

A flowering is a death. A rib empties itself into a panicle a kilometer long, loses stiffness, and comes apart, and the glass-heavy seeds fall for minutes. The kernels are starch. It is the only harvest worth the name, and it happens inside a collapse, and so there are people who run *toward* the blooms — front-walkers, who read the tracks and the delays and go in during the two-minute gap between the light and the sigh to glean seed out of a falling kilometer. They are young. They are not young for long. They are the closest thing to aristocracy the Askers have, and their families are identifiable by the pattern of small healed fractures in the forearms.

The frost gardens have their own people: tide-keepers, who build in rime, feathers and ferns and mimic-architecture that lives exactly one pressure tide. They are the opposition. Against the injury-architects, who cut for grandchildren, they make the tide-piece — an elaborate, useless, exquisite thing that will be gone on the slack. Their whole argument is that the long cut is a kind of cowardice, that a person who only builds for the unborn has never made anything.

And their highest art form, the ones who cut for grandchildren, is the *long cut*: a wound scored to a named shape by someone who will not live to see it, with the name recorded in the Reckoning so that when the strut arrives, forty years on, somebody will know whose it was.

## VII. The Reckoning

When the wells close and the world goes absolutely black, the bands sit on their platforms with their hands on the cords and rebuild the continent out of taps.

It is a poem and it is a survey. Distances, delays, load paths, the names of the dead, the amendments of the year — *the ninth cable of the Ash Fan is gone, the load has gone to the eleventh, the eleventh is thickening, do not trust it before the third dark*. It takes forty days. Children learn the Under as verse before they ever see it, and the meter is a delay-count, and a false line is not a lie but a murder with a long fuse.

This is their entire literature. It is a map that is also a history that is also a grief.

## VIII. The one who goes ahead

Three toes, four meters between prints, on the wide sills. Always along a load path. Always toward a bloom, and always *early*.

Nobody has seen it. What has been seen: a shed leg, found once, a hollow opal tube with wet pith inside and sap still under suction — a leg that was a stem, taut, holding a column of water at negative pressure, an ambulatory piece of the world. What is inferred: a body no bigger than a man, on legs that reach four meters, distributing its weight the way the lattice distributes load, so light on the ribs that it leaves prints only in condensate. What is agreed: it eats failure, it arrives before failure, its tracks are the only forecast anyone has, and you do not follow it unless you are a front-walker and prepared to die.

There is one credible sighting, from a woman who was under a well at the time and therefore had contrast. She reported not an animal but a geometry — a fan of white legs with a small dark knot at the hub, and the knot was a mouth, and it did not stop.

They think there are perhaps nine of them. They think they meet only at flowerings, in the falling glass, where the moths are. They think — and this is not said aloud, only tapped, and only in the dark — that the striders are not animals that live in the Under but organs the Under grew to walk to its own wounds.

## IX. The loud

Nine days down, through densening white, through lace and then marrow and then stone, and the roof of the world becomes the floor of it: black rock, wet, seeping, warm by comparison.

There is gas here. It comes out of the rock and it *burns*.

So there is fire in the Under, and it is all in one place, and the people of that place have kilns, and pots, and brick, and lamps, and soot, and voices. They shout. They drum on stone with stone. They sing in parts, badly and enormously, and their singing is made of everyone's exiled infants — the loud babies carried down and raised where noise is only noise. Every generation the Askers send their un-hushable children into the dark and every generation the below receives them and teaches them to sing, and neither people is entirely comfortable about the arrangement.

They mine the ochre. They fire it. They receive the dead. They are the only Askers who ever cast a shadow, and their word for *shadow* is their word for *soul*, and their word for the surface is a word that means, approximately, *the place where you have none*.

A pilgrim who goes down once — almost nobody goes twice — reports the same thing every time, and it is never the fire. It is that they looked at the rock and there was a black self-shaped thing lying on it, and it moved when they moved, and it was theirs, and they had gone forty years without one. Some of them cannot come back up. It is not the climb.

Soot goes up with the ochre. It is the only substance in the Under that eats light. A black mark on a rib is visible at forty kilometers — the single long-range signal in a world of near-field marks — and the Askers use it sparingly and hate it, because black is the only color that tells you the truth about distance, and they have built an entire civilization on not needing to know.

And in the polar night, when the wells are shut and the lattice is shattering invisibly in every quarter, there is sometimes a faint redness at the bottom of the world, thirty kilometers down, where the fires are.

The children are told it is the dead. The children are told correctly, in the sense that the dead are there, and so are the loud babies, and so is everything warm that this world could not afford to keep.

## X. What actually lives here

By surface area, one hundred percent of the Under is alive, and the people are a few thousand kilograms of it, hung in the crotch of a fan, wearing white.

But consider what they do. They score, and the plant answers. They wound where they want a strut and the load does the rest. For four thousand years the wounds have been in the same kinds of places, made by the same kinds of hands, and the plant has had four thousand years to notice that a particular kind of injury — small, shallow, deliberate, ochre-smeared — is followed by a load it can use.

It grows toward the red now. A thumbprint alone, no cut at all, will start a member in a good place on a rising tide. Nobody knows when this began. It is in the Reckoning as an amendment, somewhere in the middle, with no author.

So the marks are not a survey. Or they are a survey, and they are also the only sentence in a language with two speakers: one of them a lineage of glass that gave up light and has no eyes and solves for force, and one of them a small warm animal with two bare fingers that has appointed itself the organ of asking. The Under grew a hand. The hand learned to read slack, and to muffle its young, and to grieve a rib, and to press a print at eye height so that a stranger not yet born will know how far away the wall is.

Which of them is the inhabitant is not a question they would understand. They would think you were still looking.

Here is what you would see, if you were close enough to see anything: a hooded figure, white on white, kneeling on wrapped knees at a junction where three routes meet and a fourth is only a intention. She takes ochre from a horn flask. She presses her thumb against the rib — the same patch, the same door-sized smear, eight hundred years of thumbs — and then, a hand's width to the left, on empty air, at exactly eye height, she makes a second mark on nothing at all, on the place where the strut will be in thirty years, when the long cut her mother made finally arrives.

It hangs there, a red thumbprint on nothing, until the wind of the tide takes it.

She will not see the strut. Her daughter will press it.
