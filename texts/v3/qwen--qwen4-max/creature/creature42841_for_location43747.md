The nine are not the population. They are the largest of the residents and by far the fewest, and anyone trying to count the minds in the Kettle would give up long before arriving at them.

Start with the swifts, because the swifts are the census. Nine or ten thousand, depending on the year, and the number is not really a number, it is a weather. They nest in the solution pockets of the west wall, each nest a shallow bracket of saliva-glued chaff stuck to bare vertical rock, and the chaff they build with is stolen from the chaff-line — the one theft nobody minds, because the birds are only borrowing what the hole was holding anyway. A swift chick hatches on a wall. Its first act in the world is to fall. Nothing in its life afterwards is horizontal except by accident. They mate in the air, they drink by skimming the pool's face at a speed that leaves a scar on the water for half a second, and on the nights when the breath is strong they go up out of the mouth and sleep aloft in the cold pour, three hundred paces above the plateau, in the dark, with their wings still working.

So they are the only residents who know the whole volume. The people know the lip, the ladder, the Seam's sideways traverse, the floor. The bats know the fluting and the night column. The swifts know the shaft as a country with regions and seasons and roads: the dawn downdraught, which is a cold river down the north side and must be crossed diagonally; the noon wedge, which is where the insects are; the evening lift, which is an escalator and free. They have no words for any of it. They have routes.

There is one bird the tally-keeper has been recording for six summers, a swift with two primaries missing from the left wing, which no longer hunts the fast high drift but works the chaff-line all day at a slow tilt, taking midge bodies out of the suspension one at a time like a woman picking lentils. It circles with the catkin. In the tallies it has a mark of its own, a scratch with a nick in it, and the mark has been made six times, and nobody knows what will be done with the mark when it stops.

---

The whistlers live on the rim turf in a town of slab-roofed burrows, and they are the only creatures here with two skies.

Most of them never cross the mouth. They keep to the turf and the lichen and the brown speckling of fallen insects on the snow, and they post sentries, and the sentries whistle — one sharp note every few seconds, which is not fear but a kind of accountancy: *I have seen you, I am still seeing you, the situation is unchanged.* A traveller crossing the plateau hears the notes hand themselves along the rim ahead of him, burrow to burrow, and understands that he is being described.

But one lineage goes down. It has always gone down, for the rowan. There is a route — nine ledges, the third moss-beard, a diagonal across the Seam where the weeping is thinnest — and it is taught, and it is taught badly and correctly, the way routes are taught, by an aunt who is impatient and a juvenile who is nearly killed once. They come into the floor's warmth in the late afternoon and strip the rowan before the berries redden, and they carry the plateau's news down and the floor's news up in their bodies, in their dung, in the seeds caught in their fur. Everything else in the Kettle is either above or below. The whistlers are the seam between the two worlds, and they are the reason the rowan is not simply a tree but a negotiation.

The bats come out of the fluting at dusk in a column, and the sound is a thrown handful of gravel that goes on being thrown. They hang in the same scallops their grandmothers hung in; the rock behind each colony is stained and polished, a greasy darkness shaped like a crowd. The mothers know their own young by voice in a chorus of four hundred, which is a thing no person on the rim could do and no person on the rim has ever thought remarkable.

The bats' Kettle is not the people's Kettle. They see it by delay, and the far wall is far enough that its answer comes back a half-beat late, and to a young bat a late answer means a distant surface, and a distant surface across an opening means a room. There is a room behind the east wall that does not exist. Every generation some fraction of the young go hunting in it, and work it thoroughly, and come back thin, and the older bats let them.

---

The Seam has citizens too, and most of them are slow.

The hanging marsh is one animal, or behaves like one: a quaking mat of sphagnum that has been growing upward on its own dead for longer than the rope has existed, holding four paces of water in its body the way a held breath is held. Push a finger in to the knuckle and the mat trembles for two paces in every direction, and the trembling is the only conversation it has. Under it the liverworts lie in green plates like something spilled and set. The moss-beards, two paces long, turn over in the night downdraught and lie back down at the turning, twice a day, patiently, for centuries.

In the mat's wet pockets live the newts — black, orange-bellied, unhurried, cricket-hunting, four generations a decade. They have seen the sun for two hours a day for their entire lineage. Their ancestors arrived by falling. So did the crickets', and the crickets have stopped flying, since there is nowhere here that flying improves on walking, and their wings are two brown scales that do nothing.

The crickets are silent. Not mute — they have the apparatus, they rub, they are heard. But nothing they could say would carry through the seep-hiss and the fall-slap and the tearing fabric of ten thousand birds. So they court in the Quiet. Twice a day, at the turning, when the great bass note under everything cuts out and the Kettle becomes merely loud, the whole mat starts to tick and shrill at once, and every mating that will happen in the next twelve hours is arranged in three minutes, and then the note comes back and the marsh goes dumb again mid-sentence. The people time their arguments by the same window. They have never once mentioned the coincidence.

Down in the sump, where the pool's overflow goes and the light has never been, there are the blind loach: a hand's length, colourless, the eyes gone to two smudges under skin. They have no sky at all, not even an ellipse. Their world is pressure and the near field — the shudder of a midge hitting the surface, the shove of water entering the slot, the grain of the note coming through limestone into the bones of their heads. Which means that twice a day the Quiet arrives for them as an event of enormous violence, the fundamental stopping, the whole architecture of their sense going suddenly hollow, and they hold still in it, all of them, until it resumes. They are probably the oldest continuous thing in the hole. They may exist nowhere else in the world. Nobody has a word for them; the tally-keeper calls them the sump-fish and does not count them, because they cannot be counted, and the tallies are for what can be counted.

---

The eleven birches are siblings. One tree came in on the wind before there was a rope, and blew down, and threw up eleven leaders from the fallen trunk, and they are all still leaning out from that original prostration like people getting up out of a chair. Bark like wet paper, roots in ten paces of steaming bird dung, crowns bent permanently toward the noon wedge, because that is the only direction light has ever come from. They have no idea there is a plateau. Their whole sky is an ellipse, and their whole year is two hours wide, and they spend the other twenty-two doing the arithmetic of scarcity.

The rowan is older than the rope and has never had a child. Every berry it makes is taken green by the whistlers, and it has been robbed for two hundred years, and it goes on making berries, because a tree cannot learn. There is one seedling, three paces tall, in a whistler's cache on a ledge in the north wall, sixty paces up, where no person can go without a second rope nobody has ever thought worth making. It is the rowan's entire posterity. It is doing fine. It will never be a tree; the ledge has four fingers of soil. It will be a shrub for forty years and then it will die, and the whistlers will still be coming down.

And under all of it the floor is eating. Ten paces of guano in the deep corners, warm as a body, working: mites in numbers that have no useful name, springtails, fly larvae in a stratum each, the pale fungus that fruits only in autumn and only where a collapse block keeps the heat in. The people lower their dead into it in the Quiet, wrapped, without a cairn, because a cairn means *the hole is that way* and the dead already know. So the birches are made of insects that died four days' walk away and downhill, and of ten thousand birds' patience, and of every grandmother either household has ever had, and when the leaves come out in the warmth of the rot they are, in the plainest material sense, the family coming back up.

---

Then the nine.

Two households, and they are named for what they keep: the rope and the tallies. It is not a division of labour, it is a division of anxiety. The rope-household wakes thinking about the saddle in the lip, the sharp part below the grooves, the finger's width the rope must be shortened each generation; they are the ones with the ruined hands, the ones who can splice hair in the dark, the ones who go down first and come up last. The tally-household wakes thinking about number: birds, creels, mouths, the second column of marks that measures the rope's shortening and therefore, indirectly, the wearing away of the world.

They have two names each. The use-name is what you shout across the mouth — Ash, Second, Aunt, Small — a handle, worn smooth, belonging to everybody. The other name is given in the Quiet, said once into the chord, and belongs to the chord thereafter. It is not spoken again. Not by the mother who gave it, not by the child who owns it. When someone dies you do not say their name; you say the use-name, and the hole already has the rest. Children learn early that they have something down there, held, in the dark, in the sound, and that this is not frightening but simply where it lives, the way a spare rope lives in the niche.

One of the nine has been south. Four days downhill to the obscenity of trees, and back, in a bad year, for salt. She is the only person on the rim who has seen more than the rim, and it has made her poorer, not richer: she has a thing in her head that will not fit into any conversation available to her. She has tried. She has said *they go on* and been nodded at. Now she mostly does the creel-work and lets the others tell her what the world is.

And there is the copper needle, older than both households, eye mended twice with sinew, kept in a fold of felt, used for rope-work and for nothing else. It is the only resident that was not grown here, born here, blown here, or dropped here. It came up the mountain in a trade nobody remembers, and it has been in perhaps thirty pairs of hands, and every one of those hands is in the floor now, warming the birches, and the needle is still sharp. It has a use-name too. That is the joke, and it is a very old joke, and it is still funny in the Quiet.

---

Two more, and they are harder to call alive.

The cloud has an address. It comes home at dusk to the same forty paces of shaft and lies there, three thick, rising a little when the breath is strong, sinking when it weakens, and it is read every evening of every warm month by people who have never once been surprised by it and would be devastated if it failed to arrive. It has a name — not a chord-name, a use-name, the kind you'd give a cat or a difficult uncle. Children are told its height at their birth and remember it the way people elsewhere remember a frost or a comet. It is the household's one possession that cannot be lost, mended, sold, or eaten, and it is therefore the one they are least grateful for.

The stretched hide across the niche's mouth is the other. Nobody built it to sing. It hums when the breath is strong, usually somewhere above the fundamental, sometimes beating against it in a slow throb you feel in the teeth, and no one would remove it, and no one tunes it, and in winter when the ice plug closes the throat and the hide goes slack and dumb, the four who stay will tell you that this — not the cold, not the six weeks, not the missing birds — is the part they had not been warned about.

---

The largest resident is the note.

Its body is a hundred thousand cubic paces of air. It breathes twice a day and its breathing is the day. It has a throat, a sump, a mouth, a temperature, a mood, a fixed height where it condenses; it warms its own floor with its own digestion; it takes in the wind's insects at the top and passes them down through ten thousand birds and a mat of moss and a marsh and a pool and a slot, and everything alive in the Kettle is a thin working skin on the inside of it, a flora in a gut, living off what the great thing cannot use. It has been playing since before the species arrived. It shows no interest in stopping. It does not know it has a voice, in the way a throat does not know.

Which leaves the slot.

The rule is not a belief. Nobody on the rim holds a theory about what is down there; nobody has a shape for it, a size, a hunger, a face. The rule is a muscle. In the Quiet you may lean over the north lip of the floor and call down the slot, and you may hear the water still moving somewhere far below, and if the sound that comes back has the shape of an answer you close your mouth. That is all. Children are trained in it the way children elsewhere are trained near fire: an aunt's hand over the mouth, once, hard, and never again needed.

What the households actually keep, when you get them alone in the third minute of the Quiet with the crickets going and the hide humming, is not the fear of something that answers. It is the other thing, the one they will phrase as a shrug and a change of subject: that whatever is down there has been down there a very long time, longer than the rope, longer than the grooves in the saddle, and has never once come up, and has never once spoken first.

That is not the behaviour of a hungry thing. That is the behaviour of something careful. Something with a rule of its own. And if it has a rule, and the rule is the same rule, then the two households on the rim are not the only household in the Kettle, and the courtesy runs both ways, and has been running both ways for longer than anybody has been keeping count — which is why, in the six weeks of the ice, when the breath stops and the swifts are gone and the bats hang torpid in the fluting and the whole enormous instrument is silent enough to hear a name come up out of the rock, four people stay, and melt snow, and listen to the ice tick, and one of them is always the one who does not call.

Nobody says anything has ever answered. The eleven birches go on getting up out of their chair. The blind loach hold still through the hollow minutes and feel the world come back. The catkin circles at the height where lift equals the weight of small dead things, and the swift with two feathers missing picks along the line beside it, and the cloud comes to its address, and somewhere in the dark under the floor a name that was given away sixty years ago is still being kept.
