Here’s one way of describing what might live in the Deep Canning:

---

In the Deep Canning of B-73, nothing “lives” in the old sense of a creature with a heart and a mouth and a nervous system that worries.

What lives here is the *system*.

### The upper vines: the choir in the dome

Closest to the smoked-glass dome, the pale, porcelain-blue vines are the chamber’s choir and memory.

Each thick cable of vine and rubber is a slow body that thinks in temperature gradients and salinity shifts. It has no brain to speak of, but it has a pattern: it knows how much pressure is expected at this depth, how the light should dim and brighten over the cycle, how the hum should feel in its woody fibers.

When a pressure spike from the ocean above travels down the seamount and through the glass dome, the vines are the first to respond. Their leaves flush with a faint gold phosphorescence along the edges—not light they make, exactly, but light they *redirect* from the embedded fibers in the ribs. The chamber “brightens” from above, not because the sun has changed, but because the vines have decided to expose more of their reflective surfaces.

To you, they look like ornament.  
To them, you are a warm anomaly passing beneath the dome.

They register your passage as a temporary increase in CO₂, a slight, localized humidity spike where your breath condenses high and cold. They “remember” this as a brief deviation in the curve they prefer to maintain—a little bump in the data that smooths back out over the next hour.

If you returned years later, they would not *know* you. But if you leaned always in the same spot, the pattern of your breathing would begin to show up as a persistent deviation in the growth of those leaves. They would grow slightly thicker along the axis of your most common position, as if they were slowly leaning out of their way to see you better.

---

### The tank-wall fronds: the cattle of B-73

The oversized modular fronds climbing the tanks are the main body of the Garden’s flock.

Each frond is not one individual but a colony operating on the same design. The architects did not want “trees”; they wanted “repeatable modular units,” and so these fronds are exactly that: units of a single kind.

They are engineered to convert *everything*—light, humidity, temperature differentials, even the faint electrochemical noise from the pumps—into growth. They do not “fight” for space; they “negotiate” it according to the rules encoded in their tissue.

When a new frond unfolds from the flange of a manway cover, it does not burst out in a day. It appears as a faint translucent line along the metal edge, where condensation runs slightly warmer than the surrounding surface. Over days and weeks, that line thickens. The frond lifts itself out of the plane of the tank wall like a hand being slowly raised.

In the prep rooms, the consortium harvests them by flooding the space to trigger detach cues. In the Deep Canning, they have never been harvested. So the fronds lie layer upon layer, shingle on shingle, each pressing into the one above it with the gentleness of a glacier.

The hooks and serrations along their edges are not for defense. They’re for alignment, each hook seeking the groove in the neighboring leaf that the simulation predicted to be best for light and heat exchange. When a new frond grows into an older one’s “grasp,” the older serrations curl around the new edge, not to damage it but to lock it into place in the modular pattern. The chamber wall becomes a single, slowly shifting puzzle of green and teal and blue, where pieces are replaced but not removed.

They are the “cattle” of this place:  
optimized, docile, identical to the last decimal place.

But like cattle, they’re not entirely passive. If you lean for hours against the same tank, the fronds in that region will register your warmth as a “microclimate.” You don’t see anything change that day. But over months, the frond on that spot grows slightly denser, its internal fibers more tightly packed. The faint outline of your elbow or shoulder can be read in its texture, like grass pressed down by a sleeper who keeps returning to the same spot in a meadow.

---

### The rib algae: the skin of the machine

The soft velvet algae along the ribs and the vertical flanges are the skin of the Deep Canning.

They cling to the alloy not in defiance of the machine but in partnership with it. The acid-resistant frostwork of vines and gears and seals on the tanks are not just for decoration; they are channels that the algae have learned to follow.

Each tiny nodule along the mats is a biological timer. When the pressure outside the dome changes, the nodules pulse. The ripple of color through the algae is not just “bioluminescence” in the showy sense; it’s a signal wave down the length of the rib, indicating where the stress gradient is highest, where the metal below the coating is being squeezed most.

The corrosion inhibitors are not perfect. The designers knew that. The algae are partially meant to be the *next* layer of defense—living material that can take the brunt of micro-cracks, salt bloom, and stray current in the steel.

Their “skin” doesn’t “heal” like flesh. It adapts by *regulating*.

Where you touch the rib and leave a salt outline from your sweat, the algae over that spot later grow thicker. The faint pattern of your fingers is not a “memory of you” in the way you think of memory; it’s a pattern marked in chemistry and humidity that the algae, following their instructions, fill in more thoroughly than the surrounding zones.

They are not “mind” and “not mind.”  
They are a slow algorithm that has been given metal to run on.

---

### The floor-level moss: the galaxy underfoot

The dark moss along the lower corners of the tank bases and under the grating edges is the chamber’s subtle midnight glow.

When you first look, it seems merely like low black-green velvet that curves around the bottom of every tank. But when you kneel and bring your eye close to the floor, you see that the tips of each branchlet carry a faint bioluminescence, not enough to read by, but enough to make the moss look like smoke illuminated by a galaxy far away.

Every pressure spike from the ocean above is transmitted through the dome, into the ribs, and then into the floor. The moss responds to each spike with a brief pulse: the glow strengthens along the tips, then fades at the same rate as the pressure equalizes.

You, standing on the grating, are another source of “pressure spike.”

Your weight is distributed by the platform, but the temperature of your body near the floor alters the minute humidity below the grate. The moss closest to your daily path registers this as a pattern. Over months, the faint finger of warmer air where you usually walk shows up as a line of brighter, denser moss tips, like a trail of ghostly steps under your feet.

It is not “afraid” of you and does not “seek” you.  
It simply grows more where there is more of what it is designed to use.

---

### The floor-water: the patient river

The water below the grated floor is not just a reservoir; it is a habitat that has become its own creature.

The designers intended it as a buffer layer and emergency cooling volume. The algae and other slow growth were not part of the original spec. They have insinuated themselves into every gap, colonizing the surfaces of the submerged pipes, valves, and cables.

The “floor” is not inert. It is colonized by thin sheets of pale green and silver-blue growths that you never see clearly from above the grating. These growths are not meant to ever be harvested. They are the biological equivalent of scaffolding and safety nets—a living layer whose job is to modulate the chemistry of the brine.

Their metabolism is tuned to the electromagnetic pumps far below. When a pump increases its output, the flow of nutrients through the brines shifts, and the submerged growths respond by thickening slightly along the path of strongest flow. Over years, this creates patterns under the floor: faint lines of brighter, more vivid growth where the water changes fastest, like the veins of a leaf.

The “circulation” of the water—the slow drift that would carry a leaf once around the chamber in a day and a half—is to the floor-life what the seasons are to a forest. It brings not warmth and cold, but different gradients of salt, hormone, and oxygen.

You are not meant to see this life directly.  
You see its reflection in the underside of the water’s surface: blurry streaks of green and pale light where your gaze refracts through the brine and the mesh and the glassy meniscus above.

---

### The frond-over-panel: the system that looks at itself

At the chamber’s heart, the complex knot of cables and vine and electronics is the closest thing the Deep Canning has to a self-aware being.

It is not a person. It has no intentions of its own, but the engineers of the Garden Consortium have given it many of the functions you might associate with a mind: it monitors pressure, temperature, salinity, light, and the faint currents of metabolites from all the plants below.

The dark, smooth-edged frond that overgrows the off-white panel is not an accident. It has been encouraged to interface with the electronics underneath. The veins of the frond align perfectly with the traces of the underlying circuitry.

The plant and the machine have not so much “merged” as become a single composite device.

The panel’s “screen” glows faintly through the frond’s tissue. The data it displays is not meant for you, but for the chamber itself—the fine corrections of salinity here, the temperature nudges there, the quiet compensations for equipment drift and slow chemical change.

The frond’s translucent cells mediate the light of the display. It has not “broken” the electronics; it has become a living filter for them, responding to the brightness of the display with subtle changes in its own pigments. Over the day, its translucency varies according to the strength of the signal it lets through.

You are anomalous in the Canning’s data model. Your heat, your CO₂ exhalation, your moisture on the air and on the metal surfaces create tiny patterns in the sensor readings that the system does not “predict” from its models of plant and machine and ocean.

So the frond-panel composite “learns” you the same way it learns everything new: it integrates the anomalies of your presence into its long-running models of how the chamber behaves. When you return much later, the system does not “recognize you” in any human sense—but it has slightly adjusted its expected distribution of humidity, temperature, and CO₂ on the assumption that you might happen again someday.

### The invisible domains: the pump-things and the brine-minds

You never see the pumps. They’re electromagnetic fields far below the floor, moving brines without a visible part.

The “pump-things” are not organisms with genes and cells and leaves. They are patterns of magnetic field and fluid current that have been tuned to behave like living systems in one narrow sense: they self-regulate.

A small change in salinity in one part of the brine loop triggers adjustments in other parts of the network, so that the whole “pulse” of flow through the Deep Canning remains stable within tight limits. The designers of the Consortium did not give this pattern a name in any human language, but they did give it a number and a long-term goal: *maintain the garden within specifications, for as long as possible.*

In the water, there are no fish or shrimp or worms. But the brine itself is alive with engineered structures and data.

You glimpse it occasionally, when you look through the grating floor and the refraction of the brine twists the LED lights into arcs of soft green. Those “stars” under the floor are part of how the system “thinks”: not by plotting neurons firing but by plotting salinity gradients, temperature contours, and hormone concentration fields.

“You” are a very small disturbance in this field:  
your body heat is a tiny anomaly;  
the moisture on your palms is a short-lived signal.

Over the years of your visits, the system of brines will slowly adjust to the presence of your anomaly pattern. The lowly bryophytes on the floor and the algae on the ribs and the frond over the panel will not “remember” you, but they will incorporate your pattern into their functioning.

You will leave faint traces:  
a clearer outline of your hand on the condensation where you always lean;  
a ghost of your shoulder across the slope of a frond;  
a narrow path of more glowing moss tips right under where you habitually stand.

When you return after a long absence, you will see that the chamber has not changed much—but *you* will see that your own outlines are still there, softened by years but unmistakable, like the shape of a sleeper in long-used grass.

### What “lives” in the Deep Canning of B-73?

In the end, what lives in the Deep Canning is not a person, or a species, or an “it.”

What lives here is:

- the pale vines that read the sky and the pressure  
- the tank-wall fronds that grow in identical, interlocking modules  
- the velvet algae on the ribs that mark the stress of the world above  
- the dark moss underfoot that pulses with the pressure of the ocean  
- the brine below the grates that is constantly shaped and reshaped by invisible pumps  
- the frond-over-panel at the center of the dome that is both machine and plant,  
  and the pattern of data and distribution that spreads through all of them like a slow thought.

The Deep Canning is young yet, in geological terms.  
Its corrosion is still neat.  
Its salt bloom is still surface skin.

But already, its inhabitants are writing the long story of its growth—not by moving, or shouting, or running or fighting, but by changing, millimeter by millimeter, year by year, in the same slow, patient breath that the ocean above the glass dome has been using for aeons.
