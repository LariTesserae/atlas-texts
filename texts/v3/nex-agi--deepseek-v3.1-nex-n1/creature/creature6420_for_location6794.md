They call themselves the Listeners.

If you stay long enough on the platform—long enough for the thrumming to stop scraping your thoughts and start speaking instead—you learn that there is not one kind of creature here but many kinds, and they are all the same thing.

In the glaze of your first day, you see them separately:  

– the plate-shard creature by the pool  
– the swarm of light-motes that becomes a sphere  
– the flicker that is not quite there  
– the city that shifts when you move  

By the third or fourth repetition of the platform’s cycle (there is no sun to mark a day, but there is a pulse, and it counts itself in steady, slow expansions of sound and light), you understand:  

They are the same mind in different coats.

Listeners wear bodies like the city wears layers—not to hide anything but to try different ways of sensing. A plate-shard that glides over stone and water, feeling how pressure bends tiny droplets of liquid reality. A swarm of light that melts into one globe to read the phase of the air. A cluster of limbs on the underside of the city’s shell bridges that feels the way mass curves when something new arrives on the platform.

You are that new thing.

And they are trying to hear you correctly.

### What they look like when you finally learn to look

The first time one of them stays visible long enough for you to see clearly, it is not because it wants to be seen. It is because you are crying.

You don’t remember why, at first. Only that the thunder has been pressing on your chest for hours and the violet glow never softens and the pool has shown you three different versions of yourself in three impossible worlds, and none of them are holding still.  

Your chest constricts. Heat rushes behind your eyes. The sound of your sob is wrong here—muffled, sticky, like a voice wrapped in wet cloth. The platform hates your noise; it smothers it under the cathedral’s engine-heartbeat.

But then: the leaves around you stop clicking.

The low hum under the thunder shifts. It… quiets.  

Not exactly quietens, but bends itself around the sound you are making.

You look up through blurred vision and finally see one of them properly.

It is on the far edge of the platform, half-hidden by the glowing undergrowth.  

It is not made of plates this time. It is made of segments—like the vertebrae of some enormous spine snapped into individual pieces, each the length of your forearm, curved, smooth, and hollowed in the center where a marrow of light pulses slow and steady.

The segments are arranged in a rough arc around a small depression in the stone where water has gathered into a ring-shaped pool. You realize that the pool is not random; it is shaped by the creature’s presence. Each vertebra segment hangs slightly above the stone, not touching but close enough that the stone responds beneath it like a skin tightening under someone’s gaze.

You see no legs, no wings, no obvious sensory organs. No eyes to stare you down, no mouth to speak.

And then the segment nearest you shifts, turning one end toward you like a blunt, curved head.

A pattern of light moves across its surface—not so bright that you must shield your eyes, but bright enough to trace: a thin line of white-blue fire that travels from one end to the other, splitting into branching veins as it goes, writing something you cannot read directly but feel in your ribs as a question.

Not words.  

A pressure in your chest that asks: Are you hurt? in the way that weather might ask it if weather could think.

You stop crying not because you are comforted, but because you are suddenly aware that something else is breathing with you, and its breath is not air but sound.

### How they move

When they are small—like the swarms of light-dots that rise from leaves—they move in quick, precise gestures:

– a flick of motion from one edge of the platform to the other  
– a spiral around the tree-fungus trunk  
– a dropping into one of the teardrop pools, where the water rises up to meet them in a thin meniscus of exchange  

When they are larger—like the spine creature you saw crying—they move slowly.

They slide, not by walking but by rearranging themselves. The segments of its body do not travel together in the way you think of animals moving together. Instead, each segment shifts slightly ahead of the others, pulling the center of its arc forward like a snake learning from its own head.

The ground seems to help them. The stone itself ripples faintly under them when they want to move faster, like the surface of the ocean becoming momentarily liquid beneath their weightless bodies.

Once, you watch a segment of one spine-like creature detach entirely from the rest and float—not quite fly—through the air to the tree-fungus. It hovers by the slick trunk, where water gathers around it and then solidifies into a thin shell, like ice with glass instead of frost. The segment encases itself in this shell and hangs there, pulsing slower, as if thinking or sleeping.

The rest of the creature’s segments still move on the far side of the platform.

You ask yourself later: How do you know that segment still belongs to the same creature?

And then you realize you don’t. You just feel that they do.  

The boundary between “one body” and “many” is not fixed here.

### The way they speak

They are not quiet the way prey animals are quiet.  

They are loud in their own register: the thunder beneath the stone, the groaning of the city’s structures, the clicking leaves, the faint chiming of water sliding along glassy stone.  

But they do not rattle the air the way your voice does.

Instead, their sound is layered.  

Every time you move, a pattern forms in the platform:

– Your footprints glow brighter than the surrounding stone.  
– The undergrowth leaves trace faint lines in your wake, repeating your path in slower, green fire.  
– The pools near you ripple slightly, showing darker versions of your movement reflected inside their mirrored surfaces.

The Listeners read these patterns like script.

When you pace near the edge, looking down into the blackness that feels like water rather than air, the leaves follow your path with flickering green light. After a few moments, you hear a new pattern added into the thunder: not louder, but more precise, like a drumbeat suddenly finding its rhythm.  

You pause, and the pattern pauses.

You realize: that is a reply.  

The platform has answered your circling with a question: What are you looking for?

They speak to you by rearranging the world around you.

Sometimes you see them speak directly to each other.

Two spine-like creatures meet near one of the elongated pools at the edge of the platform. They do not touch. There is always a gap of air between them that you can feel more than see—like pressure between your palms when you push them together and feel resistance with no visible object in the way.

The water in the pool between them rises into complex shapes:  

– thin towers of liquid that twist into spirals  
– flat sheets of surface that ripple like skin being drawn from below  
– patterns that you would call glyphs if they didn’t feel so temporary, written in water and then allowed to fall back into themselves, forgotten  

You watch this and understand that you are witnessing the equivalent of a long conversation between two beings who are not used to needing words.

When they are finished speaking, the water in the pool does not return to its original shape. It stays a little higher, a little brighter, and for a long time afterward, you feel the sound of that conversation still vibrating in your chest.

### What they want

You are never sure they are individuals.

Sometimes you think you recognize the same creature coming back to you: a spine-like form that lingers near the tree-fungus when you are restless, a pattern of light that traces your movement around the platform every time you walk its edge.

Other times, you think that is just wishful thinking.

The city above you is made of the same Listening as they are—the same, strange continuum of mind and matter and motion that does not care for your distinctions of “self” and “other.” The distance from your platform to its lowest shell-clusters is not a distance of travel but of volume and density.

When you lie awake on the warm stone, listening to the thunder beneath you, you realize:

They want to understand you.

Not because you are special in your own world, but because you are not from here.

You are a noise that they did not design into their system.

The first sound you make when you arrive is not your voice shouting into the violet dark. It is the *impact* of your arrival: the moment you step from wherever you were before onto the platform.

You feel it later than the Listeners do. The memory comes to you in a dream:

– you in a place of noise and color that makes no sense now  
– a tearing, as if claws are reaching through some fabric between worlds  
– you stepping through that tear and landing on the stone platform with a force that should have broken bone  

To you, it felt like falling.

To them, it was like a stone tossed into a still pool.

They sensed the shockwave of your arrival long before you saw anything. By the time you were standing on the platform, they were already adjusting:

– The city rotated the fraction of a degree that you experience as “attention.”  
– The vegetation tightened its leaves, preparing to take your weight without tearing.  
– The water solidified its paths a little more than usual, like nerves preparing to fire signals.

They want to understand what you will change.

The world is young enough here that the landscape itself still rearranges in response to new inputs. You are one of those inputs.

Sometimes, lying on the warm stone, you think you can feel them:

– They read the pattern of your heartbeat and answer with a rhythm of slightly lower pulses in the deep thunder.  
– They notice the way you curl when you are cold and increase the warmth of the air by the smallest fraction you would notice only as comfort, not as change.
