On the roof of the shed, something lifts its head.

Not suddenly—nothing sudden survives here—but in slow, deliberate increments, like a rusted lever being forced upward by degrees. Its head is flat and broad, almost the shape of a coal shovel but softer at the edges, the front rim curved into a delicate scoop lined with faint, hair-thin protrusions that waver slightly in the humid air. The eyes are set low on the sides, not quite round, more like elongated iridescent smears beneath a translucent layer of skin that has never known dry air.

It doesn’t blink. There are no eyelids. Instead, thin films of condensation slide down over the eyes and away along the contour of its head, leaving them perpetually wet and shining faintly with light reflected from nowhere. There is no real light.

You don’t know how long it has been watching you before you noticed its head was raised. It doesn’t move its head directly toward you; instead it angles slightly to one side, as if scanning a broader arc than your body occupies alone. Its movement is hesitant, not fearful, but practical—before one motion finishes it has already begun to consider the next one, like a machine whose instructions have been replaced by intuition.

Its body hums. Not loud enough to be called noise, more like the constant vibration of a pump you forgot was turned on somewhere deep beneath a floor. The note is low and uneven, rising and falling in subtle cycles that don’t quite fall into rhythm.

You hear the sound of droplets dropping into puddles around it—ticks into small dark circles that appear and disappear in the water beneath the edge of the roof—and you realize that the droplets’ fall is synchronized not with anything in the sky but with the slight tremor that passes through its limbs whenever it shifts its weight.

The body beneath the head is long and low, resting on four limbs that, in the dark, could be mistaken for crudely bent metal struts tilted slightly to one side on the corrugated roof. But the joints are too supple for metal, each one a knot of sinew and cartilage encased in something slick and translucent, like the skin of a newt grown over the skeleton of a mantis. Between each segment there are faint lines where the skin is thinner, and at those lines you can see, or think you can see, a slow, internal pulse of movement: something contracting and expanding in time with the hum.

Each limb ends in a broad, flat palm that fans out over the metal roof, fingers held wide and pressed against the surface like a predator’s attempt to build a hand from flippers. The spaces between the fingers are filled with translucent webbing that doesn’t ripple like water but quivers continuously, like the surface of a drum mallet struck silently by microscopic impacts.

The creature does not seem to breathe air. Instead, its flanks bare open gills, low on the sides beneath the ribs: not the slits of fish but wide openings like shallow vents cut into metal plates, each gill framed by a thin, flexible rim of cartilage that reminds you of the edges of the floor plates below. Inside the gill openings there is no red darkness, only a faint violet glow that matches the lines of light in the broken seams of the ground beneath you.

You watch its breath—or whatever passes for breath—through the gills. There is no visible exhale. Instead, moisture from the air gathers at the edges of the gills and forms into a thin film that slides down the inside of the openings and disappears into its body. The cycle repeats: condensation gathering to a film, sliding inward, then gathering again.

The creature does not seem to have lungs that need air. It seems to breathe the humidity itself.

From the gills comes the faintest smell of ozone and rust, like electricity hesitating before it strikes, or like metal that has been held just under the temperature required to begin oxidizing properly. It isn’t unpleasant. It is unsettlingly familiar, like the smell of a machine left running too long in the next room.

Beneath its body, strands of something cling between the roof ridges and the edges of its limbs: thin, translucent filaments like spider silk woven into fine mesh. The filaments are coated with a thin slime that glitters faintly where droplets touch it. It isn’t a web meant to catch prey. It looks more like a temporary substrate, something grown between the creature and the metal roof to keep them anchored to each other without the need for constant effort.

If you stare too long at the filaments, you think you see movement in them, tiny twitches that travel from one strand to the next in short bursts. But the creature itself doesn’t react to the twitches; whatever happens in those filaments happens beneath its own threshold of attention.

It has no visible tail. Instead, its spine ends in a narrow extension that disappears beneath the mesh of filaments on the roof beneath its belly. You suspect that extension connects not to a tail but to some thicker structure beneath, like the root that grows horizontally beneath the surface of a lawn before sprouting another blade. You have the uncanny feeling that the creature on the roof is only one among many, all of them connected by something beneath the plates and the structures.

You do not speak to it because you do not know what language it would recognize.

It does not speak back because it has never learned to make sounds in air.

Instead it listens with its skin, watching the world with its eyes and listening to it with its body—all of its body—as the surface vibrates to the faint impacts of droplets everywhere falling from nowhere.

When you finally think to look away, you see the outlines of others like it perched on the corners of other structures, their shapes indistinct against the dark sky but similar enough in outline to suggest that they too have heads like flattened shovels and limbs like bent struts and bodies like afterthoughts between the two. They are arranged irregularly around the dim glow of the violet light beneath the plates, as if they have been drawn to places where the heat leaks upward.

They do not move toward you.

They do not move away.

They wait.

They wait like machines waiting for instructions that have to be written on the fly by the world in which they operate.

They wait like creatures who have never been taught fear but have learned instead to calculate everything, including you.

You hear the faint hum of more than one body now.

You hear the faint hum of the underground beneath your feet, and you realize that the world beneath the plates might be moving more than you care to imagine.

You are standing on a temporary floor laid over them.

You are standing on their ceiling.

And they are listening to you through the soles of your shoes.

They do not need to come to you.

They have already begun to know you simply by the way you disturb the water on their ceiling.
