Within the chalk–white throat of Jelibeth, where every stone is still deciding how to be stone, the people are shaped like the bodies they hide beneath the streets.

They are not numerous enough to swarm the city, but they are everywhere the city lets them be. They live inside the bone–pale walls the way lichen live inside the rock: not placed on it but grown from within it. They hollow their chambers where the pressure of the mound–beasts and the new foundations of Jelibeth are least insistent; and so they inherit, by a subtle refusal of conflict, the forgotten spaces of their own making.

Their bodies are vague at first sight. They are not particularly tall, nor broad, but they seem to take the shape of the space they occupy: narrow shoulders and thin torsos when they are moving in the tight capillary lanes of the inner wards; slightly longer limbs when they are walking the half–salted scars of the outer districts where the ground is still barely learning what slope is. They dress in layers that are more membrane than garment: pale chalk–cloth and chalk–felt that they tease from a certain down of the roof–mat and then press into stiffness with the sap of the gel–lined canals. When the wind—which here is never more than a slow, thoughtful breath—crosses the city, they move like hangings drying over the void, edges of their sleeves and hems riffing softly.

They wrap their hands and feet in thin strips of the same material, so that they leave no clear prints in the raw soil of the courtyards or on the half–grown cobbles of the streets. The tracks of Jelibeth are not the marks of feet but the pressure–signs of mound–beasts and the delicate, faintly sticky trails of the smaller creatures that climb the walls like slow thoughts. The people’s presence is recorded instead in the way the city sheds itself: a slight hollowing of a threshold step where the same shoulder has touched for years; a slight darkening of the pale plaster at the corners of rooms where bodies have brushed the wall; a slight compression of the plant–mat where they have chosen to sleep.

Their faces are not full of their story; they are not masks of sorrow or pleasure or rage. They are bare of drama the way the sky above is bare of storm clouds. The eyes are set a little farther than usual from the nose, the cheekbones low and smooth, like new clay being shaped but not yet pressed into features. The skin of Jelibeth’s people is the colour of the streets before dawn—pale tan, chalky, with faint, uneven patches of white that resemble the salt–scars where night dew has never had time to learn itself into water. They rarely sweat; the big sap–sacs of the city do not permit them to lose moisture that easily.

They are quiet in the same way that everything else is quiet: because there are no engines or gears to compete against. The city of Jelibeth has not yet learned metal noise, and so they have not yet learned shouting to be heard. They have timbre and pitch and rhythm but they lack the impulse of urgency. When they speak to each other in the narrow rooms where the plaster is still damp from the squeezed walls of the chalk, they speak like stone settling into its new shape: low, murmuring, with the faintest click of their teeth, which resemble small, pale shells of the same limestone that makes their city.

The children are smaller versions of themselves, but their motion is different. They do not yet know how to choose where the pressure of the ground is least. They wander into the half–raised hummocks of the city edge; they are there when the ground under the outer quarters of Jelibeth pushes itself upward into possible hillsides, and they are just the right size to be lifted by these new half–shapes without being harmed by them. They are the first to learn the new contours of the world, because they are present when those contours are being formed.

They play with stones that are not yet stones, and with the small creatures that are not yet fully animals. The mound–beasts, whose backs are paved with the slabs of new plazas and roads, ignore them in the same way they ignore everything else: the children run up their sloping backsides and slide down the other side as if they were sliding along the slight curve of a new plaza before it has had any use to learn of ceremony. The smaller, fur–like creatures that creep the walls like growing vines accept them into their mats of fibrous down, and the children sleep with them in the shallow alcoves of the chalk–white houses where the plaster is still warm.

They are not taught in the sense of lessons being spoken into them from above. They are taught the way a plant is taught to grow: by proximity to light and pressure and shade. They learn by following where the adults go when they choose to move; by watching when their older kin turn their head—not toward anything but away from something—sensing, before any visible signal has occurred, that the mound–beast under their dwelling is beginning to shift, and that the rooms of their house might be needed by the new street a few weeks later. They learn by the absence of teaching: the adult whose hand slackens just slightly in the evening’s dim light; the child who learns that this slackening is a sign to be ready to leave the room they have been sleeping in for three years.

They are its people: not the rulers of the city but the least forceful part of its geology. They are not the builders that the mound–beasts are, nor the slow eroders of stone that the gel–lined canals are, nor the shade–weavers that the plants are. They are the small pressure gradients that keep the city in balance with itself.

They are governed by nothing else. They do not bow to the city as if it were a ruler; they are not its servants nor its priests. They are obscurely necessary to its sap–circulation, but no more necessary than the roof–mat is or the chalk–shells in the canals. The city of Jelibeth does not protect them nor threaten them; it merely has them the way it has heat in its ground and gel in its canals and sky in its air. They are the city’s inhabitants, in the same way that the white salt–script on its outer walls is its name: faint but present, always there, being written and erased and rewritten by the motion of the world before the sun takes notice.
