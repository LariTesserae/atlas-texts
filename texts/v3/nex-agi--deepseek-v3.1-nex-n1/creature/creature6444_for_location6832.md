They are called *kith* by the engineers who seeded them, but they do not use that name among themselves. They are the ones who stay.

While the sky is still low iron cloud and the fog clings in ragged scarves to the basalt, they are already moving.

You see them first as smudges against the stone—tall, narrow figures that seem to have grown out of the plateau’s own cracks. Their bodies are layered: a core of flexible, heat‑retaining tissue sheathed in overlapping plates of pale chitin that has been reinforced, strand by strand, with metal drawn from the bedrock. To human eyes they look like a compromise between insect and machine, but there is nothing hesitant in their design. Every joint is sealed against the wet; every seam sheds water like stone.

They walk on legs that end in broad, splayed feet, each toe edged with small hooks and pads that grip damp rock without slipping. Their hands are long‑fingered and surprisingly delicate, capable of tracing hairline fractures in the basalt or coaxing thin streams of water into new channels. A faint warmth pulses just under their plating—the slow burn of internal chemistry and captured geothermal energy—so that wherever they pause, condensation beads on their shells like sweat on cold glass.

Most carry tools grown from their own bodies: curved spurs of bone‑metal along forearms for scraping; hollow spines along their backs for drawing water; filaments that extend from their wrists to taste conductivity in rock or sense vibration in hidden conduits.

### The Work at Dawn

At first light—such as it is under seamless cloud—they move to the cracks.

The plateau settles through the night: clicks and sighs as slabs shift by millimeters, as heat differentials pull at half‑welded seams. The kith know these sounds individually. They crouch over fractures where warm vapor rises thin and steady, listening not with ears but with receptors under their skin that map pressure and temperature as soundless song.

One lowers herself into a low depression where water has pooled shallowly over dark glassy slag. She sweeps her hand across the surface, ripples spreading in precise arcs. Her fingers trail filaments into the water; they branch, delicate white threads sinking until they touch the stone beneath.

Information returns along those threads as pressure and chemistry: heat flux here is too high; this pool will boil off if left alone; here there is a buried vein of metal fused into basalt that can be persuaded to carry warmth away.

She hums—a subvocal vibration more felt than heard—and others converge without needing to call aloud. They move around her like extensions of her will: one sinks its spurs into an adjacent slab to open a new seam; another kneels to guide water along a freshly scraped channel; a third presses palms flat against stone over a buried conduit and feels for irregularities in its pulse.

No orders are exchanged. Their communication runs through gesture, scent markers left on rock like invisible ink, and low frequencies passed through stone or air. It is too easy for spoken words to be swallowed by fog and distance; better to let the plateau itself carry their intent.

### The Adjusters

Among them are those especially shaped for this work: their internal organs reconfigured around dense nodules of crystal that resonate with engineered veins in the plateau’s foundation. They are translators between life and infrastructure.

These adjusters move more slowly than the others. Where they step, faint traceries of light follow under translucent layers of chitin over chest and spine—subtle echoes of what flows hidden in bedrock beneath them. They rarely eat solid food anymore; instead they press themselves against warm rock faces for hours at a time, letting geothermal seepage sustain them while they listen deep.

An adjuster finds a fracture line that has begun to widen too quickly—microquakes spiderwebbing through young basalt. He sinks down beside it until his body is aligned almost parallel to the break: head near one end, feet near another. Contact points along his limbs adhere softly to stone as if magnetized.

Inside him, crystal arrays vibrate sympathetically with frequencies embedded in distant machinery far below. Information returns as sensation: strain maps itself onto his nervous system as phantom tension along limbs; projected failure paths flare briefly behind closed eyes like branching lightning.

He sends back corrections not by speaking commands but by shifting his own internal frequencies—tiny nudges that couple with buried actuators kilometers down. The plateau responds slowly: stresses redistribute; metal veins embedded in rock reroute energy flows; temperature gradients smooth out by fractions of degrees.

To anyone watching without his senses it would look like nothing more than a figure lying motionless beside a crack for half a day while fog curls around unmoving limbs. But when he finally rises again there is new stability underfoot—a subtle rightness only other kith can feel clearly.
