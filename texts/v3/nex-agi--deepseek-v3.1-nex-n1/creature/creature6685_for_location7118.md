Radiating from the ends of those translucent, pulsing veins are thousands of colonies of burrowers: not insects, exactly, but something like them if insects were soft and boneless and covered in a fine, iridescent down. They have no beaks or mouths, but instead press a circular pad of flesh against the warm root-walls. When they do, the wall softens and liquefies underneath; the burrower drags its head slowly along the living ceiling, carving a thin groove of glittering sap that thickens behind it into a new capillary. The constant noise is the combined scratch of their feeding pads and the whisper of their bodies sliding over each other, as they crawl endlessly along the glowing rootwork of the world.

Wherever the tree has fewer mouths to feed, it grows small transparent nodules on the vein walls. These are not fruit to be picked, but pockets of symbiotic larvae: pale blind crawlers with too many legs and the translucent jelly of unborn tree cells in their stomachs. When they hatch they will drink the tree’s sap and, in return, seed the next layer of capillary rootlets with their own bodies. In the meantime, they are a faint percussion of tiny heartbeats under the burrowers’ scraping.

Nestling in the thicker intersections of the rootwork are the sleep-mothers, each one a swollen, sac-like creature, longer than a man is tall, curled like a question mark around its own warm egg-mass. Theirs is the rotten smell you notice first: the rich, fungal stink of old brood-cloth and the waste of a thousand hatchlings. The sleep-mothers are not blind; but their eyes are countless and very small, and they are not used for looking at things. Instead, they are tuned to the faint shifts of light in the tree’s veins: the brighter and clearer, the more the water flows; the darker and more opaque, the more the tree is consuming itself. The sleep-mother’s body is an answer to the question of light, and so she contracts to release more of her brood when the world is bright, and swallows what she cannot safely bear when the veins are thin.

Moving just beyond the root network, in the darker, tougher wood of the world’s supporting frame, are the drill-beaks: long, slender beasts with heads of glassy bone and mandibles that hum when they grind together. They bore not to eat, but to make room. The tree’s outer frame cannot grow itself; the drill-beaks carve it open and keep the living wood from sealing over. The sound of their work is a low, intermittent coughing under the chorus of clicks, as they spit out dust and bone-chips and retract to make new channels.

All of these are the tree’s children and its bodyworkers. They consume its sap and its flesh, but they also maintain its flow, and they are never not walking over its living heartwood. When they die, they are eaten by the tree: not devoured in any sense of violence, but simply allowed to fall into folds of soft bark where enzymes slowly melt them back into the world-water. Theirs is a life of endless maintenance and intimate digestion: a world that both builds and feeds itself with the same bodies.
