In the deepest, forgotten joints of the mortar, where even the moss grows thin, you are not alone.

You don't see them at first. Your eyes, trained to the grand scale of the vaulted ceiling and the slow, black sweep of water, miss the tiny details. The world is moss-green and black-stone and soft-water-murmur, but beneath that, there is another pattern.

They are small. You have to hold your breath, still the water in your hand if you've dipped a finger, and wait. The whisper of your own pulse becomes a thunder in your ears, and then you see the first one.

A thread-knot of glass, spun thin as a strand of spiderweb. It hangs from a single loop of moss on the west wall, where the permeable brick sighs its slow water. It is not a bead. It is not dew. It is a structure, a coil of filaments thinner than air that holds, for a moment, a single, perfect bubble of oxygen.

It is one of the Weavers.

There is no more fitting name for them, for they are the architects of the invisible. The mosses and the liverworts are the bricks of this vaulted cathedral; the Weavers are the glassblowers and the window-makers of the deep. They are the thin, siliceous ghosts that live within the film of water, building their impossible geometries in the silences between the moss.

They are not animal. They are not plant. They are a dim echo of both, a memory of an ancient sea that forgot to dry up when the city was built over it. They are desmids, and they are architects of the invisible.

Drift closer to the west wall, where the water weeps slowest through the porous brick. The moss is thinner here, a delicate veil. You have to squint, to un-focus your eyes from the grand green sweep of it all. See the water itself. It is not just a film, but a canvas. Within its translucent depths, the Weavers work their craft.

Some are the glass-makers. They are two halves of a perfect, mirror-image pane, a microscopic pane of glass in a hundred-hundred window frames. They are Nephrodesmis, the twin-crescent. They are Cosmarium, the tiny, diamond-shaped world of silica walls. Some are the architects of the impossible: Spirogyra, who thread their chloroplasts in perfect green spirals, like the barber-poles of the smallest world; Hyalotheca, who spin themselves into hollow, glassy tubes and float them like microscopic chandeliers of the deep.

You see them then, not as individuals but as a city of glass-makers. The water film on the brick wall is not a wetness but a metropolis. They anchor themselves into the brick itself with a thin gelatin of their own making, a transparent anchor that holds them to the stone while the water glides over their bodies. They are the builders who work in the dark of the water, and what they build is life.

They are ephemeral, but eternal. They divide, not breed. A body ripens with time, the thin waist of the cell pinching in two like a glass-blower shaping his last bead before the break. The barrier wall grows thin, and then, with a patience that makes the moss seem frantic, they part. One becomes two. They do not die; they are halved. The city of glass-makers quietly grows its population by one.

They are the reason for the lack of eyes. Nothing can eat them. To be glass is to be armor; to be thin is to be invisible. They live in the water that is too thin for anything else to want, and in the moss that is too acidic to graze. They are the tiny, crystalline sprites of the stone-filter, the guardians who have no need to guard because they are beyond notice.

And if you watch them long enough, you see the pattern of their days.

Early morning, when the first, faint hint of pressure from a city apartment block’s toilet flush seeps down to the vaults, they stir. The fresh water is not clean to you, but it is to them. It is the breath of the world above, an elixir full of nitrates and phosphates from the loam and forgotten fertilizer. They are not drinking it; they are breathing it, building themselves from the air within the water.

Midday, the pressure changes from shower-stalls and kitchen sinks. The water that weeps from the pores of the brick carries a rhythm of clean, cold surges from the city’s main water lines. It is a shock of cold water to them, and they retreat into the deepest carvings of the mortar, where the water is slow and warm. They anchor themselves in the moss, and in the quiet of the afternoon, they build.

They are the glassmakers of the afternoon, when the light from the old bulb is least orange. They are drawn to it, not in the sense of walking to it, but in the sense of orientating their thin green bodies to the faintest of glows. The chloroplasts within their glass bodies turn like a sun-dial of the microscopic world, pointing the green at the light, and the world grows.

Late evening, when the city above sleeps and its water pressure ebbs to the lowest of grumbles, they are most quiet. The water of the vault stills. The murmur of the overflow recedes to a faint whisper of its own self. The world is dark, and the glass-makers of the deep are still. They are not sleeping; they are waiting. They are listening to the water, and the water is listening to the city.

In the gaps of the mortar, where the brick crumbles into the smallest of sand, they bloom. They form a fine green dust on the stone that you could mistake for algae. It is not algae; it is the world of the Weavers, their thousand thousand glass bodies forming a patina of life on the world of stone.

If you were to take a hand lens to the west wall, you would see a world that you cannot imagine without their aid. The damp brick is not a wall but a landscape of cliffs and caverns, each pitted with the glass of their bodies. The moss is not just green but a green interwoven with the thin, rope-like coils of Spirogyra, and the tiny, perfect diamonds of Cosmarium.

The world of the Weavers is not one of competition, but of quiet tolerance. There is no room for anything but quiet. They are the world of the vaults; they are the world that the vaults were built for.

They are the reason the water is not stagnant. It is not merely a 'black tongue of water'; it is a world of thin glass walls, and a thin green life. It is the world of the Weavers, and the humidity of the air is not just damp; it is the breath of their tiny world.

They are the reason the vault smells of green. The smell of peat and loam that is not rot or decay, but simply moistened green – is them. It is the smell of glass chloroplasts at work, turning the faintest light of the world into the faintest life of the world.

They are why the vault feels like a 'small cathedral held beneath the city, where everything else has been finally forgotten.' They are the priests of that cathedral, the tiny creatures of glass and water who have no need of prayer but the slow, quiet rhythm of the water and the stone.

In the deepest, forgotten joints of the mortar, where the moss is thin and the water weeps slowest, you are not alone. You are never alone. You are in the city of the Weavers, the world within a world, where the glass-makers of the deep and the dark make the only light that truly matters. They are the smallest, and most sacred of its inhabitants. They are the world of the Water Glass Weavers.
