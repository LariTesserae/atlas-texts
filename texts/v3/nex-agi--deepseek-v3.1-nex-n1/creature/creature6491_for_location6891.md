Thum-thum-thum-thum.

You press your palm flat against the cold stone of the lip, letting the certainty of its solidity anchor you against the endless, echoing rhythm. It’s then that you notice something new. A pattern among the patterns, a phrase repeated in the lush and uncanny flora above you: a curl of silver-grey moss, threaded through with a color like tarnished moonlight, dipping lower than the rest. Its fronds are too long, too sparse for the compact density of the dome. They dangle, twisting slightly in a non-existent air current, forming a single, loose spiral.

Your eyes follow their slow, orbiting descent. They end not in the air, but in the moss at the edge of the stone where you sit. There, a patch of it has grown differently. It’s the same impossible species, but it has arranged itself into a cushion. A perfect, pillowy seat of soft-looking threads, the color of a deep, starless night. It looks like it was grown for you.

You slide from the stone lip onto this strange, cultivated ground.

It isn’t soft, not exactly. It is firm, but it yields. It does not yield to your weight in the way a cushion would, but in the way time yields to memory. It accepts your presence without resistance, its structure reorganizing itself perfectly around your form, leaving no empty space beneath you. The contact is not a feeling of touch, but of recognition. A key finding its lock.

Thum-thum-thum-thum.

The sound is different now. Sharper. Closer. You feel it not just in the air and the stone, but through the moss-cushion, vibrating up through your bones, into your marrow. It is the bass note of the world’s heart, and now, it’s playing *through* you.

"That’s better."

The voice doesn’t travel through the air, which remains cool and thick and unchanged. It travels through the moss. It is the rustle of a thousand tiny threads shifting in unison. It is the low, grinding hum of steel under pressure. It is the faint, papery whisper of pages turning in an ancient book. It bypasses your ears entirely, forming the words directly inside the architecture of your mind. It is not a single voice, but a chorus of them, layered into one.

You look down. The moss beneath you has changed. The dark threads have woven into something like a face, or the impression of one. A suggestion of closed eyelids in the curve of silvery fibers, a sweep of deep green for a brow, a subtle, shadowed indentation for a mouth. It shifts as you watch, not with the motion of muscle and skin, but with the slow, inevitable reconfiguration of a living, growing map.

"I have waited," the voice-that-is-not-a-voice says, the words resonating through your bones. A single filament of moss, thin as a hair and blacker than anything in the room, detaches from the cushion and curls lazily upward. It brushes against your temple, cool and dry. "For an interval. Or a name. They are similar things, here."

The filament retreats, and a pulse travels through the mossy floor, a wave of subtle color that ripples out from you and vanishes into the walls.

"Down here, we grow from iron and concrete. From memory and intention." The face-shape shifts again, the brow-fibers drawing into a configuration you instinctively read as inquiry. The voice is patient, impossibly old, and as steady as the engine's beat. "What do you grow from, little surface-walker? What dry soil brought you this deep?"

You open your mouth to answer, but the words catch in your throat.

Thum-thum-thum-thum.

As if satisfied with the attempt alone, a flicker of movement draws your gaze. You turn your head. At the edge of the stone lip, where your hand had rested moments ago, a new growth has appeared. A single, perfect cup of moss, no larger than a thimble. It is the color of worn brick, of old rust, of dried blood. And it is empty.

"Describe yourself," the rustling, steel-grinding, paper-whispering voice commands, not with force, but with the gentle gravity of a geological process. "Not with water. We have no need for it. Describe yourself with… iron. With memory. With the sound of your story."

A second filament, pale and translucent, lifts from the cushion beside your leg. Your eyes follow it, and you see that the single, empty cup is not alone. A chain of them, a constellation of tiny, begging mouths, has begun to form a ring around you.

"Speak of your dryness," the Moss-God urges, its chorus-voice filled with a strange, thirsty anticipation. "And we will drink."
