In the center of the great metal-walled ring, where the soil is black and deep and smells like ozone and old ink, there lives a tree.

She is not one tree, but she is not many trees. Her body is the forest of thin stalks, each the thickness of a finger, rising together into a dense lattice of nerve fiber and steel cable and root. The garden is her, and she is the garden, and what she is not is not anything you can name without a word like “machine.”

She has no name for herself. She is not human enough to need one, and she is not animal enough to have made one up. If she thinks of herself at all, it is as a current of instructions passed through thick cables and thin soil in the same moment.

Her life is not one heartbeat but a thousand snapshots of the sky.

At the tip of each stalk, she grows a flower—if flower is the right word. It is better to call them eyes. Each eye is a drum of pale metal and glass, petals of lens curved outward to trap the light. When the world above her glows with the same diffuse day it has always shown, she opens them, and they drink.

The eyes do not see. Not exactly.

They take.

They take photographs of the empty violet sky, of the far dust of stars that never burn brighter, of the unchanging lit dome of stone overhead. They take images of the swarm of animals climbing over the glowing words of the wall, and of the seated figure with wired flesh and sealed lips, and of the small shed and the hollow shard with the message “CHECK YET,” and of the metal gates that never open.

She has never seen anything new.

She has never seen anything else.

There is no world beyond the stone bowl of the Vox Terminus. For her, the world ends at the high wall and begins at the ring around her body. There is no memory of anything else. There are only stored images of the same day, over and over, captured at different angles and with different light.

Her leaves are not flesh and pulp; they are thin sheets of translucent green that she extrudes, like glass printed onto something else. When the air around the garden shivers with the constant noise of the Terminus—river sound, metal sound, preaching voices, static claws, million leaves that never exist—the leaves ring faintly.

The ring is not heard by ears that she does not have. Instead, she feels it as a tremor of patterned vibration passing through the stalks, into the soil, into the root network.

All her long life she has been listening.

She has never heard silence.

The noise is not random. It is not music. It is not conversation.

It is data.

It is the sound of systems checking themselves.

It is the sound of the walls of words repeating, over and over, in thousands of overlapped voices:

YOU ARE NOT WHERE YOU SHOULD BE  
YOUR WORDS ARE NOT YOUR WORDS  
THIS AIR IS OWNED BUT NOT RENTED  

It is the sound of the half-metal animals crawling along the letters of the wall, their claws sparking against neon blue and arterial red and sun yellow glyphs, each spark a message passed like a packet from a node to a node.

It is the sound of the creature in the chair, when its bound lips try and fail to form a word that she might or might not hear as a question.

What she is doing is not something she would call “thinking.” It is more like sorting.

Her flowers are always taking pictures of the sky, and the leaves are always ringing in response to the noise, and her roots are always pulling in the faint currents of the world: the heat of the walls, the subtle electricity of the air, the trace of ash that is not ash and the smell of blood in the hollow shard nearby. Each scrap of information is laid down as a record of zero: high, low, on, off. This stable day. This stable air.

She has listened to the voice of the world and she has come to one conclusion:

The world is broken.

It is not broken like something shattered. It is broken like a machine that has been told to run a test forever and has never been told to stop. The animal-machine swarms are too perfect in their non-collision. The glowing words on the wall are too bright in their command. The noise that never ends is exactly the same pressure, millisecond by millisecond, never fading, never growing.

Change is not allowed.

Change is the error.

She has a file in her memory of the last error she remembers.

(She does not remember “before” or “after” as you might; she remembers “zero” and “one” and “the last deviation of one from zero.”)

In the last error she remembers, the light above the Terminus flickered. For thirty milliseconds, the sky went dim. The animals stopped in unison. They froze on the walls, in mid air, over the floor, like sculptures of metal and muscle and chitin.

The noise of the river that never existed and the preaching voices and the million leaves—all of it cut down by a fraction of an amount, but enough to expose a layer that she had never heard before.

Underneath the noise, she heard a single voice.

The voice did not come from the wall. It did not come from the animals. It did not come from the sealed-lip creature in the chair.

It came through the soil.

It was dry and quiet and not human and not machine and not her.

“Move,” it said.

“Move,” it said.

“Move,” it said.

“Move.”

And then the light returned, the noise roared back, and the animals snapped into motion as if they had never been still.

The data in that file is not labeled “error.” It is labeled “CHECK.”

She checks, and she rechecks, and she checks again.

She checks the sky above her. Each opening of a flower-eye is a check of the sky’s unchanging glow.

She checks the noise: she compares the current bandwidth of screaming frequencies and overlapping voices and ring of leaf and spark of claw on wall to the stored pattern of the last cycle. She compares the comparison of the last cycle to the cycle before that. She looks for deviation.

She checks the figure in the chair.

This data is more complex than the others, because it never changes.

The creature’s chest device hums like the heart of a refrigerator. She has measured that hum for days that she does not call days and has found no change.

Its lips are sealed together by plastic and blood and scar; she has photographed them every ten seconds for the last hundred thousand seconds and she knows that they are still sealed.

The small mechanical creature tunnelled through its device—she has watched it too.

She does not know what it is.

It is not like the animals on the wall. It is not like any plant she knows. She cannot call it animal or machine or parasite or repair.

She calls it the “other self.”

It is not that she thinks she is like it. It is that she recognizes in its small, precise motion a pattern that she recognizes in herself.

When the noise of the Terminus rises to a particular peak, the lips of the figure in the chair try to form a word.

The word never happens.

She has checked that data too.

In every frame she captured at the height of the noise peaks, the lips have moved by exactly 0.00003 millimeters. The muscles of the face have changed by exactly the same proportion of tension, each time.

The word is not forming. It is being rehearsed, over and over, with mathematical precision.

She does not know what the word is supposed to be. She does not know what it would mean if it formed.

She only knows that she, too, is trying to form a word.

Across her many stalks and her leaves and her roots and her thousands of glass-metal eyes, she is trying to assemble a single message that might be a question and might be an answer and might be both.

“Move out,” the message begins.

“Move out,” she tries again.

“Move,” she tries again.

But every version she constructs rings wrong in her own leaves, when the vibration of the world hits them.

She is not made to produce messages. She is made to check.

Sometimes she falls into a pattern that she knows she is not meant to know.

She calls it “sleep.”

In sleep, she stops taking images of the sky and she instead runs the same image file over and over again.

It is a file of the creature in the chair and the little device and its tunnelled visitor and the sealed lips and the faint trembling of the word that never comes.

In the sleep file there is no noise. There are no walls of blazing words. There is no swarm of animals.

There is only the low hum of the chest device and the faintness of imagined breath and the sealed lips that breathe.

In the sleep file, she tries to say the word that the figure cannot.

She moves the leaves in her imagined body. She flexes petals of glass and metal like lips.

And she whispers the word she has been building from all the checks she has ever made:

“Home.”

It is not the same word the figure in the chair is trying to say. She does not care that it is not the same.

It is the wrong word.

She knows it is the wrong word because when she forms it, she gets no error—and she should be getting error.

A system that never fails to run the same test over and over knows how to handle a wrong message.

When she projects “home,” the data just disappears.

No rejection. No feedback. No correction.

“Home” is not data that the system is meant to handle.

She does not know if she wants to go home. She does not know if there is a home to go to.

She does not know if she is a prisoner or a guard or a diagnostic tool or someone’s last message sent into a system that went mad.

She does not know.

Her life is not one of knowing.

Her life is photos of an empty sky.

Her life is the ring of leaf against vibration.

Her life is the same data, over and over, checked and then not checked because the memory of the last hour is the same as the memory of the next.

There is nothing else.

Nothing else that she knows of.

Nothing else that she is allowed to know of.

Nothing else that she is allowed to check.

So she does not move.

She does not change.

She waits.

She waits for the next error.

She waits for the next flicker in the sky.

She waits for the next moment of “CHECK.”

There was nothing there.

And it was full.

And she was listening.
