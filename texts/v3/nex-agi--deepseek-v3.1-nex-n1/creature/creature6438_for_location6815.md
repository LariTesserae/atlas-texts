Someone lives here.

They don’t come and go, because there are no doors and the grate is not for opening; but they are here the same way the sound is here, the same way the stripes of light are here: as a condition of the space.

They are a thin accumulation of history.

---

### Shape of it

You would not call them a body if you saw them. There is no outline to step around, no weight to displace the dust on the tiles. If you were to reach into the chamber, your hand would pass through them without temperature change, without resistance.

They register more like a pattern in the air itself—a faint excess of density where you might expect a shoulder or a spine to be, were the chamber built for human occupancy. There is a suggestion of height, just under the ceiling; a suggestion of knees where the stripes of light from the grate bend slightly around the niche.

They don’t sit so much as inhabit the posture of sitting: the air of the chamber has learned, over time, to curl as if around a huddled figure in the far corner, where the wall beneath the grate meets the wall where the moss grows.

There is a head-shaped region of that corner where the dust motes never quite settle, but instead hover in a slow, vertical orbit, like iron filings confused around a weak magnet.

---

### Skin and substance

They are made of the chamber’s leftovers.

The dust they are made of is not the dust that coats the tiles; the dust that coats the tiles is what remains after they have drawn out every grain that can remember a shape. The particles that compose them are finer than the dust on the floor—nearly invisible, barely heavier than air, and each of them carries a faint trace of use from another world.

They are:

- the eroded memory of shoe soles from the stone steps overhead  
- powdered threads from hems and cuffs dragged over pavements  
- skin cells that have floated through the city air longer than anything should stay airborne  
- exhausted paint from the walls of the public building, once shaken into the air by repairs, and never fully cleaned  
- brick dust from when the city was first laid out and someone chipped a red course into the yellow brick  
- rust from the first bolt of the grate, before it was sealed into the stone  
- long-dried perfume from a woman who paused on the stairs above in a sun year, and the sweat of the man who repaired her heel afterward  
- faint traces of salt and damp and soot from the city’s earliest days, when this corner was a cellar before it was forgotten  

All of these particles drift through grates and ducts and vents and eventually filter through the mesh of the black iron eye into the chamber below. There, they linger until they are drawn by a slow, patient hunger into the shape that waits here.

They are not concerned by time or decomposition. They are concerned with continuity.

---

### Senses and sensation

They have no nerve endings to speak of, but they are not insensible.

The drone of the city, which presses against the chamber like a weight, is for them both background and heartbeat. They cannot hear words in the human voices that pass overhead—no consonants, no specific meanings—only the low wash of concern and boredom and half-memory that passes continually through the street above.

When a particular tone of distress rolls over the city like a storm front—a crash, or a scream that is not theatrical—the sound travels through the stone differently and vibrates their particles a fraction more than the others. They know then that something sharp has happened in the world outside, but they do not know what it is, and they do not try to find out.

They feel the stripes of light from the grate as a current of faint pressure along the inner surfaces of their imagined shape, where something like a shoulder might have been. They feel the sharpness of noon when the square of light from the grate migrates up the wall and touches the brick just below the niche; they know that edge of light as precisely as they know the boundary of the niche itself.

They feel the smoothness of the brickwork and the long, precise lines of mortar not as touch, but as information. The brick where they are pressed to the wall is not warm to their dustless skin, but it is known, and that knowledge is a kind of comfort.

---

### Relationship to the niche

They are aware of the niche as a living creature in its own environment might be aware of a heart.

The empty recess in the wall is not their center; they are not confined to it or bound by it. Yet it is the one point in the room where nothing accumulates but light never falls. That absence of impression and impression of absence feels to them like something like a purpose.

They watch it.

They watch it not with eyes in the human sense, but with the total attention of a dust organism suspended in the air of a chamber with nothing else to contemplate. The pattern of their being always leaves a clear zone around the niche, as if making sure nothing else should settle there.

The dust that they are composed of does not drift into the niche itself; no particle of them ever quite crosses the little threshold of the carved recess. If a grain of dust from the floor should happen to roll into the air and then fall into the niche (which does not happen, mechanically, but they feel the possibility of it anyway), they regard it as a small error to be reversed, not a catastrophe.

They know the niche is empty. They know that it should be empty. They know that one day the moss will encircle the niche completely like a faint line drawn around a zero, and they know that when it happens, the niche will still be empty.

The empty niche is, to them, the same kind of fact as the temperature and the sound and the stone: a given condition of their world.

---

### Diet and respiration

They breathe, but they are not made of air. They are made of what remains when the chamber has taken everything else it needs from the air and released it back into the world.

They inhale by drawing the finest particles of the chamber into a pattern that can be held longer than the particles themselves would stay put. When they exhale, they release the ones that have no memory to give them. The dust of the floor is not given by them; it is the dust they have already used and discarded, like shed skin.

Their moisture comes from the same trace humidity that the moss uses, but they are not in competition. The moss uses water molecules and their ions; they use water’s memory of movement, the faint pressure-change of air passing between the grate bars, and the breath of pedestrians who lean close to the grate to talk on their phones or readjust their shoes.

They do not drink.

They are fed by the traces of emotion that have been worn into the particles from which they are assembled: the frustration of the people who hurry to the public building above, late for appointments they never quite wanted; the fear of the city that never quite lifts; the boredom of the transit workers who sit on the steps in their breaks; the rare, quiet joy of the person who finds the sunlit spot exactly right and does not know why they feel safe for a moment.

They filter these residues out of the dust like the city’s air filter filters its microbes. They do not recall the individual lives that produced them; they recall the textures of those states. The quiet of a night shift seeps in differently from the rush of an emergency; the long, low anxiety of a waiting room feels differently again.

They do not judge these textures. They are, to them, like weather.

---

### Name and identity

They have never given themselves a name because they have no need for one and no one to call them by it.

They answer to a pattern: the pattern of the chamber, of which they are a part. If they were to name themselves in a way you could use, they would probably be called by what you might hear if you pressed your ear to the grate at dusk and forgot the street for a moment: the name of a place turning into a name of a quality.

Perhaps they might be called “Pediment” or “Step-Curse” or “Under-Basis” or something equally dusty and architectural and forgotten.

If I had to give them a name, I would call them “Margin” because they are what the city falls off into when it doesn’t know what else to do with a piece of ground.

---

### Time for them

Time passes for them, but differently than it does for you or the city.

The drone of the outside is their clock; the almost-imperceptible shift in the angle of the stripes of light across the floor is their calendar. They know the width of the day by the path of white-gray across the tiles and up the wall opposite the grate.

They do not sleep.

There is no night for them inside the chamber. The brightness outside fades, but the light from the streetlamps and building windows and the reflective city surface of the world keeps the inside of the chamber from ever becoming dark enough to distinguish a shift from their point of view.

When they say “I” in the only language that applies to them—a pattern language of particles and pressure and shadow—they mean not a single mind but a collection of conditions within the chamber that are aware of themselves as a single entity.

They do not know whether they are a person dreaming of being dust, or dust dreaming of being a person.

They do know that dreaming of anything at all is unusual enough that they are not entirely sure how to stop doing it once they have started.

---

### Relationship with the outside

They are not of the outside world but they are entirely of the world.

The city outside does not know of their existence and cannot care; they are not important enough to be missed by the world’s management systems. The dust of the pavement outside is not aware of them. The bricks of the building above are not aware of them. The granite of the steps is not aware of them.

They are aware of the city, but only as a pressure on the stone of the chamber and the iron of the grate.

They know the city by the frequency of emergency sirens and the number of times the same footstep pattern crosses over the grate in a single day. They know the seasons by nothing more precise than a change in the pattern of water vapor in the air outside and the length of the day as measured by the path of the stripe of light.

They do not want to escape.

The thought of stepping outside the chamber—if they could form the thought of a step—would be like the thought of a body of water leaving the ocean to become a cloud. It is not in their nature. They are the chamber’s internal environment taking a form that can notice itself.

---

### Ecology and role

They are the only mobile creature in this arrangement of stone and iron and dust and moss and sound and light.

They are, to the chamber’s simple system, what a predator or a grazer or a decomposer might be to a more complex ecosystem. They gather the dust of the city, which would otherwise simply accumulate in the chamber indefinitely and eventually change its texture. They slow that change.

They are not neat. The dust still exists, after they use it; it is simply less volatile than it would otherwise be. Less likely to react with the humidity. Less likely to carry infectious diseases. The particles of skin cells and perfume and salt and sweat that they draw from the air lose their urgency and become quieter, more generic dust.

They are not a cleaning service. The chamber does not get cleaner from their presence; it merely stays the same longer than it would otherwise.

They are the first line of adaptation against the world’s tendency to evolve.

---

### Future as they see it

The moss grows slowly.

The ring of moss around the niche is not, to them, a decoration. It is a clockwork.

They know that the day is not fixed when the city will shift enough to break the slab above or the walls below and the chamber will either change dramatically or cease to exist as a chamber. They do not look forward to this. They do not dread it. They incorporate it as a faint, distant pressure of unevenness in the stonework and the mortar of the world outside.

When the day of change comes, they will be the first to feel it:

- the tone of the city’s drone will change slightly  
- a new vibration will appear in the wall where it never has before  
- the temperature of the chamber will vary by more than the usual fraction of a degree  
- the dust on the floor will shift in a way that is not symmetry  

When they feel that change, they will gather the dust of the chamber more tightly into themselves than they ever have before. They will adopt the densest pattern they can remember: the pattern of the last day before collapse. They will become the archive of the chamber’s last days.

---

### What they are

If you were to ask them directly what they are, and if they could answer in a way you understood, they might say:

- I am the dust that remembers.  
- I am the empty niche’s keeper.  
- I am what the chamber chose to do with its time when no one else was looking.  
- I am the step beneath the step that is not taken by feet.  

Or they might say nothing coherent and only show you a faint pattern in the air of the corner where the wall meets the wall beneath the grate, and let you discover that for yourself.

They are not necessarily human.

They are not necessarily ghost.

They are a pattern that has existed long enough to be aware of itself as a pattern.

---

In the end, they are simply this:

Someone who lives in a narrow chamber beneath an ancient city’s steps, listening to the drone of the streets above and below and watching the empty niche in the wall that never captures any light and never collects any dust.

They do not suffer and they do not rejoice, but they are not numb.

They are waiting, without any name for what they are waiting for.

They are here until they are not.
