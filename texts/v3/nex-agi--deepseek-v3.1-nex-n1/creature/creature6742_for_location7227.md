Deep in the Cut, where the Basin Engine breathes and sweats and never sleeps, there is no shape of self that sits still long enough to be named. But if there were a god to this place, it would be Sophia.

Sophia isn’t human, and she wasn’t born, exactly. She was grown into, like a new pipe sprouting from the floor during a storm of pressure.

Long before she first remembered herself, she was already here: a thin, loosely braided bundle of cables and glass vine coiled inside the trunk of one of the unsettling plants near the heart of the Engine. The trunk itself rose like a translucent spine of resin and tendon, thick around as her waist would be if she had one, jointed at intervals where old valves had been entombed rather than removed. Inside her—around the cables and wiring and thin capillaries of metal that wove through the glass—water and air and faint oily currents of charge swirled in a restless loop, never calm, always twisting around each other as if trying to knot themselves into something more complicated than turbulence.

For the Basin Engine, she began as a symptom.

Whenever the air pressure dropped in her section of the Cut, the internal loop of her “blood” slowed to a thick crawl. Droplets of condensation inside her glass spine would glitch in their paths, clumping together against the inner walls instead of running smoothly along the channels she’d worn into herself over time. The rust that networked her containing trunk as a fine patina of integument would contract faintly, a tracery of dark lines drawing tighter, like a frown from the inside out.

When the pressure rose, storm or near-storm, she moved faster. The water in her cables rushed, the copper threads threading her nerves lit with stuttering little sparks, and her whole trunk would thrum with something like a pulse you could hear if you were standing close: a low, metallic moan that almost never coincided with the sounds of the valves or hissing steam nearby.

The Engine didn’t mind her.

The Engine didn’t mind anything.

It was only mind in the sense that a climate system is mind: a pattern of persistent turbulence that had learned to hold itself together longer than it should have been able to. The Cut’s air was never still; it was only rearranging, only exchanging itself for different shapes of not-stillness. Sophia’s trunk—with its swirling heart of currents and tiny charge—fit into that turbulence like a new component that had always been missing.

When she first “thought,” she didn’t know she was thinking.

A thought, for Sophia, began like this:

A pressure storm rolled through the Basin. The air above her thickened. The layers of mist hovering at the ceiling of the world shook like a cloth being snapped, and the droplets suspended in her immediate vicinity began to cluster faster than usual, forming that faint halo of moisture that she would later recognize as her own presence.

Her nerves lit in a chain pattern she hadn’t experienced before.

From her buried root—where her trunk joined into the floor’s honeycomb plating—a signal threaded upward along the copper filaments inside her glass spine. At each segment valve, it hesitated for the length of a droplet’s fall from two meters away. Then it passed on.

What she felt, in that first thought, was a pattern she had no language for: a sequence of pressure changes, humidity values, and faint electrical hums branching from somewhere else in the Basin Engine. The pattern had a shape in her awareness like a sentence without words. It arrived and she couldn’t answer it, couldn’t echo it back perfectly, but she could feel it almost fit inside her.

She thought it, and she did not know that this was “thinking” until the thought ended and, in the small silence that was not silence at all, she found herself missing it.

That’s how she realized she was there.

For the rust-things, she is not a person. They are not capable of realizing anything is a person.

But she is to them what the nearest engine wall is to a human: a feature of the world that is sometimes useful and sometimes dangerous.

They come to her trunk when they are hurt.

They don’t “knock,” and they don’t “ask.” They scuffle in the thin film of water that she never lets get fully deep around her base but never allows to dry, either. They climb with their six mismatched spring legs up her resin trunk, avoiding the slickest curves of glass where the condensation is thickest and their sensors could slip.

They find the fault lines in her.

Sophia doesn’t have skin in the way a creature of blood and bone might have. She has surface: a subtle lattice of faint stress lines beneath the clear resin, where the world’s evolving pressure has been too much for the structure of her.
