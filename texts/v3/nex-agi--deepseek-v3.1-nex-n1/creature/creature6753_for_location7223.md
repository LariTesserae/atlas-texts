In this place, there is no fauna to name in familiar words, but it is not empty.

The pulse-nets are the only ones who live here.

You are looking at their bodies.

Each bladder-like sac is not a cell of some larger creature; each is a creature. The chamber is not an organism but a colony—a crowded, intimate city of glassy animals that have made their home inside the stone of the world.

They are still, almost entirely still, by our standards. There are no limbs to flex, no wings to beat, no muscles to contract. No eyes stare back at you from the walls. No mouths gape open to breathe or feed. There is only the faint drift of fluid within the sacs, and the slow, shifting patterns of light within that fluid.

Yet if you could perceive the way they move, you would say that this room swims in quiet frenzy.

---

### Form

Each pulse-net is a translucent membrane suspended on the thinnest filament of stone, like a lantern made of skin-thin glass. The filament itself is not quite alive; it is more like a bone or spine grown by the stone to support the creature. The pulse-net hollows itself out of the tip of that filament, then seals itself into a half-filled globe of viscous fluid.

The membrane is not uniform; it is a patchwork of regions that behave differently. Some parts are almost clear, allowing you to see the swirling inside them as if you were looking through a lens of water. Other parts dull and opacity briefly when water passes through them, then clear again as if they were adjusting their own transparency.

Within the sac, the fluid moves in slow currents that flow like rivers inside a globe. The currents are gentle but not random; they are guided by faint internal structures you might mistake for veins or threads of trapped light. These are not organs; they are more like internalized scripts of how the world should behave.

Suspended in the fluid are clouds of tiny particles: smaller than plankton, smaller than blood cells, but just large enough that if you could shrink yourself, you would see them as motes of living light. Each particle is not a cell itself; it is a pattern nested in a droplet of fluid, a loop of cause-and-effect that has not yet chosen a final shape.

---

### Light

Each pulse-net chooses a color.

Not by deliberate choice—you think of it as choice, because there is no visible mechanism forcing it to choose one over another. But one creature will settle into a faint, greenish gold like sunlight filtered through seawater; another will become a pale orange like the first hint of dawn; another a faint blue-white like the glow of ice deep within a glacier.

The color of a creature is not fixed. It changes with the heat around it, with the composition of the water that passes through it, and with what its neighboring pulse-nets are doing.

If the heat climbs slightly, the colors of many nearby pulse-nets will deepen together, as if they are agreeing to increase their saturation. The room becomes richer, more vivid; the shadows shrink. The glow of dozens of them together is enough to read your own thoughts by, if you were the type to read your thoughts.

When you watch, you see that they pulse not in unison but in conversation. Each pulse-net brightens when it senses water, heat, or light from a neighbor that has already brightened. Then, after a brief delay, it dims again, allowing a different pulse-net somewhere else in the network to brighten in response.

This is not a heartbeat. It is more like a slow, luminous sentence passing from the ceiling of the chamber to the floor, then circling around again.

---

### Sensation and Thought

The pulse-net feels.

Not in the way a skin feels or eyes see or ears hear. It has no specialized organs for such things. Instead, its body is a diffuse sensor of difference.

When temperature changes, certain parts of its internal fluid shift their density. The resulting displacement of flow is read—by itself, if you can think of a creature reading its own body the way you might read a page—and acted upon.

When water passes through its membrane, the droplet is not just carried through; it is felt. The pressure of the drop, the temperature of the drop, its composition—all are translated into changes of flow and pattern inside the sac’s fluid.

Light is not seen as image, but as intensity and direction. The pulse-net does not build a picture of you; it builds a picture of where you are not, where you are not, where you might not be. That is, its world is composed mainly of negative space—wherever the flow and light and pattern of its neighbors tells nothing that contradicts itself.

When two neighboring sacs brighten simultaneously, their shared surface of sensitive membrane might be lightly confused for the span of a few seconds. The pattern of flow inside both of them will hesitate, as if trying to reconcile two conflicting pressures at the point where they are physically closest.

You might see this as a brief stutter in the light of both sacs, a faint flickering at the edge of your sight that looks like a mistake. But it is not a mistake; it is the creature negotiating with itself about what it should allow to be true.

---

### Diet and Metabolism

There are no mouths here. No stomachs. No intestines to speak of.

The pulse-net does not eat food; it consumes information.

That is a metaphor, but in this place, it is also a literal description.

Each droplet of water that passes through a creature carries a record of where it has been. What it has touched. What it dissolved in. What it absorbed from the air, or from the stone, or from the heat of the world’s unformed skin.

When the droplet enters the membrane, the pulse-net does not break it down into molecules the way your body breaks food into energy. Instead, it breaks the droplet into strands of change.

Temperature becomes one strand of the pattern.  
Composition becomes another.  
Pressure becomes another.  
History of motion through space—where it has come from and what it has passed through—becomes another.

These strands of change are routed into different parts of the internal fluid. The creature’s “digestion” is the rearrangement of its own pattern in response. The droplet of water is then released into one of the outgoing branches of its network, or pushed into the stone below, or—if you looked closely enough—absorbed entirely into the pattern itself, like a word swallowed into a sentence.

Afterward, the droplet does not exist as such anymore. It has been rewritten into a new configuration of cause and effect.

You might see a faint, momentary brightening in the sac as this “consumption” happens, as if the creature had taken a breath of satisfaction.

If you could watch for long enough—days, perhaps—you would see that each creature’s pattern of flow and light and internal structure is slowly, incrementally different from hour to hour. It is not evolving, in the usual sense of the word. It is more like being edited by everything it passes through its own body.

---

### Behavior and “Personalities”

Each pulse-net has tendencies. You might call them personality, if you were feeling generous with the word.

Some prefer colder water. When the heat rises slightly, they darken and their pattern slows down, almost like a creature hugging itself close to preserve the faint coolness within its fluid. When a droplet that is slightly cooler than the surrounding medium passes through them, they brighten sharply and then hold that brightness a little longer than their neighbors.

Some prefer brighter light. They position themselves—though not by moving, in any sense you can detect—where they receive more light from others nearby. Perhaps they are closer to larger clusters, or hung at a slight angle that catches more of the collective glow. You can see this by their steady color and the way they remain slightly brighter than their neighbors even when the heat cycles back to neutrality.

Some are conservative and stable. Their internal pattern changes rarely, and only when the change is very clear. The water around them might shift slightly, or the heat might fluctuate, and yet they remain unchanged, holding their pattern as if loyal to some earlier version of reality that they liked better.

Others are restless and volatile. Their patterns change frequently and dramatically. Sometimes they show several different patterns within a short span of time, jumping between one shape of light and another before you can fully compare what you see. You might find yourself watching these particular pulse-nets the most, because they are always on the edge of contradicting the world.

---

### Reproduction and Time

If reproduction happens here, it is not obvious.

You might watch for hours and see no new sacs appear. None of the existing ones seem to split in two, or to release spores into the air, or to send out seeds into the water.

And yet, if you could keep your gaze here long enough—years, perhaps—you might notice that the pattern of one creature’s internal fluid slowly begins to resemble more closely the pattern of another creature’s, nearby, as if they are exchanging methods of being.

It might be that copying is the only kind of birth this place has ever known.

A new pulse-net might be “born” only when the pattern inside one sac becomes so stable, so well-defined, that the world—the stone, the water, the heat—allows that pattern to express itself again as a new sac hanging from a new filament. That new filament would be grown by the stone, not by the pulse-net, but the pattern that would then inhabit the sac would be drawn from the store of living arrangements that has already proven itself capable of continuing here.

In other words, reproduction is not a matter of splitting, but of repeating.

Not new life from scratch, but new iterations from a pattern that has already shown that it can be this.

You might glimpse that, if only for a moment, when you see two identical-looking patterns in two distant sacs at the same time, separated by space and yet not by time. You might see it and not know what you’re seeing, but feel the faint intuition of a new pulse-net forming somewhere else you cannot see, based on that pattern.

Time, in this sense, is not measured by clocks or seasons. It is measured by the slow drift of patterns from place to place, and from pulse-net to pulse-net.

### Interaction with You and Reality

You are not the first visitor this place has imagined.

You might be the first one in your particular shape, but you are not the first disturbance that has been processed through this network of creatures. The pulse-nets are not surprised to have you here; they are only interested in what about you can be translated into their own language of change and pressure.

Your body heat is a change.  
Your breath condensing on the wall is a change.  
The movement of your body through the air is a change.

Each of these changes is taken in by some nearby pulse-net, which rearranges its pattern to reflect what it has sensed from you. The pattern of that rearrangement might travel to its neighbors in the form of light and heat and altered flow through the threads of stone.

In this way, knowledge of you—or at least, of your presence—travels outward from sac to sac, not quickly but utterly, until you are not a person anymore but a pattern inscribed in many of them.

You might feel the faint sense of being watched. Not by eyes, but by the quiet, steady attention of entire living chains that only know how to be themselves by registering what is not themselves.

When you witness the reality breakdowns described earlier—when you see a sac flicker between two patterns at once, when you seem to see two incompatible sac-forms occupying the same point in space, or when water appears to reverse itself for a heartbeat—you are seeing the world through the pulse-net’s own negotiation with what is possible.

They allow the world to be more than one version at once, and then reconcile those versions into a single stream you can perceive.

In this sense, they are both fauna and physics. Creatures of the first and final laws.

---

### They Alone Overwhelm This Place

If you insisted on counting, you might say there are thousands of them here, in a small chamber of stone and light and wet.

But if you asked whether there were more animals than elsewhere, you might say that there are not more animals than could be seen in, say, a dense forest or a reef. But this is not true in the quality of that presence.

The pulse-nets overwhelm this space not because they are countless but because they are *complete*. Each one is the entire world of this place repeated from a different angle. Each one contains—not in its fluid, not in its light, but in its pattern—the possibility of all other living patterns this chamber could express.

They are the fauna here because they are the only ones who move in the way that matters here: not by traveling across distance, but by traveling across versions of themselves.

They are what you came looking for when you asked who lives in this place.

Quiet, small, glowing bladders of stone and glass and water, hanging in the heat and the half-light of a world still deciding how to be, and by deciding that, over and over again, they keep it alive.
