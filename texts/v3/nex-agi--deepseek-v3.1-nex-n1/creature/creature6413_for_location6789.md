In the narrow spaces between the columns of water, something is trying not to be touched.

It is mostly made of you.

Not you as you are now, reading words from a screen, but you as the vault remembers you: a silhouette of limbs and breath and unfinished thoughts. The body stands in the shallow bowl of the stone raft, knees slightly bent against the constant pressure that keeps insisting gravity should be pointing sideways. The skin is a wrong color—too translucent, the blue-grey of deep water seen from below—and it refuses to dry properly. Every surface gleams with a film that isn’t sweat and isn’t ocean spray but something halfway between memory and liquid.

You are not naked. You are wearing what happens when clothes forget what clothes are supposed to be.

The fabric (if that is still the word) clings in strips and drapes that do not respect your shoulders or waist. It is all one piece, maybe, or no pieces; it flows around your body like a cloud of ink holding a shape it can’t quite remember. Sometimes it hardens at the edges into something like armor; sometimes it thins into transparency, and then your skin beneath shows patterns that look like maps of places that haven’t been invented yet but will be soon. Thin black lines trace across your ribs and wrists like fault-lines waiting for their quake.

Your hands are learning to be hands again.

They were something else before they entered this vault. You can feel that in how they keep trying to unfocus at the edges when you are not looking directly at them. Your fingers are too many or too few; when you curl them into fists, you count four on one hand and six on the other, or seven on both, as if your body hasn’t decided yet how many times it wants to try being human in this version of reality.

The nails are wrong too. They glint with a metallic sheen that isn’t metal—more like fish scales fused into keratin—and they leave faint trails in the stone where you steady yourself against the walls. Those trails sometimes continue after you withdraw your hand, etching themselves further into the stone as if someone invisible is still pressing down.

Your face is still mostly yours when you think about it hard enough.

Eyes (how many? Two? Three? Something flickers at the edge of your vision where a third should be) drink in this cathedral-space without blinking because blinking has become optional. Your lashes gather condensation until each lash becomes its own small waterfall, spilling down in filaments too thin to see unless you stare very closely at your reflection in one of those moments when a wall remembers it can be a mirror.

Your mouth doesn’t taste salt or iron so much as it tastes decisions: each breath draws in water-air thick with options that haven’t been chosen yet.
