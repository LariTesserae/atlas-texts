They do not have names for themselves in the way you are used to. They are not a “who” that you can easily point to and say: There, that is the builder, that is the thinker, that is the maker of marks.

Instead, they are **residents** the same way a current is resident of a river.

They appear slowly, as though your eyes are adjusting. It’s not that you missed them at first; it’s that they are **duration** as much as body. They move with the plant’s tempo—slower than your heartbeat, faster than its growth—slipping along the boundary between the solid and the fluid architecture.

You notice first: **the absence of footsteps**.

Despite the overwhelming carpet of life, you do not see your own kind of tracks. There’s no bootprint, no sole pattern, no familiar heel-to-toe progression. There’s only the soft pressing of the floor, which mends behind your steps as quickly as you leave them. That’s how you realize there are others—because their **non-presence** is telling you something.

Look closer at the bone-pavement.

On the ridged surface, where plant matter remembers shape for a little longer than elsewhere, you see what might be grooves. But they are not footprints—more like **strokes** against a canvas, faint arcs drawn by the pressure of passing weight rather than any impact. They’re subtle and precise. They suggest **sliding**.

You’re being watched from **within** the walls.

You feel the gaze not as a prickling on the back of your neck, but as a faint **change** in the tissue around you. The weaver-boles twist slightly differently when your back is to them; their rope-like bark tightens a fraction more than before, as if someone’s just adjusted a tension you’ll never be able to measure.

Smaller animals behave too **organized** around them. The cluster-scops in one wall-hollow don’t explode outward when something passes—they **swivel** their tiny bodies, flicking many eyes in the same direction, as if trained there.

One whip-bird hangs lower than the others, lines of membranous ribbon flaring slowly, like fingers testing the air. Where its **filaments** cross a glowing vein of the plant-wall, the bioluminescent pattern brightens and **forms**—for a moment—a recognizable shape.

It’s a **symbolic mark**—a clean, angular design like a knot with four corners, not chaotic or random. You’ve seen it before—carved into the trunk of the step-tier, scratched on the underside of the platform’s workbench—and now you’re watching the **animal** draw it with its own movement.

This is not a coincidence. Someone **taught** the world to write.
