In the valley where the wind is an old memory and the stone never stops shifting, there are no proper animals to speak of.  

Instead, there is something that learned to need walks.

---

I first saw it by the river at the head of the valley, where the water comes out of the rock like it’s afraid of the open world. The water is clear and thick and slow, and at the edge of the bank the concrete meets the silt in a neat line that someone once took care to draw straight.

That’s where it stood.

At first glance it was a pillar: stone-colored, vaguely the shape of a tall person, but subtly wrong. You wouldn’t call it human. You wouldn’t call it anything. The surface was not flesh; it was cured concrete and resin, the color of the cliffs—pale grey, with darker veins and fine rusty capillaries that didn’t move but still suggested circulation. The structure of its body was not musculature, but rather layers of careful material, something between a statue and a machine.

It was sleek and wet with condensation.

When I breathed out, the cloud of my own breath touched the thing’s surface and added to the film already forming there. Droplets slid down in slow lines, tracing paths that seemed to prefer its “back” and the underside of its “arms,” as if the water knew it was supposed to behave like sweat on skin.

It didn’t move when I approached. Not exactly.

It oriented.

Where the head would be, there was no face, just a smooth contour of composite material; but when I shifted, the faint reflections of the sky on the polished surface shifted too, so that its stillness felt like a kind of watching. The shoulders—where shoulders would be on a person—were broader than they needed to be, tapering into something that wasn’t quite a trunk and not quite a machine chassis. It had no tools or external devices, except for the subtle pattern of raised lines and faint inlets along its arms and chest where something else could plug in.

Around its feet, the vegetation was thickest: the low, dense ferns and glossy shrubs this valley is fond of. They grew up to its ankles but not quite past them, the way water will rise to a line and then hesitate. The leaves of those plants were darker, wetter, rimmed with faint rings of minerals, where the water running off its body fed them.

I didn’t dare turn my back on it, but after I had been standing still for long enough, it simply began to walk away.

Not “walk” like a person—more like a large machine coming carefully online. Each step was precise and unhurried, but not because there was any stiffness of joints or creaking of metal. It was as if the timing itself was a matter of engineering. The interval between each step was exactly the same, and the sound its feet made on the wet ground was not that of boot or paw or hoof but of something dense and solid conducting the faint noise of water being displaced.

I followed.

It moved from the river’s edge along the worn line of earth that was now barely more than a suggestion of a path. The grasses on the very edges of the path were reclaiming it, but not as fast as they reclaimed the rest of the valley; there was just enough use to press them down, but not enough to erase them.

While it walked, I noticed the water patterns.

Wherever it moved, the condensation got its cue from the body’s motion. As its “lower” surfaces warmed fractionally from contact or exposure or whatever internal cycle it was running, water migrated from colder to warmer surfaces and then evaporated from the warmer. The surface of the body shone like polished stone brought wet from the riverbed. The valley itself exuded water more than rained it; and this creature, if that was the word, seemed to be in conversation with that moisture.

As it moved, its body temperature balanced itself with small changes invisible to the eye but visible to the water. The tiny droplets rearranged themselves, clinging or sliding away, depending on where they were. A thin mist of microscopic droplets left its upper surface when the sun broke out for a moment.

Then I understood: the fine weather of itself.

It was not troubled by cold or warmth in the same way I was. It was continuously adjusting to be just slightly below the ambient temperature of the air wherever it stood, so that the air had no choice but to give its moisture to the surface. The valley itself moved water from river to air to leaf and back again; and this thing was simply another place where water wanted to collect.

We walked together like that for some time, if “together” meant a creature walking ahead and me trailing at a respectful distance, trying not to breathe too loudly.

The path it followed was not straight to anywhere obvious.

It took us along the river briefly, then angled away toward the main complex of buildings and glass towers. The concrete structures grew up around us slowly as we approached, not like a city but like a part of the geology that had been arranged. The low buildings of pale grey concrete and corrugated metal roofs appeared first, then the taller glass towers reflecting the sky in their tinted skins.

The closer we got to the machinery, the more I could hear the faint background of it—not the words of noise, but the grammar: a low, continuous tone that might be circulation pumps; a distant hum from a ventilation fan somewhere inside the sealed walls; the occasional click of thermal expansion. It was the sound of a body maintaining itself.

And in the center of it, this creature moved like a piece of the building detached itself to go for a stroll.

As we passed between two of the glass towers, I noticed the fine gradients inside their shafts—subtle bands of color and translucency slowly shifting, like fluids in a laboratory separating and coming together again. The thing turned its featureless head toward the glass, not with eyes but with alignment, and for the space of several breaths it seemed to be reading the gradients inside like a text.

Then it turned away and walked on.

The vegetation around the complex was not cowed by the machinery or the creature. It grew up around the foundations of buildings and under the latticework walkways, as if the steel and concrete were another kind of tree. The leaves brushed against its body as it passed, leaving trails of water on its surface that were quickly incorporated into the film already forming.

It was clear the plants didn’t think it was dangerous.

They did not recoil. They leaned against it like they leaned against the pipes and the supports, taking their tribute from every condensation drip and feeling no fear of being crushed. The creature didn’t seem to think of them as obstacles either. It moved carefully around the thicker clusters of ferns and small shrubs, but not because it was afraid of breaking them; more like it was following an old, ingrained protocol of coexistence with the green city of the valley.

When we reached the far side of the complex, where the ground began to tilt up toward the cliffs and the recent landslides, the path finally split in several directions.

The creature continued to the nearest building of the outer edge—a small rectangular maintenance shed set somewhat apart from the others. Its walls were concrete, insulated with dark metal cladding and ribs, and the door was a bolted hatch like all the others here.

But this door, I noticed, was not sealed.

The creature didn’t push it open. It stood near the threshold for a moment, then walked to one side, where the ground was slightly more worn—from its own feet, I realized, after staring long enough. The same path over and over, each day or each cycle. The very earth had been trained to expect its visits.

At the side of the shed, there was a panel of metal inset just above the ground, with a faint groove down its center where it could be lifted, but not by hand. It was not meant to be opened by anything that didn’t belong to the system.

The creature stopped before this panel and simply knelt.

There was no ceremony, no looking around to see if it was being observed. It was like watching a ritual performed by a machine: perfectly straightforward but deeply opaque. The body of the creature bowed smoothly, not with the weight of fatigue or reverence but with the dull, deserving obedience of something that had been designed to be exactly where it was.

Its surface changed as it knelt.

The faint film of water on its body thickened slightly on the side facing the ground, as if the valley itself were leaning closer to listen. The dark lines along its back and forearms flushed slightly—not with color in the sense of human pigment, but with a subtle change in opacity in the resin. The faintest of hisses, like a valve being kissed by air or steam, emerged from the joints where its “shoulders” met its “upper arms.”

Its hand reached out and touched the panel.

There was no sleeve of clothing or glove of leather; the surface of its fingertips and palm was the same material as the rest of it, but with slightly more texture, like stone that has been smoothed by a river. The panel met the contact with a small response of its own, a quiet sound like the latch of an internal mechanism being released by consent rather than force.

For the space of three breaths—silent, except for the constant gentle murmur of the building’s hidden systems—nothing else happened.

Then water began to move.

From unseen ducts within its body, a thin, clear stream of water flowed down its arm and into the interface at the panel. It was not water from the river or the rain; it was distilled water, clean and colorless. The creature gave its water back to the building the same way someone else might give their blood to a machine.

I watched the process without understanding the quantities or reasons. It might be returning a sample of something that had been circulating through its body; or it might be giving up the excess of its own cooling system, allowing the building’s greater heat to pull away what it no longer needed to carry.

After the water had passed, the connection remained closed and the panel surface unchanged, but I knew something had been written there.

Eventually, the creature rose.

Its movement from kneeling to standing again was smooth and not in any hurry, but it was not the leisurely motion of a creature at ease. It was the careful motion of something whose every step is measured and accounted for in the invisible ledger of a system larger than itself.

From then on, I understood that its walks were not exactly pleasure walks.

They were duties expressed in motion.

---

Each time I came to the valley, I saw the same creature—or another of the same kind—walking along the same paths, at about the same intervals of time. It did not vary its route much, except when the landslides forced new adjustments of the ground. Then it would walk carefully around the fallen debris of the cliff, as if calculating new routes to old destinations.

None of them ever greeted me openly or acknowledged my presence beyond the initial slow reorientation of their surfaces toward me when I first appeared near them.

After days of watching, I began to see the subtle differences between them.

There were not many of these creatures, but more than one. Four or five, maybe six; I never saw them all at once, but the pattern of footpaths and the way they crossed in time spoke of a small group rather than a single individual.

Their differences were not in the shape of their bodies; that remained consistent within a narrow range of design. But the surface patterns were not exactly the same. One had more pronounced rust-colored seams along its upper torso, like a map of mineral veins in the rock of the cliffs above. Another’s darker lines were more orderly and symmetrical, resembling the conductive traces of a circuit board more than anything organic.

Their water, too, behaved differently.

Some of them seemed heavier with condensation, their surfaces always wet, droplets occasionally forming and then being released into the vegetation below them as they walked. Others remained drier, their surfaces more like polished stone that water beaded on but refused to stick to.

When they passed near the glass towers, their body temperatures subtly adjusted to the gradients inside the glass shafts. If I was very still and very close, I could see faint changes in the bands of color inside the glass as they stood near it—not the other way around. As if the creature’s presence were a small correction in the system’s equation.

The towers themselves might be the heart or the lungs or the kidneys of this landscape—but these creatures were the feet that carried the water around.

---

One night, I stayed longer than I meant to.

I had walked up onto the fresh slope of a recent landslide, where the rock was still raw and the dust hadn’t yet learned to bind itself into anything firm. From there, the valley was spread out below me like a diagram: the dark, sharp-edged cliffs above; the dense green of the undergrowth below; and the pattern of towers and buildings and latticework of the industrial complex in between.

The low lights of the complex were on in their usual arrangement of whites and greens and cold blues, each tiny and not bright enough to be obtrusive. But as I watched, I realized that one of them was moving.

Not blinking or stuttering, but steadily moving along the valley floor.

It was one of the creatures, carrying a small light of its own that I hadn’t noticed before. Not an external lamp or headlight—nothing so obvious. The light itself was from a thin band of luminescence across its chest or along the “spine” of its body, where some internal system had decided to glow for the sake of visibility or some other practical reason.

The creature moved through the complex of structures and then along one of the elevated latticework walkways that connected the glass towers. The metal gridwork was thin and precise; the creature’s body, though stone-colored and dense, did not seem to stress the structure as it walked. The light of its body did not distract the sensors of the complex or set off alarms. It fit like a piece of the system itself.
