What dwells here is more than its inhabitants; the basin city itself is a form of life—cold and fluid and unhurried.

---

The city is not empty.

It is filled with a people who have never known metal.

---

### The People Without Metal

They are slender, but not fragile.

Their bones are thin and dense, like the water‑worked stone of their basin—strong, but not meant for sudden stress. Their frames are compact, built for the tight latticework of their homes: narrow shoulders, hips that can twist sideways, knees that bend comfortably into shallow, water‑filled channels.

Their skin is the colour of wet stone: pale grey‑white with faint blue undertones, like the chalk veins in the obsidian they carve. It is translucent enough to show faint traceries of blue veins beneath—smaller channels than the city’s, but just as constant. They are perpetually damp not from sweat but from condensation; the air thickens on them like dew on rock.

They wear what they need and no more—short layered wraps of moss‑cloth, tightly woven from the older, shedding layers of the wall‑growths. The colours are dark green and black‑green, with faint hints of yellow where the veins of the original moss run through. Nothing is fast‑fashioned or brittle; everything is worn until it becomes part of another layer’s food.

Their feet, when they can be seen, are bare and broad, toes spread wide for balance on slick stone. Each foot has faint suction ridges along the arch, like the pads of a salamander, but softer. They do not swim; they slide. When they move quickly, they bend low and let the water film carry them along the natural channels, walking like skaters on an invisible current.

Their eyes are large, round, and almost entirely pupil, black enough to reflect the faint glow of the city’s bioluminescence. They do not squint because there is no bright sun here; instead, they gaze evenly, without blinking more often than necessary. The corners of their eyes always glisten faintly with moisture.

They move quietly because they have never heard noise made to order: no whistles, no bells, no shouting. Sounds here are slow to form and slow to die.

### Social Structure: Living by the Water’s Rule

Density without pressure.

They are a city of people pressed close together—but the pressure is not from crowding; it is from stone. Stone presses the city into shape; water bends the stone into pathways; the people slide between them.

There are no kings in this basin, but leadership exists as water exists—moving, always, but in predictable ways.

Certain families are known to live along the main channels of the basin floor where water flows most strongly. These families are not rulers by decree but by default. They are the Record Keepers of the water’s rhythm: they know when the drains will run thick, when the ceiling will weep more, when the stone channels are likely to overflow onto certain walkways.

They live in the places where others must pass if they want to move upward from one chamber to another without backtracking. The thresholds are carved narrow, not by accident but by design—compliance to the water’s bias, not by human law.

The city’s “social map” is not on paper or stone tablets but in the collective memory encoded in the architecture: where a threshold widens unexpectedly, it means that some generations ago, the people of that chamber and the next came to an agreement. The stone remembers longer than they do.

Old habit is their law.

### Language That Slides Like Water

They have a language of sound, but it is not like surface languages.

There are no “sharp” words here.

Their consonants are softened by water: every “p” becomes a “b,” every “t” becomes a “d,” softened at the edges and trailed into the next sound by a faint exhale. The rhythm is uneven like the dripping of the ceiling—rounded words landing every now and then, with long pauses, where nothing resists but the flow.

They are more comfortable with silence than with speech.

When they must speak—to give direction, to warn of water rising high at a point, to confirm a trade of moss‑cloth for food colour—their voice-pattern follows the water:

- Short sentences fall like drops from the ceiling:  
  - "Here."  
  - "Go."  
  - "Wait."

- Long sentences flow like the channels carved beneath the floor:  
  - "Under the left wall, where the stone whispers…"  
  - "The channel grows thin before it thickens."

Their meaning often lies in what is not said: in the way their body is positioned relative to the water channel, in the direction they point with the side of their head, not with their eyes.

There are songs, but they are not sung for entertainment.

They are sung to remember the paths of water.

As children, they are taught long sequences of syllables that describe the flow of water from the edges of the basin toward the deepest chamber, where the water disappears through unknown pores into the lower rock. If they forget the song, their feet might still remember—but the song forces the mind to remember the path of the water, not the path of light.

### Daily Work: The Gentle Cycle of Labour

They are busy without frenzy.

The city provides no sudden harvests and no sudden calamities. Everything grows slow, including the work.

**The Gatherers**

Every day, small groups of Gatherers move along the walls with their stone‑blade knives.

The blades are sharpened flakes of stone, painstakingly worked from the harder veins of the bedrock. They have no metal handles; they are wrapped in thin moss‑cloth that is soaked in water and then dried to shrink tight. The blades are small enough to be gentle with the moss and careful with the stone.

Gatherers do not cut the living moss deeply.

They are like the city itself: they never take the whole layer. Only the older parts of the moss—sections that have already begun to shed—are removed. These are taken back to the chambers and woven into cloth, or laid into shallow basins where water, dark with stone minerals, will soften them into food.

**The Channelers**

Channeling is their most technical skill—and to them, it is sacred not by religious decree but by necessity.

The Channelers are those who “shape the water without blocking the water.”

They are identifiable by the thicker pads of moss on their knees and elbows, where they have spent long hours lying flat against the stone floor, listening to the shifts beneath the surface.

Their tools are minimal:

- Thin, pointed fingers of stone used to explore the unseen capillaries,  
- Rounded stones of differing sizes that they place into the channels to test the water’s reaction,  
- And finally, their hands.

They do not force the water to go where it does not want to go.

Instead, they observe where the water already flows stronger and where it thins. If they wish a chamber to receive more water, they rarely dig new channels; they deepen the faint paths that already exist in the stone’s memory.

They know when to stop approaching by the feel of the stone under their fingertips and the sound of the water in the hidden channel. If the stone grows softer under their probing, they step back and wait for the water to do its own weathering.

**The Weavers**

Inside the niches and hollows of the walls, where the faint glow can pool a bit more than elsewhere, Weavers sit—often older, with fingers long accustomed to constant moisture.

They are draped in layers of moss‑cloth that they themselves are always making or mending.

The moss is beaten into fibres by soaking it in basins of water and then pounding it between smooth stones. The resulting fibres are thin, flexible, and surprisingly strong. They will be woven into the wraps, curtains, floor pads, and sleeping bags that are the city’s fabric.

They have no metal needles, but their tools are no less precise:

- Thin splints of stone serve as weaving needles,  
- Thicker stone slabs are used as frames,  
- Simple weight of water itself pulls tension into the weave.

Their patterns are not abstract. They weave the channels of their basin into the cloth—ridges of colour that reflect the water paths under their feet. Someone walking from one chamber to another can read their location on the floor‑pattern of the curtains they step through.

**The Children**

Children are everywhere but never noisy.

They are tiny versions of their adults but thinner, more tentative on their feet, until they are not.

They are born to the water like the salamanders are; they are taught to walk by placing them in the shallowest channels and letting them feel the pull of the film. Before they can form words, they understand the direction of water.

They are forbidden to block the channels with their hands or feet—the rule is enforced more by example than by punishment. When someone absentmindedly blocks a small channel, the water’s detour is visible and obvious to everyone, including the child. They learn the wrong of blocking by watching the water’s pain.

Games are not about competition or speed.

Their games involve pattern recognition:

- A child stands in one spot and tries to predict where water will run next when someone else lowers a new slab of stone into the channel.  
- Another child holds a handful of wet sand and lets the droplets of water from the ceiling fall into it, trying to “shape” the sand before it all melts away.

They explore the city the same way they approach the water—carefully, experimentally.

### The Mindset of a Basin People

They are cautious not because of fear but because of water.

They are oriented to the world in terms of flow direction.

When they greet you, they do not ask “Where are you from?” but “Which way does your water run?”

The answer reveals not just your chamber of residence but your relative position in the hydrology of the basin—and therefore, your social context.

They are comfortable with darkness.

There is no “night” in the surface sense; there is only dim light and slightly less dim light. The glow of the moss and the water itself is constant enough that they never fear isolation in darkness. They sleep when they are tired, regardless of the faint glow’s colour shift.

They are conservative without being conservative about anything in particular. They resist change not because change is morally bad, but because change means water might change.

A sudden wall collapse in a chamber above—even if the stone reformed itself over time—could mean new water paths, and the water might be confused by its new choices. They fear the water’s confusion more than the stone’s collapse.

Their greatest fear is being “blind to the flow.”

Physically, this means a channel blocked beneath a floor that you cannot see.  
Socially, it means being excluded from the news of where water is rising next.

---

### Beliefs in the Living Basin

They are not religious, but they are reverent.

They do not worship a deity or a spirit; they worship nothing.

But they believe deeply that the basin is alive.

The basin—

The city is not just “the city” to them; the basin is the unity of stone, water, moss, and themselves.

They believe that the basin notices:

When someone carves stone without listening to the water’s song under it,  
the basin may answer by shifting the flow subtly,  
shortening a path here  
or making one sinkhole unexpectedly slow.

They are not sure the basin does this intentionally;  
the basin is not assumed to be angry or righteous.  
It is simply assumed to be... attentive.

They say the basin is their “memory that does not forget.”

The stone records the paths of water;  
the water records the paths of stone;  
the people are the temporary observers who look between the two and  
try to make sure nothing is broken.

---

### Differences Between Chambers and People

The chambers differ from place to place.

In **High Chamber**, where the faint light shafts sometimes break from the surface world above, the people are slightly taller and thinner. They gaze more to the ceiling than elsewhere. They wear garments of slightly brighter green, where moss choked with more faint light grows thicker.

In **Deep Chamber**, where water is thick and the light dims, the people are shorter, broader, and more comfortable with low silence. They are also darker in colour, their skin tinged with a faint green-blue like the salamanders they share the space with. They are the ones who shape the deepest channels of the basin and remember the longest songs of water.

**Side Chambers** are scattered unevenly around the basin, where the floors are steeper and more uneven than those in the center. These people are the most physically skilled at balancing on slick stone surfaces, and they are often used to inspect edges of the basin where new channels are forming.

### Life and Death in Water Memory

Births are quiet.

Childbirth happens in small hollows in the stone walls, where the water just seeps through the rock rather than flows over it. The water film is thinnest here, gentle enough for the skin of the newborn to not be overwhelmed.

The newborn is placed in the channel of water from the chamber where they were born. They scream rarely—the sound is not necessary in the quiet city—but they breathe immediately, the lungs of the basin-baby accustomed to air thick and cold.

Deaths are equally quiet.

When someone dies, their body is not buried but placed into a narrow channel in the stone wall where the water is allowed to flow slowly. The body is wrapped in a layer of moss‑cloth and then laid into the shallow grooves carved for that purpose.

The water, over time, breaks the body down.

The minerals remain;  
the bones, slowly softened by the water’s work, become part of the stone wall.  
The family remembers the water channel where the body was placed.

They do not have “tombs” but “watermarks.”

A channel that is known to hold someone’s final remains is treated differently than others. It is not avoided, but it is handled carefully. The stones of that channel are not shaped by tools anymore but allowed to flow into their own shape; the water taught there.

---

### What They Like of Theirs and Others

They are not suspicious as a rule.

Strangers are not dangerous because they might hurt them—  
strangers are dangerous because they might block water.

They are cautious observers of anything new.

When they counterbalance this with interest in what you bring from outside, they prefer:

- **Stories of the sun.**  
  - Not bright light—but the idea of it.  
  - They do not understand the concept of things drying, but they are entranced by the thought of movement that is not restricted by stone.

- **Patterns.**  
  - Any surface that you bring from aboveworld with patterns on it—from carved stone to woven cloth—is examined carefully.  
  - They are drawn to anything that looks like channels or flows.  
  - Straight lines and sharp angles, they ignore; they have seen stone but not its geometry.

They are not aggressive because they have never owned weapons or metal.  
They are just not patient.

They are impatient for the water to behave correctly.

---

### Conclusion: Who Dwells Here?

To summarize the dwellers:

- They are **stone‑skinned**, **water‑balanced**, **small people** adapted to cold, wet, narrow spaces.

- Their **social structure** follows the water’s flow—families near the strongest channels are naturally where information comes together.

- **Labour** is quiet and specialized but without hierarchy—Gatherers, Channelers, Weavers, and the children who are the future listeners of water.

- **Language** is slow and sound-softened, full of the silence of dripping water and narrow channels.

- **Beliefs** revolve around a basin that is actively alive—not divine, but attentive—and the water which is memory.

- **Life and death** are absorbed into the water’s work, with bodies becoming part of the stone’s new layers.

- **Their greatest fear** is not death or injury but being “blind to the flow”—outside of the water’s news, and therefore the city’s news.

---

In the end, what lives in this basin beneath the root‑nets of the world above is not just “a people who have never known metal.”

It is a **people of water**.

They are not its master but its messenger—quiet, careful, and always listening to the stone beneath their feet.
