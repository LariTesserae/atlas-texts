In the Settlement at the Edge of Formation, the people are themselves thresholds: bodies half-emerged from the raw earth of their own becoming.

They call themselves the *Still-Deciding*, though they rarely bother with the name aloud. When they speak—which is not often—their words are slow and thick, as if each syllable must surface through water before it can be heard. Conversation is a rare luxury; meaning requires too much definition, and definition is what this world has not yet chosen.

---

**Physique**

Their bodies are not soft, but they are not entirely fixed either. They stand like the stone of their city: roughly hewn, but not yet carved into detail.

The skin of the Still-Deciding hangs somewhere between flesh and clay. It is not pale or dark, but rather an indeterminate color that seems to change when the light shifts, though the light here never truly shifts. It is moist to the touch, like the walls of the city. When they move, their skin does not stretch so much as yield, then slowly return. There is no dust on them, because dust has not yet been formed.

The adults appear thick-set and broad-shouldered from lifetimes of balancing stone on stone, but their posture is not heavy. It is the wary poise of someone standing on a floor that might become water in the next heartbeat. They are not tall; the unformed air does not encourage soaring.

Their eyes are the most formed thing about them. They gleam wet, reflecting the perpetual dim light like the surfaces of the black pools below. Their gaze does not wander. It is not restless, but it does not settle either—it simply waits, watching the world as if at any moment the mist might decide to become clear.

---

**Daily Life**

The Still-Deciding do not build according to plan or blueprint. They build according to pressure.

They rise when the water-song beneath the city changes slightly in pitch—though no one else could tell the difference. They sleep when the sky-neels leave faint trails on the stone walls, indicating that the air above the water is becoming more defined than the air below it.

Their day is not measured by the sun, but by the subtle imbalances of the world. Each stone they place is an answer to a shift that has not yet happened.

**Work**

The men and women who work the stone do not call themselves masons. They do not call themselves anything at all. They carry rough blocks up from the exposed bones of the earth to the ever-expanding tiers of the city. Their tools are simple but deliberate: wedges that know where the rock wants to crack, hammers that know when to stop short.

They work without scaffolding or rope. They balance on the skeletal structures of the unformed walls as if walking on solid ground. Sometimes they are wrong; sometimes a stone that has not yet decided to take weight collapses suddenly. They do not cry out. They fall into the shallow black water or into the mist itself, and are not seen again. No one searches for them. There is no sense of loss, only of change deferred.

The others simply continue laying stone on stone.

---

**The Water-Children**

The youngest among them are called *water-children*, though the name is not affectionate but factual.

Water-children are born with bodies so soft that they blur slightly at the edges. They do not cry when they emerge from their mothers; they sound more like a trickle of water finding its way through a fissure.

Their eyes are wide, reflecting everything without yet understanding anything. They crawl on the slick stone paths as if testing the surface of the world with their palms. They often pause to lay their faces against the stone, listening. They are not listening to sound; they are listening to possibility.

As they grow older, their bodies slowly firm into the shape they will hold when time truly begins. They choose their adult names when they lay their first block into the city wall without their hands trembling. The name they choose then is not spoken of again until they disappear into the mist or the water. They are not dead; they are simply elsewhere.

---

**Craft and Tools**

The craftspeople work with materials that have not yet chosen their final nature.

Metalworkers hammer unformed ore into blades whose edges are not yet decided. They will be sharp when they need to be, and dull when they need to be. The tools themselves are not fixed; their edges change as they are used, responding to what they are cutting. The Still-Deciding do not sharpen tools; only use them until they decide to become something else.

They weave no cloth because there is nothing yet to weave. There are no plants, no fibers, no threads. They do not even wear leather, because no animals here die in the way that would make leather. They drape themselves in thin sheets of living stone that can be taken from the walls of certain caves—a material that keeps their skin moist and prevents it from choosing too early a texture.

---

**Communion and Silence**

There are no priests or leaders in the Settlement at the Edge of Formation. There are not even families in the way other places might understand them.

There are only those who heft stone together and those who tend the quiet between stone.

Each evening—if the word applies to a time when the light never changes—they gather at the edge of a shallow pool where the water is particularly black. They do not speak. They kneel as the water-children play in the shallows. The oldest adults place their fingers into the water until the skin of both fingertips and surface ripple slightly in response.

They are not praying to the water. They are listening to what portion of the world has decided, at last, to flow.

When they rise, their fingers are a little more formed than before. The faint lines of their fingerprints are deeper, more decided.

---

**The Wingbees, Shoal-Rats, and Sky-Neels**

The Still-Deciding do not tame the creatures of the in-between. They share them.

Wingbees drink the moisture directly from the air, leaving faint trails of condensation on the skin of those who stand still. The Still-Deciding do not flinch when the insects hum past their faces and lips. They understand the wingbees as a kind of breathing that happens outside their bodies.

Children sometimes catch the beasts between steps and hold them in their mouths until the insects dissolve, leaving their tongues slick with the world’s undecided moisture. They do not think of this as eating or as cruelty; it is only exchange.

The shoal-rats move like water beneath the surface of the black pools. The adults watch the patterns of their passing without attempting to interpret them. There is no divination here because there is no future separate enough from the present to be read.

When shoal-rats occasionally swim over the toes of those who stand too long in the shallows, the sensation of their tiny bodies against living skin is considered intimate enough that no one speaks afterward for several hours.

Sky-neels slide in long, slow arcs between the structures of the city and the surface of the water. No one tries to touch them. They are treated like boundaries themselves—visible lines drawn between stone and liquid, air and reflection.

---

**Intimacy**

The Settlement at the Edge of Formation neither celebrates nor forbids closeness between bodies.

Intimacy happens when two people decide together what they will become, for a short while. When two Still-Deciding decide to become *one* they press their bodies against each other and stand still for long periods of time, letting the moisture between their skin decide what to become.

Sometimes, when they separate, their form has shifted slightly. They may become a little taller, or their fingers a little longer, or their eyes a little more defined. They do not discuss this. They go back to stacking stone on stone.

No children are born accidentally. Every birth is the decision of the world to become more specific.

---

**Thought and Belief**

The Still-Deciding do not believe in gods or in fate. They believe in *decision*—not yet made.

They think of themselves as temporary, but not in a sad way. They are the lines that existence has drawn to decide what shape a valley will take, before the valley fully exists.

They do not fear change because change has not truly started here yet. They do not hoard possessions or build grand monuments. They build only enough walls to keep the water from becoming entirely land and the land from becoming entirely water.

Their minds are not clouded by confusion; they are simply unformed. They know what a stone is when it is in their hands, and what water is when they place their fingers into it. They do not need abstractions beyond that.

---

**The Edge of Them**

Their bodies, like their world, are always on the edge of becoming more specific.

Their hair hangs like moisture that has not yet decided whether to drip or cling to the scalp. Their voices are low and steady, like the unchanging rumble beneath the settlement. Their gestures are slow, not because they are lazy, but because they do not take anything for granted—not even air.

When one of the Still-Deciding finally disappears—whether into the mist, the water, or silently between two stones—no one mourns in the way other people might. They simply pause for a moment in whatever they are doing.

In that pause, the world decides what the next stone will be.

---

**In the End**

They are the people of the threshold.

They are the ones who choose to build a home at the very edge of what is becoming, rather than waiting for what will become. They lay their stones into the unformed valley like breaths into a body that has not yet decided whether to breathe.

They belong to this pre-formation world not because they are primitive or ancient, but because they themselves are still deciding what they will be when time begins to change everything.

And they are comfortable in that suspension.

They are the Still-Deciding, living at the edge of their own making, where the world has not yet chosen to be solid and the people have not yet chosen to be anything else.
