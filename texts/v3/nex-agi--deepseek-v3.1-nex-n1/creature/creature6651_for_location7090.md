At the base of the western wall, where the angle of the flute-mouths sends light slantwise and late, there is an irregularity in the stone.

It looks, at first, like a fault in the carving of the channels—a place where one plate has shifted a finger’s width out of true. The shadows fall differently there; instead of crisp edges, they blur into a soft pocket of dimness, as if the rock has learned to drink light. The polished surface is interrupted by a shallow recess that follows no obvious geometry. Its edges are too smooth for fracture, too organic for machining.

If you put your hand to it, the stone feels warmer than expected.

Not hot—nothing so dramatic. Just a degree or two above the chill that hangs in the air everywhere else: the faintest suggestion of metabolism. The warmth is concentrated along a narrow band at about waist height, where something like a vein runs just beneath the translucent film of moisture. It pulses once every few breaths—too slow to be mechanical, too regular to be random.

If you watch long enough, you begin to see how this place is used.

The silk-things do not come here by accident. Every so often one will detach from its usual patrol along an updraft and glide in lazy spirals toward this warm seam in the wall. It doesn’t land so much as drape itself across it: a translucent ribbon laid carefully over stone. For several minutes it might lie motionless—or almost motionless; there is a subtle quivering along its length, as if it is listening with its whole body.

Then it leaves again, moving more purposefully than before: climbing directly into a specific draft, following an invisible line that leads toward one of the higher flute-mouths in the ceiling. As it rises, it seems thicker somehow—not heavier, but more substantial, its edges less prone to fraying in turbulence.

Nothing visible has been exchanged. You never see anything pass between ribbon and rock; no fluid emerges, no dust falls away. Yet something about this contact feels like commerce.

On certain days—days when some distant adjustment in pressure or heat sends an unaccustomed sigh through the mountain’s ventilation—the warm seam appears more active. Then you might see two silk-ribbons converging from opposite sides of the cavern at once. They approach along intersecting arcs of air and hang side by side over that glowing vein without touching one another. For a time they vibrate together in what might be coincidence or might be conversation; then they part again on altered trajectories that seem more deliberate than before.

It isn’t only ribbons that come here.

Once or twice in a long watch you may notice something else: a disturbance in the water on the floor nearby. The channels that carry condensation from wall to mosaic usually run with smooth predictability; each rivulet maintains its separate path until it meets another at precisely calculated angles and merges without turbulence.

But near this recessed patch of wall there is always more water than seems strictly necessary for drainage alone. It collects into a small ring-shaped depression just beyond where shadows end—a shallow bowl carved into one plate of flooring with such subtlety you could mistake it for wear over centuries rather than intention from inception.
