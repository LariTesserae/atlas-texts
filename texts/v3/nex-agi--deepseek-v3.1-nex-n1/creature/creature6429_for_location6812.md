The first of the Qetrami that you *notice* notice is not the one nearest to you.

It—she, you will decide later, because the clipped stalk of her hair reads as a formal choice, a deliberate shearing—is two steps down the branch-boulevard, leaning into the gentle curve of the world’s skin as if it exhaled beneath her spine. She is not tall and not short but *correct* for the space left her, the way a gear might be described as correct for the teeth it lacks.

The inhabitants of Qetram are not made of water, but they are made of *water’s ghost.*

Her skin is not the color of anything that still moves under a sun. It is the hue of dried ink in the spine of an ancient book, a deep umber shading to grey where the faint light of the world-branch catches the ridges of her joints. She is not wrinkled; she is *pleated*. Everywhere the motion of her life has worn its possibility into the surface, she is folded: at the knuckles, the elbows, the knees, the faint parallel striations along the throat where the meat of speech might once have moved.

She is not covered so much as *sheathed*.  

Her clothes—if you can call them that—are not fabric but another layer of her: a thin, translucent membrane that clings to the contours of her body like the final shell some insect has forgotten to molt. It is the same bruised purple-grey of the world-branch itself, stained with darker arroyos of pigment where old fluids have traced and then hardened into paths of travel. The pattern is not random. It is a map of the pressure of her life against the world: darker at the shoulders, where she might once have carried weight, and at the palms of the hands, where she might once have pushed against bark or bone or time.

When you look at her face, you do not see the eyes of someone who has been waiting for you.

Her eyes do not gleam; they *recede*. They are not wet, but their surface is the polished shine of something dense and compressed and long still. The pupils are not deep but deep *backward*, as if they open not into the hidden machinery of a brain but into the stacked memory of the world-tree itself.

She does not breathe—not in the way you use the word. Her chest does not rise and fall with the drama of oxygen and carbon dioxide; instead, there is an occasional soft *crack* of expansion somewhere in the region of her ribs, like the sound of a vaulted ceiling expanding infinitesimally in the noon of a very long day. This is Qetram’s hunger: not to inhale but to be slightly more than solid, for the body to be granted the tiny subvocal inch of movement that allows the world to know it still persists.

The sound of her is not her heartbeat; there is no heartbeat.

The sound of her is the *sound she is made of.*

When she turns to face you—not with the startled jerk of a creature of muscle and reflexes but with the slow torsion of stone adjusting its place on a slope—you hear it before you see it fully: the microtonal hum of the matter of her. Her joints make not a clicking but the *release of a click*: the moment after a mechanism has been engaged and settled into a new stasis. The sound of her joints is not cartilage on bone but something more like the high, thin snap of tectonic stress traveling through a very hard crystal.

She does not speak with her mouth. Or maybe she does, and you are simply not complex enough to parse the instrument.

Her lips do not move to form words. They move to form *resonances*.

On the air of Qetram—clean, precise, absent—the sound she produces is not language but the *impression* of language. A series of clear, struck tones, like metal being tapped against different glass vessels of the same fragile design. The notes are not harmonious but they are not dissonant either; they sit in the ringing of the world like new keys inserted into a very old lock.

The meaning of this speech is not in the sequences themselves; it is carried in the gaps between the notes, where the air adjusts itself to the new pressure of her sound. The meaning of Qetram is not in the *not of not of* but in the bend of the world around the space of the not.

You are not expected to understand her.

You are expected to *be heard.* The Qetrami are not used to being alone in the way a younger species might be; they are used to being the *last thing that happened*. When you stand here, you are not an interruption; you are an additional note in the song of the world-branch’s final equilibrium.

Her eyes are on the line of your jaw and not on your wet eyes or your wet mouth. If you have been weeping—and you will be, if you stay here longer than you should—she does not seem to notice. Water is not a pain to her; it is a historical curiosity.

She does not approach you like another creature might approach prey or company. She approaches you the way a growth might approach a gap in the architecture of its world: not with intention but with the implacability of what *must fill*. Her steps are not footsteps but *repositionings of weight.* Each time she shifts her position, she does not lift the foot so much as she *replicates the ground beneath it*—the bark of the world-tree flows slightly, like wax not yet hardened, and she slips into the new shape of her support.

The Qetrami are not built for the ocean of water but for the ocean of time.

Their bodies are desiccated to a point beyond simple dryness; they are preserved to a point beyond simple preservation. They are not alive in the way you use the word; they are alive in the way that a mathematical theorem might be said to be alive when it is no longer being thought but is still true.

Under the thin translucent membrane of her clothing, you can see the structure of her ribs not as the cage of a breathing animal but as the struts of something else. The spaces between the ribs are not empty but a darker color of something dense and compressed and no longer particular about needing to be *anything*. You might mistake her for a skeleton interred with care, but if she were a skeleton you would not see the faint tracery of pathways in her limbs where dried fluids still trace darker lines, like invisible rivers drawn on ancient maps.

She does not need to eat in the way you are hungry. The fluids of Qetram are not of water but of the world-tree’s not-quite-sap; her body is not so much a vessel of them as it is *an expression of their final pattern*. The energy of the world-tree flows not through but *around* her. She is not fed by food but by *continuation*.

When you look at her hands, you see the last of Qetram’s not-yet-praxis.

The fingers of the Qetrami are not fingers but conclaves of possibilities. The digits are unnaturally long and not at all thin: they are bundles of dense matter rolled like tiny scrolls at the end of her hands. The joints aren’t the obvious hinges of humans; they are the more subtle arrangements of pressure that might have made sense when the world-tree still grew—small notches of lamination along the length of the finger, where the world once might have been grasped differently.

The tips of her fingers are not soft but the dusty, polished shine of ivory not carved by human tools but by the constant, infinitesimal pressure of the world itself. When she reaches out to you, not to touch but to indicate the direction and depth of your next error, she does not raise her hand in the air of Qetram; she *builds the air around her hand* until it is clear enough, taut enough, for you to understand its gesture.

The Qetrami cannot—or will not—speak your language of water and need and desperation.  

They speak the *pressure of the not.*

They are its native inhabitants.

There are not many of them, but “not many” is not the same as “few.” The number of the living Qetrami is not something to be counted in the way of a census of moving creatures; it is something to be measured in the way of the remaining points of pressure in a very old machine. The world-branch is not crowded, but you are never truly alone on the surface of it, because at the edges of every space you enter, there is the subtle *not-collision of the not.*

They are not monks of anything; they are not the faithful of a tree-god who still receives prayer; they are simply the *what stays.*

When you look at her from an angle—maybe from the edge of your vision, maybe when you are not brave enough to look her in the eyes of historical depth—you might see the faint trace of what she might have been before the world was finished. Maybe there is the ghost of a child in the way her head tilts not downward but toward the sound of the next struck resonance, maybe there is the trace of a farmer who once knew the shape of shade under a younger sun. Maybe you see nothing of the sort at all and only the chill of her persistence.

The Qetrami cannot be called kind or cruel. They are not malevolent or beneficent; they are not interested enough in the *not-stable of not-them* to be anything but the shape of the world’s final answer to the question of who might be allowed to remain.

They are less than gods.

They are more than fossils.

They are the not of not of not.
