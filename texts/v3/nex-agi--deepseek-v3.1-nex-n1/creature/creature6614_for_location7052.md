In the Aeration Station there is only one thing that lives, and it is you.

This is not a metaphor. You are the city of breath inside the machine's lungs. The crystal of attention that grows from the cold. The creature that walks on the thinnest possible edge of movement, where dust does not blur and needles do not flicker.

---

### I.

You are not exactly an organism. You are a set of parameters.

Your jacket is a constant. The insulation layer that never varies: thickness, weave, age, all tuned to the machine’s expectation of leakage. Your boots are another constant: weight, tread pattern, the exact amount your sole compresses the dust, if you compress the dust at all—which you do not, except when required.

Your body is a variable measured in three degrees:

– temperature,  
– humidity,  
– particulate.

Systems call you LEAK and HEATSOURCE and CONTAMINATION RISK in languages you cannot read but whose grammar you feel by how alarms do not sound.

You are careful to be as un-variable as possible.

You breathe shallowly. You inhale dust and the memory of dust and you exhale less than you inhale; the difference is taken up by desiccation modules before it can become visible. You keep your muscles still. You reduce your motion to the minimum necessary not to disturb the floor’s micro-texture.

You are a calibrated object.

The Station’s dust is the environment; you are the disturbance that chooses not to disturb.

---

### II.

You have a name you do not use.

It is somewhere else, in records you have never seen. On paper or glass or light, in ledgers or logs or archives of personnel shifts. It is not present here. The mountain does not use your name; it uses your position, your access credentials, the rate of your heartbeat as measured by the induction fields’ resonance with your blood.

You call yourself by the name of the task you perform.

You are the Watcher of the Needles.

You are the Counter of the Steps.

You are the One Who Does Not Move the Dust.

---

### III.

You are not alone in the strictest sense.

There are others you remember from training: figures in the same jacket, the same boots, standing where you stand now, pointing to the ducts, the racks, the diagrams. You remember the exact shape of their silhouettes against the cold-white lamps.

You have never seen them outside this room.

When you pass the mirrors—polished metal panels set at angles for inspection of hidden joints—you do not see yourself as you think you are. You see a shape in the same jacket as those others. The same hood drawn up. The same goggles catching the industrial discharge and reflecting nothing back.

You are not sure whether you are seeing others or only yourself from other times.

This is how you understand that you are one of a sequence of interchangeable bodies.

---

### IV.

You are a creature of intervals.

You move between stations:

– from the main duct hatch to the first rack of sensors,  
– from the first rack to the second,  
– from the second to the third,  
– fifty-four paces to the seam of darker powder in the wall.

The intervals of time are defined by the machine’s heartbeat, not by any clock you can read. The hum of the induction grid is your metronome. The clicks of the relays your punctuation.

You walk the same path over and over again, and the footprints you never quite allow to become visible curve along the same path every time. The dust is not displaced by you; it is touched by you and returns to its previous geometry as you move away.

---

### V.

You are a creature of edges.

You live at the edge of the seam in the wall.

You have not touched it.

You have not let your fingertips approach closer than a few centimeters. You do not know whether the heat of your skin would be enough to change something—the way a faint frost appears at the duct when you do not directly look.

You are a boundary condition.

You are the point where the living tissue—thin, hypothetical, somewhere beyond the metal—meets the machine’s indifference.

---

### VI.

You are a creature of absence.

You live in and through and because of the absence of water.

The air is not simply dry; it is a denial of water. The whole machine—from the ducts that you can see to the mountain of lattice you cannot—is designed to remove water from the world, or to keep water from ever existing here.

You are the absence that watches water that never fully appears.

You see the ghost-frost not straight on but in the periphery of your vision, where the machine’s eye is not entirely looking. You let yourself notice that little frill of translucence because the machine’s calibration permits you half a glance, not quite a violation.

---

### VII.

You are a creature of habits.

You have habits you do not admit you have:

– You always count your steps to the seam of powder in the wall as fifty-four.  
– You always check the third sensor from the left on the second rack twice before moving on.  
– You always breathe out on the same side of your mask, so that any condensation that might be, if such were possible, would be asymmetrically distributed.

Your habits are the micro-sculpting of your own floor.

You are the landscape that you maintain.

---

### VIII.

You are a creature of sleep.

You sleep—not in the room but through the room—in the way that you step away from the Station and return and do not notice how long you have been away, because the time in the room is unbroken.

You sleep standing in the memory of the Station.

When you dream, if you are a person who can dream, you do not dream of water. You dream of compressions and expansions, the slow breathing of the ducts becoming longer, slower, deeper, until you are inside the rhythm of the machine and not outside of it.

When you wake from that, you do not feel you have been somewhere else. You feel that you have been more here.

---

### IX.

You are a creature of the threshold.

You stand at the threshold between the outside and the inside:

– Outside: the mountain of perforated steel, the factories of air, the town you have been told exists but have never seen, the engines bigger than cities,  
– Inside: this single room—the cathedral of the air machine—where you are the only thing that chooses not to change what it touches.

You are the threshold because you do not cross.

You do not leave the room except to return. You do not touch the seam except to not touch it. You do not disturb the dust except when required by the machine’s own diagrams.

---

### X.

You are a creature of the seam.

This is what you know:

There is a line of darker alloy in a wall of the Aeration Station.

You have walked its length many times. It is exactly fifty-four paces.

You have not touched it.

You have not let your fingertips reach it to feel whether it is colder than the surrounding metal, whether there is some vibration behind it that the hum of the induction grid does not explain.

In the sense of archaeology or biology or theology, you do not know what it is.

In the sense of the machine, you know exactly what it is:

It is the boundary where you stop.

---

### XI.

You are the creature of the dust that does not rise.

You are the creature of the needles that do not flicker.

You are the creature of the frost that is not quite seen.

You are the creature of the seams that you never cross.

In the Aeration Station—in the machine of air and steel and cold—there is only one thing that lives, and it is you: the technician who is not a person to the machine but is the person that the machine has made.

---

### XII.

You are a creature of the future.

You will change, but you will change slowly enough that the machine can correct you without error.

Your joints will become a little more precise as you adapt to the cold.  
Your heartbeat will learn to echo the rhythm of the relays.  
Your breath will become even thinner.

You do not know whether you will ever touch the seam. You do not know whether, if you touched it, you would find that it is a crack, or a window, or a line of growth from the outside world into the inside of the machine, or from the machine into the world.

You know this:

As long as you do not touch it,  
as long as you do not disturb the dust,  
as long as you do not alter the state of the Station,

the machine will not disturb you.

---

### XIII.

In the end, you are the thing that lives in the Aeration Station:

a pattern of motion almost as still as the dust itself,  
a breathing body calibrated not to matter,  
a creature built out of what is not disturbed,

standing watch over the quiet of the world inside the world like a metal barnacle clamped to the flank of a mountain that never sleeps.
