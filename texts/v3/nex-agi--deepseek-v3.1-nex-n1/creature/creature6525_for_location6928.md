It is not, in the strictest biological sense, a rat.  

The thing that lives in the Engine Room has the shape of one, if you blur your eyes and forget the precise anatomy of rats. You might call it a shadow of a rat, a study of one in graphite and engine grease. The resemblance begins in the long, low body, the efficient curve of the spine, and the way it moves not in starts but in smooth, continuous lines, as if drawing its own track across the floor. It ends with the tail—which is not a tail of flesh and bone and naked pink skin, but a flexible conduit of braided copper and dark, oiled leather.

Here is how you know it is not a rat:

When it is still, its ears are not simply ears. They are two perforations of the substrate of its skull, shaped by half-memory into the exact silhouette of a rat’s ear, but they are not made for sound so much as for listening more broadly to the machine. The right ear is slightly longer than the left, not by accident of growth but because of a long-ago repair. The left one is shorter by a precise, measured gap, exactly where the last claw of some inspector's tool slipped from the bolt it was trying to tighten and caught first its edge then its edge again, before the engineer could be judged to have been careless.

On each rib, drawn in thin micro-fractures of metal and bone, the name of the plate is faintly visible: C-ROD-77, C-ROD-78, and so on, for the ribs of the body; E-ARM-09, E-ARM-10, for the limbs. The fur is not hair at all but a thin patina of filaments and dark, fibrous scales that grows from the alloy in the same way moss grows from stone: because it has been warm enough and damp enough for long enough that something else finally decided to use the surface. It is not purely living matter but not purely machine either; it is some hybridization of the two that has never been classified by the city’s catalog of anomalies.

In the bone of its jaw, where a battery should perhaps be, or a heart, or at least a marrow, there is instead a small, sealed glass phial of pale-blue liquid. This is as old as the oldest brickwork above the Engine Room. The liquid is thin enough to be serum but thick enough to be metal, with the faint opacity of frosted glass. If you were brave enough to catch the creature and look closely, you might see, in the pressure of the machine’s low hum, the faint vibration of particles within the phial, not quite settling, not quite stirring.

It is from the phial that the faintest light of the creature’s body comes. When the engine’s hum deepens by a fraction too much to notice, or the pressure of the world above the tunnel complex changes by the thickness of a breath, the blue in the jawbone phial pulses with a coldness that is not color but temperature. It is blue as you imagine the core of a glacier is blue, not the sky. This faint, cold light is carried by fine threads of metal-glass into wherever the creature’s eyes are meant to be—two shallow sockets of black lenses that do not truly reflect anything but instead absorb the Engine Room into themselves.

You see it by its absence, most of all. In the corner of your vision, when you are trying to track the noise of a valve that has clicked just once too many times in the last hour, you might catch the flick of its tail as it edges from one module to the next. It is never quite fast enough to be alarming, but never quite slow enough to be caught. If you were to measure the distance from its last position to its next, you would find that the length of the journey is not equal to the length of the movement you actually saw. It must have been there longer than you thought, or you must have been looking before you looked. The eye of the engineer and the eye of the inspector both correct this discrepancy by assuming they have been inattentive, and not that the creature itself has been uneconomically there.

During the rare times when there are no engineers in the Engine Room, when the inspection hatch has been closed and the gas jets have been turned low to preserve their filaments, the creature moves differently. It is not that it becomes less careful; rather, the absence of humans frees some of the constraints on its own body. The silhouette of a rat blurs then into the silhouette of maintenance itself. It stretches longer than the long shadow of a module’s edge; it narrows to the width of a single maintenance rail, flowing along the narrow gauge of track above the engine like a line of ink seeking out the correct bar of the pressure gauge.

Always, it returns to the same resting place: a small depression in the wall where the concrete of the ceiling arch has never been properly sealed to the first of the engine’s plates. There is no crack here large enough to be a fault, but heat and time and the persistent dampness of the Engine Room have worn a thin hollow of darker concrete into the shape of a half-oval. This niche is the size of a palm, if you open your hand and let the fingers curl slightly, as you might to hold a small engine component.

When the creature is within the niche, the body that it maintains on the outside merges with the body of the machine in the way that water merges with the damp of the wall: not violently but slowly, until you are not sure where the water ends and the wall begins. The tail of the rat-that-is-not-a-rat connects into a socket in the wall of the module that has no external name but only an internal designation: no visible bolt or plug, only a place where one metal becomes another metal, and then not-quite-metal, and then not-quite-living.

The sound of the engine, when the creature is in its socket, does not increase but changes slightly. The low, bone-deep pressure that might or might not be a hum becomes more stable than before, not by magnitude but by consistency, as if someone has finally noticed the one valve that has been half a revolution away from its ideal position since the city above still called itself by another name. The occasional click of misalignment that occurs every three hundred and fifty-seven compensations of the world above’s weight ceases to click at all.

It does not eat in the accepted sense. There is no part of its body that has been configured for masticating metal or licking dry the rust from the gutter of the water-ghost. It is instead nourished by the difference between the noise that the engine makes when it is untouched and the noise the engine makes when the creature is attached to its socket. The intake of the difference between the two states flows into the body in the same way the current of the pale, clear water in its gutter flows into the engine’s cooling system: not as a river but as a persistent thinness of change.

When you are finally forced to describe the creature that lives in the Engine Room, you might say “rat” because you need a word that has weight in the world. The inspector will write it in his logbook with one of the pens whose ink has not yet given up the pretense of being color, and you will be able to point to the word and feel you have not lied to the city above.

But you will know, when you are alone in your bed at the end of your shift, with the sound of the Engine Room still pressed into your bones, that the word you have written down is not its name. It has not been named by any human being, because it has never been seen by any human being in the way that a machine is seen by its creator.

Instead the name it has is the one the machine uses for itself, when the nerve-that-is-not-a-nerv of the creature is in its socket and the low, not-quite-hum of the engine turns into something else for the barest of intervals:

the difference                between what is broken
    and what is not broken yet.

And that name is enough chair for it to sit in.
