Below the dripping pipes and the faint blue halo along the stone ceiling, the **Underheat** sleeps.

They are not one being and not many. They are the chamber’s other pulse—neither machinery nor human, but something stretched between the two.

An **Underheat** looks like a bundle of limbs wrapped in thin, pale membrane that clings where it touches and gleams faintly under the yellowed bulbs. It has no face you would recognize. No clear eyes, no mouth that can form words. Instead, its head is enclosed by a smooth, pale casing that curves down over the tops of tentative shoulders, more like a hardened cap than any skull you’ve seen. It doesn’t break where you expect a jawline to end; it flows seamlessly into the membrane of its chest, where faint ridges mark where ribs might once have been.

The membrane is not skin exactly, nor cloth, nor plastic. It’s thin enough to show the shadow of movement beneath—slow expansions and contractions, like the breathing of a deep furnace—and thick enough to catch condensation. When the air grows heavy and droplets gather on the underside of the pipes, those same droplets gather in the shallow dips of the Underheat’s shoulders and spine, pooling in tiny beads of warm water.

Where they embrace the metal around them, the membrane fuses just slightly. It doesn’t weld, but it seems unwilling to part entirely. When they move—which they rarely do—they leave faint, translucent traces behind, like a slug’s trail but thinner and more deliberate.

Each Underheat has a **core**, low in the torso where a stomach might once have been. You can’t see it directly, but on certain breaths of the chamber, when the pipes overhead grow warm and the blue glow along the ceiling brightens, the core glows dully beneath the membrane. It’s not a bright light; it’s more like the faint orange glow of a heating element seen through fogged glass. When you’re near one, you feel it on your skin—a localized warmth, like standing near a radiator turned to its lowest setting.

When the pumps cycle on and the fluids shift through the pipes above, the **Underheat’s** core flares slightly hotter. Their membrane trembles. A faint vibration hums through their body and into the metal they press against, so that the catwalks and railings and supports themselves seem to carry their heartbeat. This is how you know they are alive: not by movement, but by the heat they contribute and the steady, slow rhythm they impose on the chamber around them.

They breathe, but not in air alone.

Their **breathing** is slow and shallow. Every few minutes, the membrane around their chest tightens subtly, then relaxes. The air around them grows damp, then slightly drier, then damp again. The humidity clings to them, then slides away in thin filaments along their surface. When you stand close, you can feel the faint disturbance of air—a warm exhale that doesn’t quite smell human. It carries a faint yeasty tang, like dough rising in a warm room, or the faint after-scent of warm metal after the machine has been powered off.

You hear them occasionally as **soundless sighs**. Water vapor condensing around their exhales becomes the faint soft whisper that travels on the chamber’s thicker air. It’s not a word, not a cry, not a complaint. It’s simply a shift of atmosphere—the sound of a body breathing in a place where the air is already half-liquid.

When they sleep, they **curl**.

They curl around support beams, railings, the edges of floor plates and catwalks. Limbs tangle idly among themselves, not so much embracing as **interlocking**, like roots that have grown through each other without quite deciding whether they share one trunk or several. The membrane at their joints seems more fluid in this state—less defined than when they move. It softens to a near-transparency, allowing the faint outlines of bone and sinew beneath to show through.

They sleep high and low. Some hang above the catwalks, near the ceiling where the stone meets the concrete. They cling to the thickest of the pipes and to the bundles of wiring wrapped in black insulation, their bodies conforming to the shapes like wax poured over hot metal. Others rest near the floor, where the faint channels of water thread between the plates. They sprawl across the lowest plates, half on their sides, half curled in on themselves, as if listening to the trickle beneath.

There is **no hierarchy** you can discern from their placement. No bigger Underheat near the center, no smaller ones pushed to the margins. They are scattered more like fungi than like animals—each in its chosen niche, each responding to the local heat and humidity. Some prefer hotter spots near the warmest pipes; others have pressed their bodies into the cooler corners, where the faint blue glow is strongest.

Where they rest, the **thin white plants** follow.

Those pale, hypha-like strands grow toward them. Not quickly enough to see, but if you spend time between one cycle of warm pipes and the next, you notice the subtle rearrangements: the way a strand loops around a forearm; the way a cluster of them encircles an ankle, like a living brace. The Underheat don’t seem to mind. They neither pull away nor reach toward the growth; they simply **endure** it, as the concrete endures the dripping water.

The blue glow that crawls along the stone edges also flows faintly along their membranes. It’s not uniform; it appears in thin veins from their shoulders to the tips of their limbs, like faint circuitry beneath their skin. When the chamber’s machinery deep in the wall sends a low thrum through the metal supports, the veins glow a little brighter—just for a heartbeat—then fade again.

The **plants** respond to the same surge. The glow whips faintly down their lengths, like nerve signals seeking endpoints. For a moment you see the true shape of the room’s life: a network of blue veins in stone, concrete, metal, and flesh.

They don’t hunt you.

They don’t seem to **notice** you in the way you expect to be noticed. There are no eyes turned your way, no heads turning toward your footsteps. If you make noise, they may breathe a little quicker—the membrane over their lungs tightening—then relax again. If you touch one lightly on the shoulder, you feel the faint heat of its core beneath your fingers, and the membrane softens under your touch like it’s been expecting something heavier, more intense than you.

When you pull away, the faintest film of moisture remains on your fingertips—not exactly water, not exactly sweat, but something **between fluids**.

Their **movement**, when it happens, is languorous and reluctant. They loosen their limbs one by one, like a machine slowly coming up to temperature. First a finger loosens, then a hand shifts, then the whole limb slowly slides away from whatever they’ve curled around—their bodies leaving a faint, translucent trace behind. They don’t walk so much as **slither**, dragging themselves along the catwalks or the ridged floor plates, using the railings and pipes as handholds.

When they move between levels, they climb with a slow certainty that suggests they know the dimensions of the chamber the way you know the shape of your own body. They never hesitate in front of a gap or a ladder; they simply flow over it, their membranes clinging to the metal just enough to keep them steady.

Every few movements—though you cannot name a reason—they pause. Their core glows slightly hotter, and they **droplet**. They exhale in a steady stream, and a thin film of water gathers on their skin and falls away in droplets that slide along their limbs and down onto the metal surfaces beneath. The droplets gather in faint rivulets along seams and ledges, joining the slow trickle that already moves beneath the floor plates.

They don’t have **jobs**, exactly.

But they have **functions**.

The heat they generate and hold keeps the chamber from ever cooling fully. They warm certain sections of the catwalks and supports, making them easier to traverse for their own bodies, and perhaps for whatever maintenance might happen down here someday. Their breathing keeps the humidity high enough that the thin white plants can thrive without soil or sun.

When they cluster—sometimes two or three Underheat curl around one another—the heat between them amplifies. The membrane around each blends subtly into the next, forming a temporary **shared surface** across which the faint blue veins connect more directly than through air. The plants around them grow thicker; the faint trickle beneath their joint becomes slightly louder.

You suspect they **exchange** something in these clusters, though not by speaking or even looking. They hum at slightly different tones—sometimes harmonizing for a moment, sometimes slowly drifting away from each other into discordant rhythms. It’s not a song you can understand. It is more like the tuning of a machine: each frequency tested, adjusted, let go.

They rarely go near the door you came through. The **door** is a hole in their world—a spot where the air is thinner and colder, where the humidity breaks abruptly. If you stand near it for a long time, you might see one of them approach, slowly, hesitating at the edge of that colder current. It might extend a limb toward the cooler air, the membrane tightening faintly as though tasting the temperature, then withdraw.

They don’t chase you.

They **mark** you.

When you stand near them long enough, your skin grows slicker with their condensation. Your clothes pick up faint smears from their membranes—translucent traces that dry slowly into nearly invisible films. For days afterward you might smell that faint yeasty warmth on your skin, like you’ve carried their chamber with you in your clothes.

When you finally leave, stepping back onto the spiral staircase and shutting the metal door behind you, the **Underheat** do not stir as a group. There’s no alarm, no sudden awakening. They continue breathing slowly, droplets of water forming on their invisible surfaces, the faint blue glow tracing their veins.

Yet somewhere in the chamber’s quiet, **one** of them moves slightly. A hand curled around a railing loosens, then tightens again. A membrane quivers, like the surface of a pool into which a single droplet has fallen. The hum they all emit shifts, just a fraction: the frequency changes slightly, as though they have noticed, after all, that you were there and now you are not.

The **Underheat** is not hostile to you.

They are simply **very young**.
