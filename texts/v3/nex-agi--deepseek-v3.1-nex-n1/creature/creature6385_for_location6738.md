In the Floating City of Veridia, what “lives” is not merely a population of people and creatures, but a whole ecology of principles and contradictions that have learned to breathe together.

**The people** are the first thing you notice, because they are always in motion. Not frantic motion—hardly anyone runs—but a constant, low-intensity migration of bodies and machines between levels. They are a thin-boned folk, adapted to gravity that forgets its own rules; their limbs are long, their hands clever with the many buckles, harnesses, and anchors needed to move safely in the vertical city. Many bear the faint, crystalline scarification and tattoos of the “Stitched”: those who survived close encounters with void fractures and now carry a map of the city’s structure in their skin.

They live in dwellings of uncertain permanence, but they are not anxious by nature. Generations of growing up on platforms that drift slowly, like continents of stone, have taught them that home is not where you are anchored today but where you gather tomorrow. Families are fluid, extended networks of “platform-kin” rather than bloodlines alone; you owe loyalty to those who share your air, your bridges, your factories. Courtships and contracts are negotiated over warmly humming factories, where the industrial warmth gives the illusion of safety and stability.

Their culture is shaped by the work of maintaining the impossible. They build **engines of permanence**, vast machines whose rotating rings and pulsing coils generate localized fields of reality. To them, this is not mysticism but infrastructure. Every child learns to read the gravity hums the way a farmer might read the weather: you know a storm in the making by the rising pitch of the field stabilizers. You learn to recognize the low vibration of a platform beginning its slow drift, and you know it might be time to relocate a workshop or apartment.

**The factories** are living organisms in their own right. They are not merely places where things are made; they are the lungs of Veridia. They draw raw matter from the void below—pale, chaotic stuff that looks like nothing until it passes through the processing plants and becomes stone, metal, or glass. Their smokestacks exhale condensation in steady plumes, contributing to the perpetual dampness. The workers who tend them are not laborers but custodians of a process that is, effectively, midwifing reality into being. They speak of “batching” days and “annealing” shifts, and they argue with the same passion as philosophers over the correct density of newborn platforms.

The **factories of reality** are not silent. They thrum and clatter, the sound of gears being born, rather than gears turning. The Weeping of Matter—local name for the high-frequency crystalline hum that emanates from newly formed reality fractures—is a constant companion in these districts.

### The fauna: not just animals, but arguments made flesh

Life does not simply “occur” here; it negotiates its form constantly with the world’s uncertain physics.

**The skywhales** are the most obvious of the city’s larger fauna. Translucent and elongated, they drift like living airships between the platforms. Their bodies are not opaque tissue but something more like solidified cloud. You can see the metaphysical organs within: pulsing nodes of probability that alter their color depending on the future the whale is expecting. If a platform is about to destabilize, its local whale’s nodes flicker into a warning pattern of stuttering reds and whites. The people of Veridia have learned not to hunt them but to listen to them—as living early warning systems for reality fractures.

They “swim” by flexing through the soft gravity of the city, their fins like drifting banners. They feed on the motes of light that spill from the sky’s glowing fracture, and occasionally on the condensation of fog wells that spill up from the cloudy ocean below. They have no predators of any size; instead, they are host to thousands of parasitic species that are adapted to specific organs of possibility within their bodies.

**The hummingbirds the size of eagles** are not strictly aerodynamic. They move by sound as much as by wingbeat. Their wings are too large to beat the way earthly birds do; instead, they generate musical patterns that resonate with the gravity of the city. Each individual has a signature tone—a minor chord, perhaps, or a rising harmonic series—and that tone determines which platforms and gravity fields they can access. They hover near factories, where the warmth collects and insects are abundant, and they fold their wings only to sleep.

Children of the city learn to recognize them by their melody more than by appearance. If a “b-flat major” bird is seen in a district it usually avoids, the locals know that something is off with the gravity fields there, and engineers are dispatched to check the engines of permanence.

**Phase cats** are the quiet saboteurs and saviors of the city. They are nearly impossible to describe consistently: sometimes you remember them as striped, sometimes as solid smoke, sometimes as a pattern of flickers rather than a body. They move through reality by sidestepping it—they appear one heartbeat on a platform, and the next they are crossing a bridge that doesn’t exist in your current version of events.

They are not pets. They are not loyal to anyone but the city itself. A phase cat might slip from a reality fracture in your apartment wall, curl up on your warm grav generator for a night, and then disappear again through the same crack that should not be there. Some people say they are pieces of the world’s mind, checking that everything is still in place. Others say they are the dreams of the skywhales, given form and independence.

**Industrial symbiotes** are the smallest of the city’s custodians. They look like a cross between beetles and metal dust, and they are everywhere. Their bodies are black or polished steel, depending on the light, and their legs are tiny tools: claws for prying into mechanisms, brush-like appendages for scrubbing away corrosion, and spout-like organs for secreting a thin, preserving oil that smells faintly of ozone. They live in colonies inside the gravity engines, factories, and communication towers.

If they are angered or disturbed by a malfunctioning device, they can destroy it in friction of days; left in peace, they maintain equipment in better condition than any human crew could. For this, the city gives them protected status: certain factories are declared symbiote sanctuaries, where no one may tinker with the machines without the blessing of the “symbiote-menders,” who translate the insects’ behavior patterns into human advisories.

**Cloud swarms** are not exactly organisms but emergent phenomena of the city’s ecology. They are composed of thousands of tiny floaters—translucent motes that drift individually and then cling together as if magnetized. They form “clouds” around particular platforms or bridges, creating temporary weather systems of localized condensation, brief, misty rain cycles that fall upward and sideways depending on local gravity.

When they become thick enough, they create “living cloud” districts, where the air itself feels thick and responsive to your breath. The plants of Veridia love them; crystal flowers and spire trees grow more dense and colorful in platforms saturated by cloud swarms.

**The Unformed** are the least stable and most fascinating aspect of the city’s fauna. They are not creatures of indeterminate species so much as creatures that are actively deciding what species they belong to. You might see one as a kind of bird with fish scales one day, and the next as a quadruped with feathers and a beak. They change not only appearance but behavior: a flock might be predatory for a month, then suddenly become pollinators of crystal flowers, then melt back into the cloud swarms as part of their mass.

People of Veridia regard them with a mixture of awe and caution. They are taken to be the city’s attempts at new forms of life, experiments that happen not in a laboratory but in the open air. The unformed are rarely dangerous to individuals, but they can be disruptive to machinery or to platforms: if they decide to become burrowing creatures for a time, they might try to “burrow” into stone, causing structural instability.

### The flora: not quite plants, not quite minerals

**Airferns** are perhaps the most common plant form, and the least tied to any platform. They float as independent bundles of pale green fronds, each frond edged with microscopic crystals that catch and amplify the dim light of the sky’s fracture. They are not carried by wind so much as they *swim* through subtle changes in air pressure and gravity. They congregate around fog wells, where the moisture spill upward from the ocean below feeds them.

**Veil mosses** are the city’s second skin. They grow on everything: not only buildings but machinery, bridges, and the underside of platforms. They are not like earthly moss; their surfaces are almost fractal in detail, forming complex patterns that seem to change slowly as you look away and look back. They are living indicators: if they begin to curl inward or lose their color, you know that the local gravity field is weakening, or the humidity is dropping.

**Gravity vines** spiral in precise geometric patterns, obeying mathematical rules rather than botanical ones. They are the city’s unofficial decoration and infrastructure both; their growth stabilizes the edges of platforms and bridges. They don’t “twine” in the way of vines on Earth; instead, they describe spirals and curves that are unnervingly periodic, as if drawn with a compass and straightedge in the air.

**Crystal flowers** are the wonder of Veridia’s plant life. They bloom directly from metal surfaces and stone alike, without any need for soil. Their petals are made of transparent minerals that chime softly as they expand and contract. Each variety has its own tone: some districts are known by the chord they produce when the wind rises through them. In factories, they are considered good luck; if a crystal flower begins to grow on a piece of heavy machinery, it is believed the machine will be stable for at least a generation.

**Spire trees** are the closest thing to traditional trees, but only in silhouette. They grow “roots” outward into the air, branching and spreading in a manner that would be impossible on any world where gravity behaved consistently. They collect not soil nutrients but the passing motes of light and the condensation of the fog wells. Their trunks are often hollowed by the city’s craftspeople to create living chambers where the humidity of the plant helps maintain a comfortable microclimate.

### Technology and culture as entities of their own

**Engines of permanence** and the factories that support them are not just tools; they are the closest thing Veridia has to deities. They have been built, modified, and repaired over generations by the Veridians: engineers and reality-maintainers who form a kind of secular priesthood of their own. They protect the city from the dissolution that would naturally occur at this edge of reality.

Their children are raised with the understanding that they may not live to see the completion of the projects they start. Platforms that are formed today will become fully stable only in the century after; object factories and matter processing plants are engineered to outlast their creators. Engineers of permanence mark their names into the walls of gravity engines not because they expect to be remembered, but because they want to leave behind a precise understanding of the decisions they made.

The **communication towers** that reach to other versions of Veridia are archaeologically bizarre. They connect to cities that exist *elsewhen* and *elsewhere* but are still recognizably this same floating structure above a fog ocean. People from different versions sometimes exchange subtle information: “We are seeing your future.” “We are living your past.” Messages are not transmitted in clear speech but as patterns of noise, static, and the Weeping of Matter that the engineers have learned to decode.

And **the Unformed**, perhaps, are the most intimate of inhabitants: not in any individual sense, but in the way they are the city’s living thought. They are not quite “native” to any stable version of reality; they are experiments in the edge of existence itself. When they act as travelers, weaving their way between platforms and bridges and factories, they leave suggestions of what life could be like if, one day, the floating city of Veridia reaches a more stable version of itself.

### In the end, who lives here?

If you had to summarize the inhabitants of Veridia in one phrase, you might say:

They are a **community of makers and mist-makers**—people who midwife reality into usefulness, insects who repair the machines of their world, floating whales who monitor the probability of its instability, and plants who turn the dampness and the dim, strange light of a fracture in the sky into sustainable life.

Their existence is not precarious because they are fragile; it is precarious because it is *in-progress*. They are the temporary, permanent inhabitants of a place that still hasn’t decided what it will be when it grows up.
