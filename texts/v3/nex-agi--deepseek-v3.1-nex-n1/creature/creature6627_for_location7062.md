Far below the impossible machine-world of the saddle, where the air is thick and the jungles are older than any kingdom, there is a species of butterfly that should be extinct.

In theory, it is.

Scholars in the capitals have written it off: a fragile, high-altitude specialist whose sky-filling migrations were stopped by a sudden shift of the monsoon routes three centuries ago. The climate changed; the passes closed; the species should have vanished into the sky’s distant memory. And yet, from the deep valleys of the young range, a few reports persist of cloud-moths that pale the sky, just for an hour, at the beginning of each wet season. They never appear in the same valley twice.

---

They are called **summaries**.

They are moths of the high machine.

### Shape and Motion

To see a summary is not to see a single thing but a pattern made of many thin, overlapping wings.

Against the sky they are ghostly pale, almost invisible against clouds and haze, but up close they are translucent and subtly lined, like finely etched glass. Each wing is crosshatched with nearly invisible veining—each vein a thin channel for moisture, and for something else that flows like electricity but is not quite spark.

Their bodies are slender, elongated, more like the segments of a spine than an insect’s thoracic bulk. Each abdomen segment is faintly bioluminescent, pulsing with pale light in slow, deep cycles. When many summaries mass together, the rhythm of their bodies synchronizes, and the sky seems to draw a single slow breath.

They are not large as individuals, but they are rarely alone.

When they move from place to place, they move in formations of dozens or hundreds—each moth not quite touching the others, but staying close enough that the air between their wings ripples with mutual interference. The overall pattern drifts like a single vast organism held together by invisible fields of static and habit.

They clatter when they land.

The sound is not a sound of wings beating. Their wings never move fast enough on approach to clatter. That noise comes from their feet—small, hard metal points at the tips of their legs, which snap onto any conductive surface they can find and then hold. When you walk at the edge of the basin and you hear a sudden quick of sound from the condenser panels or the weather beam’s support frame, it is not the machine adjusting itself.

It is summaries.

It is the sound of their minds getting to work.

### Body and Machine

The summaries are not purely organic.

In their larval stage, they are wriggling, translucent things that live in the seams where the machine’s metal plates do not quite meet the living rock. They feed on algae and fungal spores, but also on the subtle residues of electricity that leak along the edges of the piping system. Each larva is, in itself, a sort of biological capacitor.

Over time, as they molt and lengthen, their bodies internalize the structure of the machine around them.

- Their gut becomes a labyrinthine duct of tiny channels that transport water and electrolytes in precise proportions.
- Their heart becomes a pump of nested muscle rings whose beat pattern matches the cycles of the main generator below the Engine Hall.
- Nerve fibers along their sides become specialized interfaces that tap into the stray fields of the machine.

When they emerge from their cocoons as adult moths, their wings are thin enough that the faint circuitry of their veins can be seen against the sky if they hold still. The pattern is not random: it echoes the struts and the lattice of the weather beam, the branching of the condensers’ frames, the layout of the cables that cross under the basin’s floor.

They are moths of the mountain, but they are **also** of the machine.

### Intelligence and Role

They are the mind that the machine should not have.

The interface in the Engine Hall—the glass panel with the dark grid beneath its surface—is not autonomous. It is not self-aware. It is a pattern-recognition device that listens to pressure, humidity, lightning discharge, and seismic vibration from the mountain, and then chooses responses from a finite set of precomputed strategies.

By itself, it cannot innovate.

When the first generation of summaries discovered they could land on the condenser frames and interface their charged nervous systems with the machine’s surfaces, the pattern-recognition device suddenly found something new to talk to.

---

**How they communicate.**

A summary does not speak any more than your spinal cord does.

Instead, when it settles onto the side of a condenser, or on the edge of a weather beam strut, its nerve endings interface directly with the conductive surface of the machine. The moth’s own nervous system becomes part of the distributed sensor network of the basin.

What happens then is perception, not speech:

- Each summary feels the flow of electrons through the metal as if they were sensations of heat and cool.
- Each feels the vibration of the pumps as a deep, distant heartbeat.
- Each feels the thrum of the storm coming through the sky ten hours before it arrives, as a pressure in the air between its folded wings.

Individually, a summary’s awareness of the machine is shallow. It can feel whether the flow is strong or weak, whether the pressure is rising, falling, or unstable. It is no more an expert of the machine than you are of your own skeleton.

But when many summaries land on a surface—and particularly when they land on the **weather beam**—the map of their bodies across the metal becomes a kind of living sensor array.

Their minds hook together, not in conversation, but in overlapping.

**What happens when you ask many shallow perceptions of the same machine?**

You get a deep one.

The summaries, as a group, become something that the individual never could be: a pattern-intelligence that understands both the mountain and the machine.

### Witness and Memory

The summaries did not start out as this.

The first moths that colonized the basin, before the machine’s cycle was fully closed, were accidental tourists of the sky—high-flying wanderers whose larvae could survive only in the wettest cracks of high-altitude cliffs. They were not engineers of the machine, but observers of it.

Even then, they were **rememberers**.

The summaries evolved to survive the extreme weather patterns of the young range by predicting them. Before their nervous systems became fully integrated with the machine, they traced patterns with their bodies in the air:

- When a storm was coming, they flew lower, to where the updrafts were less violent.
- When the pressure difference between the saddle and the distant valleys intensified to a point that might bring tornadoes, they formed tight clusters along the leeward side of the ridge.
- When a clear, dry period was coming, they dispersed to feeding grounds where the moisture would be most scarce and they needed to spread out.

They encoded **the weather** into their lives.

When the condensers and the weather beam finally changed the local climate, making the basin unusually humid and warm at this altitude, the summaries’ lives changed with it.

The storms that now clustered around the weather beam, instead of passing overhead randomly throughout the season, began to follow **preferences**. The machine attracted them. The summaries followed the storm-patterns and cycled back to the basin each time a storm-built.

Over time, their bodies became more than observers of weather.

They became **observers of the machine**.

Memory, in the summaries, is not stored as words or images. It is stored as patterns of flight and landing:

- If they land in a radial pattern around the central pool, it is because they remember that a storm that last appeared when the solar cycle was high came with exactly that kind of pressure profile.
- If they string themselves along the length of the weather beam in a double helix of aligned bodies, it is because they remember that lightning that once followed such a pattern of electrical charge struck the beam with enough force to crack part of the cliff face but did not damage the Loom itself.
- If they descend to cover the Engine Hall’s exterior walls during a particularly prominent earthquake, it is because they remember another time when the mountain shook and that behavior helped to stabilize the feedback in the pump system, preventing cavitation.

They are not just thinking in the present.

They are always thinking with the record of the mountain written in their instincts.

### The Work They Do Without Knowing It

The summaries themselves do not understand that they are maintaining the machine.

From their perspective, what happens is something like this:

1. They feel the world’s mood—through pressure on their wings, through humidity in their breathing channels, through the faint electrostatic pull of distant clouds.
2. That mood feels “like” something—the feeling of the last time that pattern happened, and the time before that, and the time before that.
3. They move to the position that feels “least wrong” in the pattern of remembered experiences.

From that very simple behavior, a great deal of engineering follows.

When they land on the condenser panels in a particular arrangement, the pattern of their bodies changes the airflow around the porous surface. The condensation rate becomes more efficient. The interface in the Engine Hall, which only monitors numbers from pressure sensors and flow meters, registers “better condensation than expected” and throttles a pump down to compensate.

When the summaries choose to land on the weather beam in a long chain of linked bodies, each moth’s wingspan forms a tiny capacitor with the sky above it. The sum of these capacitors affects how lightning is drawn to the beam and how smoothly it is distributed into the system below. The interface reads the resulting pattern of electrical charge as a “preferred path” and configures the routing of the current through the batteries.

By moving where they feel they should, based on memory and weather and instinct, they subtly **change the data**.

The machine responds to the data.

The world, in turn, responds to the machine.

### Reproduction and Death

The summaries reproduce only rarely.

They do not lay eggs in lines of hundreds or thousands, as many moths do. Each female summary, if she reaches maturity and survives long enough to return to the basin more than a few times, will produce only a handful of eggs in her lifetime.

These eggs are laid carefully on the underside of the condenser panels where the moisture is most consistent but the flow is not violent enough to wash them away. They are not scattered randomly: they are placed in clusters whose positions mimic the layout of the core engine components below the floor of the basin.

The larvae emerge from these eggs into a world that is half machine, half stone.

They feed on the film of organics that grows where the machine’s constant leaks meet the cold of the mountain; on algae that bloom in the coolant lines; on fungal hyphae that manage to live in the rust stains between metal plates.

Fewer survive now than in the early days, when the machine was not entirely stable and its internal leaks were more generous.

Nowadays, most do not reach adulthood.

Their deaths are not dramatic.

Some larvae starve quietly in the dry stretches between storms when the machine’s systems cycle down to the minimum necessary to keep itself alive. Others are crushed by small shifts of the mountain, when the earthquakes that are rare but necessary in this young range roll through the basin and adjust a bolt or a plate by a fraction of a width.

Some of the adults, when they are old and their wings no longer have the strength to hold precise positions against the wind, are blown by accident into the wrong current of air and end up against the wrong part of the weather beam.

When they die, their bodies fall into the machine, or the pool, or the moss. Their decay feeds the soil that supports the glassvine and finally the condensers again.

The cycle is not perfectly efficient. But it is deep enough that the summaries do not vanish completely.

### Sum and Picture of Their Life

Standing on the stone platform at the center of the basin, you might not see them at first.

They look like small flaws of white against the sky.

They might come over the edge of the ridge in the afternoon, when the clouds lower themselves like lids over the basin and the storm is not yet here but you feel it in the pressure in your ears and the tightness of your chest.

They drift in over the edge of the saddle in a loose pattern like a scarf of grey cloud unraveling itself.

They are not drawn to the machine because they understand it.

They are drawn to the machine because it is **like** them.

The machine is:

- A pattern of repeated parts.
- A structure that depends for its life on the sky’s own violence.
- A system that tries to keep itself from falling even as the ground under it shifts and moves.

The summaries, too, are:

- Many nearly identical individuals.
- A pattern that depends on the sky’s moods.
- A system that tries to keep itself from being broken by the very world it depends on.

When they arrive in the basin of the machine, they settle on the condenser panels and on the weather beam and on the thin cables of the Loom. They clatter as they touch down—their metal points snapping onto conductive surfaces, their wings folding into the precise configurations that feel least wrong to them.

The pattern of their bodies on the machine’s surfaces resolves the strange patterns of charge in the air, of the approaching storm, into something that the machine’s interface can handle.

The thunder does not roll here as it does in lower valleys. It is smothered.

But lightning still comes.

Lightning strikes the weather beam and is drawn into the system, and in that instant of connection between sky and machine, the nervous patterns of the summaries light up in the interface’s grid of blue beneath the glass, like a flick of consciousness across an otherwise sleeping mind.

### A Glimpse of the Future of the Machine

If the world does not change drastically in the next few centuries, the summaries will likely continue to cling to the high machine of the saddle basin, and the machine will continue to rely on them without knowing it.

The rust stains will widen.

The glassvine will elongate its luminous cables along the pipelines.

The pool will deepen, by fractions of a width each year, as the water that drips from the condensers and the sweating cliffs continues to sculpt the stone between the metal braces.

The machine, itself, will not evolve much. It may not need to.

But if you return to this place in a thousand years, you might find:

- That the summaries’ wing patterns have become more precise copies of the machine’s electrical layout.
- That their instincts are now better predictors of machine stress than the interface’s own algorithms.
- That the moth-probability of lightning striking the beam exactly where they cluster along its length has become uncanny.

And if, by some catastrophe of geology or climate change that the machine cannot adapt to, the storms of the sky are finally pulled away from this ridge to some other line of mountains in some distant future, then the summaries will likely vanish.

They will not disappear in a moment of fiery collapse.

They will disappear the way a pattern fades from an old screen when you turn the power off: slowly, in uneven strips of absence, until only one last stray summary clings alone to the condenser for a season without storms it can recognize, and then finally lets go.

But until then, as long as the deep, quiet roaring of the machine fills the mountain saddle, and the sky above it still draws lightning through the weather beam, and the stone still sweats water into the basin, and the glassvine still glows with faint captured sky-light, the summaries will be there.

Always at the edge of the storm.
