Who lives here?  

No one name fits, but they are, collectively, the Keepers of the Least Path.

No humans dwell here; not anymore. If they ever did, they have long since become something else—or perhaps they designed the place to be tended without them, to be inhabited by smaller and more patient things. The true denizens of the bowl are not architects who imagine before they build; they are opportunists, improvisors, each generation making its own microscopic adjustments until the bowl itself shifts slightly. They are the water and its instruments, the same way that an orchestra is its instruments and their music at once.

### The water-birds of the threshold

They are the most visible inhabitants—small, round-bodied, scale-feathered birds that nest in the hollow cavities beneath the troughs. Their feathers are not really feathers at all but layered scales, smooth as river stones polished by centuries of flow. Each bird is no bigger than two closed fists together, but what they lack in size they make up for in clarity of voice and movement.

They move by creeping along the edges of the troughs, using hooked claws to grip the soft wood and wet slate, bodies low and close to the water. When they drink, they do not dip their beak in the water but catch the droplets that form on the undersides of the troughs’ rims and on the bronze chains hanging above the pools. Their eyes are large and dark, reflecting the faint luminescence from the water below like two polished stones of obsidian.

Each bird has a distinct call pattern—a set of high metallic notes repeated like the chiming of a broken clock. When you listen carefully, you can distinguish individuals by the rhythm of their song: one who calls in three sharp chirps followed by a longer pause, another who sends out four quick notes, then waits as if listening for an answer. Their songs are territorial signals, but they are also something less practical: they seem to sing because the bowl itself hums, and their voices slide into the intervals between tones of water and wood, creating a chord across the entire bowl-world.

At times of water-glut—when the condensation on the ceiling bronze thickens and drips faster than the drains can carry it away—the water-birds cluster more densely along the upper rings of troughs. Their claws leave scratch-marks where the wood has already been softened by water and time; these faint scratches align like the rings of tree trunk, recording not years but rainfalls within the bowl’s internal weather. Each mark is a refusal of chaos: the bird marking its steady path along the least resistance of the wood.

### The translucent swimmers of the troughs

If the water-birds are the voice, the swimmers in the troughs are the blood. The name “fish” does not belong to them properly; they are more like elongated larvae of glass or moving water itself given shape. Their bodies are translucent tubular sacs, segmented into faint rings along their length, each segment slightly lighter than the next, so they look like threads of solidified light. When they move, they eel along the shallow water channels, using thin rows of legs or tiny fins along their sides—not to swim strongly, but to trace the edges of the path where the water pulls them least, brushing the moss-covered stone and wood.

They glow faintly from within, each creature a minute lantern of pale blue or green-white, depending on which ring of trough they prefer.
