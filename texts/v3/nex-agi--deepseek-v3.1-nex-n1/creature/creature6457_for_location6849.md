Whoever lives in Eelbridge is not called “a citizen” or “an inhabitant” but *a rootling*.

They say it first as a joke, then a habit, then a badge. Somewhere between the moss that seals their walls and the algae that greens their soup, the word took root and now they wear it like a second skin.

Their bodies are small, compact, made for movement through tight spaces.

You never see someone tall here without at least a faint network of fading scars along their scalp, where the ceiling beams have tried to teach their head some humility. The rootlings are mostly of middling height or below, with a low center of gravity and a way of walking that looks more like sliding than stepping. They rarely stride. They creep, edge, side step, duck under the lintel of a world that has grown over and inward and together.

Their bones are lighter than you’d expect from the compact way they carry themselves.

Medical scans on the rare travelers who pass through show surprising calcium density, but in thin, graceful matrices like root hairs rather than thick pillars of bone. Generations of damp and uncertain load-bearing have shaped the architecture of them: ankles are strong, knees are good, spines are flexible, chins are tucked. They are not hunched in the way of fear, but in the way of people who know that every vertical space is borrowed from the branches above.

Their skin is always damp.

Not sweat in the usual sense of exertion, but the quiet sheen of constant humidity. When they step out of the room, the air seems to rush in to replace them. When they return, the moisture on their neck and chest beads up as if the room itself exhales relief at their presence.

Color is thrifty on them.

They are not pale, but their blood moves under a thin veil of gray, as if the light under the canopy has been absorbed into their capillaries and stored there like reserves in a root. Tan never has time to dig into them before the next soft rain disperses it. Rosy cheeks are a luxury of the sky-born world outside; here, cheeks are flushed in the narrow band of temperature between “stuffy” and “too stuffy,” and the color goes away the moment the air moves slightly.

Hair is thick, but controlled.

Curly or straight, it clings to foreheads and necklines in dark, wet ringlets, no matter how many times they tie it back. The more practical among them cut it short or shave it away entirely, leaving the scalp to be a collector of condensate instead of a tangled canopy of its own. On others, hair is worn braided and close to the skull, reducing the surface area for dripping and snagging in low-hanging cables.

Clothes are thin, but layered.

No one wears heavy fabric unless they work near the engines in the deep root-levels. The common rootling dress is a thin undergarment of breathable mesh to keep the skin from chafing, and then over it, one or two more loose layers that can be opened at the neckline when the humidity spikes and the temperature crawls upward.

Everything wicks.

Every sleeve seam, every seam along the shoulder of their shirt is designed with tiny channels to redirect water from neck to elbow, from elbow to cuff, where it can drop off without letting the damp pool into a cold rash on the skin. They call it “meeting the water halfway,” and they design their clothes the same way they design their plumbing: not as barriers to moisture, but as guides of it.

Their feet are never bare in public.

Walk barefoot on the boards too long and you’ll learn the difference between “damp” and “deep soak.” The soles of their footwear are thick, but pliable, with little domes of synthetic rubber on the bottom like the pads of a tree root on the bark of another branch: they grip and give and release continuously.

There are always patterns.

On the bottom of every shoe is a pattern of shallow channels—not so deep that they can be caught by uneven nails, but deep enough to redirect a thin film of water away beneath the footstep. You’ll see them on the stonework of the older stairs: a faint pattern of stars and spokes and murmuring rivers stamped into the damp stone dust over the years.

*Their hands are their tools, and the tools have their hands.*

Long before children can be trusted to be near the engines, they learn to work with small objects.

Their fingers are thick without being clumsy. They are not dainty—people whose fingers never bend into tight spaces are not shaped by Eelbridge—but they are strong, and the joints are flexible in a way that suggests more than just human inheritance. A small subset of rootlings, usually those whose ancestors rode out some long-forgotten industrial accident in the low root levels, can bend their thumb knuckles further backward than you’d think comfortable, and curl their little fingers completely into the palm.

They call this “the rooter’s curl,” a sign that you’re best suited to working in narrow spaces between pipes and trunks.

Their hands don’t show age in years but in exposure.

New hands are pale pink, and the water from the dripping gutters leaves translucent tracks on them. Old hands are dark, polished by the constant companionship of wet and oil and algae and grease. Oil lines thread into the lines of their palm like tiny rivers into silt. You can read a life’s trade in the patterns:

- Single-thin river down from the index finger: electrician.
- Weathered patches between first and middle finger: plumber.
- Slightly curled ring finger that always has a faint trace of rust under its nail: metalworker.
- Softened pads of fingertips and the faint edges of many small cuts: harvester of the algae trays.

They smell like wet stone and moss and engine.

There is no soap on earth strong enough to erase Eelbridge completely from their skin. If they wash thoroughly with the harsh, alkaline cleaners from the deep purification loops, they smell briefly like metal and clean plastic and the faint tang of ozone. Then, within half an hour of stepping back into the humid air, the city is back: wet stone, wet wood, green algae, and the faint sweat of living in a place that never exhales.

Their breath tastes like latent thunder.

Not because they are gone bad from the inside, but because the water they drink is filtered through the same root mats that accept their waste and the same biopathic systems that crackle with electric charge. To kiss someone in Eelbridge is to taste the city’s circulatory loop.

Their voices carry low, but they don’t need to raise them.

The main gallery of Eelbridge hums with enough noise of water and tree and machinery and people and birds and insects and dripping from above to mask all but the most fragile of whispers. But rootlings are not prone to shouting.

They talk in low, continuous tones, like the water that runs under their floorboards.

Their voices have been coaxed into the city’s register: not loud enough to be noise over the engine, but clear enough to be heard over dripping water. When they need to be heard across a room, they don’t raise their volume; they lower the background of their body—halting the movement of their tools, steadying the breath in their chest—so that their voice can emerge through the same quieter channel.

They have their own language, but it isn’t a separate tongue.

They call it *dropping*—the act of saying something that needs careful water.

Dropping is done when the speaker lets the sentence fall into the space between the next drip of water from the ceiling, and the listener has to catch it before the next hum of the transformer drowns it out. It’s how they argue, joke, whisper secrets, and announce things that are not technically allowed by the inspectors.

If you know how to listen, you’ll notice that everyone pauses at slightly different intervals in the same sentence. *Constructor.* *Inspector.* *Harvester.* *Engineer.* Each branch has its own rhythm of pauses and jump-ins, so that rootlings from the same branch of life can finish each other’s sentences without effort.

Their eyes are adapted to the green.

The first thing you notice when you meet a rootling’s gaze is the color—not the iris, but the way the world reflects off it.

The eye catches not just sky and lamp and person, but also the faint leaf tint from the glass above and the algae tint from the canal water below. The white of their eye is rarely truly white; more often than not, it is a faintly greenish, pearly gray, like the belly of an eel caught in the surface of the water for just a moment.

They blink more often than you expect.

Living under the low roofs of the world, where countless drops can be suspended from each edge, they’ve learned to tread the line between vision and protection. The eyelid comes down not like a barrier, but like a squeegee: rounding down from the forehead, over the eye, and then slightly curling up at the tip of the eyelashes to catch the next drip.

Many of them have a second line of lashes underneath the first.

Not true lashes in the sense of long, black strands of hair, but tiny nodules of clear skin along the lower edge of the eye socket that help direct water away from the pupil and into the tear ducts at the corners. The result is a kind of internal water-cycle of their own, where any excess moisture from the air gets reused instead of flooding their vision.

They are nearsighted by default.

The longest distance you can see clearly in Eelbridge is rarely more than fifty meters before you hit a trunk or a wall or a bend of the canal. The rootlings, as a result, are excellent at details close at hand: the lines of corrosion on a valve, the faint pattern of new growth rings on the edge of a board, the barely visible difference between two shades of green algae in the water trays.

They struggle with horizon lines.

Witnesses who have visited the outside world say that rootlings who come up with the trade crews step onto the surface only to be overwhelmed by the distance, the way the sky is open and unstoppable above them. They blink against the brightness of a sun that has not been filtered by leaf canopy and tinted glass, and they habitually walk in close to the walls or buildings, even when there is no roof above to drip.

But inside their city, their eyes are precise.

They can read the humidity in the air by watching the way lamps reflect on the wall: more droplets of light on a wall equals rising humidity; where they start to streak and join together is a warning to close the working window before the atmosphere inside the room becomes too oppressive to breathe.

They can see temperature changes.

No rootling child ever learns to read “degrees” on a dial before they learn to read the color of the trunk’s bark near the engine joints. When the engine of the house gets warmer than the air around it, the bark nearest the metal joint becomes slightly paler, as if the color of the tree has been diluted by the heat. The children of engineers and mechanics are taught that this “color rise” indicates the engine is running hot.

Their sleep is not continuous.

Living in a place where the water reverses flow quietly at night and the engines maintain their slow heartbeat below and above, sleep is not something that happens in eight-hour blocks like you would expect in a quieter world.

Rootlings sleep in short cycles of a few hours at a time, interrupted by brief wakefulness.

When they wake up, they usually don’t turn on the light—light is expensive, and the bioluminescent walls of their homes keep a faint, steady glow after dark. They will check the humidity in the room by touching the wall of their trunk home with the palm of their hand:

- If it feels cool and the surface of the bark is slightly raising with the moisture, the humidity is high and safe for sleep.
- If it feels warm and the bark is slightly tight to the touch, the cycle of the engines is changing, and they might need to adjust the vent window or their blanket layer.

The “smell of night” is different from the smell of day.

At night, the human smell retreats into the homes. The plant smell grows stronger: wet leaf, more decomposed root, the faint smell of new biomass from the algae tanks in the deep baths. The air is thinner in the upper layers as the high humidity sinks closer to the water level below.

When rootlings sleep, they breathe deeply.

They sleep on their sides or stomachs more often than on their backs, because that way they can breathe closer to the wooden floor where the humidity of the air is slightly less than at head level above them. Their beds are not soft but yielding: platforms made of a thin mattress of wet-synthetic on top of a more resilient layer of root-mesh and bioengineered wood that supports their body without allowing it to sink into the damp.

They eat the city that they maintain.

Most of the food in Eelbridge comes from the same closed systems that provide their oxygen and humidity and cooling.

They eat algae cakes.

These are dark green or sometimes brown, depending on the season of nutrients in the root bath below. Algae cakes are not like bread; they are more dense, like wet compressed moss that has been baked quickly enough to set the surface. Rootlings don’t say they are “eating cake”; they say they are *eating shadow*, because the algae cakes are the shadows of the waters below given something like body.
