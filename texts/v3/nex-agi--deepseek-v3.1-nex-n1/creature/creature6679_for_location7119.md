A delta of lichen has begun to creep from the rim of stone down into the lowest curve of the basin.

The rim of this place is not crossed by mistake, or by accident; it is trespassed by slow, microscopic degrees. The clinging plants that ring the basin edge have, over time, made their own claim of space. Their roots have massed in the shallow soil, then pushed outward into the mineral-rich seam of damp where the stone begins to slope downward. The mosslike forest that hangs above the basin floor finds this narrow seam and sinks itself into it like teeth into a wrist.

At the center of this encroachment, a single patch of lichen has begun to spill over the edge of the world.

It is not, properly speaking, a single organism. It is a crust of white, branching fingers that has learned to knit itself through the black deer of a down-plant’s roots. Where the plant reaches its limit, the lichen continues. Where the stone offers the smallest hint of moisture or minerals, the lichen follows, like dried ink soaking into damp paper.

The white branches of the lichen body are no more than half a centimeter wide each, but they rise together in a mat that blankets the curvature of the stone like frost. The surrounding basin rim is already soft with the moss-forest’s dripping, dark undergrowth; the lichen seems to have chosen to set itself just a little lower, where the stone slope begins to remember the downward pull of the center.

The growth of this delta is not self-important. It does not spread out to show off its dominance or its courage. It grows like a mistake someone is making with infinite care. Each new branch that emerges from the main body of the lichen is identical to the last in shape and width and shallow color, but infinitesimally longer in reach. The result is a pattern that resembles the inside of an ear or a shell—whorls and loops and lazy false starts that never quite close to a circle.

The lichen isn't stiff or brittle like its distant kin on high mountains. It's soft, plasticky, almost like skin made thin by the warm breath of the surrounding plants. Each branchlet bends slightly under the weight of the condensation that collects along it, and some of the lower ones rest on the basin floor itself like pale fingers reaching for the bottom of the world.

The sound of the sky does not seem to touch it. The lichen moves when the faintest breeze pushes down the slope of the basin floor, but its motion is not hurried or dramatic. It ripples slightly, like the surface of a pond given an extra breath of wind. One of the lower lobes of the lichen patch has discovered a minor crack in the basin floor, and has begun to test the edge of it, curling its growth along the inside of the break as if testing the temperature of water with the back of a hand.

The presence of this delta marks a change that the stone of the basin has not permitted before. The valley of shallow bowls, the center of which has always refused to allow any seed to root, does not seem able to decide whether to accept or reject the lichen. The center of the basin floor is still bare. The pools of water mirror the sky; the stone around them is still unwrinkled by any root or tendril. But here at the edge of the world, in the narrow seam where the basin rim begins to remember the pull of gravity, the lichen has negotiated a temporary treaty.

If the valley of erased footprints and forgetful stone had a memory, it might be that this lichen delta is merely the rim of the world trying to remember itself a bit farther forward. The stone at the delta’s leading edge is not as smooth as the stone at the center of the basin floor. It has been worn by something else, something that has not yet given up its memory of erosion or resistance.

The plants of the rim do not seem to mind its presence. The dark, layered fronds of the moss-forest hang over the delta, and occasionally one of their leaves brushes the top of the lichen patch, but no shadow of conflict lingers between them. They are both the residue of the same faint warmth that the plants exhale into the cold air of the basin.

There are no animals that call the delta home. The silver insect-visits that drift from the moss-forest above the basin rim occasionally pass over the lichen patch, but they do not land. The cold air of the basin gathers in the hollows between the lichen branches and presses the warmth of the surrounding plants out from them, creating the faintest thread of temperature difference that the insects might perceive as a border.

If someone were to sit down beside the delta of lichen, they would feel the slight difference between the stone of the basin floor and the stone under the leading edge of the lichen growth. The stone beneath the lichen is not smoother than the surrounding rock; it feels more like it has been lightly etched. The impression of thousands of tiny root-like filaments has been left behind, and the stone’s texture has responded to them, turning itself into a support structure instead of a rejection.

Over time, the delta of lichen will forget the center of the world. It will not send its branches into the middle of the basin floor where the stone is still sure of its solitude and where the shallow water refuses to keep any seed. The delta of lichen is not the trespass of the center; it is the slow, unimpressive compromise of the edge.

It endures the roar of the sky by the simple device of ignoring it. Its noise is not the sound of the endlessly breathing clouds but the slow, invisible conversion of water and air and stone into itself. The lichen grows like the sound of something else; a sustained, quiet hum that never rises to the level of the sky's note but also never stops to contradict it.

If the valley of the shallow bowls and the roaring sky remembers any of this change, it does not remember the lichen delta itself. It remembers the slow reshaping of the edge. One day the footprints that someone makes when they stand to leave the delta of lichen will be erased by the stone they walked on. The basin floor will remember its emptiness.

The lichen will not protest. It doesn't remember the footprints of the visitor. It doesn't remember the visitor at all. It remembers the shape of the stone it grew on; it remembers the seam of moisture and warmth and mineral; and it remembers the faint change of temperature when the moss-forest’s warmth and the basin’s cold air meet over the curve of its life.

It lives, indefinitely, on the wilt of that change.
