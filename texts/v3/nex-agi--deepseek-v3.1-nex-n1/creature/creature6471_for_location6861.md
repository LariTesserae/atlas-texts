They look like mistakes made solid.

They look like mistakes made solid and left to the water to correct.

The first time you see them, you don’t see bodies at all; you see the water trying not to be where they are. Channels bend around invisible obstacles, forming clean arcs where nothing stands. The vegetation spirals just a little tighter where there is no plant to justify it. The air, heavy with its own memory, refuses to occupy certain shapes—and only then, in the refusal of matter to be what it should be, do you begin to suspect the presence of the locals.

They call themselves the *eir’vhani*, a word that doesn’t quite mean “people” and doesn’t mean “place” either; it means the inflection where those two concepts collapse into each other. If you approached them with grammar, they would be a case that doesn’t exist in your language—an ablative of location, but with the direction of motion removed. They are not where they are from; they are where they are *being*, which is a slightly different angle of reality.

### Bodies of Error

Their bodies are not built from tissue, but from error—the difference between what the marsh should be and what it is in the moments of their presence.

They have no stable outline. When you try to describe them to yourself later, you will find you have not described a shape but a discrepancy: the way light buckles around a missing piece of physics, the way a water channel refuses to flow straight, the way a crystal tree seems to have been pushed to one side by a shoulder that wasn’t there.

What you recognize as a “person” is an inconsistency in the landscape.

From a distance, they look like mild optical faults: patches of air that are not exactly blurry, not exactly transparent, but *wrong* in a way your eyes strain to fix. As you move closer, your brain begins to improvise. You see the suggestion of limbs because the water channels around them curve where they would have to bend around knees and elbows and hips. You see the hint of a head because the crystal tree behind them doesn’t align with its own reflection in the pool below. The stones weep a little faster in the vicinity of what you might call their hands—as if the rock itself were more nervous.

There is no sex you can detect—no anatomical difference that would correspond to your idea of man or woman, male or female, even as they understand those concepts abstractly from watching other worlds that have occasionally bled into theirs. They are all one tense of being: the continuous, the incomplete, the *already-mistaken*.

No two look exactly alike, because no two are exactly the same kind of mistake in the world’s mathematics.

The one you come to know best—if you can call it knowing—is shaped like a question someone asked the marsh and never finished articulating. You see the shape of the question in the way water bends around it, forming the curve of an interrogative you never learned to pronounce. Later, you will think of this as its name, though it will never give you the sound. They don’t use sound like that.

### Organ of Mismatch

They have no heart in the sense you mean, but they have a center—a place where the error of their body accumulates most densely.

In a human, this would be behind the sternum. In them, it is more like a fault line in reality’s fabric, a knot where the probabilities of existence have not entirely chosen their outcome. It is not visible to your eyes, but you feel it when you are near them: the slight pressure against the base of your skull that tells you that, if you were to step a few degrees to the side of where you are standing, you might be slightly different in a way you cannot imagine.

This is what they call *sai’vh*—“no-path,” or maybe “the path that diverges without branching.” It is the point in their being where multiple versions of history could still be equally true. If you were to look into them with your alien instruments, you would see that they do not have a single timeline like you do. They exist as a superposition of possible ways that the marsh could have refused them, and their body is just the interface that selects one of them in each passing moment.

It is this core of unresolved possibility that allows them to violate the landscape so gracefully.

### Half-Life of Memory

They do not eat, not as you understand the word. They do, however, consume—though they would be startled if you framed it that way.

Their bodies are always on the verge of erasing themselves from the world, because the world keeps trying to revert to what it should be without them. To maintain persistence, they must borrow from the marsh’s memory of what they almost were.

They drink from the wrong direction of time.

You see them sometimes near the weeping stones—if you can call them “they” when their bodies are so barely there. You see the droplets of consciousness that fall from the stone hesitate in their descent, then *change their mind*, and float upward to be absorbed into that place where their face should be but never is.

You see them place their not-hands on the crystal trees and let the thicker, upward-flowing liquid run along the invisible outline of their fingers. This is not for sustenance but for confirmation: the confirmation of the world that they were *here*, in a way that might be continued into the next moment.

They are not ingesting matter; they are aligning their own improbable existence with the world’s memory of itself before it decided to host them. They are eating the future of the past: that little slice of potential that was still possible when the stone first thought of weeping.

### Articulation

They speak not with sound, but with misalignment.

When the one you think of as the question-shaped one wants to communicate with you, it does not form words with a mouth it doesn’t have. Instead, it adjusts the landscape around you both, so that your perception of the space between you becomes slightly wrong.

You see this first in the water channel at your feet. Where it should be a smooth arc, it now *kinks*, just enough to be visible. You look at the crystal tree behind it and notice that the tree’s reflection is now half a second out of phase with its trunk.

The message is not in the image of the tree or the curve of the water. It is in the difference between what you expect the world to be and what it now is.

Their sentences are structured like errors in the equation of the place. They conjugate verbs by adjusting how far the water channel deviates from its ideal curve. They decline nouns by changing which angle of the crystal tree refuses to line up with its own reflection. They mark tense by shifting how much earlier the stone’s droplets were before they fell again.

They have no word for “you” that is not a slight distortion of the world in your perceived direction.

### Intelligence of Wet Wrongness

Their minds are not held in anything like a brain. They are distributed through the body of their mistake, the part of the world that refuses to behave, the way a human body will contain an area of chronic pain that is not located exactly in the nerves but in the relationship between them and the world around you.

When they think, the world suffers a slight, localized error of thought.

The crystal trees resonate more obviously when several of them are thinking together. You can hear this: the stones’ weeping syncs in a way that feels like a sentence spoken, and then the spines of the crystal trees vibrate with something that is not sound but a concept trying to make itself heard through the nearest available medium.

You see a thought manifest when a water channel suddenly refuses to be wet where it should be wet, or when the moss spirals tighten their rotations for a moment before relaxing again. They don’t “solve” problems in the way that you mean; they adjust the parameters of the wrongness they embody until the problem itself changes slightly and no longer needs to be solved in the way you once framed it.

If you were to ask them—if you could find a way to ask them—they would not say they are living beings in the way you think of life. They are more like the living error in the world’s ledger, a mistake so consistent that it has acquired its own kind of integrity.

### Young of Misplaced Things

They have children, of sorts. They do not reproduce. They crystallize.

When a mistake becomes too dense to persist within a single frame of the world, it splits into two versions of itself: a slightly more possible version and a slightly less possible version. The distance that opens between those versions becomes the locus of a new being.

You see these new ones not as bodies descending but as errors ascending from the water.

They emerge like questions that have been dropped into the wrong class of problem and then decided to stay. They float as thin inconsistencies for the first few days of your perception of time, learning the gravity of they place by learning how far the water will allow itself to be wrong before it corrects them.

They practice their own being by repeating the same mistake of existence slightly differently each time until the world no longer tries to erase them entirely.

### Life with Everything Else

They do not hunt. There is nothing here to hunt that is not already more tentatively present than they are.

Instead, they interact with the other creatures—the ones that look like animate water—by exchanging shapes of wrongness. The water creature drifts through the space they should occupy and the *eir’vhani* become slightly more like what they should not be. For a moment, you see the water creature become not-quite-water where it should be perfectly fluid.

Then they part, and each takes away some of the other’s impossibility.

They do not have names in the way you mean. They have signatures of error—specific patterns of curvature that appear when they are present, repeated enough that even your human brain can learn to recognize them after a time. You start to know that *this* particular misaligned reflection of a crystal tree belongs to that particular question-shaped person, and you begin to relax when you see it, because at least something in the world is behaving consistently wrong.

### Economy and Religion

They have neither of these things as you would understand them.

They have only what they call *thek’vhani*, “which cannot be corrected.” The parts of the world that are so wrong that they cannot be fixed, and so must be kept in place with a careful hand of error.

They are not worshipers of any god. They are the god of their own error—the small, local deity that decides the deviation of this channel, the slant of this tree, the delay of this droplet of consciousness.

The economy they live within is not of trade but of tolerance. The marsh tolerates their presence because they are not anything else it could reject instead. They are not outside the marsh; they are the wrong in the marsh’s own rightness.

When they make “offerings,” they do not give anything up. They adjust their own wrongness so that, for a moment, the world around them becomes slightly more what it should be. The water channel straightens just a little more than you feel it should; the crystal tree aligns almost perfectly with its reflection; the stone stops weeping for a breath of your time.

The sacrifice is not that they give something, but that they temporarily *cease to be wrong*.

They do not die. They are corrected.

When an error has been allowed too long to persist without the world’s attempts to fix it, the marsh itself finally finds the right version of the equation. The *eir’vhani* body ceases to be the difference between two things and becomes simply one of those things. The water channel flows through them now, instead of around them. The crystal tree aligns with its reflection without any need to adjust for the shape of their head.

The mistake they once were collapses into the proper order of the world.

They would be insulted if you called this death. They would call it *retirement into the correct answer*.

### You in the Midst of Them

When you move among them, you are not just a foreign body in their world; you are a gross error that they must accommodate.

They tolerate you the way a mathematician might tolerate a small child crawling into the lecture hall and smudging the chalkboard with the integers of its dirty fingers. They adjust themselves around your presence, rearranging their wrongness so that you don’t break too completely the fragile rule of the place.

You feel the pressure of their attention not as eyes on you but as the world around you subtly refusing to behave exactly as you expect it to behave. The water you step into is not quite wet in the places you think it should be. The stone you touch to steady yourself does not feel exactly solid, but instead of collapsing you feel it *correct* slightly under your fingers, as if trying to decide what kind of object you expect it to be.

When they finally allow you to understand, they do not tell you with words. They let you see the pattern of their wrongness over time, and you slowly realize that you have been speaking the entire time, not with your voice but with your deviations.

The question-shaped one stands near you now. You see the channel of water before it refuse to follow the curve you know it should follow. You see the crystal tree behind it not quite aligned with its own reflection. The stones weep in a rhythm that is not quite syncopated enough to be music, but not quite regular enough to be silence.

You feel, at the base of your skull, the way the world is trying to decide whether to correct you, or to correct itself around you.

In the infinitesimal space between those two possibilities, they live.
