Deep beneath the breathing ceiling of ocean, there is no need for hurry or for fear.  

The People of the Undershelf are slow and quiet and endlessly patient.

They call themselves the *Svarish*—an old sound made of compressed air and water together—and they have been here as long as the basins have been deepening.

---

### 1. The Body That Fits

Svarish bodies are adapted to a world of thin air and heavy water.

Their height is low, often no taller than a child’s chest, to keep their head safely below the lowest curve of the ceiling. When the ocean overhead swells and pressure thickens, they crouch instinctively, lowering their center of mass and shortening their silhouette to fit into narrower layers of air.

Their skin is pale, not with the absence of sun—there is no sun to avoid—but with generations of life beneath dim, blue‑green twilight. It carries faint shifts of color and pattern like silt settling under moving water. When agitated, or thinking deeply, or listening to the pressure of the ocean above, their skin mottles with faint, slow‑moving rings of darker and lighter shade.

Their hair—if hair it is—grows in thin, translucent fronds that remain saturated, always damp with mist and spray. Each strand is faintly phosphorescent, holding patches of life similar to the algae clouds in the water columns: little more than faint smears of light in the dark, a faint halo of self when seen from the side. They do not brush or comb it; it moves with slow, weightless currents in the air layer, like seaweed suspended just below the surface of a wave.

Their hands are broad and strong, with thickened palms, from pressing weight against wet stone, steadying themselves in uneven basins, and testing the pressure of currents. Their fingertips have hardened edges where they have spent years tracing the ridges and worn paths of the floor, feeling for fracture lines and fragile deposits of mineral.

Their feet are wide and flat, with toes that can spread widely, to grip the uneven stone of the basins. When they stand still, they sink slightly into the layer of silt and water that coats the floor, and their feet leave shallow prints that show the faint markings of their pressure and balance—imprints that, in the stillness of this place, can remain undisturbed for days.
