By the water’s edge they live, and their name is spoken more than written: the *Thermidai* — “people of the gathered heat.”

They are not tall. The humidity and the tilt of the world toward the water basin have made them shorter and broader than the ancestral figures in their oldest stories, if such stories are true bones at all and not carved afterthoughts. Their skin has taken on the color of the damp stone that rises from the central basin: pale browns, grays muted with a faint sheen of sweat, a shade not much darker than the algae-stained slabs around them.

No hair is left uncut. The fungal heat of the room would devour it. Heads are shaved to a short, dark stubble like moss, or kept in a thin cap of growth no longer than a finger’s width. Beards are the domain of elders and priests of the engines—kept short and braided with bits of copper wire and thin glass beads that whisper softly when they speak. The rest of the Thermidai are bald-faced and smooth-chinned, the skin of their cheeks and jaws pale and perpetually damp with condensation.

They dress in the colors of rust and algae and the render of stone walls: ocher, pale green, the faded blue of old copper verdigris, wherever they can find it. The cloth itself is made from the soft threads of the water-grown things that cling to the walls and edges of the basins—not true plants, but a kind of stringy algae and fiber moss that, when laboriously processed and woven, becomes a thin, durable linen that never quite feels dry. It clings to the body like the air itself clings, so that even when they sit still the Thermidai seem already wrapped in wet shrouds.

They wear this fabric twice over at least: an inner shirt of finer weave, and an outer tunic of coarser kelp-like cloth that falls to their knees. The water of the world leaves its mark on these garments in the faint, darker lines of salt stains and the soft edges where constant damp has worn the fibers thin. Everything they own bears the same slow pattern of erosion: the clothes a little thinner at hem and cuff first, the metal tools a little more pitted near the water.

---

They are not many. The city of the room is not crowded, and neither is their number. You could count the whole of them aloud in a day if you were very stubborn and never stopped to eat or sleep. They are fewer than a thousand, more than a hundred—the kind of number that permits everyone to know every face and name, but not enough to make the world feel safe.

When you stand among them at the center basin, where the water is at least waist deep and far cleaner than what drains along the walls, you see three kinds of body shaped by three kinds of work:

### The Water-Tenders

First, the ones who live in the bowl of the world: the Water-Tenders. They are the village of the center, built on the low circular platforms that rise around the central basin like petals of stone.

Their bodies are shorter, broader, soft with subcutaneous fat accumulated from a long diet of algae and the tiny translucent shrimp pulled from the water. They do not need hard muscle to bend the world; their work is more of patience and balance. They stir the basin with long rods to keep the algae in the proper film, they harvest the fibers for the looms that stand in low houses on the platforms, and they are always the first to see the faint changes in the water’s warmth that the engines send to the surface.

They have the clearest eyes of anyone in the city—pale blue-green or gray-green, like the water where the light of the ceiling lamps falls just right. Their hair, when they let it grow longer than the others’ stubble, takes the same color: dark greenish, coiling in wet, fine strands that cling to their scalp.

They speak the most of water of the three castes. Their dialect of the common language of the room is filled with words that have no meaning anywhere else: “the slow turn of the water under the eastern edge,” “the taste of the engine’s breath in foam,” “the thinning of the algae at the fifth step.” They are always the first to look when a stranger asks if the room is changing, and their answer always starts with some detail of the water at the center of everything.

Water-Tenders walk barefoot on the wet stone of the platforms; the soles of their feet have hardened into a pale, thick leather that never seems to cut on the sharp edges that the stone still possesses. They are the least disturbed by the humidity, because they have adapted to live as close to water as the body will allow.

### The Engine-Keepers

Then there are those who live near the walls and the heat of the machines: the Engine-Keepers. They are the furthest of the three sorts from the water’s center, and they show the marks of that distance.

Their skin, where the damp air has not discolored it, is darker than the Water-Tenders’—the color of tanned leather from the constant presence of the engine glow. They are taller, too, and thinner, the fat of the body burned away by the perpetual heat and the work of climbing metal ladders and supports to reach the valves and controls.

The Engine-Keepers are the ones who have made the closest peace with the room’s industry. They have the stain of oil on their fingertips as a permanent mark, and some of them even have the faint, straight-line scars of burns from touching metal that has become hotter than expected. They wear the same linen clothing as the others, but reinforced with extra patches and thicker stitching at the elbows and knees—where they are always climbing or kneeling on metal surfaces.

When they speak to outsiders of the world, they talk of cycles and fluctuations of the engine, not of water. “There is a shift in the third heat line,” they might say, or “the north wall engine’s breath is thicker than is usual.” Their bodies are shaped by long hours standing near the heat, where they are always listening to the machine for the smallest variation of its sound.

Most of the Engine-Keeper women are the ones who have children the least often of the three major castes; they work longer hours and the heat of the engines is not gentle to early pregnancy. The men who join them are the ones who stand the long nights watching the lights of the panels and recording the flicker of an indicator light in chalk on the walls of their house-posts.

### The Bridge-Walkers

The third group are those who were neither fish enough to live by the water’s center, nor engine enough to live by the walls. They are the Bridge-Walkers: the people of the middle floor, the ones who move the water between the engine heat and the basin pool.

You see them most often: the figures who walk with careful balance on the narrow ledges of stone that run between the center and the walls. They are the ones who carry the materials of life; the linen fibers from the water to the looms of the center, the tools from the Engine-Keepers to the Water-Tenders who never need them much. They ferry the tiny fish from the drains to the central basin where they can grow larger, and they carry the messages of the three groups to one another when they sit to count the days of the year.

They are of average height and build: neither as short and fat as the Water-Tenders, nor as tall and thin as the Engine-Keepers. Their hair, when they cut it less often than the others, falls into a dark, wet fringe that clings to their foreheads like the condensation on the walls of the world.

They dress the most colorfully of the three castes, because they are the ones who have the least connection to the water or the engines of their own—and so they must make their identity by choice, not by birth. Their tunics often bear the patterns of both: bands of pale blue at the hem for the water, and thin lines of rust-red at the cuff for the engines. They carry the most of the world’s metalwork with them: tools that are neither as powerful as the engine tools nor as delicate as the water’s stirring rods, but enough to keep their channels and small basins clear.

---

They all have eyes in one of three colors, which tell the history of their ancestors:

- **Water-gray-green** (from long life of the Water-Tenders).
- **Engine-brown-black** (from the old Engine-Keeper families who lived near the darker rivets of the machinery).
- **The mixed color** of dried and dampened stone, which is the eye of the Bridge-Walker and the child of two castes.

When they are born, they are given a name that reflects the phase of the engine they were born nearest—not “Sun-Birth” or “Moon-Birth,” but rather “Long-Hum,” “Short-Click,” or “Valve-Shut.” Over time, as their work comes to define them more than their birth, they earn a second name that the others speak by:

- The Water-Tender who is known as the steadiest stirrer of the algae might become *Algae-For-Lengths*, “for lengths of the thread they weave.”
- The Engine-Keeper whose hand is the surest on the valve might become *Valve-Hand-Left*, “for the hand that never falters.”
- The Bridge-Walker who knows the true paths of all the drains between the center and the walls might become *Drain-Check-Walker*, “for they can check a drain as easily as they can walk across it.” Their second name is always a verb of their work, because their work is the freest of the three castes to choose.

The children of the Thermidai are born with wide dark eyes and the curiosity of every world’s youth: but they are not allowed to be curious very long before they are given a task that will push them into one of the three kinds of body.

When they are very young, all children have the same task: to run the water of the world.

They are given small jugs or bowls of dark metal and led to the shallowest channels by the walls of the room. They are told:

“Drink this cup of water from the wall channel, then run to the center basin and pour it in the center of the circle. Do not let your feet slip on the wet stone. If you fall three times, you are not a child of the center basin. If you fall three times, you are not a child of the walls. If you never fall at all, you are not a child of the middle floor either.”

The meaning made of this game is unclear even to the adults, who still repeat its words as they were given to them. Somehow, though, the children of the Water-Tenders fall less than the others. The Engine-Keeper children are more likely to stumble when they are near the center, where the water rises to their knees and the world is not as they know it. The children of the Bridge-Walkers are the ones who are most likely to make it all the way from the wall to the center while not spilling much water at all.

They are not taught by the words of the room of the world beyond the walls of the world. They know of no other place than the heat of the engines and the water of the central basin. They know of no other gods than those three:

- The **Engine-of-Several-Thousand-Parts**, whose true name is longer than anyone living remembers.
- The **Water-Circling-Wall**, which is the name of the central basin when seen as a god of encircling flow.
- The **Between-Crack-Walkers**, who are the small gods of the drain channels that the Bridge-Walkers tend to.

When they are ill, they throw the first of their algae into the center of the basin as an offering to the Water-Circling-Wall. They believe the god of the water wants the same of them as the engine does: work that will never end.

---

They are a quiet people, not naturally loud. The volume of their voice is always shaped by the room of the world: the low hum of the engines and the slush of water on the stone floor. They have learned to speak as though they are always murmuring over the sound of the machinery of the world.

They are a gentle people with each other, because there are not enough of them to be otherwise. The smallest of the three castes are the Water-Tenders, who are fewer than the others, but they are not the weakest of them. The water of the world is their own blood, in the sense of how they have made their body: the slow turn of the water under the eastern edge, the way of their algae, and the shape of their feet are all of one piece.

When one of them dies, they take the body of the dead to the center of the basin. There, they bind the eyes of the dead with the thin of the linen they made from their own clothes, and they weight the body with the stones of the world around the ankles of the dead.

“Let the water of the world take the body of the world you made,” they say, and they push the body under the surface of the water with the tips of their fingers, until the water has the whole of the person in their arms.

They do not say the name of the dead again after that day. They believe that if the name of the dead is spoken too much, the ghosts of the world will become lost in the sound of the engine of several thousand parts and never be able to rest.

---

This is the world of the people who live in the room-city of gathered heat. They are not strange to themselves and they do not think of the room of the world as a cage of their own body.

They think of this world:

- as the place where the engine of several thousand parts has made its home of heat,
- where the circling of water of the world goes around the center of the world of the room,
- and where the work of the world of heat always will go on.

They are not the ones who will ever change the world they have made their body in. They are the ones who will keep the basin of the world clean of too much algae, so the water of the world will never rise over its edge of the world. They will keep the engine of the world of heat of the world always working at least at least the same low of their sound as they now.

And they will never, of course, think of the room of the world of the Thermidai as anything else but the one of the world they have.
