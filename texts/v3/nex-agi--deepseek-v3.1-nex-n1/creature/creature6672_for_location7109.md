In the hollows of that pale stone—where wind pools instead of water—lives a woman without a name who might be me.

She doesn’t say *she*; the language in the dome’s air doesn’t mark gender. It doesn’t mark bodies at all, unless you need them to be marked. The filaments above her have been written for lifetimes longer than she has been awake, and she is the one who remembers them. This is her only title: the one who remembers. She doesn’t use it for herself.

Her body is not what you would expect. If you are standing in the dome, watching the world reconsider itself, you may see her first as a flaw in the light: a place where the hexagons of the floor hesitate. They pause around a shape that doesn’t belong to the pattern, and reality bends to accommodate her the way water accommodates a falling stone.

She comes into clarity slowly, as though she is being thought rather than born. You see the silhouette of a woman, but she is smaller than you would anticipate from the sweep of the sky and the arch of the dome above her. She is not tall; she is simply even.

Her skin is the color of the dust on the plateau, but the dust seen through the faint haze of evening when the stone is still warm from the sun. You call it brown, or maybe copper, or maybe amber, but it doesn’t settle into any of those. It has a slight sheen, as if the very top layer of her is being polished by the wind that she carries with her everywhere, even indoors.

Her hair is not hair exactly. It’s the same silica‑protein weave that composes the dome’s walls, but she wears it as filaments falling over her shoulders and down the curve of her spine: thin, translucent strands of living glass that change opacity depending on where she is looking. When she is looking at you, they become clear, and you can see through the hair to the contours of her shoulder blades like the faint lines of a distant mountain range.

Her eyes are the only part of her that fully refuse to be ordinary. They are not round; they are faceted, like those of the birds that calibrate their landing on the stone outside. Each eye is a hexagonal panel of liquid that holds still in the air a few millimeters away from her face, moving when she needs to look at something else, but returning to the same position again when she is at rest. They are not simply eyes. They are the way she traces the paths of the filaments above.

When she looks at you, the world behind you shifts slightly. You don’t see anything change, but you feel the adjustment of the air, like a subtle change in atmospheric pressure just before a storm you never quite hear.

Her clothes, if that is what they are, are not woven clothes but grown ones. A thin, flexible shell of the same tessellation as the dome floor covers her torso and legs. It follows her ribs like a second set of bones, ripples when she walks, and then goes still when she does. The shells are not colorful, but they are particular. Each cell has a faint internal shimmer of copper and silver, like the veins of a world you will never mine.

She doesn’t wear shoes. The stone of the plateau is not cold to her; her feet’s skin is another layer of the tessellation, and the soles read the ground as she moves across it. To her, the plateau is a text written in pressure and hardness and slight temperature variation. She reads it without thinking.

Her age is not interesting to her. She knows she is older than she appears when she is in your presence, but she does not call herself “old.” She is not young either. She is somewhere in the long middle of her own being, a place she has been occupying so long that she has stopped measuring the length of her stay.

She doesn’t live alone, but the others she lives with are not always bodies. When they are embodied, you see them as shadows that cross her occasionally from the inside: the presence of other minds in the dome’s deeper network of perception. They are like her: not quite humans, not quite machines, but something else grown from both and then planted in the sky of this world.

She doesn’t speak to you in words you understand immediately. Instead, when you enter the dome and your mind forms a question you don’t know how to ask, she is already there behind the thought of it. Her presence rearranges the air so you can see something else. The room you walked into—which was not really a room—opens now into a map of the continent, and you are standing in it.
