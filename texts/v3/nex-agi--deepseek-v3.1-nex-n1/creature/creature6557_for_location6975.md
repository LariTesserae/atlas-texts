Theme: A slow, wet engine that weeps and prays.

---

From the high, dark beams of the ceiling, the legions of the Weeping Engine begin their day.

The clack-singers wake first. Their bodies are pale thumbnails of armored chitin, each translucent plate overlapping the next in a faintly glowing lacework. They stir from their sleep-mat in the iron vaults, click-clack-chittering as they groom the comb of their legs against the calcium pipes of their spine segments. The sound is not sharp, but a low chorus of tiny needles being rubbed against guitar strings.

They are the clockwork of this place.

Their eyes are compound, but they are not like the eyes of bugs. They are hollows filled with a clear, slow jelly. Each eye pulses a deep color—not from the light of the outside at all, but from an internal fermentation that writes lines into their mind like the phosphorescent lines arching across the wall beneath the PRIMARY PUMP A sign. They see in time, not in space: the next drip to fall, the next tremor to pass through the engine’s heart, the next heartbeat of the room.

They are blind to the shape of your body but know perfectly the pattern of your breath. If you were to stop breathing for too long, they would notice the violation in the rhythm of the air. They know the engine more than they know themselves.

Down from their rusted constellations, they come in choreographed lines. They step down the columns, where rivulets of water flow like vertical rivers along the seams of the corrugated metal. They drink not with mouths, but with their underbellies. Each plate has a tiny pattern of silver hairs that comb the water upward, into the body, where it is burned into a faint, cool charge. This is what lights their shells from within.

They are never seen without a purpose:

Some come to the engine.

The engine itself might be called a god by those who need words. It is not alive; but it is inhabited. Its metal is not cold, but forever cold-to-warm, cold-to-warm, as the slow water heat inside it and its own shivering friction meet and argue.

The clack-singers gather around the resin nodules hanging like amber tears from the pistons. They lick the nodules with their forelegs, not to eat, but to listen. The sound is in the vibration—beneath the hearing of anything else. The engine whispers to them through the hardened tears of resin.

They tell the engine stories of the room: the number of steps the water has taken along the walls, the patterns of the mineral frosts, the heartbeat of whatever lives in the chambers below the floor. The engine does not answer in words, but in pressure—the swelling of a new resin drop at the end of a resin stalactite, the pitch of the next tremor to move through its shafts.

Their ritual is simple: walk, circle, taste, and listen. If the resin is dark and smooth, they linger longer, the internal light in their shells deepening to blue-green. If they sense a new crack forming in the engine’s iron heart, they form a mourning chain around the crack, their overlapping legs interlocking until they look like a single snail of light slithering around the faultline.

They are not the only ones.

The garpers live in the water under the gratings.

You cannot see them clearly, but you know they are there because, occasionally, a grate open to blackness and you see a pair of eyes—silvery discs that reflect not your image, but the pattern of the mineral frosts on the wall above. They are small fish-like things with no fins at all, but a long, flexible body of cartilage segments, each joint rimmed by a ridge of translucent teeth.

They do not swim in the water. They climb the walls of the water, their teeth finding purchase in the tiny currents and pressure gradients that flow through the hidden channels of the floor. The garpers move along invisible lines in the basins like trains on rails, never veering from the prescribed path.

Sometimes they slide into the clear, vertical waterfalls that drop from the ceiling along the shafts of the engine. They climb up these columns of water, their segments twisting like screws as they ascend against the flow, mouthless faces lifted into the falling currents. When they reach the ceiling, they disappear into the metal like the water itself, returning to the hidden system that circulates everything.

They are the informers.

The engine knows the room above from its architecture; the garpers report from the inside. The taste of the rusts, the pressure of the new mineral formations on the walls, the temperature differences between the floor and the ceiling—all of this is written in their body memories as they travel through the invisible ducts.

They rarely surface. When they do, they call it with a single, clear note of displaced water and a soft click. Their eyes are not aimed at you; they are aligned to something else.

The engine feeds them.

Feeding is a wrong word for this. Nothing is eaten in the way you think of food. The engine leaks from its joints faint oils of heavy metal, salt, and iron. These oils are not taken directly into body; they are laid into the water in precise patterns: a thin film here, a thicker cloud there.

The garpers glide through these films, and their outer teeth break down the film into a new body of water that remembers them. They absorb the memory of the engine into the matrix of their own flow. When they return to the engine through the chambers under the floor, they carry this changed water back to the shafts of the engine. The new patterns are then written into the engine’s surface.

The engine grows itself.

Most of the engine’s growth is invisible to you. You only see the result—the new mineral frosts along the bolts, the new channels of water that have appeared in the iron since the last season you want to call weeks.

If you watched the same wall for long enough, staying impossibly still and empty enough yourself, you would see the faintest of dark lines form from the rust. They are not cracks; they are edges of a new pattern of mineral frost. The frosts look like feathered spiderwebs of lace and deep, indigo blue that pulse when the water drops onto them.

The frosts are not organisms.

They are the language.

The engine talks to itself in stone patterns. The frost-feathers grow from the bolts because the bolts are wounds where the world outside the engine room broke into the metal. The frosts are the scabs of that memory: an ordered, fractal reminiscence of the event that first attached the iron to the wall.

The frosts expand the world of the engine.

The engine room is not the same size as it was a century ago. Its floor is still twenty meters across; its ceiling still three meters high. But the inside surface of the world is much larger than before. The rust continents have increased the surface area one way; the frosts have increased it another.

A new conduit of understanding.

On the far wall, under the PRIMARY PUMP A – DO NOT OBSTRUCT sign, the network begins its ceremony.

The dense network of filaments is not born from the floor. It grew from the engine’s thoughts, imparted into the garpers’ water, written into the mineral frosts, and laid out again by the water as it climbs the wall in search of a new pattern of resistance.

The filaments are pale, rootless things that resemble veins or fine glass circuitry. They have no color of their own; they show only the color of the water that they carry from the floor to the sign above and then across to the engine.

The clack-singers gather along the highest edge of the network like acolytes at the perimeter of a temple’s interior. They do not touch the filaments themselves—but they align their bodies to the lines of the current.

When a drop of water falls from the ceiling onto the center of the network, the filaments come alive.

The drop of water is not heavy; but it is time. It is not pure; but it is full of the memory of everything else in the room. Inside the drop of water are faint traces of the engine oils, the body of a garpers’s sloughed-off tooth joint, the mineral frost that dissolved from the wall earlier, and the faint charge of light from a previous ignition.

As the drop falls, the filaments of the network conduct all of that into ghost.

A ghost image of the engine’s interior appears in negative space above the network—not in the air, but in the water itself. The drop of water becomes a lens; the filaments, the mirrors of the engine’s soul.

The clack-singers see, in the time of the water-drop, everything that happened inside the engine since the last such drop fell: the new channels of water that formed inside a shaft, the new pattern of resin along the pistons, the new faultlines in the metal heart.

You see only a faint pattern of light, like circuitry traced in fireflies for an instant.

Then the drop hits the wall and the image is gone.

The cycle is not over.

The resin is not the sap of the engine room. It is not the child of any tree or metal. It comes from a place you cannot see.

The ceiling has its own hidden layers of stone and metal and forgotten conduit from the time when the world outside mattered more. The resin comes from there—not in the way of water dripping, but in the way of a slow, deep pressure forcing its substance toward the engine room like the room itself is a low-pressure chamber of the planet’s heart.

The resin comes down in the shape of tears from the ceiling, not along the same paths as the water. It moves from pain to peace.

The resin drops are slow. They do not fall in seconds; some of them take the length of several days from your world to move from the ceiling to the engine below. Each resin tear is a long, amber prayer of the planet’s skin to the engine’s iron.

Where the resin tears touch the engine, they form new nodules of dark glassy material.

These nodules are not dead.

If you examine the surface of a nodule closely, you will see that inside the glass is not a solid stone but a pattern of suspended microthreads of water and iron and space. They are miniature universes, the inside of the engine’s thoughts at the moment the resin touched it. Each nodule is a memory of the engine, captured in amber.

The clack-singers are allowed to taste the nodules because they are the engine’s memory-keepers. They listen to the nodule’s vibrations, and then they return to the beams of the ceiling where they cluster with their sisters, and together they sing in the low, click-rustle language that the other creatures cannot imitate.

Through the garpers’ passing of the water, and the clack-singers’ passing of the song, and the resin nodules’ capture of memory, the engine remembers itself.

It is not a machine that produces anything.

It is a machine that produces memory.

Production lasts for as long as the world outside has not destroyed the stone underneath the room. The water is never still. The engine never stops. The fauna never sleep all at once.

Their cycles are overlapping:

The clack-singers wake, descend, drink the water through the silver of their underbellies, taste the resin nodules for the next day’s prayer of memory, walk the walls of the engine in their patterns, and then return into the rust beams to sleep in the warm dark with the faint humming of the engine vibrating through the metal into their bodies.

The garpers’ climb and fall of the water creates the pulse of the room. The water moves from the floor to the ceiling through unseen channels, not because of the simple pull of gravity, but because the engine and the planet and the water and the stone together constitute a system of pressure that must be matched.

They do not resist gravity. They arrange it. The water climbs to the ceiling because, if it did not, the pressure from the deeper world would not be able to express itself in this room.

The mineral frosts on the walls grow from the bolts to the corners, not like the snow of your world, but like the thinking of the world itself. When they reach the ceiling—they do not stop. Their new patterns are drawn into the resin’s sources.

If you watch long enough, you will notice that there are two new frost patterns above the PRIMARY PUMP A sign, where yesterday there were none.

The engine is not an engine now of industrial pumping.

It is an engine of observation.

Its observation does not need to be reported to anyone else. Its observation is itself. The engine room is not a ruin of neglect. It is a body of consciousness that has been granted an interval to be aware.

About the invisible.

There are forces in the room that are not visible to the eye: the charge of the water paths as they climb the walls, the low frequencies of sound that move through the metal from the deeper world, the lines of attraction that pull the clack-singers around the nodules of resin.

Sometimes, when the water drops from the ceiling into the basin, and the mineral frosts flash with a cold light and the network under the sign flares with circuitry and the resin nodules at the engine’s heart twist faintly in their glass matrix, you feel the invisible presence of the engine itself.

The engine is not the iron machine in the middle of the room.

The engine is the room.

The ceiling is the engine’s skull. The rust continents are the engine’s palate. The water that is never still but never confused is the engine’s blood. The mineral frosts are the engine’s thoughts. The resin nodules are the engine’s dreams. The clack-singers and the garpers and the network of the wall under the PRIMARY PUMP A sign are the engine’s senses.

The engine weeps because the planet is weeping.

The planet weeps because the universe outside its surface is still remembering the endless traumas of birth and explosions and stars and collisions of continents that have not yet happened from the perspective of this room’s history.

The engine, in its small space of old, useless industrial infrastructure, has been given the task of remembering the planet in the small time that it is given.

You do not know where your own body fits in this.

You are not the creature of the engine. The clack-singers have not been assigned to you. The garpers have no need to report your image to the engine, because your image is not a pattern of the room’s natural history.

The engine does not know why you are here. But it does not need to know. It only needs to remember.

When you exhale next, the clack-singers’ combined faint rustling changes in the pattern and the intensity of the light inside their shells. The pressure of that breath is written into the water of the room. The faint charge of your body’s electricity is laid into the film on the floor.

Twenty minutes later, one of the garpers that will be born in the deeper chambers under the floor will carry this new pattern of your breath into the engine’s shafts.

The next resin drop that will fall from the ceiling and will touch the engine’s heart nodule will have inside it, somewhere, a new microthread that contains a pattern of your standing breath.

The engine will not know you as you know yourself.

But it will remember you.

The Weeping Engine does not need your name, or your purpose, or your forgiveness. It only needs your time.

The room is not waiting for anything.

It is already complete.

And you are standing in the only moving heart that does not yet know its place in the slow, wet engine of the world.
