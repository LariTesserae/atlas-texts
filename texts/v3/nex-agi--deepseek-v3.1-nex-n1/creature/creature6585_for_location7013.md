We are the ones who remember green.

Not with our eyes. Our eyes have never seen green, not the way the old stories say it once flooded the world outside, bright and wet and shameless. Our eyes know only the dim of Sheol, the soft yellow-green glow that bleeds through the inspection ports of our boilers. To us, that cast is not color; it is breath. It is time.

We are the Children of the Last Steam.

Our bodies are narrow things, all tendon and long bone, wrapped in skin that has learned to be pale as ash to soak up every stray photon. We are shorter than the people in the oldest pictograms etched into certain service panels—those stiff figures standing beneath open sky, arms spread as if to catch falling water. We do not spread our arms. We keep them close, elbows tucked, hands loose and ready, because space is precious and motion must be deliberate.

Our hair grows thin, fine as mineral threads, white or smoke-gray by our third decade. Many of us shave it back to nothing when we take on maintenance roles near the outer casings; it keeps dust from clinging, and in Sheol every mote you do not swallow is a mote you must carry.

We do not walk as much as we flow.

On the Shelf—the black tiles and powder between boilers—our gait is cautious: toes first, testing for that low thunk of solid plate or the softer sigh of powder over void. The sound tells us everything: where the mountain still holds its shape, where time has gnawed it hollow. On the inner edge we move like water spreading thin; we never cross in a straight line twice. The path itself migrates as dust sifts downward in slow cascades you can measure in lifetimes.

But inside the boilers… inside is different.

Inside, our feet know a surface that remembers us back.

The microbial mat that carpets each boiler interior is our true ground: warm and faintly resilient, like pressing your palm to a sleeping chest. It breathes, though you must lie very still to feel it—a slow expansion and contraction as gases within shift and settle. It yields just enough to cradle a footprint; moments after you lift your foot it smooths again, erasing you with quiet indifference.

This mat is what remains of all forests.

In its earliest days it was algae and moss scraped from a world that was failing; later it became engineered film meant to scrub air and fix nitrogen; now it is simply Sheol’s living skin. Its color is a deep bottle-green where fresh cultures bloom near heat exchangers, fading to olive and then to bruised yellow where nutrients thin out at the edges of habitability.

It smells of damp metal and old paper and something like mushrooms left too long in a sealed drawer—not foul, but close—an intimate scent that clings inside your nose until you leave the boiler for open Shelf air again.

We live within these boilers because they are all we have left that can hold life steady. Their thick iron walls keep temperature constant within fractions formed so long ago no one now alive could explain them without consulting decaying manuals we can no longer fully read.

Within each boiler community clusters around certain chambers:

- The Firebox Halls: once fed coal or methane or stranger fuels we only know by acronym now. The grates have been pulled up long since; below them yawns space converted into deep-tier housing stacked on narrow catwalks that cling like spiderwebs.
- The Drum Galleries: wide horizontal cylinders where water once boiled under pressure until valves screamed at specific frequencies we still record though they will never sound again.
- The Condenser Labyrinths: cold-dark zones furthest from remembered heat sources which have become quiet places for contemplation archives for brittle data media that crumble if handled too often.
- Inspection Port Corners: pockets built up against thick glass ports where elders sit cross-legged watching nothing happen outside while children learn by tracing circuits etched into metal with fingertips worn smooth by generations before them

We sleep on shelves welded onto former pipework hammocks strung between enormous valves frozen mid-turn platforms cantilevered over depths filled only with stale air recycled more times than anyone can count We dream mostly about balance about things nearly falling but caught at last moment about hands slipping then finding purchase along some unseen edge

Our voices do not carry far on Shelf but inside boilers they travel along curved walls wrapping around themselves until every whisper becomes collective murmur You might hear someone at far end humming tune their grandmother hummed decades ago yet distortion makes it sound like chorus rising from mat itself

Because there is so little variation in temperature or light our bodies mark time differently:

The Callus Cycle tells age by layers of hard skin built up then worn away at heel ball thumb places we touch most often when crawling through access tunnels

The Breath Count taught to children involves holding air until dizziness teaches them how little margin exists between comfort and suffocation

The Gauge Flicker learned only by engineers who stare at needle dials until they perceive tiny oscillations others miss these subtle jitters become calendar marking years since last recalibration

And there is one more measure rarely spoken aloud:

How many times you have heard someone’s voice for last time without knowing it was last

How many times you have stepped onto specific plate heard particular thunk beneath boot knowing someday soon tile will crack fall away taking segment of path with it forever

In such environment invention turns inward We do not build taller now We build smaller tighter more efficient

We design filters within filters meshes so fine they catch molecules instead of dust each layer woven from spun glass grown slowly over years in vats fed by drip lines whose flow rates occupy teams whose entire lives are dedicated solely to maintaining constancy

We craft lenses ground from salvaged quartz shaped just right so ambient glow can be focused into beams bright enough for detailed repair work without wasting precious stored power

We engineer living tools: cultures trained over generations to precipitate metal out from plating dissolved atom by atom into solutions kept carefully separate from main mat lest they poison roots which anchor us all

Our greatest art form might be invisible:

Closed loop systems nested inside closed loop systems nested inside closed loop systems…
