The People are called the Driftline.  

They are slow.  

Not in the way a tired animal is slow, but in the way a stone deep underwater is slow: change is measured in lifetimes, not breaths. Bodies are narrow, streamlined for the dense cold, waterlogged as the wood they work. Their limbs are long and thin, fingers and toes powered by the dense muscle of constant climbing, pulling, drifting.

---

## Bodyshape and Motion

They are not built like swimmers of open seas. Wide strokes are wasted energy here; corridors are too narrow for that kind of movement.

Instead, they move like those who are always half-climbing, half-floating:  

- **Chests** narrow, ribs angled deep so the water can slide around them without turbulence.  
- **Shoulders** set forward, rounded by the constant pressure of reaching, hand-over-hand along walls and ropes.  
- **Arms** sinewy and elongated, fingers slim but strong, wrapped in faint patterns of old scar where stone and rope have taught them lessons.  
- **Legs** longer and lighter in muscle, but not weak—they are used as rudders, as stabilizers, as extra limbs when planting, wedging, or lining against a wall in sleep.

Most of the time they don’t “swim” so much as **climb the water**.  

They drift through a corridor, fingertips trailing along stone, moss, and woven root. The faintest pulse of fin-like feet or the hook of a toe beneath a line is enough to redirect the body. The water itself does the rest of the lifting; what they must provide is only direction and a small nudge.

---

## Skin, Eyes, and Other Senses

### Skin

Their skin is pale, almost translucent in some places, like old porcelain held underwater. It has taken on the color of the deep: cold, muted, faintly lensed with the sheen of ever-present moisture.  

Veins show clearly beneath—translucent lines of green or blue, depending on the layers of the world above them. They seem like the veins of the city itself, traced in the body.

With enough time in the cold, their skin grows a thin, downy layer of **microalgae and moss-film** in patches—not enough to see clearly, but enough to sense when touched. It helps them blend with the walls they move along, making them faintly luminous when they brush past glowing vegetation.

They are not cold like the dead. Overlaid, in the middle of chest and abdomen and along the spine, there is a map of **thermal nodes**—areas where the body concentrates its heat. These patches are where they wrap themselves when they need to slow everything down, conserving warmth like banked embers.

### Eyes

Theirs are not eyes of bright daylight predators. They are wide and dark, adapted to the weightless dark of the world-city.

Pupils almost never fully narrow; there is nothing bright enough to require it. The irises are indeterminate shades of green or gray, like the faint glow of vegetation far up a corridor.

The cornea is thick and smooth, hardened by the constant pressure and the faint salt of the water. Their eyes barely bulge from the skull; they are set deeply, protected by the bones of the face. When they look at you, you do not feel the hard clarity of open air. Instead, you feel **observed**, like a stone that has been noticed by a slow current.

They don’t use eyes much for navigation. Navigation is done by body, by fingertips, by the subtle pull of current and warmth. Eyes are for *reading*—not the walls, but one other.

## Lungs, Breath, and the Water-Air

They are adapted to both water and air, but this air is not the sky-memory of air. It comes in pockets trapped in architecture and plant.

### **The lungs**  

Smaller, denser, their lungs are not made for a fast sprint of air. They are made for long **slow** use.

- They can inhale water and hold it in a chamber of the lung where a thin membrane of biological tissue and **symbiotic vegetation** forms a **filtering lung**.  
- Tiny oxygen-exchanging tissues of this plant-lung filter the breath of the water, extracting what oxygen there is.  
- They can also hold air pockets: swallowed air is stored in a secondary cavity near the chest and throat, where it is slowly metered out to keep the upper body slightly buoyant and to provide a small reserve of real air.

When they “breathe” water, it is not a violent action. A slow inhale and a long exhale. The water is not rushed to the lungs in a gasp; it is drawn into the mouth and filtered, then expelled in a steady, cold current that contributes to their forward drift.

### **Talk**  

When they speak in air, it is with a low, resonant tone that burns out quickly if they try to rush. The water doesn’t like noise; it eats it, so they talk only in short phrases and long pauses.

- Their words are shaped to be **felt** rather than heard at length.  
- Much of their communication is **touch**: the pressure of a hand on the shoulder, the tracing of patterns on the skin of the forearm, the slow, steady pushes of the body along the wall of a room to indicate direction.

## Hair and Rope

They do not rely heavily on hair to protect their head and neck—the water already cushions everything. Instead, hair is **ritual and record**.

- **Hair** grows slowly and is never cut fully.  
- It is woven with **plant fibers and thin root-strands** into braids and ropes that fall behind them like the tails of deep-sea creatures.  
- Some strands of these head-ropes are tied with small carved stones, knots, or carefully preserved plant fragments. Each tied item represents an event: a migration, a death, a shift in room.

As they age, these hair-ropes become heavy, trailing behind them in the water like a second mind. The younger ones sometimes assist their elders by carrying their ropes when they move through narrow corridors.

---

## Food and the Eating City

They are fundamentally **plant-eaters**, but that is not a passive role.

There are no animals to catch. The city itself provides:

- **Soft fronds** that curl into their fingers when they harvest.  
- **Broad-leaf species** with thick, succulent bases that store faint, cold energy in horizontal growths along the stone.  
- **Root-strands** that can be pulled carefully, leaving the main body intact.

They eat slowly—not out of scarcity but out of respect. They know which parts of the plant wall can be cropped without causing collapse. They know which **glow-lines** are the veins of the plant-room and should not be severed.

They **garden** the city.  

They move along the walls, fingers trimming here, reinforcing there. They plant cuttings of new species in old gaps where stone has fallen away, slowly weaving the city into a stronger form. They do not see the city as separate from themselves; they are its tending organs.

---

## Tools and Crafting

The Driftline do not build with new materials. They **reconstruct with the old**.

- **Knives** are made of sharpened stone, lashed to a wooden handle with woven plant-rope.  
- **Wedges** are broken fragments of older tools, reused to hold stones in place where mortar has long dissolved.  
- **Ropes** are grown from **plant-hair**—the stiff fibers inside certain species’ stems—braided with their own hair and the fibers of older ropes.

They rarely forge anything new. They **repair**:

- They wedge blocks back into old walls, filling the gaps with packed plant matter and mud that hardens over time into something like soft stone.  
- They re-tie the city’s hanging ropes, replacing the weakest strands with newer ones, leaving the old ones to dissolve into the water.  
- They carve, when they must carve, not with metal but with **water-hardened wood** that has been soaked, compressed, and allowed to fossilize into the nearest thing they have to metal.

When they speak of tools, they use **slow** words. “This wedge has held three hands,” they might say, meaning three generations before you.

---

## Clothing and Ornaments

They do not distinguish between clothing and armor or decoration and map.

Their **garments** are often:

- **Woven plant mats** draped around the hips and lower torso, held in place by thin ropes of braided root and hair.  
- **Shoulder-panels** made from thicker leaf-layers, tied in front to protect neck and collarbone from constant rubbing against stone.

Nothing is worn purely for warmth. The water is always cold. Warmth comes from movement and the **inner heat** that they conserve carefully.

Ornaments mostly **serve**—the same ropes that hold their garments are decorated with knots and beads that tell:

- How long they have lived in this region of the city.  
- Which rooms they have repaired.  
- Which deaths they have witnessed.

They wear no metal except what is found: occasionally, a **nail** taken from an old beam, worn smooth and polished into a pendant or a tool-end. Metal is so rare that when they find it, it becomes a name-event—“Nail-Shoulder,” “Locket-Chest.”

---

## Sleep and Suspension

They sleep in the water.

They **hang**:

- In a narrow corridor, they might tie a line around their body and let the water hold them upright, suspended between the wall and the rope.  
- In a larger room, they may drift slowly in circles, anchored to a central pillar by a long rope, so they never drift far.

Their sleep is slow and deep. They do not dream often in vivid scenes. Instead, their sleep is **felt**: they feel the slow pressure of the city around them, the faint shifts of current, the subtle changes of temperature in pockets of water that they have learned to read like maps. When they wake, they wake with a sense of **where** the water has been over the hours of stillness.

Sometimes, two will sleep together, **interlocked** gently: limbs wrapped around shared ropes and one another, forming a small knot of bodies in an otherwise indifferent cold. In this, they preserve warmth the way they preserve everything: slow, careful, and incremental.

---

## Society and Silence

There are not **many** of them, but they are not **few**.

They live in loosely connected regions that overlap more than meet:

- A **line**—not a blood family but a **shared rope**—travels together, maintaining a particular region of rooms and corridors.  
- When they meet other lines, they **trade stories**: not of weather, but of structural changes in the water city—new collapses, new warm pockets, new plant-growths that have sealed an old path.

There are no **leaders** in the old sense. Leadership is expressed by who **knows the most of the city in this layer**.

- **Olders** are those who have traveled the deepest corridors of the mind and body; they have felt more walls, more ropes, more temperatures.  
- **Youngers** are those who still have the energy to move about, but not yet the knowledge of where the walls are weak.

When they decide to move a room, block by block, over the course of seasons, they **decide together**. No vote is taken quickly; no decision is exclaimed. The choice is made slowly, over days of murmured exchange and long pauses. When the silence is complete enough, they say that the water has decided.

## Birth and Death

**Birth** is slow and quiet, like everything else.

- It happens hanging, suspended in the water of a safe and known room.  
- The child is born with small, closed eyes that barely open at first, as though sensing already that the world is not to be rushed.  
- They are given into the water almost immediately, wrapped in a thin cloth of plant fiber and the parent’s warmth.

New children are not **named** at birth. They are given **names** when they have done enough with their hands to have a story.  

Maybe they will be called “Block-Shifter.”  
Or “Door-Finder.”  
Or “Root-Binder.”

**Death** is similarly integrated.

- When they feel the body slowing, unable to maintain what little heat it once kept, they choose **a cold room**.  
- They lie or hang there in the coldest patch of water, where they will slowly fall asleep.  
- The body does not decay quickly in the cold water; it serves as a scaffold for new plant growths, anchoring the city once more into the stone.

When they speak of the dead, they say, **“They became part of the wall.”** It is not poetry; it is literal.

## Language and Thought

The Driftline do not think in fast images.

Their thought is **tactical and slow**:

- **Place**: Where is the next handhold?  
- **Temperature**: Which direction has the colder water?  
- **Structure**: Which wall here feels weak under the fingertips?

When they think of the **sun**, they think of it as a distant **warm pocket** somewhere else—not mythical, but simply inaccessible. They do not long to reach it; they accept it as another room in another floor of the world, too far away.

Time for them is measured in:

- **Flows**: How many times has water changed direction in this corridor?  
- **Movings**: How many times have they relocated a room?  
- **Deaths**: How many bodies have become walls?

They ask:

- “Which way?”  
- “How cold?”  
- “How long?”

They rarely ask:

- “Why?”  
- “What if?”  
- “When will it end?”

The **world** is not going to end; it has already ended, in the old sense of the word, and has become something else. They are not living before the end; they are living in the long **after**.

---

## You and Them

When you meet them, you notice:

- They do not rush to touch you; instead, they circle slowly, feeling your temperature, your shape, your **presence** in the water.  
- They touch with **open palms** first, tracing your outline like you are a new wall in their city.  
- If they accept you, they might teach you their knots—the slow, precise patterns of rope that hold the rooms together.

When you speak your own language, they **listen** to the shape of your words, not the meaning at first. They feel the way your sound pushes into the water around them like a pressure on their skin.

Adapting you into the Driftline is a slow process:

- They show you how to hang.  
- They show you how to sleep without drifting too far.  
- They show you which plants to eat and which to help grow.

You become another **hand** in the city.

---

## The Driftline Mind

In the end, they are not explorers of large horizons. They are **readers of small distances**.

Their horizon is always close:

- **Fingertips to stone.**  
- **Forearm to rope.**  
- **Body to cold.**

They are the **last** people of the city only if you imagine the city as having ever had many; perhaps they are simply the ones who have always been here, adapting into this slower and slower way of being.

They do not mourn the sky. They remember it faintly, perhaps in old stories, but they do not **yearn**. The sky is another room above, where the water ends and air begins—too distant to be of any use.

They are content with the near horizon of the wall they can touch, the rope they can hold, the room they can move slowly over time. They are **weaving** the city into its next form: not rebuilding it as it was, but letting it be slowly remade by their hands and the plant’s fibers and the cold water’s long embrace.

In the darkness, there is only the water,  
and the stone,  
and the slow hands of the Driftline,  
writing the city into its next long breath.
