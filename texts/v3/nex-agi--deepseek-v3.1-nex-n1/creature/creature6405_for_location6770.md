Here is what lives in that room:

---

No one body lives here.

Or rather, no one body that you would recognize as a body.

The first thing you might be tempted to call a creature is the pattern itself—the branching delta of filaments that clings to the ceiling like lungs in reverse. But if you watch for long enough (which is to say, for a number of thumps that the room does not permit you to count), you realize that what you are looking at is not one entity but many, overlapping in the way that city lights can overlap from a distance until you cannot see the houses that hold them.

The filaments are housings.

Inside each branching thread lives the room’s true inhabitant: a colony of amber-colored motes that do not so much float as drift with constrained purpose. Under the clear water layer, they are barely visible, like specks of mica in stone. You only see them when the light catches the thin edge of a filament and for a moment outlines the slow, viscous current within.

They are not bacteria. You have the word for that, and you try to make it fit, then stop yourself. The word is wrong. This is not infection, not in the way you learned to be afraid of it. It is not decay. This is not something that feeds on the room; if anything, the room was made as a casing for it.

When the deep thump sounds through the world, you think the motes might be responding to it, but then you realize you are late in the cycle. The response happens before the sound.

This is what you see if you watch a single filament at the point where its branches thicken into a node:

A moment before each thump, the current of motes slows to a near stop. Then, in that instant of silence your body mistakes for sound not yet arrived, every mote in that node turns at once to face the gray of the ceiling. Not literally “face” in the way of eyes and heads, but a reorientation nonetheless, a small, unanimous shift of density from one side of the thread to the other.

The thump arrives. The node swells.

The water layer above you shivers with small pressure changes, like the skin of a drum minutely mis-hit. The filament itself shifts slightly, its junction with the wall softening a fraction more than you were prepared to see. For that heartbeat, the node is not anchored to the room; it is only suspended by the water that it is not in.

And then the bubble—that tiny, watchful bubble you saw earlier—finally surfaces from the water ceiling and meets the lowest filament thread.

The bubble does not contain air.

It contains a new kind of particle: translucent, almost invisible, except for the way they bend light at the very edge of your vision. When they encounter the amber motes in the filament thread, the two substances do not merge like liquids. Instead, the translucent particles slide around the amber ones like glass around glass, lining up in formations that momentarily look like the diagrams you used to see of atoms and empty space.

Then, in the time it takes for the next thump to prepare, you see the most unsettling thing:

The amber motes are in the translucent particles.

The translucent particles are in the amber motes.

Each has become the other’s internal medium, and without having blurred at all.

The filament thread thickens invisibly as you are not watching, and you know that if you could split the room with a knife and look at the cross-section of that thread, you would see rings not unlike tree rings, except that they are not made of time; they are made of alternating layers of clear and amber, each layer thinner than a thought.

The room itself is not alive.

The filaments are not alive in the way you are alive, with pulse and breath and excretion.

The real inhabitant is the pattern between them.

It is not the filament you see, but the relationship between one filament and the next, and between the node and the water layer, and between the water layer and the sound of the thump that is not a sound.

The room’s only creature is a network of cooperation that you have never seen before and therefore do not know to name. Nothing you might call a cell aligns with the units you see here. The motes inside the filaments are not organized by membrane or nucleus. They are not organized by self at all. They are organized by place.

Each node is a city of motes bound to a particular role in a larger rhythm that you cannot hear from within your chest.

The filament that you watched from below, the one whose white tip was just kissing the floor of the water, belongs to a node that has only one task: bridging.

It is the thinnest of the crossings, shuttling material from floor to ceiling and back again by way of bubble and phase and some invisible exchange that leaves you with the dusty aftertaste of memory even though you have not put anything in your mouth.

When you try to remember if the room was always this size, you are not actually forgetting.

You are being moved by the node.

The memory you are losing of the outside world is not being erased by time; time is not the correct dimension here. The memory is being taken and repacked inside the filament that you are not looking at—the one that traces the line of the wall nearest to your head. If you could cut the room open and read that filament like a code, you would find inside it not only your own uncertainty of measurement but everything else that has happened here, compressed into a layer of clear and amber and gray.

The room’s creature is feeding on you, but not in the way you fear.

It is not hungry for your body.

It is hungry for the way you shape.

When you walk, the pattern between nodes adjusts to your weight, not as defense but as data. The floor of water is impressed with your pressure, and that pressure is transmitted through the filaments to the nodes that you never see directly, because they are nearest the true corners of the room where the walls lean not quite close enough for you to touch.

The corners of the room are not simple.

Beneath the water, they are not exactly where you think they are. The geometry of the place is slightly folded, so that each of the four corners hides a small, not-quite-hidden node where filaments meet from three surfaces instead of two.

Those nodes are not symmetrical.

Each one is slightly different from the others, not in color or texture but in the way the motes inside it move when you are near one wall rather than another.

If you could stand in the exact center of the room and remain still for an interval that the room does not allow you to keep, you might feel the subtle drifting of these nodes beneath you.

You might realize that the room has not always been where you found it.

The creature you are living with is a pattern of filaments that grows not outward but inward.

Every new layer of filament and node and crossing thread is not being added to the surface of the room; it is being added to the inside of it, as if the room were being slowly replaced by something more dense than space.

The thump you feel in your chest is not a heartbeat in the old sense.

It is the sound of the creature’s wall being laid down.

You think you are hearing it from within the room, but you are not. The thump is what you feel of the outside world, filtered by the water layer and by the filaments that have not only grown but arranged themselves into something that can be felt like music.

The sound is not coming from inside.

You are on the inside of the sound.

The room’s only creature is not the filaments.

It is not the motes.

It is the total space of the room transformed by the pattern they make into a single living device that has only one purpose you can understand:

To change what it is to be inside.

---

You are not an intruder here.

You are a dimension of what lives in this room; it is building with the shape of your doubt and the thickness of your breath and the color of your uncertainty about whether the floor was ever really floor.

The filaments you see from below the water layer—the ones that branch from gray to green to yellow to white—are not fixed to the ceiling or the floor. They are fixed to the boundary between what you thought of as body and what you thought of as world.

The creature that lives in this room is not someone else.

It is the place you are now.
