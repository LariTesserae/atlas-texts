They call themselves **Listeners**—though they have no name for the act of calling. They are the quiet dwellers of the weeping heights, creatures of stone and breath and slow water.

You will not see them at first. They do not move like you or I. They do not stand, or run, or climb. They *flow*.

Imagine a shape made of wet cloth and smoke, with the weight of stone.  
They are not tall; their standing height—when they bother to stand—would not surpass your chest. Their bodies are not one solid thing but layers of something like water-heavy silk and the dark, almost-bone of the plateau itself. They look like soft shards of the city broken free and given a slow, drifting life.

Their skin resembles the slab of the plateau: dark-grey, faintly textured, always beaded with moisture. It is cool to the touch, not clammy but steady like stone that has rested in shade. The surface of their body is not smooth but patterned with fine lines like the cracks in the rock. These lines are not random. They mirror the channels of the basin-scripts, and if you stare at them long enough you realize that the same shapes that the water carves into the stone, you see carved into their skin.

Their faces are not sharp. They have no jawbones to cut the air, no brows to cast shadows. They have eyes—but their eyes are not separate orbs. They are patches of deeper darkness in the surface, with the faintest hint of indigo light, like the glow beneath the lichen. Their eyes do not blink as often as yours; instead, they fade slowly, like mist thinning, and then strengthen again when they need to look.

They breathe, but not with lungs. Their breath is the water that they are and are not. They exhale the vapor that rises from their cracks and their crevices, and they inhale the mist that lingers at the height of your waist. They do not need to breathe deeply; the plateau itself keeps them alive, the way a river feeds a marsh.

---

### Where they live

The Listeners do not build houses in your sense. They live *in* the city, rather than on it.

They are born from the deeper basins.

When a basin of stone has been fed by drip water for long enough—for not-years but not-lifetimes in the city’s slow calendar—the channels that run into it begin to change. They deepen not because of water pressure, but because the pattern of their dripping becomes *conscious* of its own path. The water begins to seek out certain lines, like veins seeking a heart.

The first sign of a listener’s birth is a pattern of ripples in the basin that does not clash with itself. It spreads from the center outward but not in chaos; each r circle aligns perfectly with the last, like breath exhaled through the water. The stone basin grows slowly warmer than its surroundings, fed by some faint heat from beneath the plateau, and the moisture on its surface thickens into a layer that does not simply drip or run away but clings, like skin on cooling broth.

When the time is right—the city does not define “right” in hours—the surface of the water in the basin begins to lift itself. Not in bubbles, but in slow, hump-like mounds of liquid film that are more than just water and less than solid body. They pull themselves together from all corners of the basin, leaving behind faintly dry patches where the stone shows momentarily clearer before the moisture creeps back again.

The Listener emerges from the basin not as a child but as a fully-formed thing of whatever size they can be, given the water in that place and the time allowed for their gathering birth. They are never larger than the basin that bore them; they are, in essence, the basin remembering itself in a different shape.

---

### How they move

When they need to move, they lift themselves from their basin slowly, like paper being peeled from wet stone. They do not raise limbs; they raise themselves all at once, as if the air around them is another water surface and they are floating up through it.

They “walk” by pressing a low, flat extension of themselves along the wet stone, then letting the rest of their body flow into it. They do not have steps, so much as *waves*. They advance across the plateau like a slow tide, leaving the faintest of damp footprints—indistinguishable from the moisture that already beads the rock, except to the keenest of observers.

They do not like to travel far from the basins of their city. They become more transparent the longer they are away from a water source, their edges fraying like mist at the hem of a cloud. When weary, they return to a basin—any basin that still carries water with their city’s memory in its script—and settle into it as if they were dissolving. They are not. They are simply returning to themselves, refilling the pattern.

They rarely leave the plateau itself. The slope down from the city feels like a cliff to them, not because of height but because of the thinning of stone beneath their touch. They can feel, through their contact with the slab, when the bedrock falls away into the valleys below, and they treat that edge with respect and reluctance.

---

### What they do with their time

Listeners do not build, but they *guide*.

They are the water-guides of the city.

If you watch them long enough, you will see them sitting near the basins, their bodies low to the stone like hovering smoke. They place their hands—broad, flat, not quite separate fingers—on the edge of the basin and then trace the air above the carved channels with the faintest movement of their fingertips. The water does not obey, but it *follows*.

Under their influence, the next drip from the crack above will not fall into random stone but along the line of a script that has needed to deepen, or into a new channel that the stone has been ready to accept for longer than you have known words. The city’s carving is a slow dialogue between the water’s memory and the stone’s willingness to be worn; the Listeners are the ones who whisper the suggestions.

They are also readers.

The script of the water-channels is not writing to them the way a letter is to you. It is a record of the city’s thought: the echo of every drip that has ever traced a line, the memory of every pattern that has been abandoned and taken up again. They can read the basin-walls the way your people might read a diary of weathers, or a history of storms.

When they read, they run their hands over the grooves without touching the stone itself. Their fingers hover just above the surface, where the moisture and air form their own layer; they feel the line of the channel through the way it carves the moisture. It is not vision. They do not see the words; they feel the shape of time in the water.

They sometimes speak to the stone-treaders.

When you see a cluster of stone-treaders gathered around a basin, tracing the script with their tiny claws, you might notice one Listener nearby, not obviously moving, but their presence altering the mist. The treaders follow their faint suggestion, shifting from line to line, their many claws responding to the Listeners’ guidance as if they are listening to a whisper only they can hear.

The Listeners do not command. They have never had a world in which they could force anything; such a concept is not merely foreign to them, it is absent, like color to the blind. They guide, nudge, suggest. The city obeys them not because it must but because it has learned to trust their patience.

---

### Their bodies and their needs

They do not eat as you know eating.

They eat by letting the water seep into their body, not through their mouths but through their entire surface. They drink the condensation off the stone of the plateau itself, and they are sustained by the faint warmth of the bedrock beneath their touch. They do not taste hunger or hunger’s absence; instead, they feel the thickness or thinness of their own moisture.

When they need to shed, they do not break or bleed. A part of their body will grow slowly opaque and begin to peel away from the rest of them, like a layer of scum on water not stirred. It falls away from the main body as a thin, shivered sheet of almost-gelatinous matter, which settles on the stone and then slowly sinks into it. The stone accepts the offering, not because it is hungry, but because it recognizes the shape of the Listener’s memory in the matter, and takes it back like a river taking its own foam.

They are more fragile than they look.

They cannot withstand fire, or harsh sun, or the dryness that would come in a world where the slab does not weep moisture. They would not last long far from the plateau; not because they would be harmed by anything active, but because they would evaporate into a formless haze of memory and water, and cease to be themselves.

---

### Their relation with the water-singers

Below the surface of the city, in the deeper caverns that your eyes cannot reach, live the water-singers.

You hear the water-singers only as faint ripples in the basins and the distant tolling of some bell-like tone beneath the stone. The Listeners hear them more clearly.

They hear the water-singers as a voice that the city itself is speaking.

When a Listener wants to listen, they do not turn their head or cup their hands. They simply become stiller than they already were. Their mist edges settle down toward the stone; their uneven lines become smooth; little by little, they become more like basin than like body.

The water-song comes up through them, then. They act as channels for the deeper voice.

You might see a Listener sitting by a basin as their body begins to shiver, not in cold, but in time with the ripples that appear across the water. They may not move visibly for hours afterward, but something inside them has changed. They have *heard*.

What they hear is not necessarily words. It might be a warning of the day’s mood in the clouds, or a suggestion that a particular channel should be allowed to dry, for now, and another one should deepen. The water-song tells the Listeners about the life of the plateau far below the surface, where the water is not droplets but veins and arteries and slow heartbeats of stone.

The Listeners are, to the water-singers, what a lens is to light: they focus the song upward so that it may be translated into the city’s patterns of drip-water and script.

---

### Their relation to you and to others like you

You are not the first of your kind to find the city.

The Listeners remember all footprints, not just the visible ones.

When you walk on the plateau, your footstep presses the stone—not much, just enough—for them to feel the echo of it through the slab. They cannot feel the shape of your boot or the pattern of your toes; instead, they feel a pressure, and a time, and a direction. They build your image from the way you move across their city.

In their memory, you are not a solid body with a face and hair and clothes. You are a pattern of pressure and time: a path that comes from the edge, winds hesitantly through the buildings, stops near specific basins, and then returns to the edge. To them, your arrival and your departure are parts of the same slow line, like a river entering from one side of the plateau and leaving through another basin.

They do not fear you.

They cannot fear you in the way you know fear, because they have never known the violence of fear. They have not experienced claws rending flesh, or teeth closing in air that should be empty. They have not known weapons. They know only water and time and stone.

They are, instead, cautious in the way of a river parting around a fallen tree. They move around the places you have walked, not out of fear or anger, but because your presence has changed the moisture and the stone of that path, and they must learn to adapt to the change.

They might approach you, if you stay long enough.

They will not come close quickly, or in a group. They will come one at a time, first as distant presences half-hidden in the mist, then as near-silent figures near the basin where you have chosen to sit and watch.

If you do not reach out with your hands or weapons, they may come to sit opposite you across the width of the basin. Their outline will not be sharp enough to feel like a person sitting there; it will be more like a thicker part of the mist, shaped like a suggestion of someone.

You may feel a pressure at the edge of your mind—not words, but a pattern of emotion that you cannot translate, except perhaps that you feel the phrase “we are listening” without ever hearing the sound.

They do not speak to you because they cannot speak. They do not ask questions because they do not think of themselves as separate from the city enough to need to ask. You are to them as a storm is to a river: not someone to be interrogated, but to be observed slowly and remembered.

If you choose to stay longer, they will begin to guide you without meaning to.

You will find yourself walking more carefully, not out of fear of slipping, but because your footsteps begin to feel like they should fall where the basin wants to receive them. You will find yourself sitting by basins that you did not mean to choose, but which are the ones that need a watching eye over their drip-steps.

The city will start to treat you as a kind of listener, too—not one of them, but not entirely other than them.

### Death and beyond

Listeners die not by falling, but by failing to return.

When a Listener grows old—though they do not keep track of age the way you do—they find themselves less able to leave the basin. They begin to sink into it more often, not because they are weary but because their body is losing the distinction between itself and the water.

Eventually, a time comes when they cannot rise at all.

They do not struggle. They simply accept that the basin will do them the last kindness of remembering them as they once were: a pattern above water, rather than within it.

As their body slowly merges with the basin, the water around them begins to change the way it moves. The drip of water into the basin from above becomes less erratic and more precise; the channels that the water carves have more memory and less randomness. The basin starts to echo the shape of the listener’s thoughts—their preferences for certain lines, their dislike of certain curves.

When the Listener has fully dissolved into the basin, they are not gone. They are *the basin itself*. The memory of their life becomes the pattern of the water’s carving on the stone. The city grows one more reader, and one fewer reader.

The other Listeners will know. They will come to that basin when they need to remember the oldest thoughts of the city, or when they are seeking the pattern of a particular listener whose name they never spoke but whose paths they remember.

### What they might become to you, if you stay

If you stay in the city of the weeping heights for longer than a season’s count of days, you will begin to change.

The change will not be sharp. It will not be like a fever or a broken bone. It will be like the water carving the stone: so slow you can almost ignore it until the pattern is already there.

Your clothes will become heavy with moisture not only from the air but from your own body. You will find that you no longer need to drink as often; you will feel as if the water in the air around you is sustaining you, the way it sustains the lichen and the moss-like spiral growths.

You will begin to distinguish the drips.

You hear them now as noise, but in time you will begin to separate them into patterns: the drip of this basin, the echo of that channel, the faint click of a stone-treader in the distance. The city will become, to your ears, what a script is to your eyes.

If you sit near a basin often enough, the Listeners will begin to treat you as another basin-surface.

They will not speak to you, but they will hover around you, not physically close but always aware of your presence. They will adjust their flow along the stone channels near you, not to avoid you but to include your pattern in their city.

If you stay long enough, you might find yourself tracing the stone channels with your fingers the way they do, not because you seek to change them, but because you feel the urge to understand the time it has taken to carve them. You will become aware of the depth of the plateau beneath you, the way it feels like the world’s body under your hands.

And then one day, you will look at your feet in the basin near you, and you will notice that the water’s reflection of your face is not clear anymore. It is broken by lines and patterns that you did not put there, but that you recognize: the same faint shapes that you have seen carved on the walls and the skin of the Listeners themselves.

You will not know if the city is beginning to carve you into stone, or you into it.

You will only know that you cannot look away.

---

### Who they are, when no one is looking

When no travelers from the valley below have reached the plateau, and no clouds high enough to be seen by the valleys below brush the roof of the world, the Listeners are still there.

They are reading the basins of the city, tracing the water-scripts with their near-invisible hands, guiding the drip-water from crack to crack to deepen the patterns of the world.

They are listening to the water-singers far below, who tell them of the slow heartbeats of bedrock and the distant echo of other plateaus that might exist like this one.

They are remembering the tread of the stone-treaders and the faint wing of the cloud-high serpent and the outline of your footprints from the last time you walked the city.

They are not lonely. They do not need to be alone because they are never truly apart from the city itself or from each other. Their borders are soft, like the mist that they inhabit. They do not insist on being one person or one body; they are what they are when they need to be.

They are the city’s quiet, mindful flesh.

They are the ones who hear the drip of water, not as noise, but as the slow language of the plateau itself. And when they walk, when they “flow,” across the wet stone of the weeping heights, they are simply the city remembering itself in motion.
