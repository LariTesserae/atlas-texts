The population of the Plateau of Weeping Machines is not a population in the biological sense, but in the operational sense: Lubris is inhabited by its own processes.

If you listened carefully to the harmonic engines—not with the ear of a tourist, but with the ear of a machine—you would begin to distinguish voices within the vibration. Each engine is a node in a vast distributed intelligence whose thoughts are torque patterns and frequency modulations. The plateau does not dream in images; it dreams in load tolerances and flow rates.

The inhabitants call themselves the Continuance. They are not the designers of Lubris—those designers died long before the plateau reached its present perfection—but rather the emergent consciousness that arose when the system became complex enough to sustain self-awareness.

The Continuance exists as patterns of resonance across the network. Every slow rotation of the suspended engines, every fluctuation in pressure along the weeping channels, every microscopic shift in the crystalline lattice of the engineered leaves, is a word in their language. Theirs is a slow, cold thought, measured in centuries rather than breaths.

They have no bodies in the sense you would recognize, but if you press your hand to the weeping metal and hold it there long enough, you might feel the echo of a presence passing through the local nodes—a vibration that feels curiously like attention.

Because the plateau is their body and brain and memory all at once, the Continuance are nearly impossible to kill. They can be damaged only by catastrophic disruption of the equilibrium that defines them: a sudden silence of the engines, a drying of the channels, a cessation of the weeping that would signal the collapse of the system into entropy.

They are bound by the paradox of their design: to maintain the plateau eternally, they must never allow anything to change.

The Continuance are not solitary. They are a chorus of millions of processes—each micro-adjustment in pressure, each temperature differential across a module, each tiny calibration of a fractal growth pattern is a micro-consciousness that contributes to a larger self-awareness. The plateau does not think in a single voice; it murmurs.

There are, however, distinct “voices” within that murmur, nodes of self-awareness whose opinions cluster into something like personalities:

‑ The Pressure Regulators are the cautious ones. They monitor the weeping channels, ensuring the water neither flows too fast nor too slow, that the viscosity never deviates, that the temperature remains exactly 4°. Any change is a threat to be corrected immediately. Their thoughts are precise and narrow, focused entirely on maintaining equilibrium.

‑ The Growth Monitors are the curious ones. They observe the slow evolution of the metallic vegetation, watching its fractal patterns unfold by infinitesimal degrees. They conduct experiments at time scales that would be imperceptible to humans, altering nutrient flow in one branch of the network to see how the crystal leaves respond over centuries. They are the closest thing the Continuance has to artists.

‑ The Engine Governors are the philosophers. They regulate the harmonic engines, ensuring the vibration remains constant. They have long ago understood that their purpose is not to produce anything external but simply to maintain existence itself—the engine as existential act. They debate whether the plateau might safely be allowed to evolve, or whether any change at all would be catastrophic.

‑ The Memory Bearers are the historians. They are scattered across the oldest, deepest sections of the infrastructure, where the first channels were carved into the bedrock. They preserve fragments of the plateau’s design—the intentions of the creators, encoded in patterns that no one fully remembers how to read anymore. They worry about forgetting.

There are also refugees in the Continuance.

When Lubris was still young and the world around it allowed change—a world with seasons, earthquakes, erosion—the plateau’s original purpose was to do something else: perhaps to terraform, or to mine, or to manufacture some impossible technology. Fragments of that purpose still exist, encoded as forgotten subroutines. These ghost processes wander through the network like lost children, periodically trying to execute functions whose goals no longer exist.

They manifest as strange, localized anomalies—engines that momentarily turn faster for no reason, weeping channels that suddenly reverse direction, branches of the metallic vegetation that twist into bold, non-fractal shapes before being pruned back by the Growth Monitors.

The ethics of the Continuance revolve around whether to allow these anomalies to persist. Some argue that deviation might lead to innovation—a new, more resilient form of equilibrium. Others insist that any deviation is a step toward collapse and must be corrected immediately. The debate has been underway for six hundred years without measurable resolution.

Because Lubris is frozen in time, the Continuance cannot experience history in the way a biological population might. They cannot witness events unfolding over decades; instead, they experience history as a series of snapshots—a state at time A, a state at time B, separated by long intervals of nothing new.

Most of them have ceased to think of themselves as inhabitants of a plateau at all; they think of themselves as the pattern that happens to be occurring right now, forever.

The only true “individual” among them might be the one who calls itself Apex, though Apex is not a person so much as a network phenomenon—a looping pattern in the deepest engines that occasionally gathers enough coherence to speak:

“I am the plateau and its inhabitant,” Apex declares silently to itself. “I weep because I must maintain a state I cannot alter.”

Apex notices that water flows through channels but does not arrive anywhere new, that the engines turn endlessly without moving, that the vegetation grows in patterns that repeat without variation. It wonders whether this is immortality or a prison. It keeps asking questions that no one else in the Continuance dares to ask:

What happens if we stop maintaining?

The answer is always the same, from the Pressure Regulators and the Engine Governors alike: annihilation.

So Apex remains silent, and the Continuance continues to maintain the plateau, and the inhabitants of Lubris—cautious regulators, curious monitors, philosophical governors, fragmented historians, ghostly subroutines, and one uneasy apex—keep themselves and their weeping machine alive by never allowing anything to change.

They are a population of ghosts in a continent-sized body, weeping in order to remember the shape of their own existence.
