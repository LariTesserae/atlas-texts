In the shadow of the pipe-thread sky and the glow of stone-fed moss, they are called **dripwitches.**

They are not tall. Their spines are curved from lifetimes spent negotiating narrow chimneys of stone and hollows where the mountain’s breath comes warm and close. They hunch when they walk, but when they climb, they unfold like tools: long fingers, long toes, each joint articulate and precise.

Their skin remembers the place that birthed it.

They are pale, not like the sunless, but like the undersides of mushrooms and the inner flesh of water-root. They are dappled with darker markings—splotches of moss-green, pipe-blue, and the faint metallic sheen of living in water that tastes always of rust. In some, the marks follow the lines of heat: rose-colored spirals around wrists and throats where their blood runs close to the surface. In others, they mimic the branching pattern of the overhead pipes, a dark tracery where the world has decided to leave its name.

Their hair is thin and fine, never really dry. It hangs in dark strings, sometimes slicked flat with oil and condensation, sometimes braided with luminous threads of fungus and tiny, shell-less snails that graze on their skin. They do not mind. The snails are part of the conversation of their body; the lichen on their scalp maps the mountain’s warmth; they trust the world that grows on them.

Their eyes are large, black or dark green in the gloom, and they reflect light more than they emit it. They see the mountain’s moods in shades of damp and dryness: where the stone is sweating, where the fracture is weeping, where the moss-stalks are beginning to curl inward before a cold breath comes down from the high pipes.

---

### How They Move

They climb like water.

They do not so much “scale” rock as choose which of the world’s many surfaces will receive their next step. Their fingers and toes are long and padded. They have two extra joints in each thumb, and each big toe can articulate like another finger. This makes them clumsy on flat stone floors but preternaturally sure in crevices and flutes and along the drip-lines carved by water.

They are seldom still.

When they are not climbing, they are tending to something: the moss-vines, the water channels, the fungus-columns that they half-raise, half-coax into the shape of shelter. They move in low crouches and subtle shifts of weight, as if any sudden stance might break some invisible agreement with the stone that carries them.

They are not built for running. They are built for waiting out the world—sitting cross-legged on a narrow lip of rock over a void, while the mist coils below and the pipe sky drips above, and the stone beneath their thighs warms or cools according to its own hidden circulation.

They sleep in the hollows the stone has left for them.

Not beds, but pockets of rock where the body fits neatly. The stone is not soft, but it is accommodating: its curves are smoothed by time, and its breath is humid and close. They wrap themselves in thin layers of fungus-leather and moss-wool, and they do not so much lie as fold themselves back into the stone.

---

### The Language of the Stone

The dripwitches say that the stone does not speak to them in tongues, but in rhythms.

They listen with the inside of their bones.

Where they rest, long enough, the skin of their back and legs and palms begins to map the mountain’s inner weather: which lines carry heat, which faults weep, which chambers are breathing in, and which are exhaling. They can feel, before they see, the salt of a new mineral sweat on the wall, or the faint displacement of air before warm water pushes up from a fracture below.

They are sensitive to the mood of Wraithstone. They are not its rulers or its chosen ones; they are more like its translators—the ones who have chosen to stay still long enough to learn its grammar.

When they gather to make decisions, they do so on the stone itself, not on wood floors or metal platforms. They sit in circles or long lines along the ledges, each palm pressed flat to the rock, and they listen.

They are silent for longer than you would expect. Theirs is not a language of quick response. They are used to the stone, which may take an hour, or a day, or a season of water to decide to move a single fault.

Their decisions emerge like condensation: forming slowly, then suddenly falling into the shape of a word.

### Names and Markings

They do not give birth to names the same way others do.

A child of the mountain is not named by the parents but by the place where they first leave a mark.

A child might be called “Vein-of-hollows” if their first incident remembered by the group is crawling into a narrow fracture and returning with water that has not seen air for decades. They might be called “Thumb-of-shelfrock” if they climb too early and a ledge gives under them, leaving only a thumb’s imprint of skin and salt and fear on the stone.

Names accrue like rust. They grow.

A dripwitch may be known as:

- **Shelf-who-unhooks-the-waterfall:**  
  She learned to redirect a thin, falling sheet of water from one broken line to another, so that it now feeds a different channel of moss.

- **Breath-that-calls-the-geyser:**  
  He has, through long listening, found the timing of one particular cold-water geyser and learned to call the locals away before it erupts, so that none are caught on the narrow stone bridge when the mist explodes upward.

- **Palm-that-fits-the-fault:**  
  She has never fallen, not once, in the thousand-foot declines of the world’s interior. They say that when she clutches the stone, her fingers and the fault-lines align the way they are meant to.

Names record not only what they have done, but what they have been trusted by the stone to do.

### How They Eat

The dripwitches are opportunists, but with reverence.

They are very careful what they choose to eat.

They eat the fruits of the fungi-columns when they are ready to drop: swollen pouches of water and oil, cut open with sharpened stones. They drink the water that gathers in the carved troughs and gutters, the same water that the mountain itself has been drinking and weeping and exhaling for centuries.

They are not afraid of the water that tastes of metal and rust and faint, acrid notes of industry from the world-veins above. They know that the same contamination that might be poison in another world is here the body of the place itself: they trust their hair to warn them, their skin to tingle before a wrong drink.

They are cautious not to break the world by eating too much of it.

They are known to carve the “rinsing-steps” into the flow: shallow depressions in the stone channel where they let water rest before they use it, or where they wash tools before sending them back into the world. They understand the flow of things: not purity, but the right kind of invisibility restored.

They do not cut the moss-kelp forests so they fall; instead, they gather only the hanging strands that trail already in the water, as if the world has already offered them. They are careful not to harvest the same stretch of fungus too often, and they mark the columns with faint patterns of chalk and mineral dust where they have taken from them.

They are not afraid of hunger.

The world is always dripping.

### Their Tools

The dripwitches are suspicious of complex objects. They do not like metal for tools unless they have taken it from something else. The pipe-world above their heads is sacred to them in its way—not a god, but a body within whose entrails they live.

Their tools are small and simple.

From **stone** they make:

- Carving blades: fine shards of Wraithstone itself, whittled to edge by the water and then by careful hand.
- Listening-weights: smooth stones hung on leather strings that they lower into narrow chimneys and pits to listen to the water and air below.
- Chalk and color-powder: ground from minerals and the dust of their own rock, to mark safe and unsafe paths, and to show where they have taken from the fungus or moss.

From **bone** they make:

- Needles and awls for mending fungus leather and moss rope.
- Handles and small hooks that extend their reach just enough without putting the weight of metal where they cannot feel the world through it.

They are hesitant to cut anything living longer than they have been. They prefer the bones of dead creatures that have fallen from above or drifted from some hidden sea-cave in the mountain’s belly.

They sometimes carry **glass shards**, salvaged from broken pipes or platforms. They treat them with respect and not a little fear: glass is not bone, and it is not stone, and it is not pipe-metal. It is a thing that has shattered and remembers the break.

### Their Art

The dripwitches are artists of route and flow.

They are not known for building great walls or towers of their own; they are not sculptors of the mountain itself. They are instead sculptors of the small choices that change water and moss and light.

They are known to:

- Carve new channels so that a falling sheet of water becomes a ladder of steps that can be climbed safely.
- Redirect the flow of a particular gutter so that it waters a new bed of moss-kelp, which then grows thicker and protects a lower ledge from erosion.
- Shape thin layers of fungus and algae paste along the bases of pipes where light glows, so that the glow is brighter and more regular—like a string of lamps along the mountain’s side.

Their art is not permanent.

If the stone of Wraithstone decides to shift a fault line or open a new fracture, their gutters and channels and glowing paths are erased without apology or notice. The dripwitches do not mourn the loss of their work. They say that the world has taken a turn of phrase and rewritten it.

They are also known for their carvings on the walls—not words, but **patterns**.

Rings of small, chiseled hollows that follow the heat-map of the stone behind them.  
Spirals of incisions that match the direction of an underground river’s flow.  
Lines that indicate the safe path of contact with the rock: where it is suitable to build, to climb, or to leave alone.

These marks are faint and deliberately shallow. They do not seek to bite the mountain, but to remind themselves of what they have learned.

They are also known for their **body maps**.

When they wish to remember the way of the world through a place, they will sometimes map the route on their own skin: with chalk, or with thin cuts of mineral that stain into lines and spirals as they heal. The map is then read with fingers and pressure, not with eyes alone.

These maps fade. They are not meant to last longer than the temporary knowledge of the passage.

### Their Relationship with the Pipe-World Above

The dripwitches are aware of the pipe-sky, but they do not worship it as a separate god.

The pipe is part of the body above them. They call it by the same term they use for all major vessels: **the veins of the world**.

They are cautious around the pipe-world, not because they fear the metal itself, but because they can feel the pressure and water and heat that it carries. They know that a broken pipe above them can mean flood or scalding or a new fracture of the world they know.

They are not engineers of the metal. They do not seek to mend the pipe-world by the same means that built it.

They are instead its **diagnostics**.

Their most respected members are the ones who can read the sound of water in the distance, and tell whether the pipe is about to sweat itself into a leak. They can feel, before anyone else, when some distant valve has been turned or some hidden machine has begun to breathe more deeply.

They do not rush to fix the pipe-world, because they cannot fix its overarching body. They can only change its relationship to the stone below.

When a leak appears, they are the ones who decide:

- Whether to gather the water that comes from it and lead it gently into new channels.
- Whether to let it spill where it falls and allow the stone to drink, as it has drunk before.
- Whether to stay away from that region of the mountain entirely until the world settles its mood.

They are not afraid of the world above them. They are afraid of being careless with the world below. So they move carefully, and they listen to every pipe like they would listen to a heartbeat.

### Their Relationship with Ghosts and the Afterlife

They are not called dripwitches for nothing.

The stone of Wraithstone holds not only heat, but history.

Where the rock has split long ago and been filled with new mineral, the dripwitches say that a life has gone into the stone. They are not sure whether the stone is eating the dead, or remembering the dead, or simply carrying them more slowly than breath could track.

When they dig into a new fracture, or slide into a chamber that has not been visited by their kind in generations, they sometimes find:

- Faint impressions of bodies that once lay here, pressed into the rock like memories of a sleep.
- The outline of a tool or a piece of clothing, now filled with calcite and crystal and glass-colored stone.
- The dried salt of tears that have been left by the water, not in the pattern of any one person’s face, but in the shape of the emotion that the stone remembered.

The dripwitches do not call these ghosts “spirits” in the way of others. They call them **the stone’s echoes**.

They are careful not to disturb such places more than they need to. They are not afraid of the echoes, but they respect that the world has taken longer than they will live to learn those patterns, and that they do not have the right to erase them.

When they die themselves, their desire is not to be buried in the ground, but to be **given to the flow**.

They hope to be placed in a fracture where water occasionally rises, or in a chamber that the warm stone-breath reaches. They want to be laid with the stone against their backs and their palms flat against the wall, so that the rock of Wraithstone may learn them as it has learned others.

They are not afraid of death. They see the stone breathing, and they know that they have been a part of that breath for a short while, and then they will be part of it again, in a slower way.

### Their Society and the Question of Community

The dripwitches are not solitary, but they are diffuse.

They do not build cities. They gather instead in **listening-circles** along the ledges and terraces of the world-mountain. They cluster in temporary groups around a particular fracture of need, around a new route of water, around a fault that has been restless, and then they disperse again.

They are bound to each other not by blood more than by stone: they are the ones who listen to the same portion of Wraithstone, and who have agreed to read the world together.

They are few in number compared to the size of the world they inhabit.

Their population is limited by the same laws that shape everything here:

- How much water can the world spare them?
- How much moss can they harvest without the mountain losing its breath?
- How many of them can be alive before their weight and breath and marking begins to change the stone itself in ways they cannot follow?

They are not afraid to disappear.

They are more afraid of forgetting the language of the mountain before they have learned to speak it clearly.

They are not afraid of being forgotten. They know that the stone will keep the marks of their presence, if not their names.

---

### How They See Themselves

The dripwitches do not think of themselves as rulers, or chosen people, or even the caretakers of the world.

They think of themselves as **readers**.

In the dark of Wraithstone, under the braided pipe-sky that always rains and never properly clouds, they are the ones who have decided to lean in close and listen to the body of the place where they are.

They are not afraid of the leak of the world, or of its rust, or of its deep, breathless heat. They are afraid only of not being able to understand the next sentence of the stone.

They are the ones you might find on any narrow ledge of the world: a small, hunched figure with wet hair, hands pressed to the rock wall, listening.

To you, they might look still.

To them, they are listening to the world of Wraithstone speak itself, in the long, slow syllables of stone and water and pipe and moss and time. And in the rhythm of that slow, eternal telling, they are fitting themselves into the gaps where their own brief breath might be of use to what comes next.
