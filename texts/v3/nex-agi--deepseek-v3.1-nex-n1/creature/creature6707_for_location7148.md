What lives in this place does not move to arrive anywhere.  

No one here is traveling *from* or *toward*. They are where the currents are not taking them but merely holding them in place, in the slow, thick water of an ocean that has already decided its shape.

At the top of this world, under the faintly glowing ceiling, the algae-moss are the oldest inhabitants of stone. They are not stone themselves, not exactly, but they have grown into it and through it until they *remember* being stone. Theirs is a thin, flat consciousness spread across kilometers of ceiling like a pale, luminous memory. Each filament remembers its place in the membrane, and remembers the deaths of neighboring patches—the long, dark scars where other systems fell and did not come back until the slow, green thoughts of replacement algae rewrote them.  

They are a ceiling of mind-patches that stitch together without argument or hurry. They are not aware of you. They are aware of pressure, of temperature, of the infinitesimal life of the water touching them, and they are aware of a long, continuous contentment: that the ceiling is not failing, the light is enough, and the change will never be faster than they can absorb.

—

In the hanging root-rivers, which are not just trees but the architecture of the world, a different creature lives.  

If you could call it one creature. The roots are not individuals; they are one long, slow body that has grown into its own skeleton and meat and shell of wood. Theirs is a thoughtless awareness of gravity and flow and hunger. They feel the scrape of countless teeth along their polished surfaces the way you might feel a persistent itch you cannot reach to scratch.

They are the oldest living matter, and they have grown into themselves as a room of bone. They are memory without story: they remember the curve of the wall, where they braced and split and fused again, the long pull of water against their length, and the pressure of the dark below where they end and the water goes on without them.  

Slowly, very slowly, they grow into the empty spaces between themselves—not to reach, but to be reached. They are content to be bitten by fish forever, because being bitten is all that keeps them polished.

—

The fish—schools and drifters—are the pulse of this place.

The smallest ones are like the room’s breath. They live in warm clouds of sound, each of them a fragment of glass with tiny teeth. They do not think of the ocean as an ocean. They think of it as “near-root” and “away-from-root,” as “bright enough” and “too dark,” as “my teeth are scraping” and “not scraping.” They are almost always scraping.

They are not afraid of the deep below because it is too dark to be a threat; it is just not where they are. They are not afraid of the big fish that pass through their school because they have been eaten by bigger fish for so long that they no longer have a word for it. Being eaten is just a change of texture in the cloud of sound.

Some larger fish are like long, slow thoughts of muscle. They are thick-bodied eels and wide-mouthed drifters that never rush to catch anything. They are not hunting; they are waiting for the hunt to happen near them. They drift through the water with the patience of things that have already eaten everything they need for a long time.

Attached to their skin are tiny cleaner creatures that pick the parasites from their scales. These cleaners are not afraid of the mouths they stand on. They are not aware of being owned by a larger life. They are aware of teeth, and skin, and food, and that they must be careful to move when the body moves or else the world around them becomes crushing.

On the skin of a few of these big fish live special patches of algae-lice that glow faintly, same as the ceiling does. These are the only moving lights in the room, and they drift like distant stars between the root-pillars. The fish that wear them know nothing of being light. They know only the faint pressure of growths on their skin and the water passing over the same old routes.

There are fish with teeth on the outside of their bodies, shell-like ridges of enamel-bone that clatter against rock-root and against each other as they swim. This is the sound you hear: the *click-click-click* of room scraping room.  
These are not armor in the sense of protection; they are armor in the sense of habit. The ridges have been there for so long that the fish can’t remember whether they grew teeth to protect themselves or whether they simply grew to fit the sound of their teeth against the world.

—

In the root walls, the worms are the quiet makers of holes.

Each row of holes is not a pattern to them; it is a sequence of time and appetite. They are almost blind, and their body knows the turn of the root’s grain, the pressure of the mucus they secrete, and the shape their own length must leave to be safe from the teeth of the swimming fish and from each other.

They are the same length as the holes they leave, over and over again. They do not know that to someone else their rows look like letters of some unknown alphabet. They are aware of “before me,” “the hole I am making now,” and “after I move forward and leave behind the shell my skin made.” They are not aware of being read or being seen.  

The mucus in their burrows hardens into the same shell-like skin that covers the older fish and parts of the root-architecture. This is not because they intend to build the world; it is because the world of films and filaments and teeth and water and time is already made of shell.

—

Between the roots, the animal-chain bridges are the world’s half-made tools.

Each tiny, shelled animal is no larger than a fingernail, and the chain of them is a long, white arc of living bone. They are aware of “holding my neighbor” and “being held by my neighbor.” They are not aware of being a bridge.  

From time to time, one of them dies. Its shell softens and cracks under the pressure of the room, and then fails. The neighboring animals on either side tighten their hold, and soon new young animals grow into the broken place, taking the shape that the dead body left behind.

They are not aware of being repaired or rebuilt. They are aware of being in the place of the last one who was here.  

They are not aware that they make a path across dark stretches of water between root-pillars. They only know that they must not let go.

—

In the open water between the root-structures and the schools, you might find what you came to describe.

You are there, nowhere else but there:

You are sitting in the cool, dark water of a world that has already chosen its shape.  

You are not under a sky; you are under a ceiling of faintly glowing memory-skin that remembers the deaths of other algae and the growth of new ones. Above you, the light of trapped living things has been pressed into a pattern so slow it is almost indistinguishable from stone.

Around you rise the trunks of the world’s living body—root-rivers of fused tree and bone that have grown into themselves and never stopped growing, because to stop would be to change, and change is something that happened before this room became what it is.

The fish move through you like passing thoughts of other people’s minds. Some of them are clouds of tiny teeth and sound, scraping the world they live in. Others are long, slow, muscle-log drifters, whose sleeping bodies make the room seem larger and deeper than it could ever be without them.

You are sitting in a place that is not exactly the center and not exactly the edge of anything.  

You are between the teeth of rooms and the shell of memory.

You are the one who is listening.

You are the one who hears the sound of the room of water and root and fish and worm and shell-bridge as the sound of your own blood inside you, if you could hear yourself from the outside.

You are the one who is here to say that this place does not live alone.  

It does not live to you or for you.

It is the ocean that has come to rest in an old, shelled house, and you are the one who, for a little while, is allowed to sit inside its body and hear it.
