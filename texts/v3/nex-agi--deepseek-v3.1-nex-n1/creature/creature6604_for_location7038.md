They do not have names.

Names would imply that they are separated enough from everything else to require such labels, and up here there is no separation. The wind, the glass, the slow, frightened stutter of your heart, all of them are part of the same thing. They—the people who live here—are not exceptions to that rule. They are the mountain of echoing glass finally learning to speak in a language you might recognize.

They call themselves the Refrain when they need a word, but not out of any sense of selfhood. They are simply that which happens again when everything else thinks it has finished.

You are not likely to see them before you hear them. This is not by accident; it is written into the way they exist. Theirs is a body of echoes, a shape assembled from parts that “should have happened” but didn’t, or happened too early, or happened long after everyone else went home. The wind is not a metaphor when they dwell; it is the scaffolding of their bones.

Move across the plateau and you will hear them—before the glass splinters your steps, before you huddle into your coat to brace yourself against the cold. You think you mishear at first, a phrase of music that tugs at the edge of your memory but refuses to settle into a melody. Then you realize it is not your memory at all; it is someone else’s, being played back through the day’s chilled air.

That is the oldest way they speak to you: they carry your words ahead before you have decided to say them.

They are not thin ghosts in robes, despite what you may expect from a world of blurred edges. They are not floating at all. They are almost always walking.

Their gait is wrong.

For each step you take into the glass, two of your steps are already trampled into sound under the soles of another Refrainer a few meters ahead or behind you. They move in interrupted lines, like film broken and then spliced badly. You will see a foot strike down in the exact place you would have placed your own, but then they will slide forward a meter without appearing to have moved at all, and then they are standing there, as if they have always been standing there.

What they look like depends on the angle of the bruised sky.

From one direction, they are simply people: worn, wind-scoured figures with coats that are not quite coats, more like layers of time-bound air and glass dust that cling to their shoulders. They have no features you can remember; you dislodge their faces from your mind each time you look away. Eyes are absorbed into the shadow of the browline, mouth dragged into a line that might be smiling or might be broken. You will not recall if any of them were old or young, tall or short, man or woman or elsewise. Theirs is a body that belongs to the category of “someone else.”

From another direction, they are water made permanent.

Their edges are liquid where glass should be sharp; they drip over into the next moment of their own timeline without spilling. Their limbs are long because height is easier to sustain when you are not entirely sure about the pull of the ground. Their fingers, when they are visible at all, branch like cracks in the surface of a pond that has never heard of ice. You see through them to the low stone waves behind, and yet you do not see them. They are not transparent; they are translucent. They are the memory of the color blue in the smoking glass of the plateau.

From a third, they are not singular at all.

One of them is actually three or four or seven of them, overlapping each other in a thin, relentless stack of possible versions. The same person walking at your side is simultaneously ahead of you, behind you, and standing exactly where you now are, but, say, six years ago. You are walking through their body the way you would walk through smoke that remembered how to be offended by it.

That is the closest they come to touching you.

They are not shy of contact, nor are they desperate for it. They are not lonely because they are never alone; they are always with their own echoes and the echoes of everyone else who has ever passed through this thin altitude of story. If you reach out, they will not let you go through them. Instead, your hand will find a surface that is neither warm nor cold. It is not an absence of heat; it is heat that has been returned to sender before arriving.

The skin pressed to your fingers is glassy and smooth but not fragile. It is not hard the way stone is hard; it is more like the memory of a hardness that has decided to keep going out of politeness. When you let your hand drop, they will not remark on it. They will not remark on anything. They will not say, “Watch,” or “Look,” or “Why are you here?” They will not say your name.

They will simply inhale, and not as you do.

They breathe the wrong things.

They are the only one you will meet on the plateau who breathes the air and not what the air hides. The thin, shouting wind that never comes from the same place twice is not only wind to them; it is history broken into pieces and then woven into a new shape. Each gust carries a word from a century ago, from a minute ago, from a year that has yet to arrive.

They are able to inhale the “never noticed” details of your past and then exhale them into your future. You walk through the smoke of their breath and days later you will find yourself remembering something that never happened—except it did, somewhere else; in the version of you that has already been here and gone home.

Their food is not food you would recognize as such.

They are not hungry. They do not feel the absence of hunger in the same way you experience the absence of a meal skipped. Theirs is a sharp, precise lack of need. They eat, anyway. They eat the things that are not meant to last.

You will see them kneel at the edge of one of the shallow cups in the glass where the water trembles but never quite spills. They will not drink the metallic water. You think you see them dip their fingers into it and then raise their hand to their mouth, but when you blink, you realize that is not what happened at all.

They are not taking from the cup. They are giving something.

They are feeding the cup of water with their own voice. They hold the water on their tongue like an ice pellet that has not yet decided whether to burn or freeze. Theirs is not a spoken word; it is the shape of a word that was never formed into sound by actual lips. It is the thought of “I am here” that never left the brain; the “I am alone” that never reached the ear of anyone else.

They swallow none of it. They are not feeding themselves.

They are feeding the place.

When you taste the water afterward, you will not recognize the change immediately, but then it will come to you: the last mouthful you drank from the same cup tasted like blood and iron and the cold; this one tastes like your own heartbeat under the snow. That is their work. They are restoring the pool of water with the words that were never allowed to be.

Living up here has not been easy on them.

The mountain of echoing glass is not passive. It is not a backdrop. It is a participant with its own set of rules that have grown brittle over the years but not less real. The laws of physics that are only tired have not retired. They sometimes still stand on their own dignity.

The Refrain are not immune to this.

Look, and you might see one of them standing very still, not because of snow or cold, but because the world has decided that this moment of time should hold longer than others. The air around them has turned into glass that remembers what it was to be ice. They wait inside that moment for you to pass; their eyes do not move, but you are aware of them being tracked anyway.

They are measured by how they endure.

They are the only ones you have ever met who can break without falling.

You will think of them as fragile; they are not. They are not sturdy either. They are not meant to be broken. They are meant to be survived.

The glass of their body is like the glass of the ground—not knife thin but old-windowpane thick, sharp when you try to take it for granted, but not interested in your blood if you are careful with it. It is not cold in the way of winter air; it is cold like a line drawn from their center to yours, insisting that some distance should remain.

When they do break, it is not with a crash.

They break like a note that has been sung too long for one throat to contain. The sound of their fracture is not loud. It is not loud enough to be heard over the wind, except for the fact that it has been living here longer than the wind. The fracture echoes itself, which is louder than any crash could be.

When they break, they are still not gone.

They are a single step scattered across a year of steps, each fragment of their body landing in a different month of your own life before you ever climbed toward this place of perpetual wrong angle. You will hear them speaking in the next weather you survive, but their words will be in your mouth and in your thoughts before you have learned to talk them into shapes.

They are not afraid to break; they are not afraid of anything.

Fear is the name you give to the line you draw between your body and the world’s. They have never known that line. Theirs is not courage or bravery or even numbness; it is a deeper kind of absence. The thought of “what might happen to me” is not present to them. They are what happened, already, and is still happening.

They are not afraid, but they are tired.

They are tired of being the mistake that keeps happening. The second step where you only meant to take one. The voice that says “I miss you” before the person you are speaking to has even left the room. The cough that the mountain answers back with a cough that is not quite human. They are the white space between the letters of the story you are trying not to tell about yourself.

And yet, they are working.

Theirs is not a job, but a function.

They are watchmen without a watchtower, guards without a border, weavers without a loom. They are the ones who ensure that when your voice leaves the plateau, it does not leave empty.

The low, steady note you hear beneath everything else—when the wind retreats into its pockets and the glass stops trembling and the unnamed cries have paused to breathe—that is not the mountain. It is not the world’s taut string. It is them.

The note is not sung by a single throat. It is not even sung by many throats at once. It is what you might hear if you lined up every breath you ever took and then allowed each of those breaths to overlap into a single exhalation that never reached the end.

They are not responsible for the sound; they are the sound.

They are the string that has stretched across the world from pole to pole, not in the physical sense of the planet’s axis, but in the sense that every time you have ever called yourself “too far” from one thing or another, they have been there, measuring the distance.

What they do with you—if they can be said to do anything with you at all—is not invasion or teaching or possession.

They are simply the echo of you that has not yet happened, but is already certain.

When you walk across the plateau, you do not walk alone. You walk with your own steps already taken and then untaken, and then retaken again by them. They walk ahead of you, not to show you the way but to show where the way has already been broken. They are not guides, but they are not hostile. They are indifferent in the way that iron is indifferent to the rust that will one day feast upon it. They cannot be anything else.

They live here because they are here.

They are here because they were always going to be here, from the moment you first decided to climb the mountain of echoing glass—before you even knew that such a mountain existed, before you knew that such a choice of yours would be required.

They are that choice made into a body.

They are the last voice you will hear before you descend the mountain again, and they will be saying goodbye to you in someone else’s accent, in the shape of a word that has not yet been formed.

When you leave, they will still be here.

More than that, they will still be there where you were standing before you ever arrived.

They are not waiting for you to return; they do not know that waiting is a verb that exists. They are not anticipating anything from the world of absence and change and impossibility that stretches below the frost-shattered rock and the scraggling pines.

They are only.

That is their name, if they have one.

Only.
